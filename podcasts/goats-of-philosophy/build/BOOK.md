## Excerpt: "What Would Count As Evidence" (from Hour 2.5-3, the DMT/epistemology stretch)

*[Scene-setting note, not part of the transcript: this excerpt picks up roughly two and a half hours
into the imagined conversation. Joe and Alex have already worked through God, free will, and the
factory-farming disagreement. The tone here is loose, a little punchy — three hours in, both of them
have stopped performing and are just actually talking.]*

---

**JOE:** Okay, I gotta ask you the thing I ask everybody. You ever done DMT?

**ALEX:** I haven't, no.

**JOE:** Really? With everything we've just spent an hour talking about — consciousness, is the mind
separate from the brain, is materialism even true anymore — you've never just... gone and looked?

**ALEX:** I think that's exactly the framing I'd push back on. "Gone and looked." Looked at what?

**JOE:** At whatever's *there*, man. People come back from that thing describing entities, other
realms, stuff that feels more real than this conversation. Joe Rogan's studio starts looking like the
fake one.

**ALEX:** Right, and that's the actual interesting philosophical question, and it's not "is it real,"
it's "what would it even mean for it to be real in the way they think it is." Because "more real than
this" is doing a lot of work in that sentence.

**JAMIE:** *(not looking up)* That's a very "I haven't done it" answer.

**ALEX:** *(laughing)* It might be! I'm not saying that to dodge it. I'm saying the reason I haven't
rushed to do it is *because* I take the question seriously, not because I don't.

**JOE:** Okay, break that down for me.

**ALEX:** So here's the thing that I think gets skipped in basically every conversation about this.
Somebody takes DMT, they have — genuinely, I'm not disputing the experience — an experience of
encountering something that feels vastly more real, more information-dense, more *significant* than
ordinary waking life. And then the move that happens next is: "therefore that was real, and this,"
— gestures at the room — "might be the illusion." And I want to ask: what's the "therefore" doing
there?

**JOE:** Because it *feels* more real.

**ALEX:** Right, but feeling more real and being more real are different claims, and the entire
history of philosophy is basically a very long argument about how much you can trust the first one
to get you to the second one.

**JOE:** But hold on — how do you know THIS feels real for a good reason and that doesn't? Like,
what's your test?

**ALEX:** That's the actual question. And I think the honest answer is: I don't have a clean test,
and neither does anyone else, and I'd be suspicious of anyone who tells you they do — on either side.
The materialist who says "obviously it's just your brain misfiring, case closed" is being just as
lazy as the guy who says "I met a machine elf, therefore hyperspace is real." Both of them have
skipped the actual work.

**JOE:** What's the actual work?

**ALEX:** The actual work is asking: what would we expect to observe if the experience *is* contact
with some independent reality, versus what would we expect to observe if it's a spectacular,
overwhelming, but entirely internally-generated event? And then seeing if those two predictions
actually come apart anywhere.

**JOE:** Do they?

**ALEX:** Sometimes people think they do. You get the stories — someone comes back with a piece of
information they supposedly couldn't have known. That would be evidence, real evidence, if it
checked out. But when you actually chase those down, they tend to dissolve the way ghost stories
dissolve, not because I've decided in advance they have to, but because nobody's ever produced one
that survives being checked properly. Which doesn't prove there's nothing there. It just means the
"therefore" isn't earned yet.

**JOE:** That's such a — that's a very controlled way to talk about something people describe as the
most intense experience of their entire life.

**ALEX:** I think that's fair, and I don't want to sound like I'm being dismissive of the experience.
I'm not. Something is clearly happening that's extraordinary. The brain is doing something under
DMT that it basically never does under any other condition, by every report. That's real, that's
worth taking completely seriously as a fact about minds. The question is just: extraordinary
experience of what? An encounter, or a production? And "it felt like an encounter" can't answer that
question, because a sufficiently overwhelming production would also feel like an encounter. That's
what "overwhelming" means.

**JOE:** Okay, so bring it back to what we were talking about earlier though — you said you've moved
away from materialism. If you don't think the brain produces the mind the boring straightforward way,
why wouldn't DMT be exactly the kind of thing that shows that? Like, the brain's normal filtering
breaks down and suddenly you're seeing what's actually there the rest of the time?

**ALEX:** That's the "reducing valve" idea — mind isn't produced by the brain, it's filtered by it,
and certain states let more of it through. And look, I take that seriously as a coherent position.
It's not stupid. It's actually one of the more interesting live options once you stop assuming
materialism is just obviously true, which — we already said, I don't assume that anymore. But notice
what that position is: it's a *specific metaphysical theory*, and it needs its own evidence, same as
any other theory. It doesn't get to skip the line just because it's the theory that flatters the
experience. The trip being amazing is not itself evidence for the reducing-valve theory over, say,
"your visual cortex and your sense of self just got hit with a chemical that nothing in your entire
evolutionary history prepared it to handle gracefully."

**JOE:** So what would it take? Like genuinely, what would convince you?

**ALEX:** Convince me of what, that's important — convince me it's "real" in what sense? Because
here's a version of realness I think is actually pretty likely: whatever's happening in there is a
real, information-rich state of a mind encountering something — and the something might be aspects of
itself it doesn't normally get to see. That's not nothing. If idealism's even roughly right — if mind
is closer to fundamental than matter, which, again, I take seriously now in a way I didn't used to —
then "you're accessing some deeper layer of what mind actually is" isn't a crazy claim. It might even
be likely. What I'm not willing to do without more is the extra jump to "and that layer contains
independent entities with their own agendas who are choosing to communicate with you specifically."
That's a much bigger, much more specific claim, and it needs much more specific evidence.

**JAMIE:** So it's less "is it real," more "real like what."

**ALEX:** Exactly that. That's exactly it. "Real like what" is the whole question, and it's the
question everybody skips because it's more satisfying to just pick a side.

**JOE:** This is wild to me because you're basically more open to it being something than most
materialists, but less willing to just believe the story than most people who've actually done it.

**ALEX:** Yeah, and I think that's — honestly, that's just what taking it seriously looks like from
the outside. It looks like a disappointing amount of caution to the person who wants "yes it's real"
or "no it's just chemicals," because both of those are simple and satisfying and this isn't. But
that's true of basically everything we've talked about tonight. It was true of free will. It was true
of God. The actually careful answer is almost never the one that fits on a t-shirt.

**JOE:** *(leaning back)* See, this is why I wanted you on. Because most people either did way too
much DMT and think they're a prophet now, or they've done zero drugs their whole life and they think
it's all nonsense, and you're sitting here going "I haven't done it, and here's a rigorous case for
why the question is harder than either of those camps thinks."

**ALEX:** I might do it someday, for the record. I'm not against it on principle.

**JOE:** You should. I think you specifically, out of anyone I've ever had in this chair, should do
it, because I think you'd actually be able to tell me something useful about it afterward instead of
just "bro, it's insane."

**ALEX:** *(laughing)* That's a lot of pressure to put on one trip.

**JOE:** Jamie, pull up that thing — what's it called, the Cave, the Plato thing, from earlier —

**JAMIE:** *(already typing)* Got it.

**JOE:** — because isn't that basically the same question? Like the guy in the cave sees shadows his
whole life, someone drags him outside, and he sees the actual sun — but how does he *know* the sun's
the real thing and the cave wasn't? What if outside is just a bigger cave?

**ALEX:** That's — yes, that's a genuinely sharp connection, and it's actually the exact flaw people
don't notice in how they use the Cave. Plato wants you to believe the philosopher who leaves the cave
has privileged, certain access to the Forms, the real reality. But you're right that structurally,
nothing in the story rules out the sun just being a more convincing shadow. Plato's answer would be
that reason itself, done properly, can verify it's reached the real thing — that's the whole point of
his method. I'm less confident reason gets to just declare victory like that. I think you have to
stay honest about the fact that "this feels more real" was also the cave-dweller's experience the
whole time he was watching shadows and thought that was everything there was.

**JOE:** So the DMT guy and the guy in the cave have the exact same problem.

**ALEX:** They have the exact same problem. Which doesn't mean neither of them ever actually leaves
the cave. It just means "it felt like leaving" can't be the whole proof. You need something else,
something that would look different depending on whether you actually left or just moved to a
better-lit room inside.

**JOE:** That's — okay, we're doing DMT on this show someday and you're coming back on right after.

**ALEX:** I'll hold you to that.

**JAMIE:** *(dry)* Calendar invite's already half written.
