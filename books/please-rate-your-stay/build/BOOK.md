# Please Rate Your Stay

The room was beige — not a colour so much as an absence of anyone having decided. Two chairs, a laminate desk, a water cooler that had never once been used and knew it. She was sitting down before she remembered arriving, holding a clipboard she had not been handed.

Behind the desk, a man in a polo shirt was eating a sandwich with the specific joylessness of someone on their fourth billion year of the same shift. His badge said GREG. Under that, in smaller letters: *Host Relations. Ask me about our loyalty programme.*

"Take your time with the form," he said. "Most people do."

She looked down. At the top, in a cheerful sans-serif:

> **PLEASE RATE YOUR STAY**
>
> *Your feedback helps us maintain quality across all realities.*

"I've just died," she said. It came out as a question.

"Checked out," said Greg. "We prefer *checked out*. Died tests badly."

## Section A — Overall Satisfaction

*How would you rate your recent stay on Earth (Sol-3, Standard Density)? Five stars = exceeded expectations.*

She hovered the pencil. Somewhere in her chest — she still had the sensation of a chest, which seemed like an oversight — a whole life was coming back online in the wrong order. A tea towel wrapped around warm bread. Her son at four, furious, refusing to wear a coat in November. A dog called Kite who ate an entire birthday cake and lived. The precise sound her marriage made when it stopped, which was not a bang but the fridge humming while nobody said anything for eleven minutes.

"Four," she said.

Greg didn't look up. "Four."

"It was very loud. And the whole thing hurt more than the listing suggested."

"The listing." He said it the way a man says *the manual*.

"There were photographs of mountains," she said. "There was a photograph of a woman laughing on a beach. There was nothing in it about kidney stones, or watching your mother forget your name in stages, or the tax year."

"Amenities are bundled," said Greg. "It's in the terms."

## Section B — Amenities

*Please indicate which of the following you made use of during your stay.*

The list ran to two pages. She read it with the growing indignation of a woman reading a bill.

- Weather.
- Dogs.
- The smell of rain arriving on hot tarmac.
- Music — all of it, but especially one song at one specific traffic light in 1994.
- Other bodies, warm.
- Grief.
- Bread.
- The knowledge that this ends.

"Grief is listed as an amenity," she said.

"Grief is *premium*," said Greg. "Only available on this property. Do you know what people pay for depth? You can't get grief in the higher densities. You can't get *toast* in the higher densities. You want to know what eternity is like? Eternity is this room, forever, and everyone is fine."

He said *fine* the way you would say *asbestos*.

## Section C — Did You Experience Any Issues During Your Stay?

She had. She had experienced roughly four decades of issues, and she had, in fairness, reported almost none of them through the proper channels.

This was the part that stung, because she had spent thirty-one years as a claims adjuster. Thirty-one years of sitting at other people's kitchen tables assigning a number to the flooded garage, the stolen ring, the car folded up at the junction. *And what would you say the sentimental value was, Mrs Achterberg?* As though sentiment came with a receipt. She had been good at it. She had been kind about it, mostly, which the company did not measure and which had cost her two promotions.

And here she was on the wrong side of the laminate, being assessed.

"There's a note on your file," said Greg, "regarding the concierge."

"The what."

"Twenty-four-hour support. Included. Zero cost. You go quiet, you go inward, you sit still for ninety seconds without reaching for anything, and someone picks up. Every time. No hold music."

"Nobody told me."

"Everybody's told. It's in the pre-departure briefing." He turned a page. "You attended the briefing. You were extremely enthusiastic at the briefing. You said — and I am quoting the transcript — *how hard can it be.*"

She had no memory of this and believed it completely.

"Your usage log," Greg went on, "shows one call. Three-oh-four in the morning, aged thirty-one, in the multi-storey behind the hospital, after your father. Eleven minutes. You sat in the car with the engine off and you stopped arguing with everything for eleven whole minutes." He tapped the page. "Best-rated interaction of your entire stay. You never called again."

"I got busy."

"Everyone gets busy," said Greg, with the flatness of a man who has heard four billion people say *I got busy* and has stopped finding it interesting. "Busy is our number one amenity by usage. It is also our number one complaint. We have stopped trying to explain this to people."

## Section D — Host Feedback

*Reviews left about you by the property and by its other guests. These are provided for your information. They cannot be contested.*

She did not want to read them. She read them.

> **Five stars.** *She came every Sunday for two years after I couldn't drive anymore, and she never once made it sound like a favour. She'd bring the wrong biscuits on purpose so I'd have something to complain about.*
>
> — MOTHER

> **Two stars.** *Difficult guest. Locked. Left before anyone had properly arrived. I don't think she was cruel. I think she was frightened, and I think she would rather have been called cruel.*
>
> — SPOUSE, FMR.

> **Five stars.** *SHE CAME BACK. SHE ALWAYS CAME BACK. EVERY SINGLE TIME SHE WENT OUT THE DOOR I THOUGHT THAT IS IT, THAT IS THE END OF EVERYTHING, AND SHE ALWAYS CAME BACK.*
>
> — KITE (DOG)

> **Four stars.** *The adjuster who came about the fire. Sat in my kitchen an hour and forty minutes and only twenty of it was the form. I don't remember what she said. I remember she didn't check her watch.*
>
> — CLAIMANT, RENDLESHAM ROAD

> **Five stars.** *You were not a very good mother between when I was fourteen and when I was nineteen, and you know that. Then you were, for thirty-one years. Nobody tells you that's allowed. That you can just start being good at it late and it still counts. It counted. It counted, Mum.*
>
> — SON

She put the clipboard down on the desk very carefully, the way you set down something that might go off.

"There's no box," she said, when she could. "There's no box on the form for what to do about that."

"No," Greg agreed. "There is famously no box."

## Section E — Damages

*Please declare any damage to yourself sustained during the stay. Note: your security deposit is not at risk. Your soul was insured at full replacement value for the entire duration and was, at all times, in perfect condition.*

"Insured," she said.

"Comprehensive. No excess."

"For all of it?"

"For all of it. The whole thing, every second — it never had a scratch on it. Not once." Greg looked at her over the sandwich for the first time. "That's the joke, if you want it. You spent the entire stay defending the one thing that was never in danger. And the things that *were* in danger — the dog, the twenty years with your mother, the man in the fridge silence, your own knees — those you left lying around like a coat on a chair."

She began to write in the DAMAGES box, and then found she could not stop.

*Two vertebrae, lower back, from lifting a sofa in 1988 to impress a person who did not notice. A finger that never straightened after a car door. A heart broken twice properly and about nine times in the amateur leagues. A stretch of skin across the abdomen that a child made. A tooth. A friendship. A church. Two languages I let go rusty. My father's voice, which I can no longer hear and would trade the mountains and the beach and the laughing woman to hear for four seconds.*

She ran out of box and wrote up the side of the page.

"That's a lot of damage," she said.

Greg took the form. He read it the whole way down, and then the whole way up the side, and something happened in his face that had not happened in some time.

"Yeah," he said. "That's a good one, actually."

***

He stamped it. She watched, and understood nothing, because the scoring made no sense.

The chart on the wall — she saw it now; she had been sitting under it the entire time — was headed GUEST RATINGS, THIS QUARTER. At the bottom, down in the red, were the immaculate ones. *Left no mess. Broke nothing. Attached to no one. Returned the property in original condition.* At the top, in green, riotously green, were the wrecks. The ones who came back with a limp and a list. The ones who had clearly used every single towel.

"You're rating us on how *broken* we are?"

"We're rating you on whether you took the room," said Greg. "Anyone can stand in the doorway for eighty years. You would be amazed. We get people back with the plastic still on the sofa." He slid the form into a tray. "Four point six. Higher than you gave us, incidentally. You were a better guest than you were a customer."

"I'd have done it differently."

"They all say that too. It is the single most common exit statement. Just ahead of *oh*."

"No — I mean I'd have —" and she stopped, because the sentence had nowhere honest to go except one place. "I'd have been less careful."

Greg nodded slowly, the way you do at the one thing worth nodding at.

"Write that bit down," he said. "That's the useful one."

***

There was a door she had not noticed, because it had not been there.

"Rebooking?" said Greg. "You're eligible. Loyalty tier. Though I should flag — the property has changed since your stay. New management, and I'll be blunt, the plumbing is worse. And you don't get to keep any of this." He gestured at her, at all of her: the vertebrae, the tooth, the eleven minutes in the car park, the boy in his coatless November fury. "It's a clean form. You would walk in with nothing, and be frightened for a while, and forget every word of this conversation inside about four seconds."

"That seems designed."

"It is *entirely* designed. The forgetting is the product." He shrugged. "Nobody would cry at a film they'd written."

She stood up. Her name came back to her then, which was the last thing to arrive and the thing she would have guessed would come first — *Ruth*, plain as bread, a name for a woman who wrapped things in tea towels — and she found she was glad of it, and that being glad of it was the whole review, really; the only entry on the form that had ever mattered.

"Put me down," said Ruth Achterberg, "for something with a dog in it."

Greg was already writing.

"They all say that as well," he said. "That one we honour."

# A Note on the Poem

James McCrae's "Instructions before visiting Earth" is a briefing note handed to a soul on its way down: you are only visiting, tread lightly, don't make a mess, don't keep your soft heart in a glass case, come back with scars and stories.

This story is the paperwork on the other end.

The poem's governing metaphor is hospitality — you are a guest, the planet is not yours, you will be leaving. Put that metaphor in front of a modern reader and it stops being monastic and starts being a booking platform, with its star ratings and its damage deposits and its cheerful little forms for quantifying the unquantifiable. That is the joke. The joke is a door, and the room behind it is the same room the poem was pointing at: that a life spent protecting yourself scores nothing, and the towels are there to be used.

Its last instruction is the whole argument, and this story is only a longer way of failing to improve on it.
