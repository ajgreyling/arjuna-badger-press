# HOMO CREDENS — Master Outline

*A History of Religion, from the First Graves to 1900.*
22 chapters in 5 parts. Each chapter is written single-shot by its own author-agent;
this outline is the only continuity mechanism. **Do not contradict another chapter's brief.
Cover your beats; trust your neighbors to cover theirs.** Bridges are mandatory.

The book's through-line (state it, vary it, never abandon it): **religion is the technology
by which strangers become kin** — humanity's oldest and most successful tool for scaling
cooperation past the face-to-face band — and every chapter shows the tool being reforged
as human societies change size.

---

## PART I — THE INVENTION OF THE GODS (before writing)

### 1. The Believing Animal — (~300,000–40,000 BCE)
Why *Homo sapiens* believes. Cognitive kit: agency detection, theory of mind run amok, the
dead who persist in memory, costly signals that bind bands. Neanderthal question (Shanidar
flowers, Bruniquel cave rings) handled honestly. Thesis of the whole book planted here.
Beats: what "religion" even means (definition trouble, hold it lightly); evolution of
over-detection; ritual before belief; why only us (or not only us?); the book's map.
Bridge → the first unambiguous evidence: graves.

### 2. The First Graves — (~100,000–12,000 BCE)
Death as the engine of religion. Sungir's beaded children, Shanidar, ochre burials; cave art
as ritual space (Chauvet, Lascaux, Trois-Frères "sorcerer" — with interpretive caution);
shamanism as the first religious profession; portable gods (Venus figurines, lion-man of
Hohlenstein-Stadel). Beats: what a grave *claims* about the world; the economics of grave
goods; trance and the nervous system; ethnographic analogy and its limits.
Bridge → people settle down, and the gods settle with them.

### 3. The Temple Before the Town — (~10,000–4,000 BCE)
Göbekli Tepe flips the story: the temple may precede the farm. Neolithic revolution as a
religious event as much as an economic one. Çatalhöyük's houses of the dead (plastered
skulls, bulls); Jericho's tower; fertility, ancestors, and the new tyranny of the harvest
calendar. Beats: how you organize 500 strangers to carve a hillside without a king; grain
as obligation; ancestor cult as property law; the costs (disease, hierarchy, toil) and why
religion helped humans pay them.
Bridge → multiply the village by a thousand: the city, and gods with ledgers.

## PART II — THE BUREAUCRACY OF HEAVEN (~3500–800 BCE)

### 4. Gods with Ledgers — Sumer & Akkad (~3500–1600 BCE)
The temple as the first corporation. Uruk, Eridu, ziggurats; writing born of offering
receipts; the Enuma Elish (humans created to feed the gods — quote it); Gilgamesh and the
flood; divination as data science; personal gods as HR departments. Beats: a temple's
balance sheet; priest-kings; how a myth legitimizes an irrigation rota; Sargon and gods
who conquer; the flood tablet's later career (plant the seed for ch. 8/13).
Bridge → downriver of another river, a kingdom bets everything on never dying.

### 5. The Kingdom That Refused to Die — Egypt (~3100–1000 BCE)
Ma'at, divine kingship, and the afterlife as national industry. Mechanism feast: mummification
step-by-step; pyramid logistics; the Book of the Dead as cheat-sheet (weighing of the heart —
quote it); Osiris; the democratization of immortality; Akhenaten's failed solar monotheism as
history's first named religious revolution — and its erasure.
Bridge → east, where the sacred is not a place or a corpse but a *sound*.

### 6. Fire and Song — Vedic India & early Iran (~1500–800 BCE)
The religion of the sacrifice: Rigveda, soma, agni, ṛta (cosmic order); ritual as technology
that keeps the sun rising; Brahmin specialization; caste's ritual roots (handled carefully).
Then the Iranian cousin: Zarathustra's radical simplification — one wise lord, one lie, a
cosmic war with moral stakes, judgment, saviour, end of time. Zoroastrianism as the quiet
donor of apocalyptic furniture to the West (plant seed for ch. 13).
Bridge → in China the sacred power isn't fire — it's the ancestors, and they answer in cracks.

### 7. The Ancestors Are Watching — Shang & Zhou China (~1600–800 BCE)
Oracle bones: the mechanics (scapula, heat, crack, inscription) and the archive — history's
best-documented Bronze Age religion. Ancestor sacrifice, Di, then the Zhou coup and the
Mandate of Heaven — theology as constitutional law: heaven audits kings. Beats: religion
without "faith"; ritual correctness (li) as social physics; bronze vessels; human sacrifice
declining and why.
Bridge → far west, a small hill people's storm god begins an unlikely climb.

### 8. A Storm God's Ambition — Canaan & Israel (~1300–800 BCE)
From the pantheon's junior weather deity to "no other gods before me." El, Baal, Asherah;
Israel emerging *from within* Canaan (archaeology vs. exodus narrative — both told honestly);
Yahweh-alone movements, prophets as street-corner critics of kings; the Ten Commandments as
treaty format (quote from the WEB translation). NOT yet monotheism — that's Babylon's gift,
ch. 13; keep the powder dry.
Bridge → across all these worlds at once, around 800–500 BCE, something snaps: the questions
turn inward.

## PART III — THE AXIAL GAMBLE (~800 BCE–100 CE)

### 9. The Great Interrogation — the Axial Age (~800–300 BCE)
The pivot chapter. Jaspers' Axial Age thesis (and its modern critics — present the debate);
iron, coinage, cities, literacy, total war; the individual soul invented; salvation replaces
harvest as religion's product. Survey the simultaneous eruptions (Buddha, Mahavira, Confucius,
Laozi, Second Isaiah, Pythagoras, Zoroaster's heirs) as one phenomenon viewed from orbit —
the detail chapters follow. Beats: why everywhere at once (trade? cities? none of the above?);
religion's customer changes from the village to the self.
Bridge → begin in the Ganges basin, with a man who sat down under a tree.

### 10. The Man Who Woke Up — Buddha, Mahavira & the śramaṇas (~600–200 BCE)
The renouncer revolt: forest philosophers against the Brahmin sacrifice economy. Upanishadic
turn (ātman/brahman, karma internalized); Mahavira's absolute nonviolence; Siddhartha's
middle way — four truths, no-self, sangha as portable community; Ashoka's imperial Buddhism
(rock edicts — quote one) as the first state marketing of a salvation religion.
Bridge → China answers the same crisis not with escape from the world but with its repair.

### 11. The Master and the Way — Confucius, Mozi, Laozi (~550–200 BCE)
Warring States anxiety; Confucius: ritual as humanism, li and ren, ancestors kept but
re-explained; Mozi's utilitarian heresy (impartial love, heaven that bills you); Laozi and
Zhuangzhi's wu-wei as anti-theology; Legalism as the cynical control group. Beats: is this
"religion"? (use the question productively); the Analects' afterlife as imperial software.
Bridge → on the Aegean, gods behave badly in excellent poetry — and philosophy is born
partly in embarrassment.

### 12. Wine-Dark Gods — Greece (~800 BCE–100 CE)
Olympians as a dysfunctional family with a great publicist (Homer); sacrifice and civic
religion (no church, no creed — religion as citizenship); Delphi's oracle (mechanism!);
mystery cults (Eleusis, Dionysus, Orphics) selling personal salvation on the side; the
philosophers' pivot — Xenophanes' critique, Plato's single Good, Socrates executed for
impiety; Alexander exports it all, and the Hellenistic marketplace of gods begins.
Bridge → in that marketplace, one exiled people is about to make the strangest claim of all:
the other gods aren't just inferior — they aren't *there*.

### 13. By the Rivers of Babylon — Exile & the birth of monotheism (~600 BCE–70 CE)
Jerusalem falls, 586 BCE. In exile, defeat is reinterpreted as Yahweh's *victory* (he
wielded Babylon) — the pivot to true monotheism (Second Isaiah — quote WEB). Torah assembled;
synagogue and Sabbath as portable temple; Persian rescue and Zoroastrian furniture arriving
(satan, angels, resurrection, last judgment — pay off ch. 6's seed); Second Temple politics,
Maccabees, sects (Pharisees, Sadducees, Essenes/Qumran), apocalyptic fever; Rome's boot.
Bridge → into this fever, a Galilean builder's son walks on stage.

### 14. The Kingdom of Nobodies — Jesus to Paul (~4 BCE–100 CE)
The historical Jesus problem handled honestly (what's bedrock, what's contested); apocalyptic
preacher of the kingdom; crucifixion as Roman routine — and the resurrection claim that
refused to die; Paul's pivot to gentiles (no circumcision, no kosher — the growth hack);
house churches: women, slaves, the poor as members; why it spread (Stark's arithmetic:
plagues, charity, martyrdom as advertising).
Bridge → three centuries later the empire that executed him will bear his cross on its shields.

## PART IV — CONQUERING HEAVENS (~100–1500 CE)

### 15. The Empire Baptized — Christianity takes Rome (~100–600 CE)
Persecution's poor track record; Constantine's bet at the Milvian Bridge; Nicaea and the
invention of orthodoxy (creeds as border fences; Arius loses); Theodosius closes the temples —
the persecuted become persecutors (tell it straight); Augustine's City of God as Rome falls;
monks flee to the desert and accidentally invent the institution that will save literacy.
Bridge → while the West's lights dim, in a cave near Mecca a merchant hears a voice say: Recite.

### 16. The Recitation — Islam (~610–1258 CE)
Muhammad in Mecca and Medina (quote Qur'an from public-domain translation); the umma as
supertribe — the book's thesis in its purest form; the explosion: how a century took Islam
from Medina to Spain (mechanics: cavalry, exhausted empires, tax logic of conversion);
Sunni/Shia fracture at Karbala; sharia as portable law; the golden age (House of Wisdom,
algebra, Avicenna) and Sufism's inward turn (Rumi era); Baghdad falls to the Mongols, 1258.
Bridge → Christendom, meanwhile, has been arguing with itself for a thousand years — and
building cathedrals with the proceeds.

### 17. The Long Argument of Christendom — (~600–1500 CE)
East/West divergence and the 1054 schism (filioque — make a two-word doctrine dispute
genuinely gripping); monasteries as Europe's server farms; cathedral mechanics (how do you
keep a Gothic vault up?); pilgrimage and relics as economy; crusades (including the Fourth's
sack of Constantinople — no flinching); Aquinas syncing Aristotle; the Black Death's theology
(flagellants, pogroms, Ars moriendi); Avignon, indulgences — load the gun for ch. 20.
Bridge → the Dharma, meanwhile, has been quietly conquering the other half of the world
without an army.

### 18. The Dharma's Long March — Buddhism & Hinduism across Asia (~100–1500 CE)
Silk Road transmission (Kumarajiva's translation bureau); Mahayana's bodhisattva turn; China
digests Buddhism (Chan/Zen), Japan (Pure Land — salvation by grace, parallel to Luther
noted for ch. 20), Tibet (Vajrayana); meanwhile India: puranic Hinduism, temple South India,
bhakti devotion (Mirabai, Kabir — the god of love vs. the god of ritual); Buddhism's strange
near-death in its homeland; Angkor and Southeast Asia; Guru Nanak on the Hindu-Muslim seam
(Sikhism, b. 1469 — plant, don't bloom).
Bridge → all of this happened in one connected world-web. Half the planet was never plugged in.

### 19. Blood of the Sun — the unconnected world (to ~1600 CE)
Religions that grew with no contact with the Old World web: Maya (calendar priests, bloodletting
mechanics, Popol Vuh — quote), Aztec (why mass sacrifice — the sun's debt economy, told without
lurid exoticism or apologetics), Inca (ancestor mummies running an empire), North America,
Yoruba/Ifá and African traditions, Polynesian mana/tapu, Aboriginal Dreaming. The natural
experiment: what religion invents everywhere on its own (offering, ancestor, priest, calendar) —
strongest evidence for the book's thesis. Then 1519–1600: the catastrophe of contact, gods
falling to smallpox and steel; syncretism as survival (Guadalupe, Santería).
Bridge → the world is now one web — just as the web's western strand catches fire.

## PART V — GOD ON TRIAL (~1500–1900)

### 20. The Broken Body of Christ — Reformation & wars of religion (~1500–1650)
Luther's 95 theses as viral media event (printing press mechanics — Knowledge-style);
sola fide (note the Pure Land rhyme from ch. 18); Calvin's Geneva; the English soap opera;
radical reformers drowned by magisterial ones; Trent and the Jesuits' global counterstroke
(Xavier in Japan, Ricci in Beijing); wars of religion through 1648 — Westphalia's exhausted
invention: the confessional state, and religion's first forced retreat from politics;
witch hunts as the era's dark twin.
Bridge → if faith can be privatized by treaty, someone will soon ask what else it can lose.

### 21. The Clockmaker's Universe — Enlightenment (~1650–1800)
Spinoza's God-or-Nature; Newton's cosmos needing (then not needing) maintenance; deism —
God as retired engineer; Locke on toleration; Hume's acid; Voltaire vs. l'infâme; the
Lisbon earthquake 1755 as Europe's theodicy crisis; American and French revolutions —
disestablishment vs. de-Christianization (Cult of the Supreme Being — history's strangest
state religion); Wesley and the Great Awakenings as the counter-current (the heart strikes
back); Haskalah; the missionary societies founded at century's end — faith goes global just
as it goes on trial at home.
Bridge → the nineteenth century will put both movements on steroids and end with an obituary.

### 22. The Twilight of the Gods — the long 19th century (1800–1900)
Darwin, 1859 — design's quiet collapse; German higher criticism dissecting scripture as
literature; Strauss, Feuerbach, Marx ("opium of the people" — quote, in context), Freud
looming; yet simultaneously: the greatest missionary expansion in history (empire's chaplain —
told straight); new gods born in industrial daylight — Mormonism (1830), Baha'i (1863),
Adventism, spiritualism's séance craze, Ramakrishna and Hindu reform, the Meiji invention of
State Shinto; the 1893 World's Parliament of Religions in Chicago (Vivekananda's ovation) —
religions meeting as peers for the first time; Nietzsche's madman announcing the death of God
to people who haven't heard. Close the book at midnight, Dec 31, 1900: more humans praying
than ever in history, and the confident Victorian prediction — on both sides — of what the
new century would do to faith. End on the thesis, one last time, and stop at the century's edge.
