# Chapter 22 — The Twilight of the Gods

> One calleth unto me out of Seir: 'Watchman, what of the night? Watchman, what of the night?' The watchman said: 'The morning cometh, and also the night— If ye will inquire, inquire ye; return, come.'
>
> — Isaiah 21:11–12 (JPS 1917)

In the last week of November 1859, the most dangerous author in England was wrapped to the chin in cold wet sheets in a Yorkshire spa town, feeling sorry for himself.

Charles Darwin had come to Ilkley, on the edge of the moors, for the water cure — hydropathy, the era's fashionable remedy for ailments that resisted every other kind of doctor. The regimen was precise and cheerfully brutal. Attendants soaked linen sheets in cold water, wound the patient in them like a pharaoh, packed blankets over the top, and left him to steam; then came the plunge bath, the douche of icy water down the spine, the vigorous toweling, the prescribed trudge across the winter moor. Darwin, fifty years old and sick in ways his physicians never managed to name — the retching, the boils, the trembling that closed over him whenever his work approached its conclusions — submitted to all of it. His skin had erupted so badly that he could barely walk. From his lodgings he wrote letters that read less like an author's on publication week than a defendant's awaiting a verdict.

The verdict came fast. On November 22, at John Murray's annual trade sale in London, the booksellers subscribed for more copies of *On the Origin of Species by Means of Natural Selection* than the 1,250 that had been printed. The entire edition was spoken for before a single reader had opened it. Murray wrote asking Darwin to begin revisions for a second edition at once. Green cloth, five hundred and two pages, one solitary diagram — a branching tree — and a sentence near the end so quiet you could miss it: "Light will be thrown on the origin of man and his history."

Fifteen years earlier, confiding the theory's germ to his friend Joseph Hooker, Darwin had described the admission that species change as being "like confessing a murder." Now the confession was in the shops, and the murder victim, everyone understood without saying so, was not a species. It was an argument — the argument from design, the sturdiest pillar under the English God — and with it, many assumed, the cathedral it held up. In the same decades, German professors were dissecting scripture with philologists' scalpels; a bearded exile in London was calling religion an opiate; and before the century closed, a Saxon professor's fictional madman would run into the marketplace crying that God was dead. The obituary, in short, was drafted. The nineteenth century wrote it early, in several confident hands.

This final chapter is the story of that obituary — and of the century's best joke, which is that the corpse declined to attend the funeral.

[PLATE: An engraving-style scene — Darwin swaddled to the chin in wet sheets in a bath chair on a windswept spa terrace, an attendant presenting a brown-paper parcel of books, moorland behind, captioned "The water cure: Ilkley, November 1859, publication week."]

Be clear about what this chapter claims, because its title is a trap. "The Twilight of the Gods" is not a fact; it is a *forecast* — the nineteenth century's forecast, made by its cleverest people on both sides of the question, and the century even wrote the music for it: Wagner's *Götterdämmerung* had its premiere at Bayreuth on 17 August 1876, the closing night of the first complete *Ring* cycle, four hours of magnificent orchestral dusk. The forecast was wrong. Here is the archival record, and it is not close: at midnight on December 31, 1900, more human beings prayed, more human beings kept a sabbath, more human beings belonged to an organized faith, than at any previous moment in the history of the species — more than in the age of Aquinas, more than in the age of Augustine, more by hundreds of millions than when the century began. The century of Darwin, Strauss, Marx, and Nietzsche was simultaneously the century of the greatest missionary expansion ever mounted, the century that minted more new religions than any since the Axial Age, and the century in which the world's ancient faiths reformed, rearmed, and — in one Chicago hall in 1893 — met one another as peers for the first time. Twilight was general all over Europe's lecture halls. Outside them, it looked remarkably like morning. The watchman of Isaiah, asked the only question this chapter cares about, gives the only honest answer: *the morning cometh, and also the night.* Both. At once. Inquire ye.

## The Watch That Wound Itself

To feel the force of what Darwin did, start with the argument he broke, in its most beloved form. In 1802 William Paley — archdeacon, moral philosopher, owner of the century's most quoted thought experiment — asked readers of his *Natural Theology* to imagine crossing a heath and striking their foot against a watch. Nobody, Paley argued, could inspect that arrangement of springs and gears, every part fitted to the purpose of telling time, and conclude it had assembled itself. A watch implies a watchmaker; and the eye, the wing, the valves of the heart are contrivances beside which the watch is a toy. Therefore: a Contriver. It was a lawyer's argument, clean and cumulative, and it satisfied the English-speaking world for two generations. Young Darwin read it at Christ's College, Cambridge — where, college tradition holds, he lodged in the very rooms Paley had once occupied — and loved it. "I did not at that time trouble myself about Paley's premises," he recalled in old age; few did.

What the *Origin* offered was not a refutation of Paley's evidence but a rival watchmaker with no eyes and no intentions. The mechanism deserves its moment under the hood, because its whole scandal lies in its smallness. Take three facts no farmer would dispute. Offspring vary — no two lambs are quite alike. Some variations are inherited. And more creatures are born than can survive — Darwin got the arithmetic of overproduction from Malthus, an economist and, as it happens, a clergyman. From those three facts the conclusion turns like a ratchet: any inherited variation that helps its owner survive and breed will, on average, spread; any that hinders will vanish; and given geological time — the deep time Lyell's geology had just opened like a trapdoor under the Victorian pulpit — the accumulation of small advantages will build eyes, wings, and hearts, with no foresight anywhere in the process. Darwin spent twenty years assembling the case, breeding pigeons in his garden at Down to watch selection work in miniature, corresponding with half the naturalists on earth. The watch, it turned out, could wind itself. That was the whole blasphemy. Design without a designer; the appearance of purpose as a *product* rather than a premise.

What happened next is stranger than the war story you have probably heard, and the difference matters for everything this chapter will argue. The *Origin*'s first champions included clergy. Charles Kingsley — Anglican priest, novelist, no one's fool — wrote to Darwin before publication week was out to say that a God who could make all things make themselves struck him as a nobler conception than one who had to intervene piecemeal; Darwin liked the thought so much he worked it, lightly disguised, into the second edition of the *Origin*. From the closing pages of every subsequent edition a reader can still catch Kingsley's voice under Darwin's: "A celebrated author and divine has written to me that he has gradually learned to see that it is just as noble a conception of the Deity to believe that He created a few original forms capable of self-development into other and needful forms, as to believe that He required a fresh act of creation to supply the voids caused by the action of His laws." A clergyman handed the most dangerous book of the century a theological endorsement, and the century's most dangerous author printed it. That is not the sound of war breaking out. That is the sound of two people thinking.

The war did break out — in places, over time — and this chapter will not pretend otherwise. But it is worth being exact about where, because the century mislabeled its own quarrel and the mislabeling stuck. The famous set piece is Oxford, June 30, 1860, seven months after publication: the British Association meeting in the new museum on Parks Road, seven hundred people crammed into a room built for far fewer, and Bishop Samuel Wilberforce — "Soapy Sam," silver-tongued, briefed by the anatomist Richard Owen — rising to demolish Darwinism, and then (so the legend runs) turning to the young Thomas Henry Huxley to ask whether it was through his grandfather or his grandmother that he claimed descent from an ape. Huxley, the story goes, muttered "The Lord hath delivered him into mine hands," rose, and said he would rather have an ape for an ancestor than a man who used his considerable gifts to obscure the truth. The room erupted; a lady fainted; Admiral FitzRoy, Darwin's old captain from the *Beagle*, waved a Bible and lamented that he had ever carried such a heretic around the world.

It is a magnificent scene, and we know it mostly from accounts written down decades later, by Huxley's admirers, after the ending was known. No transcript survives. Contemporary reports are thin and disagree about who landed the blow; some in the room thought the botanist Joseph Hooker, not Huxley, had given the better answer, and Wilberforce himself walked away believing he had won. The exchange grew in the retelling the way such stories do — polished, sharpened, fitted with its perfect squelch — until it became the founding myth of a war. Hold that word *myth*; this chapter will return to it, because the making of it is one of the century's real events, and one of its least noticed.

For here is the thing the twentieth century inherited and swallowed whole: the idea that "science" and "religion" are two armies that have been at war since Galileo, locked in a struggle in which every advance of the one is a retreat of the other. That idea has a birthday, and it falls squarely inside this chapter. It was manufactured — the word is not too strong — by two American books written a generation after Darwin: John William Draper's *History of the Conflict Between Religion and Science* in 1874, a runaway bestseller translated across Europe, and Andrew Dickson White's two-volume *A History of the Warfare of Science with Theology in Christendom* in 1896. Draper, an English-born chemist with a grudge chiefly against Rome, and White, the first president of Cornell — a university founded without a church, and stung by clergy who called it godless — needed a usable past, and they built one: a long parade of martyred scientists and obscurantist priests, complete with the enduring schoolroom fictions that medieval Christians thought the earth was flat and that the Church had forbidden human dissection. Almost none of it survives contact with the archives. Historians of science spent the whole of the next century dismantling it, and today the "conflict thesis" is about as respectable among specialists as the flat earth it slandered the Middle Ages with. Copernicus was a cathedral canon; Mendel, who cracked heredity while Darwin was still guessing at it, was an abbot; Georges Lemaître, who would propose the expanding universe, was a priest; and the man who handed Darwin his best line about a self-developing creation was a vicar. The "warfare of science and religion" is not the story of the nineteenth century. It is the most successful thing the nineteenth century invented *about itself* — a myth manufactured in exactly the decades this chapter covers, and mistaken ever since for the news it was reporting on. Keep it in view. Almost everything else in this chapter has been read through its smoke.

## The Scripture on the Dissecting Table

The graver threat to the English God was never the ape. It was a German philologist with a Greek New Testament, a red pencil, and no fear at all.

In 1835 a twenty-seven-year-old tutor at the Tübingen seminary named David Friedrich Strauss published two fat volumes called *Das Leben Jesu, kritisch bearbeitet* — *The Life of Jesus, Critically Examined* — and detonated a career that had not properly begun. Strauss did not argue that Jesus never lived, nor that the evangelists were liars. He proposed a third thing, more corrosive than either: that the miraculous stories of the Gospels — the star, the walking on water, the voice from the cloud — were neither eyewitness fact nor conscious fiction but *myth*, in a precise sense. They were the poetry a community weaves, without meaning to deceive, around a figure it has come to love; the messianic hopes of first-century Jews, precipitating around the memory of a Galilean teacher and crystallizing into narrative. It was not that the early Christians set out to invent; it was that a believing community *cannot help* generating the stories its faith requires, the way a supersaturated solution cannot help throwing crystals. Applied verse by verse across four Gospels, the method was devastating precisely because it was so sympathetic. You could not answer it by calling its author a scoffer. He plainly was not one.

The German states answered it the way they answered everything: institutionally. Strauss was removed from his seminary post. In 1839 the University of Zurich offered him a chair of theology; the resulting public uproar was so violent that the canton pensioned him off before he ever lectured, and the conservative revolt helped topple the local government. Strauss spent the rest of his life as the most famous unemployable theologian in Europe. And then, in 1846, his book found the English reader it deserved: it was translated, with immense care, by a young Midlands woman named Marian Evans, not yet known to the world as George Eliot. Rendering Strauss into English cost Evans her own inherited faith — she described translating the account of the crucifixion as "Strauss-sick," physically ill at the dismantling — and it is one of the century's quiet symmetries that the novelist who would write *Middlemarch*, the great English novel of ordinary moral seriousness, learned her unbelief line by line at the dissecting table of a German seminarian.

Strauss was one blade of a larger instrument. What the Germans built over the century was *higher criticism* — "higher" not as a boast but to distinguish it from the "lower," textual criticism that merely compared manuscripts to recover the words. Higher criticism asked the dangerous questions behind the words: Who actually wrote this? When? From what older sources, stitched at what visible seams? At Tübingen, Ferdinand Christian Baur read the New Testament as a battlefield of rival early factions and redated its books accordingly. Above all, over decades of patient work on the Hebrew Bible, scholars assembled what Julius Wellhausen crystallized in 1878 into the documentary hypothesis: that the Five Books of Moses were not written by Moses, nor by any single hand, but braided together from at least four distinct sources composed centuries apart — a hypothesis you could see, once shown, in the plain doublings of the text, the two creation stories, the two floods folded into one ark. To open Genesis after Wellhausen was to see not a seamless revelation but a magnificent anthology with the stitching showing. Scripture, the most authoritative object in Western civilization, had been placed on the table and opened like any other document of the human past.

[PLATE: An engraving-style scene — a young woman at a lamplit desk piled with German folios and a Greek New Testament, pen paused over a half-filled sheet, a crucifix visible on the far wall, captioned "Strauss-sick: Marian Evans translates The Life of Jesus, Coventry, 1846."]

If Strauss dissolved the miracles and Wellhausen unstitched the Torah, it fell to a third German to name the wound and call it a discovery. In 1841 Ludwig Feuerbach published *The Essence of Christianity* — translated, as it happens, by the same Marian Evans in 1854 — and made an argument so simple it can be stated in a sentence: God did not make man in his image; man made God in his. Every perfection we assign to the divine, Feuerbach said, is a human perfection, magnified to infinity and then flung into the sky, where we kneel to it as to a stranger. Theology, in his ringing reversal, is nothing but anthropology that has forgotten its own name. And he did not intend this as mockery; he meant it, strange as it sounds, as a rescue — strip away the illusion, he urged, and the love and wisdom and mercy remain, now returned to their rightful owner, the human species itself. It is the same move Chapter 1 of this book made from the opposite direction, that the gods are built from the standard equipment of the human mind, and a reader who has come this far may feel a chill of recognition. Feuerbach had put his finger on something real about how gods are assembled. Whether "assembled by us" means "therefore false" is a further, philosophical step that his confidence rather rushed.

Now, the declension narrative — the smooth downhill story in which each of these blows knocks another prop from under faith until the whole structure falls — wants you to read this section as demolition, and here is where honesty requires the harder fact. Higher criticism was very largely the work of *believers*. It was done in the theology faculties of Protestant Germany, by ordained men who prayed, took the Lord's Supper, and understood themselves to be serving God by refusing to lie to Him about His own book. Wellhausen was a pastor's son and a professor of theology before his conclusions drove him, in conscience, to resign the theological chair for an Oriental-languages one — not because he had lost his faith but because he would not train pastors in a scholarship he thought they could not preach. The tools that seemed to threaten the Bible were forged, overwhelmingly, by people who loved it, and who could not imagine that loving it meant not looking closely. Within two generations those same tools would be embraced across most of mainline Protestantism and, by the mid-twentieth century, by the Catholic Church itself, which would come to treat critical scholarship as an instrument of faith rather than its enemy. The scripture on the dissecting table did not die of the examination. It got up, changed, and walked out of the room — which is, this chapter will keep insisting, the thing the record shows it doing again and again in exactly the years its death was being announced.

## The Sigh of the Oppressed Creature

In 1843, in Paris, a twenty-five-year-old German with a black beard and a mountain of debt sat writing an introduction to a critique he would never finish. He had read his Feuerbach closely — "there is no need of any long dissertation," he wrote, waving the whole God-question aside as settled by better men — and he was impatient to get past religion to the thing he thought religion was *about*. His name was Karl Marx, and in a few paragraphs of that unfinished introduction he produced the single most quoted, and most mutilated, sentence ever written about faith.

You know the phrase. You have almost certainly never met it in its home. Here is the passage entire, in the standard English rendering of Marx's German:

> Religious suffering is, at one and the same time, the expression of real suffering and a protest against real suffering. Religion is the sigh of the oppressed creature, the heart of a heartless world, and the soul of soulless conditions. It is the opium of the people.

Read it slowly, because almost everyone who quotes it has read only its last six words, and the last six words mean something different when the first forty are missing. The caricature Marx — the sneering materialist calling religion a stupid narcotic for stupid people — is not in this text. The text is *tender*. Before it is anything else it is a description of pain: the sigh, the oppressed creature, the heartless world. And the metaphor a hostile reader hears as contempt was, in 1843, nothing of the kind. Opium was not a slur. It was the age's one reliable mercy — laudanum in a brown bottle, the only medicine that stood between a working family and the full raw edge of a toothache, a cancer, a dying child. To call religion the opium of the people, in the year of *our* Lord 1843, was to say that it was the one thing that made an unbearable life bearable; that in a world with no anesthetic for its deepest griefs, the poor had found the only relief on offer. Marx is not mocking the sufferer reaching for the bottle. He is indicting the world that leaves them nothing else to reach for.

Now refuse to flinch the other way, because the tender reading can be sentimentalized just as badly as the cruel one. Marx did think religion an illusion, and he did want it gone. The very next sentences make the surgeon's intention plain: "The abolition of religion as the illusory happiness of the people is the demand for their real happiness." His quarrel was not with the sufferer's sigh but with the anesthetic itself, which he thought dulled the pain that might otherwise have driven people to fix its cause. Cure the heartless world, he argued, and the sigh would stop; abolish the conditions that require illusions, and the illusions would fall away on their own, unmourned. This is a genuine and radical hostility to religion — no soft-focus reading should hide it — but it is hostility of a specific and almost backhanded kind. Marx did not hate the flower for being beautiful. He hated the chain, and he thought the flower grew on it. "Criticism has plucked the imaginary flowers from the chain," he went on, in the same passage, "not so that man will wear the chain without any fantasy or consolation, but so that he will shake off the chain and cull the living flower."

Notice what Marx, the century's most influential atheist, could not help conceding even as he swung the axe. In four lines he calls religion the sigh of the oppressed, the heart of a heartless world, the soul of soulless conditions — which is to say, he grants that in a world that offered the poor no heart and no soul, religion supplied both. That is not a description of a delusion. That is a description of a *function*, and it is uncannily close to the one this book has traced from the first graves onward. Where else, in the mill towns of the 1840s, did a servant girl or a Lancashire weaver find themselves treated as a person of infinite worth, addressed by name, promised that the last would be first? Not in the factory, not in the workhouse, not in the ledgers of political economy. In the chapel. Religion was doing in industrial Manchester exactly what it had done on the mammoth steppe: gathering people the visible order counted as nothing, and telling them they were kin — to one another, and to something that would not let them go. Marx saw the machine working and called it a drug. A reader of this book might call it the technology doing its oldest job under the worst conditions it had yet faced.

There is one more turn, and it is the kind of irony the century specialized in without noticing. Marx took Feuerbach's idea — that in worshiping God, humanity worships an idealized image of itself — and pushed it toward the future: sweep away the illusion, he promised, and the thwarted human longing for justice and a world that made sense would come home to roost in history itself. He did not live to see what became of that hope. But the longing he wanted to redirect did not evaporate when its old object was denied; it went looking for a new one. The believing animal of Chapter 1 does not stop believing when you take away its god. It believes in something else — and the nineteenth century, which thought it was watching faith drain out of the world, was in fact watching it change address, again, as it had in every chapter of this book.

## Empire's Chaplain

On the morning of May 1, 1873, in a mud-and-thatch hut in a village near Lake Bangweulu in what is now Zambia, a servant looked in on his master and found him kneeling by the bedside as if at prayer, and dead. David Livingstone — Scottish missionary, explorer, abolitionist, the most famous Englishman in Africa though he was neither English nor, by then, much of a missionary in the ordinary sense — had died on his knees, at the far end of a life that had begun in a Blantyre cotton mill, where at the age of ten he had worked fourteen-hour days as a piecer, snatching Latin from a book propped on the spinning frame.

What his companions did next is the truest single image of this whole vast, compromised, magnificent enterprise. Two Africans, James Chuma and Abdullah Susi, who had traveled with Livingstone for years, took charge. They cut out his heart and viscera and buried them beneath a mvula tree — his heart, they said, belonged to Africa. They dried the body in the sun for a fortnight, wrapped it in bark and sailcloth, lashed it to a pole, and then carried it, two men and a small party, roughly a thousand miles across the continent to the Indian Ocean coast, a journey of nine months, guarding a dead white man's corpse through territory where carrying a body was thought to invite disaster. The remains reached England and were buried, in April 1874, under the floor of Westminster Abbey, where the stone still reads that he was "brought by faithful hands over land and sea." The faithful hands were Chuma's and Susi's, and they were not invited to the funeral. That sentence contains the entire nineteenth-century missionary story: an act of astonishing devotion by African Christians, memorialized in an English cathedral that could not quite bring itself to print their names.

[PLATE: An engraving-style scene — two African men and a small party bearing a bark-wrapped body lashed to a long pole through tall grass, a line of porters behind, distant hills, captioned "Brought by faithful hands: Chuma and Susi carry Livingstone to the sea, 1873."]

For this was the century of the greatest missionary expansion in the history of the species, and it must be told straight, which means told doubled. The machinery had been assembled at the close of the last chapter — the Baptist William Carey sailing for India in 1793, the London Missionary Society founded in 1795, the Church Missionary Society in 1799 — and across the nineteenth century it poured tens of thousands of men and women out of Europe and North America into Asia, Africa, and the islands of the Pacific in numbers no faith had ever exported before. They went believing they carried the only truth that could save a soul, and they went, very often, in the wake of gunboats, on the decks of the same ships that carried the muskets, the cotton goods, and the officials of empire. Livingstone himself preached a trinity of "Christianity, Commerce, and Civilization," and never doubted that British power was providence's chosen instrument. It is no use pretending otherwise: mission and empire traveled together, drew on each other, and often could not be told apart. Missionaries baptized conquests, taught colonized children to despise their parents' gods, mistook English trousers for holiness. The record holds real arrogance, real destruction, and real complicity in the theft of a hemisphere.

And now refuse the easy cynicism, because the easy cynicism — missions were simply the chaplaincy of conquest, God as the softening agent of the gun — is exactly the kind of tidy story this chapter exists to complicate. Hold the other facts in the same hand. The single most consequential thing the missionaries did, almost everywhere they went, was reduce a spoken language to writing so that ordinary people could read a book — and in doing so they created the first dictionaries, the first grammars, the first printed literature for hundreds of languages that might otherwise have left no written trace at all. It was missionary presses that gave the Xhosa, the Yoruba, the Malagasy, and scores of others a written form of their own tongue, and missionary schools that produced, within a generation, the mission-educated Africans and Indians who would turn the master's literacy against the master's empire. Many missionaries were the fiercest critics of the colonial project they had arrived inside of; Livingstone's obsession, the one that killed him, was not conversion but the destruction of the East African slave trade, which he documented in horrified detail and hounded until his death. The abolitionist conscience that had ended British slavery was a chapel conscience, and it did not switch off at the water's edge.

But the deepest correction to the "empire's chaplain" story is the one the numbers force, and it is pure fuel for this chapter's argument. Christianity did not take root where the imperial grip was tightest. It took root, and then exploded, precisely where the missionaries lost control of it — where local converts seized the message, shrugged off the European packaging, and made it unmistakably their own. In the Pacific, whole island kingdoms became Christian through the work of Tongan and Fijian and Hawaiian evangelists, not white ones. In southern and West Africa, the faith spread fastest through African prophets, catechists, and independent churches that sang in their own languages and healed in their own idioms and answered to no mission board. The gospel, once translated into a mother tongue, stopped being the empire's and became the people's — a thing they could carry across a border no colonial officer patrolled. By 1900, for the first time in history, Christianity had a living presence on every inhabited continent, and the center of gravity of the world's largest religion had already begun its long migration south and east, toward the very peoples it had been sent to subdue. The strangers had, once again, become kin — but not on the terms the senders imagined, and not under the flag they flew. The tool had been handed over, and the recipients were reforging it in their own image. That is not the chaplaincy of empire. That is the oldest story in this book, running faster than anyone in London could follow.

## New Gods in the Gaslight

On the last night of March 1848, in a small frame farmhouse in Hydesville, New York, two girls — Kate Fox, about eleven, and her sister Margaret, about fourteen — sat up in the dark and answered the knocking in the walls. They had worked out a code with whatever was making the raps: one knock for no, two for yes, a rap for each letter counted down the alphabet. That night, they and their neighbors decided, they were talking with the spirit of a murdered peddler buried in the cellar. Within months the sisters were giving public demonstrations in Rochester; within a few years, "spirit rapping" had crossed the Atlantic and become a mania. This was the birth of modern Spiritualism, and note the exquisite timing: it began in the same decade as the electric telegraph, and it borrowed the telegraph's very logic — messages from the invisible, transmitted in code, tapped out in pulses. People called it, without embarrassment, the spiritual telegraph. The dead were now reachable, it seemed, by the same technology that had just abolished distance for the living.

Hold that image, because it unlocks the whole section. We have been told, and this chapter has been at pains to complicate, that the nineteenth century was where religion went to die under the cold light of science. But look at what the cold light actually illuminated. The age of steam, statistics, and the daily newspaper was also the most fertile era of religious invention since the Axial Age — a hatchery of brand-new faiths, founded not in the mists of prehistory but in broad documentary daylight, by named people in datable years, their origins recorded in census returns and court transcripts and the very newspapers that were supposedly secularizing everyone. Modernity did not close the workshop where gods are made. It re-tooled it, wired it for electricity, and ran it at full capacity.

Start in America, whose western New York was so scorched by successive waves of revival that contemporaries called it the "burned-over district." Out of it, in 1830, came the most successful new scripture in modern history. A young farmer named Joseph Smith announced that an angel had directed him to golden plates buried in a hillside, inscribed in an unknown script, which he translated by means of seer stones into the *Book of Mormon* — a nine-hundred-page account of ancient Israelites in the Americas, printed on a commercial press in Palmyra and published while its translator was still in his twenties. The Church of Christ he organized that April would be hounded across a continent, its prophet murdered by a mob in Illinois in 1844, before Brigham Young led the Latter-day Saints to the Great Salt Lake in 1847 and built, in the desert, a new Zion with irrigation ditches and a grid of streets. Whatever one makes of the plates, the sociological fact is stark: a wholly new revelation, delivered in the age of the steam press and the penny post, had founded a people. The gods were still being born, and now they were born with a publication date.

The same soil produced a spectacular experiment in the one thing religion is usually thought too wise to risk: a testable prediction. The Baptist lay preacher William Miller, reading the prophecies of Daniel with a pencil and a chronology, calculated that Christ would return in 1844, and tens of thousands of Americans sold farms, settled debts, and waited. When the appointed day — October 22 — passed with the sun rising as usual, the movement suffered what its own members named, with heartbreaking honesty, the Great Disappointment. And here is the detail that ought to be impossible: the disappointment did not end the movement. It *reorganized* it. Out of the wreckage of the failed date came new churches, the Seventh-day Adventists chief among them, formally constituted in 1863, who reinterpreted 1844 as a heavenly event rather than an earthly one and went on to build hospitals and schools across the world. A prophecy can fail catastrophically, in public, on a known date, and the faith it belonged to can not merely survive but institutionalize. That is not what a dying impulse does.

Spiritualism, meanwhile, swept the drawing rooms of two continents, and it repays a moment's attention because of what it reveals about the century's supposedly secular elite. Séances became fashionable entertainment and desperate consolation at once — table-tipping, automatic writing, trumpets floating in the dark, mediums delivering messages from dead children in an age when a great many children died. Serious scientists investigated; the chemist William Crookes, a Fellow of the Royal Society, staked his reputation studying mediums. Out of the same current came Theosophy, founded in New York in 1875 by the formidable Russian émigrée Helena Blavatsky, who fused Spiritualism with a romantic reading of Indian religion and helped turn the Western gaze eastward. Honesty compels the footnote that in 1888 Margaret Fox, impoverished and bitter, publicly confessed that the original raps had been produced by cracking the joints of her toes — and then partly recanted. But the fraud at the root did nothing to slow the flood, and the reason is the oldest one in this book: Spiritualism was Chapter 2 wearing a frock coat. It was the refusal to surrender the dead, the conviction that the beloved persist and can be reached, breaking out again — this time not in a furnished grave on the mammoth steppe but in a gaslit parlor with a mahogany table, among people who read Darwin over breakfast and grieved their dead exactly as hard as the mourners at Sungir.

And this was no American eccentricity. The same hatchery was running across the world. In Persia in 1863, a nobleman who took the name Bahá'u'lláh — heir to a persecuted messianic movement whose founder, the Báb, had been executed by firing squad in 1850, thousands of his followers slaughtered — declared himself the bearer of a new revelation and founded the Baha'i faith, whose scandalous, beautiful claim was that all the world's religions are chapters of one unfolding book and all humanity a single people. In Bengal, the ecstatic mystic Ramakrishna, a temple priest of the goddess Kali who fell into trances and insisted, having practiced several religions in turn, that all paths climb the same mountain, was drawing disciples who would carry a reformed and self-confident Hinduism onto the world stage; around him a whole Hindu renaissance was underway — the Brahmo Samaj, the Arya Samaj — answering the colonial and missionary critique not by abandoning the tradition but by rearming it and presenting it to the world as the equal of any faith on earth. And in Japan, the Meiji Restoration of 1868 set about *manufacturing* a religion on purpose: prying Shinto apart from the Buddhism it had been fused with for a thousand years, elevating the emperor as the living descendant of the sun goddess Amaterasu, and building State Shinto into the spiritual engine of a modern nation-state — religion consciously engineered, in the full daylight of the industrial age, to bind seventy million strangers into one people ready to modernize and, if required, to die for the emperor. From upstate New York to a garden in Baghdad to a shrine in Tokyo, the same century was doing the same thing: making new gods, and new peoples out of them, faster than the obituarists could count.

## The Parliament of the World's Religions

On the morning of September 11, 1893, in a great new hall on the Chicago lakefront, an unknown Hindu monk in a red turban sat paralyzed with stage fright while, one after another, the clergy of the world read out their prepared addresses. He had no prepared address. He had come to the World's Parliament of Religions — the first gathering of its kind in human history, convened as part of the vast World's Columbian Exposition, the "White City" of electric light and plaster palaces that drew twenty-seven million visitors that summer — almost by accident, without credentials, and had been talked onto the program on the strength of his bearing alone. When at last he could put it off no longer, Swami Vivekananda, a thirty-year-old disciple of the Bengali mystic Ramakrishna, rose before an audience of some seven thousand people, and instead of "Ladies and gentlemen," he said: "Sisters and Brothers of America."

The hall came to its feet. The ovation, by every account, ran on for two full minutes — seven thousand strangers roaring at a form of address, at the simple, startling warmth of being called kin by a man most of them had been taught to think of as a heathen. When the noise finally let him speak, Vivekananda preached not the superiority of his faith but the harmony of all of them: that the world's religions were not rival armies but different rivers running to the same sea, and that the age of sectarian contempt ought now to be over. He became, overnight, the sensation of the Parliament and then of the country, touring America for years and planting the seeds of Vedanta in Western soil.

[PLATE: An engraving-style scene — a robed monk in a turban at a podium, arms slightly open, a vast tiered auditorium rising to its feet around him, banners of many faiths along the gallery, captioned "Sisters and Brothers of America: Vivekananda at the Parliament of Religions, Chicago, September 1893."]

Consider where this was happening, and when. In the same decade that a Saxon philosopher was composing the death of God, and Andrew Dickson White was finishing his history of religion's supposed war with science, the religions of the earth were gathering under one roof to meet one another — for the first time ever — as something approaching peers. Onto the Chicago platform came Buddhists: the fiery Sinhalese reformer Anagarika Dharmapala, and Soyen Shaku, a Japanese Zen abbot whose few days in Illinois would seed the transmission of Zen to the West. Came the Jain scholar Virchand Gandhi; came liberal, reforming Jews; came a Parsi Zoroastrian, a Confucian, a Shinto priest, and an American convert to Islam named Mohammed Alexander Russell Webb, defending his adopted faith to a skeptical crowd. Came Christians of every warring denomination, sitting, for once, in the same room. Representatives of traditions that had spent centuries certain the others were damned stood on one stage and, however awkwardly, listened.

Tell it straight, because the Parliament flattered itself and later generations have flattered it further. It was organized by liberal American Protestants — a Presbyterian minister, John Henry Barrows, chief among them — and a good many of them privately expected that when all the faiths were laid side by side, Christianity would be seen to be the crown of the set, the fulfillment toward which the others groped. The framing of "peers" was, in 1893, more aspiration than achievement: the Archbishop of Canterbury declined to send anyone, on the grounds that Christianity was the one true religion and could not sit as one among many; the Ottoman sultan discouraged Muslim participation; the whole affair was staged inside a world's fair built to celebrate four centuries of European expansion, the very expansion that had subjugated half the delegates' homelands. No one should pretend the lion and the lamb had actually lain down together on the shores of Lake Michigan.

And yet something real and irreversible happened in that hall, and it is the exact mirror-image of the missionary century, which makes it the perfect note on which to turn toward home. The West had spent a hundred years sending its faith out to convert the world. In Chicago, the world came back — and talked. The traffic reversed. The peoples who had been cast as the objects of mission stood up as teachers, and a substantial slice of the Western audience, weary of the God the higher critics had dissected and the scientists had pensioned off, leaned forward to learn. The seekers who would fill Vedanta societies and Buddhist meditation halls in the coming century got their first taste of the East not from a missionary's report but from the mouths of Vivekananda and Dharmapala and Soyen Shaku. Here was the book's thesis at the largest scale it had yet been tried: the technology by which strangers become kin, attempting for the first time to make kin not within a tribe or a nation or an empire but across the whole species at once — every tradition in the room, addressed as sisters and brothers. It did not remotely succeed. But that it was attempted at all, in the supposed twilight, tells you which way the light was actually moving.

## The Madman's Lantern

We have saved the century's most famous funeral notice for last, because almost everyone has read only its headline. In 1882 a former professor of classical philology, sick, half-blind, writing in the mountain air of the Engadine because it was the only place his headaches let him think, published a book of aphorisms called *The Gay Science*. In its hundred and twenty-fifth section, Friedrich Nietzsche told a small, terrible story. A madman lights a lantern in the bright morning, runs into the marketplace, and cries out that he is looking for God. The people standing around do not believe in God, and they laugh at him — where has he gone, they jeer, has he emigrated, is he hiding? The madman springs into their midst and pierces them with his eyes. "God is dead," he says. "God remains dead. And we have killed him."

Read the rest of the passage — the part the T-shirts never print — and it is not a boast. It is a scream of vertigo. Who gave us the sponge to wipe away the whole horizon, the madman demands; what did we do when we unchained the earth from its sun; where is the earth moving now, are we not plunging continually, backward, sideward, forward, in all directions, through an infinite nothing? And then the line that ought to be set beside the watchman of Isaiah at the head of this chapter: "Is there not night, and more and more night coming on all the while? Must not lanterns be lit in the morning?" That is Nietzsche, not the prophet — the same image, the morning that needs a lamp, the night folding in behind the daylight. The madman is not celebrating a liberation. He is a man who has seen a catastrophe that no one else in the marketplace has noticed yet. For his listeners fall silent and stare, and he understands: "I have come too early. My time has not yet come. This tremendous event is still on its way, still wandering — it has not yet reached the ears of men." He throws his lantern to the ground; it shatters and goes out. And he wanders, the story ends, into the churches, to sing a requiem — for what, he asks, are these churches now, if not the tombs and sepulchers of God?

Here is the thing the headline hides. Nietzsche did not write that God is dead and good riddance. He put the announcement in the mouth of a *madman*, and he made the madman's point that the glib unbelievers of the marketplace — the very people who quote the line today with a smirk — have not begun to grasp what the death of God would actually cost. Strip out the deity who had underwritten every value in the West, Nietzsche was warning, and you do not get a sunny secular brunch. You get the horizon wiped away; you get the long project of finding out whether human beings can manufacture meaning from scratch, having smashed the only factory they ever had. He thought this the defining labor of the centuries to come, and he did not think it would be easy, and he did not, whatever his admirers imagine, think it would be painless. The prophet of the death of God spent the last eleven years of his life insane, wordless, cared for by his sister — and he died, as it happens, in August of 1900. The man who wrote the century's obituary for God did not outlive the century by a single day.

So stand, now, where this whole book has been walking: at midnight on December 31, 1900. The lamps are being lit against the new century all over the turning world. And here is the archival fact, laid down one final time, because it is the spine of everything and it is not close. More human beings were praying at that midnight than at any previous midnight in the history of the species. More kept a sabbath, belonged to an organized faith, buried their dead in hope of seeing them again, than in the age of Aquinas or Augustine, more by hundreds of millions than when the century began — not merely because there were more people, though there were, but because the *share* of them inside a living religious tradition had, on the best reckoning we can make, gone up and not down across the very century that had drafted the obituary. The believing animal of this book's first chapter, having been told for a hundred years by its cleverest members that it was about to stop believing, walked into the twentieth century believing harder, in more languages, on more continents, than ever before.

The historian Rutger Bregman has built a method out of a simple, bracing move: take the cynical story everyone knows, the one that flatters us by assuming the worst, and check it against the record — and watch it fall apart in your hands. The twilight of the gods is that kind of story. The nineteenth century told it about itself in its cleverest voices, on both sides of the argument: the confident atheist in the marketplace and the mournful bishop agreed on the forecast and disagreed only on whether to cheer. Both were wrong about the weather. What the century's cleverest people mistook for dusk falling over the whole earth was in fact dusk falling over one lecture hall, in one corner of Europe, while outside the windows the largest religious expansion in human history was underway in the daylight they had their backs to. Twilight was general all over the seminar room. It was morning nearly everywhere else. The watchman, asked what of the night, gave the only honest answer forty centuries ago and it is still the only honest one: *the morning cometh, and also the night.* Both. At once.

And why? Not because the arguments were bad — some of them were superb, and this chapter has refused to flinch from a single one. Darwin was right about the watch. The higher critics were right about the seams in the book. Feuerbach saw something true about how gods are made, and Marx something true about what they do for the crushed. The obituary was well written. It simply had the wrong subject. It kept describing the death of particular *arguments* for God — the watchmaker, the literal six days, the seamless scripture — and mistaking them for the death of the *thing this book has been about*, which was never an argument. It was a technology. Religion is the technology by which strangers become kin, and you cannot refute a technology; you can only watch whether people keep using it. Across the nineteenth century, having heard every refutation, more strangers used it to become kin than ever before in the life of the species. That is not a claim about whether any god is real. It is a claim about what human beings *are*. The animal that buried two children under ten thousand beads on the frozen steppe, refusing to surrender them to the dark, was the same animal that filled the chapels of Manchester and the temples of Bengal and the shrines of Meiji Japan, and it had not changed its nature in forty thousand years of anything, least of all in one century of clever books.

So let the book end where books like this should end: not with a professor, but with an ordinary believer, at the doorstep of the century we are forbidden to enter. Call her Anna. She is a composite — we do not have her name, as we did not have the Beadmaker's forty thousand years ago at the other end of this story — but ten million women very like her were awake that night, and so someone was. It is a little after midnight in a cottage somewhere in the turning world, and Anna is kneeling by the bed of her sleeping child, as her mother knelt by hers. Earlier this year she buried another child, an infant, in the churchyard on the hill, and she believes — with a conviction no watchmaker argument and no German folio has touched, because it was never built out of arguments — that she will see that child again, that the small person under the grass is a person still, still hers, waiting somewhere she cannot visit but will. She does not know that a philosopher died this summer announcing that the God she is praying to is dead. If you told her, she would not understand what you meant, and then she would go on praying, because the thing she is doing is not an opinion about the universe. It is the oldest thing our kind does. It is what the mourners did at Sungir with their beads, and at Panga ya Saidi when somebody tucked a child into the earth with a pillow under its head; it is *the dead are still ours*, said in a new language, under a new roof, into a new century. Cattle die, kindred die, the old verse said, and it was right about the dying. It was right, too, about the other thing — that something of the dead does not die, so long as there is someone kneeling in the dark who refuses to let it. There was such a someone at the first grave this book opened. There is such a someone at its close. Between them lies the whole history of religion, and it is not the history of a twilight. It is the history of a lantern, lit in the morning, and carried — hand to hand, grave to grave, stranger to stranger made kin — all the way to the edge of a century that thought, wrongly, that it would be the one to put it out.
