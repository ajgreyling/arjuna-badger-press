# Chapter 2 — The First Graves

> Cattle die, kindred die, we ourselves also die; but the fair fame never dies of him who has earned it.
>
> — *Hávamál* 76, the *Poetic Edda* (Thorpe translation)

Thirty-four thousand years ago, on the frozen steppe that is now the Russian city of Vladimir, an old woman sat close to a fire that burned bone instead of wood, and ground ivory in the dark. Call her the Beadmaker. She is a composite — we do not know her name, and no one within thirty thousand years of her knew what a name written down might be — but someone very like her existed, because her work survives, and her work can be counted.

The fire stank. On the mammoth steppe there were almost no trees, so fires were fed with bone, and burning bone smells like a kitchen accident that never ends: scorched grease, hot marrow, a sweetness underneath that you stop noticing after the first winter. By that light the Beadmaker took a rod of mammoth ivory, scored it with a flint burin, snapped off a blank the size of a fingernail, ground its faces flat against sandstone, gouged a hole through its middle with a stone point spun between her palms, and polished it until it caught the firelight. Then she set it on the pile and started the next one. Modern craftworkers who have tried this with the same tools report that a single bead takes the better part of an hour.

Behind her, laid out and waiting, were two dead children.

One was a boy of about twelve. The other was a year or three younger, a child whose thighbones had grown bowed and short, whose walk everyone in the band would have known at a distance. For generations the pair were described as a boy and a girl; DNA has since suggested both were boys. In the morning — or the next morning, or the one after that; the arithmetic of the beads says this took far longer than a night — the band would lay them in a single narrow grave, crown to crown, head to head, as if the two of them were one person reflected in still water. The bodies would go into the earth wearing everything the community could make: caps sewn with fox teeth, a belt strung with some two hundred and fifty fox canines — a fox yields four usable canines, so count sixty-odd foxes — ivory pins, ivory discs, carved animals, and lances of mammoth ivory, one of them longer than a grown man is tall, made of tusk that someone had learned to straighten. We still do not know how they straightened it. And over all of it, sewn onto clothing that rotted forty millennia ago, would go the beads: roughly ten thousand of them between the two children, five thousand each, laid out in rows that still traced the shape of their vanished caps and tunics when Soviet archaeologists opened the grave in 1969.

Ten thousand beads. Call it an hour a bead. That is ten thousand hours — five years of one person's full-time labor, or one hard winter of many people's — sunk into a hole in the ground, for two children who would never be seen again.

Unless, of course, they would.

This chapter makes one claim: death is the engine of religion. Chapter 1 argued that the human mind came off the production line tuned for belief — quick to detect agents, helpless not to model other minds, unable to switch that modeling off just because the other mind's owner has stopped breathing. This chapter is about the receipts. A grave is the first religious document we can excavate, and graves like Sungir are not modest documents. They are claims about the world, written in labor and ivory, and they say: *the dead are still persons. The dead still own things. The dead are still ours.* Everything this book will describe — temples, priesthoods, scriptures, heavens — descends from that claim. Religion's first job was not explaining thunder. It was refusing to surrender the dead. And in that refusal, as we will see, humans stumbled onto the tool that would remake the species: a shared invisible world, the common country where strangers can meet and find they are kin.

## What a Grave Says

Every animal dies. Only one animal argues about it.

Other species notice death; some seem to feel it. Elephants return to the bones of their dead and stand over them, touching the skulls with their trunks, in what it is difficult not to call mourning. Chimpanzee mothers carry dead infants for days, sometimes weeks, grooming a body that has stopped answering. Corvids gather around a dead crow in noisy assemblies that researchers, with commendable restraint, decline to call funerals. All of this is real, and none of it is burial. Noticing death is old. Answering it is ours.

To see what an answer looks like, go to a cave called Panga ya Saidi in the hills behind the Kenyan coast, and to a child archaeologists nicknamed Mtoto — Swahili for "child." Around 78,000 years ago, someone dug a shallow pit in the cave floor, laid a two-and-a-half- or three-year-old child in it curled on their right side, knees drawn up toward the chest, and covered them over. The excavators, reading the collapsed geometry of the bones like a slow-motion film, concluded that the child's head had originally rested on something that later rotted away — a support, a cushion. A pillow. Somebody tucked this child in.

Notice what the pit itself asserts, before a single bead or flower enters the story. Digging a grave is work — with digging sticks and hands, in packed cave sediment, hours of it. Work is the one thing evolution audits ruthlessly. A corpse can be left, dropped in a ravine, carried to the edge of the territory and forgotten; hyenas provide a free and thorough service. Spending hours of calorie-burning labor to place a body *just so*, to arrange its limbs, to pillow its head, is behavior that needs explaining. The minimal explanation is grief plus hygiene, and we should hold it honestly: a grave, by itself, does not prove belief in an afterlife. It proves that the living could not treat the body as garbage. But that refusal is already the seed. The body is being treated as if it were still, in some sense that matters, *someone*.

And then the goods begin, and the minimal explanation starts to buckle. At Qafzeh cave in what is now Israel, perhaps 92,000 to 100,000 years ago, early members of our species buried their dead deliberately — among them an adolescent with the antlers of a deer laid against their body, and a woman and small child laid close together. At nearby Skhul, in roughly the same deep window, a man was buried holding the jawbone of an enormous boar. At Border Cave in South Africa, around 74,000 years ago, an infant went into the ground with a perforated seashell that had traveled from the coast, stained with pigment. These are not hygiene. Nobody sanitizes a corpse by giving it antlers. A grave good is a transfer of property to a person who no longer has hands, and it makes sense on exactly one accounting: somewhere — in memory, in dream, in a country the living cannot visit — the dead child still exists to receive it.

The Neanderthals, as the last chapter saw, force us to share even this. At Shanidar cave in the Zagros mountains, Neanderthals put their dead into the ground across thousands of years; the famous flower burial may owe its pollen to burrowing rodents with a taste for blossoms — the case remains genuinely open — but the digging itself has held up, and recent excavation of a burial the team calls Shanidar Z suggests bodies were returned to the same spot, generation upon generation. That may be the most radical detail of all. A place you go back to, to put your dead beside the older dead, is not a hole. It is an address. The dead have begun to live somewhere.

Shanidar holds one more lesson, and it comes from a man who did not die for a long time. The skeleton called Shanidar 1 belonged to a Neanderthal who had, decades before his death, suffered a crushing blow to the left side of his skull that likely blinded one eye, and whose right arm — withered, fractured, its lower part missing — had been useless or absent for most of his adult life. On the Pleistocene steppe, a one-armed, half-blind man does not feed himself. He was fed. For years, possibly decades, his people carried him in the economy of the band, and when he finally died, into the cave floor he went with the others. The grave and the crutch are the same argument at different volumes: a *person* is not the same thing as a working body. Once a community has decided that — and demonstrably, communities had decided it long before our species buried its first bead — the extension is almost mechanical. If personhood survives the ruin of the body, why should it not survive the body's stopping altogether? Care for the broken living is the rehearsal; the furnished grave is the performance.

Almost everywhere the practice appears, it comes with a color. Red ochre — powdered hematite, rusted iron, the mineral memory of blood — dusts the bones at Qafzeh, pools in the Sungir grave so thickly that the excavators found the skeletons lying in a red bed, stains burials on every inhabited continent. Humans went to astonishing lengths for it: at Lion Cavern in what is now Eswatini, miners were hacking hematite out of a hillside more than 40,000 years ago, in what may be the oldest mine on Earth — tens of thousands of years of pick-marks for a substance you cannot eat, wear against cold, or throw at a lion. You can only *mean* things with it. What red meant we cannot know for certain, and the honest options are limited and beautiful: blood, life, the color a body loses when it dies, given back.

Imagine you are the one who must carry your mother out of the rockshelter. Yesterday this body cooked, scolded, remembered where the water was in the dry season, knew the names of everyone you have ever loved. Your mind holds a model of her mind, built across your whole life, and that model does not dissolve on contact with her death — chapter 1's whole argument, cashing out here. Tonight she will speak in your dreams, vivid, insistent, apparently *from somewhere*. Now: do you leave the body on the midden? The question answers itself, and the answer is a shovel. The grave is what it looks like when a mind that cannot stop modeling other minds meets a body that has stopped cooperating. We bury because we cannot entirely believe in death — and thirteen centuries of Norse farmers, compressing the whole matter into a verse a child could memorize, would put it this way: cattle die, kindred die, but something of the dead does not. The *Hávamál* offers two candidates in back-to-back stanzas — fame, and "judgment on each one dead." Fame and judgment: memory of the dead, and evaluation of the dead. Every religion in this book will be built from those two materials.

## The Price of a Dead Child

Return now to Sungir, and bring an accountant.

The site sits at roughly 56 degrees north — the latitude of Edinburgh and Moscow — on a steppe that, thirty-four thousand years ago, ran to the horizon as grass, dust, and wind, grazed by mammoth and reindeer and horse. Life there was not marginal; the hunting was rich. But it was work, and everything — food, fuel, clothing, shelter — was purchased with hours. Hours are the currency of the Paleolithic, and at Sungir we can audit how they were spent, because the beads survive.

An adult man — Sungir 1, a robust hunter of perhaps forty who died from a blow or a spear-thrust; violence, then as now, was part of the ledger — was buried under some three thousand beads, with fox-canine ornaments and a painted pebble. Three thousand hours: a year and a half of someone's working life, spent on a man who by any measure had earned standing — a hunter, a provider, a survivor of forty steppe winters. Costly, but comprehensible. Prestige economics; a chief's funeral.

The children break the model. Ten thousand beads — three times the man's allocation — plus the lances, the ivory carvings, the fox-tooth belts, and, laid with them, one object that stops every visitor to the collection: the shaft of an adult human thighbone, its marrow cavity packed with red ochre. Whose bone? Chosen why? An ancestor's relic? An enemy's trophy? A vessel for the color of life, made from the frame of a life? We do not know, and should say so plainly. But the beads alone are enough to force the question that matters: what had two children *done* to earn five years of labor? They had not led hunts. They had not survived forty winters. One of them walked through the world on bowed legs. Children, in a strict prestige economy, have earned almost nothing.

So either status at Sungir could be *inherited* — which would make these graves the oldest evidence on Earth of rank passing through blood, aristocracy in the Ice Age — or the goods were not wages for a life at all, but provisions for a journey. The two readings are not exclusive, and scholars genuinely divide over the mix. Both, though, concede the essential point: the band treated two dead children as persons with a future. You do not equip someone for nowhere.

Sungir is extravagant but not alone. At Dolní Věstonice in Moravia, around 31,000 years ago, three young people were laid in a single grave: the two outer bodies facing the middle one, ochre spilled on the skulls, one outer figure's hand reaching toward the middle body's pelvis, where more ochre had been sprinkled. The middle individual's skeleton is warped by congenital deformities. The tableau spent decades being narrated as a love triangle, a punished adultery, a bride who died with two suitors — archaeology's version of a paperback novel — until DNA testing spoiled the plot by suggesting all three were young men. It is a useful embarrassment. The grave keeps its facts — three youths, one disabled, arranged with unmistakable deliberation and covered in the color of blood — and we keep our ignorance of the story, which is the correct division of labor between evidence and imagination.

At Arene Candide on the Ligurian coast of Italy, around 23,500 years ago, a teenage boy — archaeologists call him Il Principe, the Prince — went into the ground on a bed of ochre with a cap of hundreds of perforated shells and deer canines, batons of elk antler, and, placed in his right hand, a flint blade nearly the length of a forearm, struck from stone that had been carried over the mountains from far away. Part of his jaw had been torn away, likely by the injury that killed him, and the wound area was packed with ochre — patched, cosmetically repaired, for wherever he was going next.

Anthropologists have a lens for this kind of spending: costly signaling, which the first chapter introduced as one of ritual's load-bearing beams. A signal that costs nothing can be faked; a signal that costs five years of labor cannot. When a band pours its surplus into a grave, it broadcasts — to its own members, to neighboring bands at the seasonal gatherings where marriages and alliances were made — something like: *we are the kind of people who do not abandon our own. Not even the youngest. Not even the lame. Not even the dead.* That is an advertisement worth paying for, because it is an advertisement about trustworthiness, and trust is the scarcest resource in a world of strangers. But the signal only works if the audience shares the premise — that the dead persist, that goods can reach them, that a band and its ancestors are one continuing firm. The funeral is a treaty signed in ivory, and the invisible world is the table it is signed on. Here is this book's thesis in its oldest costume: the dead were the first strangers — persons present but unreachable — and religion began as the technology for keeping them kin. Master that trick on the dead, and you hold the patent for performing it on the living.

## The Cave Is Not a Home

In September 1940, four teenagers and a dog named Robot went looking for a rumored tunnel on a hillside above the river Vézère in occupied France. The dog went down a hole; the boys followed with a homemade oil lamp, hacking the opening wide, dropping into the dark — and the walls around them broke into animals. Horses at a gallop, stags swimming an invisible river, aurochs bulls the size of delivery vans. The boys had fallen into Lascaux, and into the middle of this chapter's second body of evidence: the painted caves.

Some numbers, to set the scale. Lascaux, painted around 17,000 years ago, holds some six hundred painted animals and thousands more engraved figures; its largest bull is over five meters long. Chauvet, far older — its charcoal dates cluster around 36,000 to 33,000 years ago, ages so early that some scholars initially refused to believe them — holds lions, rhinoceroses, cave bears, and mammoths rendered with a draftsmanship that would embarrass most art students: shading, perspective, overlapped legs to suggest a moving herd. Across France and Spain alone, hundreds of decorated caves are known. This was not a hobby. It was an institution that ran for twenty thousand years and more — roughly four times the span between us and the pyramids.

And here is the fact on which every interpretation must balance: nobody lived down there. People lived in cave *mouths*, in daylight, near their fires. The paintings are deep — hundreds of meters in, past crawl-spaces and ledges and, at Lascaux, down a shaft you must descend by rope. The art lives precisely where living is impossible: no daylight, no food, air that kills torches, dark so total that your open eyes invent sparks to fill it. Whatever the deep caves were for, they were not decor. Nobody hangs a masterpiece where no one can live to see it — unless the point is that the *place* itself is part of the meaning. The most economical description of a deep painted cave is a space cut off from the ordinary world, entered with effort and ceremony, where images of powerful beings are made and met. We should be careful with the word, but there is no better one on offer: these are sanctuaries.

Open the hood on the operation, because the logistics are magnificent. Light first: you cannot paint what you cannot see. At Lascaux, excavators recovered more than a hundred stone lamps, including one elegant carved specimen of red sandstone found at the bottom of the Shaft — a shallow spoon-shaped bowl that burned animal tallow through a wick of juniper twigs. Such a lamp gives about as much light as a candle and burns roughly an hour on a filling; deep work meant fuel budgets, relays, someone whose job was carrying rendered fat into the underworld. Pigment next: reds and yellows from ochres, blacks from manganese dioxide and charcoal, ground on stone palettes, mixed with water or fat, applied with fingers, pads of moss or fur, chewed-stick brushes — and sprayed, for stencils and soft gradients, by blowing pigment over the wall from the mouth or through a tube, which means the painters' own breath, exhaled across their hands, made some of these images. Height last: parts of Lascaux's ceilings can only be reached from platforms, and sockets cut into the gallery walls imply timber scaffolding — carried in, joint by joint, through passages you and I would hesitate to enter with electric light.

What was it all *for*? Honesty requires the plural. For a century the leading answer was hunting magic — paint the bison to catch the bison — until someone did the zooarchaeology and found that the painters of Lascaux mostly ate reindeer, which barely appear on the walls; Chauvet's walls, stranger still, favor lions and rhinos, which nobody sensible eats. A grand structuralist reading followed — the caves as organized mythograms, animals paired in symbolic opposition — which described the layouts well and explained almost nothing. The currently loudest theory, to which the next two sections return, reads the deep caves as engines of visionary experience. And parts of the record resist every theory with a kind of cheerful insolence: at Gargas in the Pyrenees, dozens of stenciled hands are missing finger segments — frostbite? ritual sacrifice? disease? or fingers deliberately folded, a signal code frozen in pigment? We do not know. Twenty thousand years of institutional religion, and the surviving congregation is a wall of hands that will not say what they mean.

One image shows all the theories colliding at once. At the bottom of Lascaux's Shaft — the pit you descend by rope, where the carved sandstone lamp was found — waits the cave's only substantial human figure, and it is a scandal. A bison stands with its head lowered to charge, a spear across its flank, its entrails hanging beneath it in painted loops. Before it, tipped back at forty-five degrees, falls a man drawn with almost insulting crudeness next to the beautiful animals — stick limbs, four-fingered hands, erect phallus — and instead of a face he has the head of a bird. Beside him, a bird perches on a pole; nearby, a woolly rhinoceros ambles away as if leaving the scene of the crime. Readings on offer: a hunting accident memorialized — the Ice Age's first war grave; a lost myth we are seeing one panel of, comic-strip style; or a trance scene — the bird-headed man not dying but *entranced*, the bird his traveling soul, the erection (documented in certain deep trance and dream states) a clinical detail rather than a bawdy one. Each reading has serious defenders; none can be tested; and the painting has waited seventeen thousand years in the dark at the bottom of a pit, which is to say: whatever it meant, it was not meant casually, and it was not meant for many eyes. We are reading someone else's mail, and the address was not ours.

Two details, though, hint that the caves were *used*, not just decorated. Researchers who have sung and hummed their way through French caves report — suggestively, if not yet conclusively — that painted panels cluster at points of unusual resonance, as if images were placed where the mountain answers back. And at Le Tuc d'Audoubert, six hundred meters and several crawls from daylight, two bison modeled in clay lean against a stone in a chamber whose floor, when the Bégouën brothers reached it in 1912, still carried the heel-prints of adolescents. On the traditional telling the young walked on their heels in a circling dance around the bison; drier readings note we cannot date a heel-print's choreography. But even the driest reading leaves teenagers, deep beyond all daylight, in a room where images of power were made — and left untouched for fourteen thousand years.

Deepest of all, in the cave called Les Trois-Frères, there is a figure everyone in this field knows by heart. Perched four meters above the chamber floor: a rearing thing with — in the classic rendering — the antlers of a stag, the eyes of an owl, the ears of a wolf, a horse's tail, a beard, and human legs mid-caper. The Sorcerer, it was christened by the abbé Henri Breuil, whose famous drawing of it appears in every textbook. And here this book owes you its standing confession: photographs of the actual wall show considerably less than Breuil drew. Some engraved lines are faint, some of the painted antlers hard to find; skeptics have suggested the good abbé, sketching by lamplight in a difficult chamber, drew what he believed the figure ought to be. A part-human, part-animal figure of some kind is genuinely there — that much the photographs support. But the Sorcerer as poster is partly a twentieth-century painting of an Ice Age engraving, and the honest caption for it is the honest caption for this whole chapter: *something* was believed in these caves; the details are ours.

## The First Profession

Somebody, at some point in this long night, got fed for talking to the dead. That sentence is the origin of every clergy on Earth, and the Ice Age gives us candidates for the first faces in the profession.

At Dolní Věstonice — the same Moravian settlement as the triple grave — excavators in 1949 uncovered a burial unlike the others. An older woman, laid on her side under not one but two mammoth shoulder blades, one scored with lines; her body ochred; near her hand, the bones of an arctic fox. Her skull told a story on its own: the left side of her face had been damaged — the bone's asymmetry implies nerve damage in life — so that she would have looked at the world through a face half-frozen, one eye drooping. And here the site produces its almost-too-good detail: from the same settlement comes a small carved ivory head, one of the earliest human faces in art — with its left side subtly slack, drooping at eye and mouth. If the carving depicts the woman, it is the oldest portrait of a specific individual known on Earth, and the woman it portrays was important enough to carve, singular enough to bury under mammoth bone with a fox for company. That is an *if* doing heavy lifting, and archaeologists have leaned on it hard for seventy years because the load is irresistible. Let the record show: an unusual woman, an unusual burial, an unusual face in ivory, one site. The connecting lines are drawn in pencil.

She is not alone in the candidate pool. At Brno, not far away, a man went into the ground around 26,000 years ago with a rhinoceros rib and mammoth tusk, hundreds of fossil shells, drilled discs of stone and ivory — and a unique object: a male figurine of ivory, its head and torso made as separate pieces, jointed. A puppet, or something puppet-adjacent. His bones show he had endured long, painful chronic illness. A body marked by suffering, buried with a jointed effigy and a drum-kit of strange gear: the excavation report all but clears its throat and says the word.

The word is *shaman*, and we must handle it like the borrowed tool it is. It comes from the Evenki people of Siberia — *šamán* — and reached European languages in the seventeenth century through Russian accounts of Siberian practitioners who drummed, danced, fell into trance, and traveled (so they and their communities understood it) to the worlds of spirits and the dead, to heal, to find game, to negotiate. Nineteenth- and twentieth-century scholarship, finding drum-and-trance practitioners among hunting peoples from Lapland to the Amazon, promoted the local Siberian word into a universal category, and then — the step that concerns us — projected the category thirty thousand years backward onto the painted caves and the strange graves. The projection rests on an analogy with two legs of very different strength, and the reader should test each. Strong leg: the human nervous system is standard equipment, identical in Moravia then and Siberia now, and it demonstrably contains the trance capacity the shaman's craft exploits — the next section opens that machinery. Weak leg: everything cultural. The costumes, the cosmologies, the drum rhythms, the meanings — none of that fossilizes forward or backward, and the ethnographies themselves were recorded among modern peoples with histories as long as anyone's. The San of the Kalahari, the Evenki of the taiga, are not living fossils generously preserving our past for us; their traditions have been evolving exactly as long as Rome's. Analogy can tell us what kind of thing an Ice Age religious specialist *could* have been. It cannot tell us what any particular one was.

With that caution paid — and it is not a small fee — the sociology deserves a moment, because the emergence of the specialist is a hinge in this book's story. However the role was locally imagined, a religious specialist is a *division of labor in the invisible world*: one person delegated to deal with the dead, the animals' masters, the causes of illness, while everyone else hunts and gathers. Note what the community purchases. Not weather control — the weather does what it likes. It purchases an *address for misfortune*. When a child sickens, when the herds fail to come, the band with a specialist is not helpless; there is someone whose job is to find out why and negotiate. Anxiety gets a procedure. Grief gets an appointment. And the specialist's authority, interestingly, tends to be purchased with suffering — the Siberian accounts describe initiations of sickness, dismemberment dreams, ordeal — and the Ice Age candidate burials, a woman with a damaged face, a man with a pain-wracked skeleton, youths with deformities honored in death, rhyme with that pattern too neatly for comfort. The first profession may have recruited from those the ordinary economy fit worst: the marked, the strange, the ones who already lived a little way outside the campfire circle. Perhaps because a person who stands apart from the living seems better placed to stand near the dead. Perhaps — the cooler reading — because communities need somewhere to put their unusual members, and the invisible world has always been the employer of last resort. Both can be true. Neither can be proven. The graves simply show us that some individuals were *different*, in life and in death, and that their difference was expensive to honor.

## The Machinery of Ecstasy

Whatever else Ice Age religion involved, the human brain it ran on is the brain you are running now, and that brain ships from the factory with a remarkable feature: it can be driven, by purely physical means, into states in which the invisible world is not believed in but *seen*. No pharaoh, prophet, or printing press was needed to discover this. The equipment is standard; only the user manual is cultural. This section is about the machinery, because religion's oldest hardware is not the temple. It is the nervous system.

The methods form a toolkit that hunting peoples on every continent have independently assembled, which is itself evidence of how available the states are. Rhythm: drumming and dancing sustained for hours, driving auditory and motor systems with a regular pulse. Exhaustion and fasting: blood sugar down, endorphins up. Breath: hyperventilation from song and dance, tilting blood chemistry. Pain, cold, sleeplessness. Darkness and silence: hold that one; the caves are waiting. And in later eras, plants — though for the Paleolithic itself, be suspicious of every confident claim about mushrooms and painted caves: soft tissues do not survive forty thousand years, and the evidence there is speculation wearing a lab coat.

Watch the toolkit run. In the Kalahari, into living memory — and recorded in detail by twentieth-century ethnographers, whose accounts the following paraphrases — the Ju/'hoansi San hold the healing dance: the community's central religious act, held at night, around a fire. The women sit in a ring, clapping interlocking rhythms and singing; the men dance around them, hour upon hour, a pounding circling step. Toward the deep of the night a dancer's legs begin to tremble; healers describe a power they call *n/um* rising hot from the belly up the spine until it — their phrase, rendered by the ethnographers — boils. The healer crosses into the state the San call *!kia*: staggering, sweating, sometimes hemorrhaging from the nose, seeing the sick threads in people's bodies, laying trembling hands on everyone present to pull sickness out, and — in their own understanding — leaving the body to confront the dead, who stand outside the firelight wanting the living, and to argue them into going away. Then note the rock walls of southern Africa, painted over centuries by San ancestors: dancers bent at the waist, figures bleeding from the nose, part-human part-antelope beings, and around them zigzags, grids, and cascades of dots.

Those zigzags are the center of the modern argument about the caves. Your visual system, under stress — trance, migraine, certain drugs, or plain pressure on the eye — generates a stock repertoire of geometric percepts: grids, lattices, zigzags, concentric arcs, dot-fields, spirals. Close your eyes and press your palms gently against the lids for a few seconds: the sparks and honeycombs that bloom are the mild end of the phenomenon. Neuroscientists call these entoptics, and their geometry is wired into the visual cortex itself — which is why a migraine sufferer in Tokyo and one in Toronto see the same fortification zigzags. In the 1980s the archaeologist David Lewis-Williams, working from San ethnography and this neurology, proposed a model: in deepening trance, people see first the geometry; then the brain construes the shapes into meaningful things (an arc becomes a horn); at full depth, whole beings and scenes — and the seer feels *inside* the vision, passing often through a tunnel or vortex. Now inventory the caves: grids, dot-cascades, and zigzags, painted beside and across the animals; beings that are men to the waist and beasts above; all of it at the end of stone tunnels, in absolute dark and silence — an environment that is, as it happens, a first-rate sensory-deprivation chamber, in which unaided hallucination reliably begins within hours. On this reading the deep caves were instruments — the geology itself a machine for entering the other world, the paintings both records of visions and furniture for the next one.

It is a beautiful theory, and this book's ethic requires immediately arming you against it. Critics — and they are serious — note that the model risks unfalsifiability: geometric marks and animal-human hybrids are common enough in human image-making that the theory can claim nearly any cave, and a theory that fits everything explains less than it seems to. The three-stage trajectory is not as neurologically fixed as the model implies; not every tradition's trance produces it. And the San analogy, as the last section insisted, is a lantern, not a map. So hold the balanced position, which is uncomfortable and correct: the trance capacity is certainly universal and certainly ancient; deep caves are certainly the kind of place that triggers it; some cave imagery matches what trance produces; and no one can show that any particular painting was made by any particular state of mind. The machinery is proven. The user logs are gone.

What no critic disputes is the social force of the states themselves. A vision is involuntary, overwhelming, and — crucially — *shareable only by report*. The dancer who has stood among the dead returns with news, and news from the other country is authority of a kind no strongman can confiscate. Weapons can be taken from a rival; visions cannot. In the trance state, the invisible world stopped being an inference from dreams and grief and became, for some, an experience — and experience, as every tradition in this book will demonstrate, is the fuel religions run on long after arguments have gone cold.

## Gods That Fit in Your Hand

In August 1939, days before Germany invaded Poland, an excavation at the Hohlenstein-Stadel cave in southern Germany was shut down by the war it was about to be swallowed by. In the final hours, workers pulled hundreds of ivory fragments from a dark inner chamber, boxed them, and dispersed them into museum storage, where they sat out the war and its long aftermath. Not until 1969 did a researcher begin fitting the pieces together, and not until further finds and a re-excavation of the cave's backfill decades later — the final fragments were seated in the 2010s — did the object stand whole: thirty-one centimeters of mammoth ivory, carved perhaps 40,000 years ago, with the body, legs, and stance of a standing human and the unmistakable head of a lion.

The Lion Man of Hohlenstein-Stadel is, on current evidence, the oldest known image of a being that does not exist.

Sit with that. Every earlier artifact in this book's story — handaxes, beads, ochre crayons, even graves — depicts nothing, or depicts the world. The Lion Man depicts a *counterfactual*: a creature assembled in someone's imagination from parts of the real and then — this is the load-bearing step — *manufactured*, so that other people could see it too. An experimenter who carved a replica with authentic flint tools needed on the order of four hundred hours: two months of full-time work bought with someone else's surplus, spent making a body for an imaginary being. And the original's surfaces are polished in a way consistent with long handling — decades of hands, passing it around, wearing the ivory smooth. A private fantasy had become a public object. Chapter 1 argued that minds like ours could imagine such beings; here is the receipt for the next step, the one religion is actually made of: getting the being *out* of the one mind and into circulation.

The same caves — the Swabian Jura is the Ice Age's Silicon Valley — produced the other half of the ritual toolkit: music. From Hohle Fels, a few valleys away, comes a flute of griffon-vulture wing-bone, five finger-holes, around 40,000 years old, alongside harder-won flutes of mammoth ivory — and an ivory flute is a genuinely absurd undertaking: split the ivory, hollow both halves, carve the bore, and glue the halves back together airtight, all with stone tools and birch tar. Someone wanted pitched sound badly enough to engineer for it. Blow across a replica and you get a bright, carrying scale — the oldest note in this book, and a technology with an obvious use to any species that gathers at night around fires to sing its dead into the other country and its dancers across the line.

And from Hohle Fels too, in the same deep layers, comes a woman. Six centimeters of mammoth ivory, around 40,000 years old: enormous breasts and hips, careful vulva, tiny arms — and no head; where a head would be, a carved loop, polished by wear. She was made to hang. She is the oldest of the figures scholarship calls, with incorrigible nineteenth-century gallantry, Venuses — and she began a tradition of staggering reach. Over the following twenty-five thousand years, hundreds of female figurines were made across a band of Eurasia stretching from the Pyrenees to Siberia: the famous Willendorf woman, eleven centimeters of limestone still flecked with ochre, whose stone a recent analysis traces to sources hundreds of kilometers from where she was found; the black ceramic Venus of Dolní Věstonice; the slim clothed and hooded figures of Mal'ta near Lake Baikal, carved by mammoth hunters at the far end of the continent. The type varies by region and millennium, but the family resemblance is real, and the geography is the point — we will come back to it.

What were they? Assemble the theory menu, and note how each theory quietly describes its theorist: fertility charms (proposed by scholars impressed by fertility); mother goddesses (by those who wanted a matriarchal Eden); Paleolithic pin-ups (by twentieth-century men); self-portraits — the figures' proportions, one ingenious study argued, match what a woman sees looking down at her own body (by an era interested in self-representation); dolls, ancestors, midwifery teaching aids, tokens of alliance between bands. The figurines keep their counsel. Most have no faces. Honest verdict: we do not know what a Venus meant, and any author who tells you otherwise is selling a mirror.

But the Dolní Věstonice workshops let us watch the *making*, and the making may matter more than the meaning. The Věstonice Venus is fired clay — which makes her, and the site's thousands of other fired fragments, the oldest known ceramics on Earth, ten-plus millennia before anyone made a pot. The kilns were there; the craft was there; and the craft was spent entirely on figurines — of women, mammoths, bears, lions. Stranger still: an enormous share of the figures survive as scattered, fractured pieces, and one careful materials study concluded that many were placed in the fire wet, a condition under which clay does not bake but *explodes* — raising the possibility, argued seriously if not settled, that detonation was the point: figures made to be unmade, sacrificed in the flame with a bang for an audience. Others suspect nothing more mystical than impatient potters. The clay holds one more thing: pressed into the surface of one figurine, fired forever into it, is the fingerprint of a child who touched it before it hardened, somewhere around 29,000 years ago. Meaning is perishable; a fingerprint is not.

Now the geography, and with it this book's thesis, stepping out of the wings. A band of thirty or forty people cannot survive alone: it must find mates, trade partners, allies, refuge in famine — all among strangers, the most dangerous animal on the steppe. Chimpanzees solve the stranger problem with violence; humans, uniquely, learned to solve it with *shared fictions* — and here, in the Gravettian, we can see the solution glinting in the archaeological record. When a traveler crossed a watershed thirty thousand years ago and met an unknown band, what could they possibly have in common? Not blood. Not, perhaps, fully a language. But if a figure of ivory hung at the traveler's neck — a woman of the familiar shape, a familiar impossible being — then the two groups shared inhabitants of the invisible world, and people who share an invisible world have a country in common that no river divides. The distribution maps say this happened: one family of imagined forms, spanning four thousand kilometers and twenty-five thousand years, carried hand to hand across a continent of strangers. A god you can carry is a handshake extended across a horizon. Religion is the technology by which strangers become kin, and its first devices fit in a fist: a bead, a flute, a woman of ivory, a man with a lion's head.

## The Thaw

Around 14,700 years ago, the world's thermostat jumped. The ice began its long retreat toward the poles; sea levels started their hundred-meter climb; across Eurasia the mammoth steppe — that endless cold lawn that had fed the herds that fed the painters — began dissolving into forest. The great animals dwindled with their world, and the cave-sanctuary tradition guttered out with them; after the Magdalenian painters, the deep galleries fall silent, and the underworld waited twelve thousand years for its next visitors with lamps — archaeologists, crawling in to meet their ancestors.

But watch the Levant, in the warming centuries around the end of our window, because there the story of the graves takes its decisive turn. The people archaeologists call Natufians, living among newly generous stands of wild wheat and barley, began to do something no humans had sustainably done before: stay put. Round stone-founded houses, heavy grinding equipment too burdensome to carry, villages occupied year on year — and, arriving with sedentism as its shadow, the cemetery. At sites like Ain Mallaha, the dead were buried under and among the houses: not left at a sacred place the band revisited, but *kept*, permanent residents of a permanent address, the ancestors underfoot as the mortar of the community above. The dead, who had wandered with the living for a hundred thousand years, settled down first.

Two Natufian graves, both from around the twelfth millennium BCE, can stand as this chapter's closing witnesses. At Ain Mallaha, an elderly person was laid in the earth with their hand resting on the body of a puppy — the oldest strong evidence of the human-dog bond, and a grave good of a devastating new kind: not wealth, but company. And in a cave at Hilazon Tachtit, a community buried a woman of about forty-five — her body asymmetric, her gait in life likely halting — pinned beneath stone slabs, with the wing-tip of a golden eagle, the pelvis of a leopard, the foot of a human, the skulls of martens, the shell-armor of some fifty tortoises whose roasted meat, the debris shows, fed a funeral feast of dozens. Every element of this chapter attends her funeral: the marked body honored, the animal powers assembled, the community spending extravagantly on its dead, the feast binding the mourners — strangers and kin — into one body around one grave. She has been called the last shaman of the old world and the first priestess of the new, and both titles are guesses. What is not a guess is the direction of travel. The feast is communal, the village is permanent, the dead have an address.

[PLATE: An engraving-style scene — an elderly person curled in a shallow grave, one hand resting on a puppy, mourners silhouetted against round stone huts and standing grain beyond, captioned "Kin at the last: Ain Mallaha, c. 12,000 BCE."]

*Cattle die, kindred die* — the old verse knew the sequence, and knew what survives it. For a hundred thousand years, the fame of the dead had traveled light: beads, bones, ochre, a figure in a fist, a story at a fire. Now the warming world was about to offer humanity a wager it could not refuse — the standing crop, the full granary, the village that does not move — and the invisible world would sign the mortgage alongside the visible one. People were settling down. The gods, who had always traveled with the band, were about to settle too — and acquire, in short order, real estate, staff, and opinions about the harvest. On a hilltop in what is now Turkey, the strangest chapter of that settlement was already stirring: monuments raised by people who had, as yet, no farms at all. The dead taught humans to build for the invisible. The next chapter shows what happened when the invisible learned to demand architecture.
