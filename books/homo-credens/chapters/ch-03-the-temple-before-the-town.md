# Chapter 3 — The Temple Before the Town

> In the sweat of thy face shalt thou eat bread, till thou return unto the ground; for out of it wast thou taken: for dust thou art, and unto dust shalt thou return.
>
> — Genesis 3:19, the Hebrew Bible (King James Version)

Eleven and a half thousand years ago, on a bare limestone ridge in what is now southeastern Turkey, a young person knelt on the bedrock and hit the hillside with a flint pick. Then they hit it again. They had been hitting it since first light, and the hillside had slowly begun to come apart along the outline of something enormous.

Call them the Carver. They are a composite, honestly declared as such: we do not know their name, and writing lay six thousand years in their future, further from them than the Roman Empire is from us. But someone very like them existed, because their work survives, and their work can be measured. The thing emerging from the bedrock under their hands was a pillar in the shape of a colossal letter T, longer than three people lying head to foot, and the quarry around it tells us exactly how it was made: thousands upon thousands of peck marks, each one a flint point driven into limestone by an arm, a channel deepening around the pillar's flanks until the stone could be levered free. On the pillar's broad face, the Carver was working something finer. A fox, in low relief, legs gathered beneath it, teeth bared, leaping forever down the stone.

The ridge smelled of dust and fat. Behind the quarry, on the crown of the hill, smoke went up from dozens of fires, and around the fires were people, hundreds of them, more than the Carver could count and mostly more than the Carver knew: camps of hide and brush, children chasing each other between them, the carcasses of aurochs and gazelle coming apart under stone blades, great stone troughs of something wet that had been standing long enough to go pleasantly strange. The weeks after the gazelle came through were the gathering time. Bands walked in from valleys ten days apart, from people whose dialects bent strangely and whose mothers no one here had met, and for a while the hilltop held more human beings in one place than most of them would see again in a year.

And here is what there was not, anywhere the Carver could see, on that hill or on the great plain unrolling to the south of it. There was not one field. Not a furrow, not a fence, not a granary, not a sheep that knew its owner. Every mouthful eaten on that hilltop had been hunted, gathered, or carried in. These were forager bands, people of the wild herds and the wild grasses, the kind of people who, in every story the last two chapters told, moved with the food and owned what they could carry, and they were doing something no humans had ever done. They were quarrying ten-ton stones with their hands, hauling them uphill, and standing them in rings: pillars with long arms carved down their sides, hands meeting over their bellies, wearing belts and loincloths of stone, faceless heads turned toward each other in the dark. Not houses. Nobody lived in the rings. The people who raised them still, as far as we can dig, lived like their grandmothers, in the old moving way. What they built was a house for someone else.

[PLATE: An engraving-style scene — dozens of fur- and hide-clad workers hauling a great T-shaped pillar up a ramp of earth with ropes and levers, a ring of standing pillars waiting on the hilltop beyond, captioned "Raising a god with no name: Göbekli Tepe, c. 9500 BCE."]

This chapter makes one claim, and it runs against the story everyone learned in school. The story goes: first farming, then surplus, then villages, then, when people finally had wealth and leisure, temples, priests, and gods with real estate. Economy first, religion as the ornament on top. The hill the Carver knelt on, a place now called Göbekli Tepe, is the strongest single piece of evidence we have that the arrow can point the other way: that the temple may have come before the farm, and that the gathering, the feast, and the faceless beings in the rings were not the products of the Neolithic revolution but among its causes. The revolution that domesticated wheat, sheep, and ultimately us was a religious event as much as an economic one. And it needed to be, because, as this chapter will also show, farming was in almost every measurable way a terrible bargain for the individual human being who made it: shorter, sicker, hungrier in the bad years, and worked like an animal in all of them. Something had to assemble the crowds before there was grain to pay them. Something had to make the new obligations feel like the shape of the world, turn the dead into deeds of title, and convert the harvest's tyranny into a calendar of meaning. That something is the technology this book keeps finding wherever humans outgrow the campfire circle: religion, the tool by which strangers become kin. In the last chapter the tool was portable, a bead, a flute, a woman of ivory. In this one it acquires an address.

## The Cathedral on the Hill

Göbekli Tepe, "potbelly hill" in Turkish, was discovered twice, and the first discovery is a parable about seeing. In 1963, surveyors from a joint Istanbul and Chicago project walked the mound, noted the flint scatter and the broken limestone slabs, and moved on; the slabs were logged as probably the grave markers of a medieval cemetery, and the hill went back to sleep. It took until 1994 for a German archaeologist named Klaus Schmidt to climb it again with different eyes. Schmidt had spent years at a nearby site called Nevalı Çori, since drowned by a dam reservoir, where excavators had found a strange cult building with T-shaped pillars, and when he saw the worked stone lying in the grass of the potbelly hill he recognized the same T in the fragments. He later said, in effect, that he had understood within minutes that he had two choices: walk away, or spend the rest of his life there. He spent the rest of his life there.

What came out of the hill justified the sentence. Beneath the surface lay great oval enclosures, the excavators lettered them A, B, C, D, each a ring of T-pillars up to three meters and more, connected by benches and walls, and at the center of each ring a facing pair of much larger pillars: in Enclosure D, the best preserved, five and a half meters tall and in the neighborhood of eight to ten tons apiece. The radiocarbon dates from the wall plaster came back, and came back again, and kept coming back to the same impossible neighborhood: the oldest rings stand around 9500 BCE. That is nearly seven thousand years before Stonehenge, six thousand before the first pharaoh. Göbekli Tepe is further in time from the pyramids than the pyramids are from this morning's newspaper, and when the first pyramid rose, the hill had already been buried and forgotten for five millennia.

The pillars are not abstract. Look closely at the central pair in Enclosure D and the T resolves into a body: arms carved in low relief down the sides, elbows bent, hands brought around to meet over the belly; a belt incised at the waist, with what appears to be a buckle; below the belt, the hanging pelt of a fox. The horizontal top of the T is a head seen edge-on, a head that has no face. These are beings, person-shaped and person-dressed, taller than any person, standing in pairs at the center of rings of smaller beings. Whether they were gods, ancestors, spirits, or something for which we no longer have a word, we do not know, and this book will not pretend otherwise. What we can say is what the stone says: someone conceived of person-like beings at more than human scale, and several hundred someones did very hard work, for a very long time, to give them a place to stand.

Around them, the hill seethes with animals. Foxes, snakes in braided cascades, wild boar with bristling spines, aurochs, cranes, spiders, scorpions, and vultures, carved in relief on the pillars' faces, occasionally in full three dimensions crouching on their tops. Two things are notable about this bestiary. First, it is not dinner. The bones in the feasting debris are overwhelmingly gazelle and aurochs, but gazelle barely appear on the pillars; the carvers favored the toothed, the venomous, and the carrion-eating, the animals that eat you. Second, it is not decoration in any casual sense. On the pillar archaeologists call the Vulture Stone, a vulture spreads its wings above a disc while, near the pillar's base, a headless human body lies with its business unstated. We saw in the last chapter what deep-cave images could and could not tell us; the same discipline applies on the hill. We cannot read this story. We can see that it was a story, that death and dangerous animals were in it, and that it mattered enough to carve.

Schmidt drew the largest possible conclusion, and had earned the right to draw it. The hill, he argued, was a sanctuary and nothing but: no houses, no hearths of daily life, a pure temple to which scattered forager bands made pilgrimage, built by hunters and buried, deliberately and ritually, when its time was done. He liked to say, and titled a book with the thought, that first came the temple, then the city. And in the years since his death in 2014, his own excavation has done to his theory what good excavations do to most theories: complicated it honestly. The newer campaigns have found buildings on the hill that look domestic after all, grinding stones in the thousands, and cisterns cut into the rock to hold tens of thousands of liters of rainwater, which nobody engineers for a place visited twice a year. The neat backfilling has been partly reread as hillside slumping. Geophysical survey shows more enclosures still in the ground than were ever dug. Some of the people, it now seems, lived there, at least some of the time; the "pure temple" is probably too pure, and the honest current label is something like a settlement with extraordinary special buildings, one node, and maybe the grandest, in a whole landscape of such hills now coming out of the ground across the region, with a sister site called Karahan Tepe yielding a chamber of eleven phallic pillars watched over by a human head emerging from the living rock.

But notice what the revision does not touch. Revised, re-dated, and re-labeled, the sequence still stands: the rings, the beings, the feasts, and the crowds all come before domesticated wheat, before domesticated sheep, before the farm, in this very landscape where farming would be born. Even if people lived beside the temples, no one has found the fields that the old story requires to come first. The arrow of the textbook has, at minimum, been scrambled; the strong reading is that it points backward. And one more find keeps the hill tethered to the last chapter's story. Among the human bone fragments recovered from the fill are pieces of skull, carved after death with deep, deliberate grooves, one drilled clean through, as if for hanging or display. The excavators wrote, cautiously, of a skull cult. The dead, who had just learned to settle down, were already being put to work.

## How to Raise a Ten-Ton God

Now open the hood, because the engineering question is the social question in disguise: how, exactly, do several hundred foragers, people with no king, no overseer, no payroll, and no way to compel anyone to do anything, carve a hillside?

Start in the quarry, which sits on the same limestone plateau a few hundred meters from the enclosures, and which still holds its best exhibit: an unfinished pillar, longer than any that was ever raised, lying in its bed where the workers left it. Had it been freed it would have run to some seven meters and perhaps fifty tons; a crack, probably, ended its career, and its abandonment preserved the whole method. The channels around it show the flint picks' work, hour upon hour of pecking, and experiments with replica tools suggest that cutting a standard pillar free was a matter of weeks for a gang, not days. Then the move. There were no wheels, no draft animals, no metal. There were levers of timber, ropes (of hide or of twisted plant fiber; none survive, but the stones did not fly), probably sledges and rollers, and human backs. Schmidt estimated that moving one of the great pillars would have taken as many as five hundred people on the ropes and levers at once. Raising it meant hauling it up an earthen ramp and tipping it into a pedestal socket cut into the bedrock floor, a maneuver in which a moment's lost concentration kills someone, done with a coordination that has to be heard to work: chant, drum, the pull on the beat. Then do all of it again, and again; the enclosures were built, modified, dismantled, and rebuilt over more than a millennium. This was not an event. It was an institution, and it ran longer than most empires in this book will manage.

Five hundred people is the number to sit with. A forager band, as we saw, runs to a few dozen; the seasonal aggregations of the Ice Age might briefly gather a few hundred. Five hundred people pulling one rope is a different kind of object. They cannot all know each other. They certainly cannot all be kin. Nothing in the hill's archaeology suggests anyone could force them: there are no palaces at Göbekli Tepe, no rich graves, no ranked houses, none of the signatures of coercive power that later chapters will learn to spot at a glance. Whoever organized this had no army and no granary. The question of how you herd five hundred strangers without either is, quite exactly, the founding question of civilization, and the hill's answer is written in garbage.

The garbage is bones and troughs. The excavation layers are stuffed with the smashed, roasted bones of aurochs and gazelle in quantities far beyond daily eating: feasting debris, the wreckage of meals for multitudes. Among the ruins stand carved limestone vessels of up to a hundred and sixty liters, and on the inside of some of them chemists have found oxalate crusts, the residue that forms when cereals steep in water, which is what happens when you malt and mash wild grain: beer, possibly, and the claim is honestly contested, but porridge fermenting into something merrier is not a stretch for people who demonstrably soaked grain in tanks the size of bathtubs. Put the pieces together and a mechanism appears, one that anthropologists have watched running in the ethnographic present. On the island of Sumba in Indonesia, into living memory, clans dragged multi-ton megaliths for tomb monuments with hundreds of haulers on rattan ropes, and the physics of recruitment was frank: the host slaughters buffalo and pigs, the feast feeds the haulers, and the glory of the stone accrues to everyone who pulled. Use the analogy with the caution the last chapter taught us, as a lantern and not a map. But it shows the machine can run: the work-feast, in which the only wage is meat, drink, and meaning, and the only foreman is the occasion itself.

Meaning is the load-bearing word. A feast alone buys you a crowd; it does not buy you a crowd that returns every year for a thousand years, hauls until its palms bleed, and hands the obligation to its children. For that, the work itself has to be the point, and here the hill's design speaks. The labor was not spread thin to make it easy; it was concentrated to make it awesome. The pillars did not need to be that big to hold up anything. Bigness was the message, what the first chapter called a costly signal, scaled up from a bead to a monolith: a thing that cannot be faked, spent in public, by everyone together, on the beings in the rings. And the pulling itself did binding work. Humans who move in rhythm, hauling on a chant, dancing in a circle, singing at a fire, come away trusting each other more; the effect is measurable in the laboratory and was presumably discoverable by anyone who ever pulled a rope on the beat. The gathering at the hill was a machine for manufacturing kinship where there was none: strangers arrive, feast together, sweat together, stand something impossible upright together, and walk home changed, members now of an invisible clan whose totems stand on the hilltop, faceless, hands folded, waiting for next year. Religion is the technology by which strangers become kin. At Göbekli Tepe you can watch the technology being scaled: the first machine in history built to convert a crowd into a congregation, and a congregation into a workforce.

[PLATE: An engraving-style scene — a night feast among the stone rings, figures ladling from a great limestone trough by firelight while aurochs carcasses roast and a drummer keeps time, captioned "The wage was the feast: gathering season on the potbelly hill."]

There is one more thing the congregation was learning, without knowing it, in those troughs and grinding stones. To brew for five hundred, you must gather wild grain by the ton. To gather wild grain by the ton, year upon year, at the same hill, is to begin, whether you mean to or not, an experiment in botany.

## The Trap with Wheat Inside

In 1967, an American agronomist named Jack Harlan stood on the slopes of Karacadağ, a broad volcanic massif that can be seen on a clear day from Göbekli Tepe, and did something wonderfully simple: he harvested. The hillsides there are still, as they were twelve thousand years ago, covered in dense stands of wild einkorn wheat, and Harlan went at them by hand and then with a flint-bladed sickle of the sort the Neolithic used. Working an hour, he could bring in kilograms of grain, richer in protein than modern bread wheat, and doing the arithmetic he concluded that a family, working the three weeks of the ripening as it moved up the mountain, could gather more grain than it could eat in a year. His point was sharp: nobody in this landscape was hungry for want of farming. Wild abundance came first; the question was never how people found enough grain, but why they ever bothered to plant it.

Genetics has since added a fact that sits beside Göbekli Tepe like a signed confession. In 1997, a study comparing the DNA fingerprints of domesticated einkorn with wild populations from across the Near East found its closest wild relatives growing on Karacadağ, in sight of the hill of the temples. The dates of the earliest domesticated grain in the region fall in the centuries after the first enclosures rose. The mutation that matters is humble and specific: wild wheat shatters, each ear exploding at ripeness to fling its seed, which is excellent for wheat and maddening for harvesters; in a rare mutant the ear stays whole, waiting on the stalk. Harvest with sickles and you inevitably favor the waiters, because the shatterers have already left; sow from your harvest, and the favor compounds. No one decided this. Selection did it through human hands, over centuries, until there stood in the valley a grass that could no longer reproduce without people: the first domesticated organism was also the first dependent, and its dependence was mutual. We planted wheat, and wheat planted us.

Why bother, given Harlan's arithmetic? The old answers were desperation: climate squeeze, population pressure, the larder running out. Each has its era and its cases, and the cold snap called the Younger Dryas, which bit hard just before our period opens, surely concentrated minds. But the Göbekli Tepe landscape suggests an answer from the other direction, one first floated when an archaeologist convened a symposium in 1953 under the title "Did Man Once Live by Beer Alone?", and it is not desperation. It is appetite, of a particular, social, sacred kind. The gathering must be fed. The feast must be bigger than last year's. The troughs must be filled, the workers sustained, the beings honored, and wild stands, however generous, are a fixed quantity in a rising market. The first cultivation, on this reading, was not a hungry family's insurance but a congregation's catering: grain grown because the hill demanded it, surplus summoned by the sacred before anyone thought to summon it for themselves. The evidence is circumstantial and this book flags it as such; we cannot excavate a motive. But the sequence in the ground, temples first, farms after, fits the catering story better than the starvation one, and it explains the otherwise baffling fact that people took up a way of life that made them, as we will see, measurably worse off. Nobody volunteers for that because they are hungry. They volunteer because it is holy.

However it began, it ended in a trap, and the trap has a clean mechanical description. Cultivation rewards staying: fields must be watched, and a harvest is a bet placed months ahead on one patch of earth. Staying rewards storing; storing rewards more planting; and settled, grain-fed mothers, no longer carrying toddlers across a landscape, wean earlier and bear faster, so the village grows. Within a few generations there are more people than wild abundance could ever feed again, and the door back to the old life has closed behind them, not with a bang but with a birth rate. Every step is reasonable. The staircase is a ratchet. Archaeologists can watch it click at site after site across the Fertile Crescent between 9500 and 7000 BCE: camps become hamlets, hamlets become villages, wild einkorn gives way to the tame mutant, wild goats and sheep shrink into their manageable descendants, and the human skeletons begin, quietly, to record the price.

And with the trap comes a new master, one no forager ever served: the calendar. A hunter's time is weather and luck; a farmer's time is a schedule of unforgiving deadlines. Plow too late, seed too early, miss the rain, let the ripe ear stand three days too long in a hot wind, and your children are hungry next spring, with no move to make, because the family two valleys over is now bound to its own patch and its own deadlines. Anxiety of a new species enters the world: not the sharp fear of the charging boar but the long dread of the empty jar, running months ahead of the present, uncancelable by any day's success. The rites we can trace in the villages of this era grow along exactly that seam. Sowing, first rain, first ripening, harvest, storage: the year itself becomes a liturgy, each dread given a ceremony, each ceremony an address for the dread, the same conversion of helplessness into procedure that the last chapter watched the first specialists perform for sickness and grief. Millennia later, when the villages of this chapter had grandchildren who could write, the oldest festival calendars we can read are all of them harvest calendars, and the logic survives in scripture assembled long after: as Leviticus instructs the farmer, "When ye are come into the land which I give unto you, and shall reap the harvest thereof, then ye shall bring the sheaf of the first-fruits of your harvest unto the priest." The first of the grain is not yours. It never was. The harvest arrives pre-mortgaged to heaven, and the mortgage is the meaning: if the first sheaf is owed, then the crop is a relationship, not a gamble; the empty-jar dread has someone on the other end of it.

Grain, in other words, arrived in human life as obligation, and it was built for the role in a way no earlier food had ever been. Meat rots and honey is finished in a sitting, but grain is countable, divisible, storable for years, identical sack to sack: it can be owed, lent, tithed, promised, inherited, and stolen, and therefore, for the first time in the species' career, it can be accounted. A full granary is not just food; it is the past's labor made present, a frozen block of obligation, and everyone in the village knows to whom its contents are pledged: to the winter, to the seed, to the feast, to the dead, and to whatever stands on the hilltop. Keep that thought; the next chapter will watch people invent writing to keep track of exactly this. What matters here is the theological novelty. The forager's world gave; the farmer's world charges. In the sweat of thy face shalt thou eat bread: the verse at the head of this chapter is written as a curse, spoken on the way out of a garden where food had been free for the taking, and some scholars have heard in Eden's story a distant folk memory of exactly the transition this chapter describes, the foragers' easy world lost for a life of dust and toil. That reading is unprovable and this book holds it lightly. But whoever wrote the verse was a farmer's child, and knew precisely what the bargain had been.

## The Tower and the Skulls

Six hundred kilometers south of the potbelly hill, in the deep trench of the Jordan valley, a spring comes out of the ground beside a mound called Tell es-Sultan, and beside that spring, at almost exactly the moment the first rings rose at Göbekli Tepe, people built the oldest town we know. Jericho, around 9400 BCE: a huddle of round mudbrick houses on stone footings, perhaps a few hundred people, living on wild harvests going tame, gazelle, and the spring's unfailing water. They were not impressive by the standards of what was coming. Except that at the settlement's western edge they built two things that have no precedent anywhere on Earth: a massive stone wall, and, against its inner face, a solid stone tower, eight and a half meters tall, nine meters across at the base, with an enclosed staircase of twenty-two dressed stone steps climbing through its heart. A person can still climb them, up through the dark, and come out at the top of the tenth millennium BCE.

The tower earns the mechanical respect this book pays to cathedrals and pyramids, because per capita it may outrank them. Built of unshaped field stones fitted without mortar, it has been estimated to represent something like eleven thousand working days: a hundred people building for most of a year, out of a town whose whole population, children included, might not have numbered many more than that. Egypt at its height spent a fraction of itself on Giza compared to what Jericho spent on this. And nobody knows what it was for. Its excavator, Kathleen Kenyon, who cut her great trench through the mound in the 1950s with a rigor that reset the discipline, read wall and tower as fortifications, which made Jericho a walled city seven thousand years before its famous trumpets, and the reading held for a generation until someone pointed out the awkward facts: there is no burn layer, no weaponry, no massacre, no enemy in evidence anywhere in the period, and the wall, on inspection, stands against the slope of the land in the manner of a thing built to hold back not men but mud, the flash floods and debris flows that the wadi behind the town still delivers. Then a third reading arrived, stranger and carefully argued: on midsummer evening, the sun sets behind the mountain that overlooks Jericho, and the mountain's shadow, sweeping across the town, touches the tower first; the staircase, moreover, is aligned toward that setting. A solstice instrument, on this account, or a monument to the year's turning, its darkness and its climb meaning something we cannot recover. Defense, flood control, calendar: the honest verdict is that we do not know, and the deeper lesson survives every option. A town of hundreds, without king or bureaucracy, taxed itself a year of its life to build a public colossus. The organizational miracle of the temple hill was not a one-off. It was becoming the normal business of the new villages.

[PLATE: An engraving-style scene — the stone tower of Jericho in cutaway, its inner staircase climbing through solid masonry, round mudbrick houses clustered below and a long mountain shadow reaching toward it across the oasis, captioned "Eleven thousand days of labor, purpose unknown: the tower of Jericho, c. 9000 BCE."]

It is what Jericho did later, though, in the long centuries after the tower, that hands this chapter its second great institution. Kenyon's workers, digging through house floors of the eighth millennium BCE, began to find skulls. Not skeletons: skulls, detached, buried in groups beneath the floors of lived-in rooms, and treated. In 1953 they lifted seven from beneath one floor, and Kenyon, watching the first one emerge, saw a human face looking back: the skull had been packed and coated with lime plaster, modeled into cheeks, nose, eyelids, a chin, the features of a particular person rendered by someone who had known them, with seashells set into the sockets for eyes, one pair of cowries laid sideways so that the slit gleamed like a drowsing gaze. Paint on some; traces of a painted mustache on one. The jaw was usually gone, the plaster chin built directly on the upper teeth, which gives the faces a compressed, grandfatherly softness. They are among the most unsettling and tender objects archaeology owns: portrait and relic in one, a face rebuilt on the bone that had worn it.

The mechanics tell us this was institution, not impulse. To plaster a skull you must first not have buried it too deep and not too finally: the body goes under the house floor, and then, months or years later, when the earth has done its work, the grave is reopened, the skull lifted, the floor resealed. Lime plaster itself is high technology for the era: limestone must be burned at around 800 or 900 degrees for days, devouring whole trees as fuel, then slaked and worked fast. Somebody remembered where the head lay. Somebody was licensed to take it. Somebody knew the recipe, and the face. And Jericho was no outlier: plastered skulls have come out of sites across the Levant, at Ain Ghazal, Beisamoun, Tell Aswad, Kfar HaHoresh, and the wider practice of keeping, circulating, and displaying ancestral skulls ran from Anatolia to the Euphrates. At Çayönü, far to the north, one building held the skulls of scores of people, stacked; the excavators called it, flatly, the Skull Building. The dead of the last chapter had acquired an address. The dead of this one acquired a job.

## The Ancestors Under the Floor

To see the job in full, walk into the strangest town of the Neolithic world. Çatalhöyük, on the Konya plain of central Turkey, was home, at its height around 7000 BCE, to somewhere between three and eight thousand people, which made it one of the largest communities that had ever existed, and it was built like nothing before or since: a single mass of mudbrick houses packed wall against wall, no streets, no doors at ground level, no public square, and, for well over a thousand years of occupation, no temple, no palace, and no building bigger than a house. You entered your home down a ladder through a hole in the roof, past the smoke of the hearth below; the town's thoroughfares were its rooftops. Life happened in one plastered room per family: cooking under the ladder, sleeping on raised platforms along the walls, tools and grain in side bins. The walls were plastered and painted, and replastered, in some houses hundreds of times, fine white layer over fine white layer, a fresh skin for every year.

And under the sleeping platforms lay the family. The people of Çatalhöyük buried their dead beneath the floors of their own houses, flexed tight, wrapped, tucked under the very platforms on which the living slept: infants under one, elders under another, the household lying a hand's breadth beneath the household. One building held the remains of more than sixty people, accumulated across generations. Skulls were retrieved here too, curated, passed on; in 2004 excavators uncovered a burial in which an adult woman had been laid in the earth cradling in her arms a plastered skull, its face modeled and painted red with ochre, and the plaster showed it had been resurfaced and repainted several times: an heirloom face, generations old when it was finally allowed to rest, in the arms of a woman whose relation to it we will never know. On the walls above these graves the townspeople painted vultures, wings spread, stooping at human figures drawn without heads, and modeled the heads and horns of wild bulls, actual horn cores sheathed in plaster, jutting from walls and benches, sometimes rank upon rank of them, so that a family ate and slept and was born inside a room studded with the armored heads of aurochs and floored with its own ancestors. The excavator who led the site's modern investigation, Ian Hodder, called some of these dwellings history houses: houses with more burials, more bull installations, more layers, more retrieved skulls, houses that were rebuilt on their own footprints again and again, new walls seated on old walls, for centuries.

Rebuilt on their own footprints: hold that detail, because it is the key that unlocks the whole arrangement. When a house at Çatalhöyük reached the end of its life it was not abandoned; it was decommissioned like a sacred thing, its fittings stripped, its rooms filled in, and a new house raised on the stumps of its walls, so that the town rose over centuries on stacks of dead houses, each family literally living atop its own past, its floors thickening with its own dead. Ask what a family in a crowded, doorless town owns, and how it proves it. There are no deeds, no courts, no king's seal, and property, which a forager barely needs, is suddenly everything: this roof, this hearth, these bins of grain, these stored obligations. The answer under the platforms is hard to improve on. The graves are the deeds. The ancestors under the floor are a title search that anyone can audit: we live here because our dead lie here, because our house stands on our grandmother's house, and her face, replastered and repainted, is in our keeping and no one else's. Ancestor cult, in the villages of the Neolithic, was property law, the first mortgage registry, written in bone and lime. And it was inheritance law too: to receive the skull is to receive the house, the fields' use, the obligations, the position. When the anthropologists of later chapters find, on every inhabited continent, that farming peoples tie land to lineage and lineage to graves, they are finding this floor, everywhere.

The same lime-plaster technology that faced the skulls produced, at a site called Ain Ghazal near modern Amman, the oldest large statues of the human form ever found. In 1983, a bulldozer cutting a road exposed a pit; in it, and in a second cache found two years later, lay some thirty figures, deliberately and carefully buried under the floors of abandoned houses around 6500 BCE: meter-tall standing statues and busts of lime plaster modeled over armatures of reeds and twine, the reeds long rotted to hollows, with painted garments, bitumen-rimmed eyes wide as headlights, and calm, waiting faces, some with two heads on one body. Whom they portray, gods or ancestors or figures of story, we do not know; that they were made to be seen, tended, and then ceremonially retired and buried, like persons, the pits themselves attest. The dead and the divine, in these towns, were converging on a single address: under the floor, behind the plaster, in the house.

One resident of Çatalhöyük deserves her own paragraph, because for half a century she was made to carry an entire theory of religion. In 1961 the site's first excavator, James Mellaart, found in a grain bin a small baked-clay figure, eleven centimeters of monumental corpulence: a woman enthroned, hands resting on two great felines at her sides, apparently in the act of giving birth. The Seated Woman of Çatalhöyük became the icon of a grand narrative, beloved in the 1960s and after, of a primordial Mother Goddess whose peaceful, woman-centered worship supposedly reigned across old Europe and Anatolia before patriarchal invaders spoiled everything, and pilgrims still come to the Konya plain on the strength of it. The archaeology, alas, declines to cooperate. The site's decades of modern excavation have counted the figurines, and most are animals; of the human ones, many are ambiguous, and the fat, sagging bodies of the "goddesses" match, some researchers note, elderly and honored flesh at least as well as fertile divinity. The graves show men and women eating the same diet, doing comparable work, honored alike in death. No temples attended her; the burials under the platforms answer better to ancestors than to a great goddess; and Mellaart himself, it must be said plainly, ended his career trailing accusations of invented finds, which obliges caution about everything that passed through his hands alone. She may be a goddess. She may be a grandmother. She sat in a grain bin, guarding the stored obligation of the harvest, and honesty leaves her there: a powerful woman of clay, meaning withheld, presiding over the wheat.

What is not withheld is the social miracle around her. Thousands of people, packed wall to wall for more than a millennium, with no visible chief, no garrison, no district of the rich, and remarkably little evidence of violence: Çatalhöyük's skeletons show fewer cracked skulls per capita than many a later city could claim. The town cohered, so far as we can see, not on force but on the machinery this chapter has been assembling: the shared rites in every house, the wild-bull feasts whose horns became furniture, the calendar of the fields, and the dead below, holding each family to its floor and all the floors to each other. Religion is the technology by which strangers become kin; at Çatalhöyük it ran at a density no humans had ever attempted, and it ran, on the evidence, for fifty generations without a king. The village, the ancestor, the harvest rite, the feast: the kit was complete. What no one on the Konya plain could know was the size of the bill being run up, in bone and blood, for the whole arrangement.

## The Bill

Strip the romance from the Neolithic revolution and weigh it as its skeletons do, and the ledger is grim. One of the most quoted verdicts in the literature calls the adoption of agriculture, in the title of a 1987 essay by the physiologist Jared Diamond, "The Worst Mistake in the History of the Human Race," and while the title is a provocation, the data behind it are not. Across region after region, wherever anthropologists can compare the bones of the last foragers with those of the first farmers, the farmers lose. In a classic dataset from the eastern Mediterranean, average height falls by something like fifteen centimeters from the end of the Ice Age to the late Neolithic; it would take until the twentieth century for the region's people to stand as tall as their forager ancestors. Farmers' teeth rot, because porridge and bread bathe the mouth in sugars no gazelle-eater ever met; their skulls and eye sockets go spongy with the anemias of monotonous grain diets and new parasites; their childhoods, legible in the growth-arrest lines of tooth enamel, are punctuated by hunger in a way forager childhoods, with their diversified portfolio of foods, rarely were. A forager who missed the gazelle ate tortoise, nuts, and fish. A farmer whose wheat failed had bet everything on wheat.

The work itself was engraved into bodies, and nowhere more legibly than at Abu Hureyra on the Euphrates, a village that happens to span the transition, forager settlement below, farming town above, so that the change can be read in one mound. The skeletal biologist Theya Molleson, studying the farming layers, kept meeting the same strange suite of injuries, and overwhelmingly in the women: the last joint of the big toe crushed and arthritic, kneecaps and knees deformed, the lower spine collapsed into itself. Then she reconstructed the posture that produces exactly that suite: kneeling, toes tucked under and flexed against the ground, thrusting the whole body forward and back over a saddle-shaped grinding stone, hour after hour. Grain must be ground fresh daily; experiment suggests feeding a family this way costs several hours a day of that motion, every day, for a working lifetime, and the women's bones at Abu Hureyra had been remodeled by it as surely as a laborer's hands grow callus. The forager women in the layers below show nothing comparable. In the sweat of thy face shalt thou eat bread: the verse is anatomy before it is theology, and at Abu Hureyra the face it sweated from was, disproportionately, a woman's.

Disease came on the same cart as the grain. Settle several thousand people, their stored food, the rats and mice and sparrows that food attracts, and their newly domesticated animals into a warren of mudbrick, remove the forager's simplest sanitary technology, which is leaving, and you have built, without meaning to, the first reliable machine for growing plagues. A forager band is a poor host for an epidemic: too few bodies, too spread out, and forever walking away from its own filth and its own dead. A village is a banquet. The measles and the smallpox, the tuberculosis and the influenza that would cull the human species for the next ten thousand years were, at their origin, the illnesses of the animals now penned against the wall, the cattle and pigs and fowl whose diseases learned, in the intimacy of the byre, to jump the fence into human lungs and guts. Contaminated wells and grain shared with rats did the rest, and the children paid first: the growth-arrest lines thicken, the infant graves multiply, and in town after town of the late Neolithic the skeletons carry the signatures of the fevers we would not name for another eight thousand years. The forager's world had its predators; the farmer's world grew its own, invisible, indoors, and endlessly.

Set it all in one column and the ledger of the Neolithic is brutal. Shorter. Sicker. Toothless sooner. Broken at the knee and the spine by the grinding stone. Hungrier in the failed year, and now, for the first time, dying in droves of one another's breath. If a forager could have read the bones of their farming grandchildren, they would have run for the hills, and some, for a while, did. So the question the whole chapter has been walking toward stands up and demands its answer. If the bargain was this bad, and the bones prove past argument that it was, why did human beings sign it, and keep signing it, generation after generation, until there was no wild country left to run to?

The honest answer is that by the time the bill came due, leaving had already become unthinkable, and religion is most of the reason why. The trap, remember, was never only botanical. A family bound to its fields was also bound to the ancestors plastered into its walls and buried beneath its platforms, and you do not walk out on your grandmother's face. It was bound to the calendar of sowing and first-fruits and harvest, a liturgy that gave the empty-jar dread a name and an address and a rite, and you do not abandon the only ceremonies that make the terror negotiable. It was bound to the stored dead, whose graves were the deeds to the ground under its feet, so that to leave the land was to forfeit not just a house but a lineage, an identity, a place in the only order there was. The package that made farming survivable is exactly the package that made it inescapable. Religion did not cause the Neolithic's suffering, and this book will not pretend it did. But religion is what let human beings bear it: it turned toil into obedience, hunger into fasting, the tyranny of the seasons into a covenant, and the terrifying novelty of owing your whole life to a patch of dirt into the natural and holy shape of the world. The gods did not send the bill. They made it payable. Faced with a bargain that impoverished the body, our species reached, as it always does, for the tool that reorganizes the soul, and paid the Neolithic's price in the only currency that made the price feel like meaning.

And then the villages kept growing. The ratchet that had turned camps into hamlets and hamlets into towns did not stop at Çatalhöyük's thousands; it never stops. Multiply the village by a thousand, and the machinery this chapter has watched being built out of feasts and grinding stones and ancestral skulls faces a load it was never sized for: not a few hundred kin who can be gathered by a shared totem, not even a few thousand, but tens of thousands of strangers packed against a river, who must trust one another with grain, water, and their lives while knowing that they will die without ever learning each other's names. The ancestors under the floor cannot bind a crowd that large; there are too many floors. Something new has to hold it together, and downriver, on the hot flat mud between two rivers, that something is about to acquire a house, a wardrobe, a menu, a staff, and a set of books. The gods are about to learn to keep accounts. In the sweat of thy face shalt thou eat bread, and now, for the first time, a scribe will write down exactly how much bread, whose it was, and what the god was owed.
