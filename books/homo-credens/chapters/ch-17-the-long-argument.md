# Chapter 17 — The Long Argument of Christendom

> That they all may be one; as thou, Father, art in me, and I in thee, that they also may be one in us: that the world may believe that thou hast sent me.
> — John 17:21 (KJV)

On a Saturday afternoon in July — the sixteenth, in the year 1054 — three men in Latin vestments walked into the largest room in the world. The Church of Holy Wisdom in Constantinople, Hagia Sophia, had stood for five centuries by then, its dome floating a hundred and eighty feet above the marble floor on a ring of windows that made the stone seem to hang from heaven rather than rise from earth. The air inside was gold: gold mosaic overhead, gold light falling through the drum, the gold smoke-haze of incense that never entirely cleared between liturgies. Clergy were preparing the afternoon service. Candles were being trimmed. Somewhere a deacon was laying out the vessels for the Eucharist.

The three visitors did not come to pray. At their head walked Humbert, cardinal-bishop of Silva Candida, a Burgundian monk with a scholar's Greek and a litigator's temperament, legate of the pope of Rome. Behind him came Frederick of Lorraine, chancellor of the Roman church, and Peter, archbishop of Amalfi. They crossed the nave, mounted the sanctuary, and laid a folded parchment on the great altar — on the very table of the sacrament, at the hour of the sacrament. Then they turned and walked out. At the doors, Humbert shook the dust from his feet, a gesture every cleric in the building could read, because it comes from the Gospels: what you do when you leave a town that has refused the word of God.

The parchment was a bull of excommunication. It damned Michael Cerularius, patriarch of Constantinople, and his associates, in language that did not stoop to understatement — the patriarch's men were compared, over a long paragraph, to a rogues' gallery of ancient heretics. Among the charges was an accusation that would make a careful reader blink for the next nine centuries: the Greeks, the bull complained, had *deleted* a clause from the creed — the clause stating that the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father *and the Son*. In fact, as we will see, it was the Latin West that had added those words. Humbert was excommunicating the Greeks for failing to include an insertion his own church had made. He appears to have believed the charge sincerely. It did not help.

A deacon ran out of the great church after the legates, into the summer street, holding the bull, begging Humbert to take it back. Humbert refused. The parchment fell in the dust. There is one more detail, almost too neat: Pope Leo IX, the man whose authority the bull invoked, had died three months earlier, in April. Whether a dead pope's legates could excommunicate anyone was, even by the standards of eleventh-century canon law, an excellent question. Nobody paused to ask it. The patriarch convened his synod and excommunicated the legates right back — carefully, the legates only, not the Roman church. Both sides, in the moment, thought they were disciplining a few individuals. Contemporary Byzantine chroniclers barely mention the affair. And yet the date stuck. When this book closes, at the end of the year 1900, those two excommunications will still be in force.

This chapter is about the nine hundred years in which Christendom argued with itself — roughly 600 to 1500 CE — and about the strange, colossal things the argument built. The medieval centuries are usually filed under "the Age of Faith," as if faith meant quiet. It did not. These were centuries of continuous, institutionalized, world-shaping quarrel: over two words in a creed, over whether a pope outranks an emperor, over whether a pagan Greek philosopher could be baptized after fifteen centuries in the grave, over what a piece of bread becomes on an altar, over what money can and cannot buy of heaven. The argument was not the failure of medieval Christianity. It was the operating system. Every great medieval institution — the monastery, the cathedral, the university, the canon law court — is a machine built to keep an argument running without breaking the world, and each of them, at least once, broke the world anyway. And underneath the whole quarrelsome edifice hums this book's oldest theme, now scaled to a continent: religion as the technology that turns strangers into kin. A Norwegian fisherman's son and a Sicilian notary's daughter, who shared no language, no king, no law, and no ancestor, could walk into the same kind of building anywhere from Lisbon to Riga, hear the same Latin sentences, and kneel as members of one family. That was the technology working. The price of the family was the edge of it — the Greek, the Jew, the heretic — and this chapter will not look away from the price.

## Two Words

Begin with the two words, because everything else in the East–West rupture is commentary on them.

In 381 CE, a council of bishops at Constantinople finalized the creed that is still, in 1900, recited by more Christians than any other text except the Lord's Prayer. Concerning the Holy Spirit, the third person of the Trinity, the creed states that the Spirit "proceeds from the Father." The bishops were not improvising; they were quoting. In John's Gospel, Jesus promises: "But when the Comforter is come, whom I will send unto you from the Father, even the Spirit of truth, which proceedeth from the Father, he shall testify of me" (John 15:26, KJV). From the Father. The verse says nothing about the Son.

Two centuries later and four thousand kilometers west, in Visigothic Spain, churches began reciting the creed with a small addition: the Spirit proceeds from the Father *and the Son* — in Latin, *filioque*, a single compound word. The motive was local and defensive. The Visigoths had been Arians — followers of the old teaching, condemned at Nicaea, that the Son was a lesser, created being — and when their king Reccared converted to Catholic orthodoxy in 589, Spanish churchmen wanted the Son's full divinity nailed into every corner of the liturgy. Making the Spirit proceed from him too was a way of underlining his equality with the Father. It was theology as reinforcement bar: added for strength, in one province, against one heresy.

The bar migrated. Frankish churches picked up the Spanish custom; Charlemagne's court theologians championed it; and here the argument stopped being about grammar and became about power, because Charlemagne had a use for a stick with which to beat Constantinople. On Christmas Day, 800, Pope Leo III had crowned the Frankish king "emperor of the Romans" — a title that, as far as the actual Roman emperor in Constantinople was concerned, was not vacant. Christendom now had two empires, each calling itself Roman, and the filioque became a badge of the western one. In 809 a Frankish council at Aachen endorsed the addition. Pope Leo III agreed with the doctrine but — with an instinct his successors would have done well to inherit — refused to alter the creed's text, and had the original, filioque-free wording engraved on two silver shields, in Greek and in Latin, and hung in St. Peter's. The shields did not work. By 1014, at the coronation mass of Emperor Henry II, Rome itself was singing the interpolated creed.

Why did two words matter so much? Partly for a reason that is genuinely theological, and worth thirty seconds of your attention, because it is the strangest and most abstract quarrel ever to help tear a civilization in half. The doctrine of the Trinity is, in effect, a diagram: three persons, one God, connected by relations of origin. The Son is *begotten* of the Father; the Spirit *proceeds* from the Father. "Proceeds" here does not mean motion, as steam proceeds from a kettle; it names an eternal relation — who has their being from whom. The whole fight is about the direction of one arrow in that diagram. For the Greek theologians, the Father is the sole fountainhead of deity; give the Spirit a second source and you have either two gods or a muddled one. For the Latins, the arrow from the Son safeguarded the Son's full equality. Both sides could cite scripture, fathers, and councils. Neither side was stupid. That is what made it insoluble.

But the deeper injury was procedural, and here the modern reader is on home ground. The creed of 381 had been ratified by councils of the whole church. The West had altered it *unilaterally* — first a Spanish province, then a Frankish empire, then Rome. To Constantinople the question was not only "is the filioque true?" but "who has the authority to edit a document that belongs to everyone?" Rome's answer, increasingly, was: Peter does. "And I say also unto thee, That thou art Peter, and upon this rock I will build my church; and the gates of hell shall not prevail against it" (Matthew 16:18, KJV). On that verse the papacy built the most audacious constitutional claim of the Middle Ages: that the bishop of Rome, Peter's heir, held a general jurisdiction over the whole church, East included — a claim buttressed, in Leo IX's own correspondence with the patriarch, by the Donation of Constantine, a document in which the first Christian emperor supposedly granted the popes imperial rank. The Donation was an eighth-century forgery. Nobody in 1054 knew that; it would take a fifteenth-century philologist, Lorenzo Valla, to prove it. The medieval argument was conducted, on all sides, in complete good faith and partly on forged evidence — a combination history serves up more often than we like to admit.

Around this doctrinal core, grievances accreted the way barnacles do: the Latins used unleavened bread for the Eucharist, the Greeks leavened, and each found the other's loaf scandalous; Latin clergy shaved, Greek clergy wore beards; the Latins fasted on Saturdays; the Greeks let parish priests marry; and in the 1050s the two hierarchies were in open competition for the churches of southern Italy, where Norman adventurers were carving out a Latin dominion on formerly Byzantine soil. Patriarch Michael Cerularius closed the Latin-rite churches of Constantinople; Leo IX sent Humbert; Humbert laid his parchment on the altar. There had been a rehearsal already — in 867 the patriarch Photius had condemned the filioque and the papal claims in nearly identical terms, and that breach had healed. Contemporaries in 1054 had every reason to expect this one would too. It did not, and the honest way to say why is that the schism of 1054 was not an event but a label — a paper cut later infected by blood. The wound itself was inflicted a hundred and fifty years afterward, by Christian soldiers, and we will stand in the smoke of it before this chapter is done. "Is Christ divided?" Paul had demanded of the quarreling Corinthians (1 Corinthians 1:13, KJV). After 1204, the answer was yes.

## The Server Farms of God

Two o'clock on a winter morning, anywhere in Latin Europe, any year in these nine centuries. A bell. In a long unheated dormitory, rows of men — or, in the women's houses, rows of women — rise from straw mattresses, pull hoods over shaved or veiled heads, and file down a night stair into a cold stone choir, where they will stand in the dark and sing psalms for an hour. They did this yesterday. They will do it tomorrow. Some of them will do it for sixty years. It is the office of Vigils, the first of the day's eight rounds of prayer, and the community performing it believes the singing to be, quite literally, work — the *opus Dei*, the work of God, as essential to the world's running as plowing or kingship. The last chapter but one left Christian monks fleeing to the Egyptian desert to escape a church grown comfortable. By 600 CE the refuge had become an infrastructure, and its Latin architect was an Italian abbot named Benedict of Nursia, whose Rule — written around the 530s for his monastery at Monte Cassino — is one of the most consequential administrative documents ever composed. It is short, moderate, and shrewd. It prescribes obedience, permanence, and a balanced day of prayer, reading, and manual labor; it warns that idleness is the enemy of the soul; it calls the monastery a school for the Lord's service. Above the whole enterprise hangs the Gospel counsel that made monks in the first place: "If thou wilt be perfect, go and sell that thou hast, and give to the poor, and thou shalt have treasure in heaven: and come and follow me" (Matthew 19:21, KJV).

What did the monasteries actually do for the civilization around them? Many things — they drained swamps, brewed, healed, hosted travelers, prayed for the dead on an industrial scale — but one function towers over the rest in hindsight. They were Europe's server farms. Antiquity's information was stored on organic media with a shelf life. A papyrus roll rots; a parchment book survives centuries, but only if someone recopies it before mold, fire, worm, or war gets there first. A text lives only as long as the interval between copies. After the western Roman state failed, exactly one institution in Latin Europe maintained the three things continuous copying requires — literacy, labor, and institutional immortality — and it was the monastery. The monks were not, for the most part, trying to save classical civilization. They were trying to save their own souls, and the copying was penance, discipline, and obedience. Civilization got backed up as a side effect.

Watch the mechanism, because it is glorious. Parchment first: the skin of a calf or sheep, soaked in a lime bath for days to loosen the hair, scraped with a curved blade called a lunellum, stretched drum-tight on a frame, scraped again, dried, and rubbed smooth with pumice. A single large Bible could consume an entire herd — the great Codex Amiatinus, made around 700 at the twin monasteries of Wearmouth and Jarrow in Northumbria and weighing about seventy-five pounds, is estimated by modern scholars to have required the skins of some five hundred calves. Ink next: crushed oak galls — those marble-sized growths a wasp's egg raises on an oak twig — steeped to extract their acid, mixed with iron sulfate and a little gum arabic. The mixture writes pale and darkens as it oxidizes, biting into the parchment; oak-gall ink is why thousand-year-old manuscripts are still legible, and, when mixed too strong, why some of them have letters eaten clean through the page. The pen is a goose flight feather, hardened in hot sand and cut with the knife every scribe kept in the other hand. The page is pricked and ruled with drypoint lines. Then the labor: a practiced scribe managed perhaps a few high-quality pages a day; a full Bible was years of one human life. The scribes knew precisely what it cost. Their marginal complaints survive by the hundred — the ink is thin, the parchment is hairy, the light is failing, three fingers write but the whole body aches, and now that the book is finished, the writer would like a drink.

Every copy was a gamble against loss, so the system's redundancy mattered more than any single house. Monasteries formed networks — Irish foundations like Iona and Lindisfarne in the north; Bobbio and St. Gall, planted by traveling Irish monks, south of the Alps; Cassiodorus's Vivarium in Italy, founded by a Roman statesman who saw the dark coming and turned his estate into a copying operation. Texts moved between nodes as loans, gifts, and exemplars. And around 800 the network ran history's most consequential backup pass: Charlemagne, wanting correct texts for a reformed church, set his court scholar Alcuin of York and a generation of monastic scriptoria to gathering and recopying everything they could find. In the process the copyists standardized a new script — Carolingian minuscule, small, round, legible, with regular word spacing — into which the surviving literature of ancient Rome was transcribed. The oldest manuscripts we possess of most classical Latin authors — Cicero, Virgil, Livy, Tacitus — are not Roman. They are Carolingian monastic copies. Six centuries later, Renaissance humanists, mistaking those beautifully clear Carolingian pages for genuinely ancient ones, modeled their own handwriting on them, and the first Italian printers cut type from the humanists' hands. The lowercase letters on this page are the direct descendants of a monastic backup format. You are reading the server farm's output right now.

The economics of all this deserve a hard look, because prayer was the product and the product sold. A monastery ran on endowment: nobles gave land — the one asset that mattered — in exchange for perpetual intercession, above all for the dead. Over these centuries Latin theology was elaborating a third address between heaven and hell: purgatory, where the saved-but-imperfect are scoured before glory, and where the prayers of the living measurably help. (The doctrine's roots are ancient, but historians — Jacques Le Goff most famously — locate its full crystallization in the twelfth century, and the church defined it formally at the Council of Lyon in 1274.) Purgatory turned intercession into a service with customers, and the monastery into a factory of suffrages. The abbey of Cluny, founded in 910 in Burgundy with a charter that freed it from every authority except Rome itself, became the model: a liturgical corporation whose monks sang nearly around the clock, whose special commemoration of all the faithful dead — All Souls' Day, November 2 — spread to the whole church, and whose network of dependent priories grew to something on the order of a thousand houses across Europe, all answering to one abbot. Cluny's third church, consecrated in stages around 1100, was the largest in Christendom and would remain so for four hundred years. It was magnificent, and magnificence was the problem. In 1098 a band of monks left the Cluniac orbit for a swamp called Cîteaux to keep the Rule strictly, and the Cistercians — white-habited, ferociously ascetic, contemptuous of gold and sculpture — became the start-up that rebuked the incumbent. Bernard of Clairvaux, their overpowering spokesman, mocked the carved monsters in Cluniac cloisters as expensive distractions from prayer. His own order, meanwhile, perfected a different art: settling waste land, engineering water. A mature Cistercian abbey ran a channeled stream through its precinct in sequence — turning the mill, powering the forge and the fulling hammers, watering the brewery, flushing the latrines — a hydraulic spine that a twelfth-century description of Clairvaux itself lovingly traces reach by reach. Add disciplined sheep-ranching on the granges and the order that fled wealth engineered itself rich within two generations — the medieval monastic cycle, reform to riches, running at full speed.

Hold on to the network itself, though, for it is the through-line made visible. By 1100, a Benedictine from Canterbury could arrive at a monastery in the Apennines and know, before anyone spoke, when the community would rise, what psalms they would sing, and what the day's hours would be. The Rule was a shared protocol; the liturgy was shared software; Latin was the interface language. Strangers in every human particular were kin at the night office. Scale that up from the monastery to the whole Latin church — one calendar, one mass, one canon law from Portugal to Poland — and you have the largest kin-making machine the world had yet run.

## Stone, Light, and Bone

On the night of June 10, 1194, the town of Chartres burned. Fires were the endemic disease of medieval towns — timber, thatch, open flame — and this one took most of the town and gutted the cathedral, one of the great churches of France. The town's despair had a precise focus: the cathedral's supreme treasure was the Sancta Camisa, a tunic said to have been worn by the Virgin Mary, and it was presumed lost. Three days later, clergy emerged from the crypt carrying the relic unharmed — they had sheltered with it behind an iron door as the church burned above them. The mood of the town flipped from grief to fervor, helped along by preachers who drew the obvious lesson: the Virgin had permitted the fire because she wanted a grander house. Within a generation, Chartres had one — substantially complete by the 1220s, a speed almost without parallel for a project of its size — and it is the building through which we can open the hood on the age's most astonishing machine: the Gothic cathedral.

Start with the problem. A stone vault — the fireproof ceiling every great church wanted, for the excellent reason Chartres had just demonstrated — does not merely press down on its walls. It pushes them *outward*. A round Romanesque barrel vault thrusts along its whole length, so Romanesque builders answered with mass: walls meters thick, windows small and few, interiors of holy gloom. The Gothic system, assembled piece by piece in the Paris basin during the twelfth century, is a kit for cheating that outward push. The pointed arch, first: break the semicircle and steepen it, and more of the load travels down toward the ground and less shoves sideways — with the side benefit that arches of different spans can rise to the same height, freeing the floor plan. The ribbed vault, second: build a skeleton of stone ribs and let the webbing between them thin to a shell a hand-span thick, so the ceiling's whole weight funnels onto a few points. The flying buttress, third, and most audacious: since the thrust now arrives at known points high on the wall, meet it there — leap over the aisle roof with a half-arch of stone that catches the push and carries it to a massive freestanding pier clear of the building. And on top of that pier, a pinnacle — which looks like pure ornament and is in fact ballast, deadweight that bends the combined force safely down into the masonry. The pinnacle is not decoration wearing a structural excuse; it is a counterweight wearing a crown. Do all this and something miraculous follows: the wall no longer holds the building up. The skeleton does. The wall is free to become a screen of glass.

Which was the point, because light had a theology. In the 1140s, Abbot Suger of Saint-Denis, rebuilding the choir of his royal abbey north of Paris, had wrapped its new architecture in a mysticism of radiance drawn from a Greek theologian the abbey believed to be its own patron saint Denis (he wasn't — the texts were by a much later Syrian writing under the name Dionysius; the confusion was one of history's most productive filing errors). God is light, the argument ran, and material light is the ladder by which dull minds climb to the immaterial kind; therefore gold, gems, and above all glass are not luxuries but instruments. The consecration of Suger's choir in June 1144, with its ring of glowing chapels, is as good a birthday as Gothic has. Every subsequent cathedral was, among other things, a rehearsal of the Bible's last city: "And the city had no need of the sun, neither of the moon, to shine in it: for the glory of God did lighten it, and the Lamb is the light thereof" (Revelation 21:23, KJV).

The glass itself was pot-metal chemistry: sand and beechwood ash fused with metal oxides in the melt — cobalt for the blues that made Chartres famous, manganese for purple, copper for reds so dense the sheets were "flashed" thin over clear glass to let light through at all. Sheets were cut to pattern with a hot iron and nibbled to shape, painted with a dark vitreous pigment for faces and drapery, fired to fuse the paint, then assembled like a jigsaw in channeled strips of lead and set into iron armatures. Chartres holds about 176 windows. Their donors are legible in the corners: alongside kings and bishops, the town's guilds — bakers, furriers, wine merchants, shoemakers — paid for windows and had their trades portrayed in them, working at the bottom of the very light they had bought. And the engineering had limits it discovered the empirical way: at Beauvais, whose canons wanted the tallest choir in Christendom and got it — the vaults topped out around 48 meters, a fourteen-story building of the thirteenth century — part of the structure came down in 1284. The masons had no calculus and no theory of statics; they worked by geometry, proportion, template, and precedent, scribing full-size details onto plaster tracing floors that still survive at York and Wells. When they stepped past precedent, the building itself graded the exam.

Who paid for all this, and why? Here the cathedral connects to the second economy in this section's title: bone. Nearly every great church was, functionally, a reliquary with a roof. The relic was the point: the physical remains — bodies, bones, garments, instruments — of the holy dead, in which heaven's power was believed to be resident and contagious. A church with a great relic had a queue, and a queue is revenue: offerings at the shrine, sales of badges and candles, gifts and bequests from grateful pilgrims, tolls and trade through the town. Chartres had the tunic; Canterbury, after four knights of Henry II cut down Archbishop Thomas Becket at his own altar in December 1170, had Europe's newest martyr — canonized with record speed in 1173, his shrine an economic engine for three centuries, his "blood," endlessly diluted, sold to pilgrims in little lead ampullae. Paris played the game at sovereign scale: in 1239 King Louis IX bought the Crown of Thorns from the cash-strapped Latin emperor of Constantinople for a sum — around 135,000 livres, by the standard accounts — roughly three times what he then spent building the Sainte-Chapelle to house it. Read that sentence again: the box cost a third of the jewel. Relics were capital assets, and behaved like them: they were inventoried, insured by miracle stories, divided (an arm here, a finger there) like shares, and — since demand always exceeded supply — stolen and counterfeited. Theft was so normal it had a justifying literature: the monks of Conques obtained their star relic, the virgin martyr Foy, by planting a brother in the abbey of Agen for years until he could abscond with her bones, and the community's own account of the theft presents it as pious enterprise — the saint, after all, could not have been stolen unless she wished to move. Venice acquired St. Mark from Alexandria in 828 by, as the Venetians themselves delightedly told it, hiding the body in a basket under a layer of pork to discourage Muslim customs inspection. Historians call the genre *furta sacra*, holy theft, and the shrug in the phrase is the period's own. Counterfeits drew fire early: the abbot Guibert of Nogent, writing in the early twelfth century, pointed out acidly that two different churches claimed the head of John the Baptist, and that the Baptist was unlikely to have had two.

It is easy to sneer, and the sneer misses the machine. Pilgrimage was medieval Europe's circulatory system. The road to Santiago de Compostela in northwest Spain — where a shrine held what was believed to be the body of the apostle James — drew pilgrims in the hundreds of thousands over these centuries, and the route grew infrastructure the way a river grows a valley: hospices a day's walk apart, bridges, shrine churches, monasteries, and an entire service economy. A twelfth-century compilation, the Codex Calixtinus, includes what is recognizably a guidebook: route stages, which rivers are safe to drink, which ferrymen overload their boats and drown pilgrims for their baggage, where the locals are kind. Pilgrims wore badges — the scallop shell for Santiago, the ampulla for Canterbury — cast in lead-tin alloy in stone molds by the thousand, among the first mass-produced consumer goods in European history. And walk the mechanism back to this book's spine: on the Camino, a Frisian sailor, an Occitan widow, and a Polish knight — strangers by every measure of blood, tongue, and law — slept in the same hospices, walked to the same tomb, and called each other by a kinship term: fellow pilgrim. The relic at the road's end was the pretext. The road itself was the technology, making kin at walking pace. There is a fine irony in the fact that the founder of this religion had blessed precisely the believers who did *not* need to see and touch: "blessed are they that have not seen, and yet have believed" (John 20:29, KJV). His medieval church built history's greatest apparatus for seeing and touching. Humans are creatures of hand and eye; the apparatus knew its species.

## God Wills It

On November 27, 1095, in a field outside Clermont in central France — no hall could hold the crowd — Pope Urban II preached the sermon that launched the crusades. What exactly he said, we do not know. Five substantial versions survive, all written down years later, most after Jerusalem had fallen, each retrofitting eloquence to an outcome its author already knew; they agree on the gist — the churches of the East plead for aid, the Holy Sepulchre lies under infidel dominion, knights of Christ should turn their violence from each other to a holy purpose — and diverge on nearly everything else. The chroniclers report that the crowd began to roar *Deus le volt* — God wills it — and whether or not the words were said that day, they were certainly said soon and everywhere after.

Strip the drama and look at the machinery, because the crusade was above all a legal and financial instrument. Formally it was an armed pilgrimage. The volunteer took a public vow and sewed a cloth cross to their shoulder; in exchange the church granted a plenary indulgence — and here we must open the hood on the concept that will, three chapters from now, split western Christendom in half. Medieval penance ran on arithmetic: sin, confessed, was forgiven as to guilt, but justice still required satisfaction — fasting, prayer, alms, in quantities the confessor assigned, and whatever went unpaid at death would be exacted in purgatory. The crusade indulgence commuted the whole outstanding balance: make this journey with contrite heart and the church, from the treasury of Christ's merits, remits your penalty in full. For a knightly class whose profession was violence and whose ledgers of penance were correspondingly black, the offer was overwhelming. The package included temporal clauses too — church protection for the crusader's family and property, moratorium on his debts — and it needed them, because crusading was ruinously expensive: outfitting a knight for the East is estimated to have cost four or five times his annual income. Crusaders raised the cash by mortgaging land, and the institutions with liquid wealth to lend were the monasteries. The server farms became banks.

What the machinery unleashed did not stay within its legal drawings. Before the princes' armies even mustered, popular bands moved down the Rhine in the spring of 1096, and some of them — most notoriously the following of a minor noble, Emicho of Flonheim — turned on the Jewish communities of Speyer, Worms, and Mainz with a logic contemporaries recorded plainly: why march two thousand miles against God's enemies while other unbelievers prospered at home? The bishops of these cities, to their credit and by their office, tried to shield the Jews — the bishop of Speyer largely succeeded; at Mainz the archbishop's protection collapsed and perhaps a thousand or more died, some killed, some — as the Hebrew chronicles record with awe and grief — dying by their own and each other's hands rather than accept baptism, a martyrdom they named the Sanctification of the Name. This too is crusade history, from its first year: the kin-making machine at continental scale, sharpening its edge on the stranger within. When the armies of the First Crusade took Jerusalem itself on July 15, 1099, against every reasonable military expectation, they massacred much of the population, Muslim and Jewish alike; the chronicler Raymond of Aguilers boasted of the slaughter in imagery of blood to the horses' bridles borrowed straight from the Apocalypse — a chronicler's scriptural crescendo rather than a body count, but boast it remains. Christendom read the impossible victory as God's own verdict on the enterprise. The problem with reading victory as verdict is what you must read defeat as; the crusading movement would spend two centuries finding out.

Success built institutions. The conquered strip of Syria and Palestine — Outremer, "the land beyond the sea" — needed permanent defenders, and the solution was a genuinely new religious life-form: the military orders, Templars and Hospitallers, monks with swords, vowed to poverty, chastity, obedience, and combat. Bernard of Clairvaux — the same Bernard — wrote the new knighthood's apologia, arguing that the killing of evildoers in defense of Christendom was not homicide but the destruction of evil itself. The orders grew rich on donations across Europe and invented, incidentally, a primitive international banking network: deposit funds at the Temple in London, present your letter, withdraw in Acre.

Then the machine began to eat its own. The Second Crusade (Bernard preached it too) failed miserably; Saladin retook Jerusalem in 1187; the Third Crusade won back a coastline and not the city. And in 1202 the Fourth Crusade assembled at Venice and demonstrated, in one four-act catastrophe, everything this chapter has been arguing about argument. Act one: the crusade's planners contracted with Venice for transport for 33,500 men at a price of 85,000 marks of silver, and roughly a third of the expected force showed up, leaving a debt no one could pay. Act two: Venice, led by the blind nonagenarian doge Enrico Dandolo, proposed working off the debt in kind — the fleet would first reduce the rebellious (and Christian, and Catholic) city of Zara on the Adriatic, which was duly stormed; Pope Innocent III excommunicated the whole expedition. Act three: a Byzantine pretender, Alexios, promised the debt-ridden army fantastic sums, ten thousand soldiers for the Holy Land, and the submission of the Greek church to Rome if they would restore his line in Constantinople; the crusade sailed for the Bosporus. Act four: the promises failed, the pretender was murdered in a palace coup, and on April 12–13, 1204, the army of the Fourth Crusade stormed and sacked Constantinople — the greatest Christian city on earth, the city of the 1054 altar. For three days they stripped it: palaces, churches, libraries, tombs. The historian Niketas Choniates, an eyewitness who lost everything, describes altars broken up for their metal, mules led into Hagia Sophia to haul out plunder and slipping in their own blood on the polished floor, and a woman of the streets enthroned in the patriarch's seat, singing. The four bronze horses that had presided over the city's hippodrome sailed west to stand above the door of St. Mark's in Venice; a torrent of looted relics followed — much of what Louis IX would later buy traced back to this haul. Innocent III, when the reports reached him, wrote in genuine anguish that the crusaders had drawn their swords against the very people they had vowed to aid, and that the Greek church could now hardly be blamed for seeing in the Latins nothing but perdition. He was right. The schism of 1054 had been two words and two documents; 1204 made it a memory of fire, and it was never mended within this book's horizon. On the altar of Hagia Sophia, in the end, the parchment mattered less than the mule's hooves.

The crusade, once invented, proved to be a general-purpose tool, and the papacy used it as one: against the Cathar heretics of southern France (the Albigensian Crusade, opened in 1209 with the sack of Béziers, where — a Cistercian storyteller claimed decades later — a papal legate, asked how to tell Catholic from heretic, said to kill them all, for God would know his own; the quotation is probably legend, the indiscriminate massacre is not), against Baltic pagans, against the pope's own political enemies. And on the crusade's financial chassis, quietly, a fateful component was standardized: since not everyone who took the vow could fight, the church allowed vows to be *redeemed* for money — pay the cost of a crusader and receive the crusader's indulgence. Salvation's accounting had acquired a cash interface. Remember that; chapter 20 is waiting for it. One more scriptural gear should be noted, because the medieval mind ran on exegesis: at the Last Supper, the disciples say, "Lord, behold, here are two swords. And he said unto them, It is enough" (Luke 22:38, KJV). Medieval theorists read the two swords as the spiritual and temporal powers — and when Pope Boniface VIII, in his 1302 bull *Unam Sanctam*, claimed that *both* swords belong ultimately to the church, he pressed the papal monarchy's claim to its maximum. The king of France answered doctrine with procedure: his agents burst in on the old pope at Anagni in 1303 to arrest him. Boniface was rescued by the townsfolk but died within weeks — and within a decade the papacy itself had moved to Avignon, into the last section of this chapter and the antechamber of the Reformation.

## The Argument Machine

Walk now into a narrow Paris street in the 1250s — the rue du Fouarre, the Street of Straw, named for the bundled straw the students sat on because benches were an extravagance. It is a winter morning and the unheated schoolroom is packed: teenagers and twenty-somethings from Scotland, Saxony, Hungary, Portugal, sorted by "nations," all following an argument conducted in the one language every one of them owns, Latin. Twice a year the master will hold a disputation *de quolibet* — "on whatever you like" — where anyone present may raise any question at all, and the master must take it seriously in public. The institution hosting this scene is barely two generations old, is called a *universitas* — the ordinary Latin word for a guild, the same word the bakers used — and is among the most consequential inventions of the Middle Ages: a self-governing corporation of masters and students, chartered to teach, examine, and certify, and to quarrel under license. Bologna, the eldest (its law schools coalescing from the late eleventh century), was a *students'* guild, and its statutes are a joy: the students hired the professors, fined them for starting lectures late or running long, fined them for skipping difficult chapters, and required them to post a deposit before leaving town. Paris was a masters' guild under the bishop's loosening grip; Oxford grew when a twelfth-century quarrel made study in Paris difficult for the English. Popes protected the universities against kings and bishops alike, for the sensible reason that a Christendom-wide institution certifying Christendom-wide degrees was a papal kind of thing: one more machine for making strangers kin — this time, kin in argument.

What detonated inside these new institutions was a library. Latin Europe had kept only scraps of Aristotle; the full corpus — logic, physics, biology, metaphysics, ethics — had lived for centuries in Arabic, worked over by the falāsifa of the Islamic golden age we met in the last chapter. In the twelfth century, at Toledo and in Sicily, a translation industry moved it into Latin: men like Gerard of Cremona, who reportedly came to Toledo hunting one book, Ptolemy's Almagest, and stayed a lifetime to translate seventy more. With Aristotle came his Muslim commentators, above all Averroes of Córdoba — medieval Latin writers called him simply "the Commentator." The effect on thirteenth-century Paris is hard to overstate: here was a complete, rigorous, godless-in-the-particulars account of the whole natural world, produced by a pagan and delivered by rivals of the faith, and it was manifestly the best science anyone possessed. The authorities did the reflexive thing: councils at Paris banned the natural works in 1210 and 1215. The ban held about as well as such bans do. By 1255 the arts faculty of Paris had made the banned books *required reading* — the contraband had become the curriculum, possibly the purest specimen of that recurring process in the history of ideas.

The method the universities forged for handling dangerous material was scholasticism, and it deserves rescue from its dusty reputation, because at its core it is a machine for disciplined disagreement. Its unit is the *quaestio*: pose a question; marshal the strongest authorities and arguments *against* the answer you will defend — not straw men; the form required the best objections you could find or invent; cite an authority on the other side; resolve with your own reasoned determination; then answer every objection one by one. Peter Abelard had built the prototype a century earlier in his *Sic et Non* — "Yes and No" — a compilation of over a hundred and fifty theological questions on which he simply lined up the church fathers contradicting each other, on the stated principle that doubt leads to inquiry and inquiry to truth. The whole apparatus — objections first, steelmanned; sources cited; contradictions faced in public — is recognizably an ancestor of the disputation that still, in 1900, ends with a doctoral candidate defending theses against examiners. Medieval Christendom, so often pictured as the enemy of questioning, built the first institution in history in which formal, public doubt was a graduation requirement.

Its greatest operator was a fat, silent, aristocratic Italian. Thomas of Aquino, born about 1225 in a castle family that had him marked for a respectable abbacy at Monte Cassino, instead joined the Dominicans — one of the two shocking new mendicant orders (Francis of Assisi's friars and Dominic's preachers, founded a generation earlier), who took monasticism out of the countryside and into the cities and universities, owning nothing, living on begging, preaching to the new urban crowds. To his family, joining the friars was roughly what joining a street cult would be to a later aristocracy; his brothers kidnapped him and held him in the family castle for about a year to break his resolve (his early biographers add lurid details of the temptations they tried; a year in the tower with only a Bible and the *Sentences* arguably created the most dangerous mind in Europe). At Paris and Cologne he studied under Albert the Great, who — the story comes from the early biographers — answered classmates mocking the huge quiet Sicilian as a dumb ox with the prophecy that the ox's bellowing would one day fill the world. What filled the world was the *Summa Theologiae*: hundreds of questions, thousands of articles and objections, an attempt to run the entire scholastic machine over the whole of Christian doctrine and Aristotelian science at once and show that reason and revelation, rightly read, cannot finally contradict — truth being one, the book of nature and the book of scripture must agree, and where they seem not to, we have misread one or the other. His five ways to demonstrate God's existence from motion, causation, contingency, degree, and order became the load-bearing wall of natural theology for centuries — you will hear their echo when this book reaches William Paley's watch, and hear the wall crack when it reaches 1859. He dictated, the accounts say, to several secretaries at once, on different works. Then, on December 6, 1273, at mass in Naples, something happened — vision, breakdown, stroke; his companions did not have our categories and we should be wary of imposing them. He never wrote again, telling his socius Reginald, in a report that reaches us through the canonization inquiries, that after what he had seen, everything he had written seemed to him like straw. He died three months later, en route to a council at Lyon called to reunite the churches of the 1054 wound. Within three years, church authorities at Paris and Oxford had condemned lists of philosophical propositions that brushed his teaching; within fifty, he was a saint; the argument continued over his corpse, which was, all things considered, exactly how he had spent his life and might have wanted it. Paul's sentence covers the scholastic enterprise better than any other: "For now we see through a glass, darkly; but then face to face: now I know in part; but then shall I know even as also I am known" (1 Corinthians 13:12, KJV). The scholastics numbered the panes of the dark glass, and knew they were panes, and kept polishing.

## The Pale Horse Preaches

In the spring and summer of 1348, the pope of Rome — who did not live in Rome; we will come to that — sat between two enormous fires. Clement VI's physician, Guy de Chauliac, one of the age's best, advised the flames as a corrective to pestilential air, and the pope, obedient to his science, spent weeks in a papal chamber at Avignon turned into a kiln while the plague killed his city around him. The clergy could not bury fast enough; Clement consecrated the river Rhône, it was reported, so that corpses committed to the water counted as receiving Christian burial. The physician stayed at his post through the whole visitation and wrote up the symptoms and his own infection and survival with clinical honesty, noting that fear had made physicians useless and that he himself had continued visiting the sick only to avoid dishonor — a sentence worth a shelf of heroic legends.

The Black Death entered Europe through the Crimean port of Kaffa in 1346 — one chronicler, Gabriele de' Mussi, claims the besieging Mongol army catapulted its plague dead over the walls, though he was not there and the tale should be handled with tongs — reached Messina in Sicily in October 1347 aboard Genoese galleys, and burned across the continent in four years: Marseille by winter, Avignon in January 1348, Paris that summer, London by autumn, Scandinavia and Russia by 1352. The dying was of a scale for which Europe had no institutional or imaginative precedent: modern estimates put the toll at between a third and half of the population — tens of millions of people — with some towns and monasteries effectively erased. Later ages would learn to say *Yersinia pestis*, fleas, rats, pneumonic transmission. The fourteenth century had to explain the end of the world with the tools on hand, and the tools were theology, astrology, and smell. The medical faculty of Paris, commissioned by the king, delivered the official scientific verdict: a conjunction of Saturn, Jupiter, and Mars in Aquarius on March 20, 1345, had corrupted the air — miasma theory with an astronomical trigger, footnoted and internally rigorous, wrong at every layer. The church's account was older and simpler: pestilence is God's scourge for sin, as it had been for David; the response is repentance, procession, and prayer. Terrified parishes clung to the psalm made for exactly this: "Nor for the pestilence that walketh in darkness; nor for the destruction that wasteth at noonday" (Psalm 91:6, KJV).

Repentance, at plague scale, went industrial. The flagellant movement rolled across Germany and the Low Countries in 1348–49: brotherhoods of dozens or hundreds, sworn to a campaign of 33½ days — one day for each year of Christ's earthly life — marching town to town under crosses and banners, forbidden to bathe, shave, change clothes, or speak with women, and twice a day stripping to the waist in the market square to scourge themselves with leather thongs tipped with iron, singing hymns while the townsfolk wept and dipped cloths in the blood as relics. They carried a Heavenly Letter, allegedly fallen from the sky, announcing God's wrath and their commission. Town councils at first welcomed them as spiritual emergency services; then the movement began claiming to absolve sins without priests, denouncing the clergy, and — repeatedly — leading massacres of Jews, and in October 1349 Clement VI banned it, having concluded that a self-authorizing mass penance movement was more dangerous than no penance at all.

The massacres are the part of the plague's religious history that must be told without cushioning. The libel spread — before the plague itself, in many places — that Jews had poisoned the wells. Confessions were produced under torture at Chillon in the autumn of 1348 and circulated as evidence between city councils, with the bureaucratic diligence that is somehow the most chilling detail of all. Basel burned its Jews in January 1349. Strasbourg's city council resisted the libel — and was deposed by the guilds for it; the new council delivered the city's Jewish community, perhaps as many as two thousand people, to the fire on February 14, 1349 — St. Valentine's Day, before the plague had even reached the city. Mainz and Cologne followed. Debts to Jewish lenders died with the creditors, a motive contemporaries themselves named. The old poison verse — "His blood be on us, and on our children" (Matthew 27:25, KJV), Matthew's crowd-cry at the crucifixion — had been read for centuries as a standing indictment of all Jews, and in the plague years that exegesis did exactly what loaded exegesis does. Against all this stood, to his credit, the burning pope between his fires: Clement VI issued bulls in 1348 pointing out with plain logic that Jews were dying of the plague like everyone else and that the accusation was the devil's work, ordering clergy to protect them, and offering refuge in Avignon's territories. Where the bulls conflicted with profit and terror, the bulls lost. The kin-making machine, under the greatest stress test of its existence, did both of the things this book keeps finding: it bound the terrified together — in processions, confraternities, deathbed rites — and it fed its own edge to the fire.

What did the plague do to belief? Less than a modern reader might expect, and more. It did not make atheists; the sources give us almost none. It made mystics, flagellants, benefactors, and bookkeepers of death. Parish clergy died at fearsome rates — in some English dioceses, the death of roughly half the beneficed clergy can be traced within two years, many infected at deathbeds administering last rites; others fled, and chroniclers duly recorded both the martyrs and the runaways. The replacement clergy were younger, scarcer, more expensive, and less trained, and complaint about them became a fixed genre for a generation. Lay devotion, meanwhile, intensified and privatized. The *Ars moriendi* — "The Art of Dying" — became one of the fifteenth century's runaway bestsellers: a manual, produced in both a learned long version and a picture-driven short one (eleven woodcuts, printed as block books even before movable type), that staged the deathbed as a final examination with known questions: five temptations — against faith, to despair, to impatience, to vainglory, to avarice — each pictured as demons crowding the bed, each answered by an angelic counter-move, with practical rubrics for the attending family. Death, which the plague had made chaotic, was re-scripted as a procedure that could be *passed*. Around it grew a whole material culture of memento mori: the Danse Macabre mural at the Paris cemetery of the Holy Innocents (1424–25), where skeletal Death leads off pope, emperor, laborer, and child in strict social order; cadaver tombs on which a bishop's effigy lies robed above and sculpted as a worm-eaten corpse below. And the suffrage economy boomed: chantries — endowed altars where a priest sang mass for a founder's soul in perpetuity — multiplied by the thousand; wills specified masses in bulk, by the trental and the thousand; the living took out amortized mortgages on purgatory. Not all of the century's religion was arithmetic, it should be said. In May 1373, in Norwich, a thirty-year-old woman on what she believed was her deathbed received a series of visions she spent the rest of her anchoress's life expounding; Julian of Norwich's *Revelations of Divine Love* — the first book in English we can attribute to a woman — distilled a theology of almost scandalous gentleness out of the corpse-strewn century, and its most famous sentence, in modernized spelling, answers the plague's whole question: "All shall be well, and all shall be well, and all manner of thing shall be well." The pale horse of the Apocalypse — "and his name that sat on him was Death, and Hell followed with him" (Revelation 6:8, KJV) — preached to Europe for four years. It is worth sitting with the fact that Europe's answers ranged from burning its neighbors alive to that sentence of Julian's, and that both came out of the same church, the same texts, the same terror. A technology is what its users make of it. This book has said so from the first grave.

## The Machinery of Pardon

The pope between the fires was, as promised, not in Rome. In 1309, in the sour aftermath of Anagni, a French pope settled the papal court at Avignon on the Rhône, and there it stayed for nearly seventy years, through seven popes, every one of them French. Avignon was not the moral cesspool its enemies advertised — its curia included serious jurists and its palace serious administration — but it was, unmistakably, a *fiscal* capital: the papacy at Avignon perfected the best revenue machine in Europe. Annates took the first year's income of every benefice the pope filled, and the popes reserved ever more benefices to fill; "expectancies" sold the reversion of offices not yet vacant; the chancery and the courts charged fees at every seal and signature. Petrarch, who lived nearby and loathed the place, called it the Babylon of the West and made the label stick — Babylon, the captivity, the exile from Rome — and this book's reader has now met enough Babylons to appreciate the layers of the insult. To the ordinary parish, what Avignon meant was that grace had a schedule of charges, administered from a fortress-counting-house in Provence. When the end came, it came partly at the urging of a dyer's daughter from Siena: Catherine, unlettered, dictating letters to popes and princes with a boldness that still crackles off the page, harried Gregory XI — urging him, in effect, to be a man, to stop fearing kings, and to bring Peter's chair home. In 1377 he went.

He died within the year, and what followed made Avignon look like the good old days. The Roman crowd, determined never to lose the papacy again, howled outside the 1378 conclave for a Roman pope; the terrified cardinals elected an Italian, Urban VI, who promptly proved so violently autocratic that the same cardinals declared his election void — coerced, therefore invalid — withdrew, and elected one of their own as Clement VII. He returned to Avignon. Now there were two popes, each with a college of cardinals, each excommunicating the other's entire obedience, and Christendom's kin-map split down the middle by political convenience: France, Scotland, Castile, and Aragon for Avignon; England, the Empire, Scandinavia, and most of Italy for Rome. Every Christian in Latin Europe was, by somebody's arithmetic, excommunicate. The Great Western Schism ran for thirty-nine years, and the age's best canonists concluded that only a general council could end it — a doctrine, conciliarism, with revolutionary implications, since it located ultimate church authority in the assembled whole rather than the papal head. The first attempt, at Pisa in 1409, deposed both popes and elected a third; neither deposition took, and the council called to abolish two popes had thereby produced three — a result so perfectly emblematic of this chapter that no satirist would dare invent it. The Council of Constance (1414–1418) finally did the work: it decreed, in the decree *Haec Sancta*, that a general council holds its power directly from Christ and that even a pope owes it obedience; it cleared the field of all three claimants; and in 1417 it elected Martin V, sole pope, schism over. The constitutional question — council over pope, or pope over council? — was left armed and ticking; the restored papacy spent the next half-century defusing conciliarism by outmaneuvering it, and the ticking is audible in chapter 20.

Constance did one more thing, and it must be told straight. Among its agenda items was a Czech priest and Prague university master, Jan Hus, who had preached — following the Oxford don John Wyclif — against indulgence-selling, clerical wealth, and papal pretension, and for the authority of scripture. Hus came to Constance voluntarily, under an imperial safe-conduct, to defend his teaching before the assembled church. The council imprisoned him, tried him as a heretic, and — the safe-conduct being held not to bind where heresy was concerned — burned him at the stake on July 6, 1415, in a meadow outside the town, in a paper crown painted with demons; eyewitness accounts record him praying and singing as the fire took hold. His ashes went into the Rhine so that Bohemia could not have a relic. Bohemia's answer was two decades of Hussite revolt that five successive crusades failed to crush. The council also ordered the long-dead Wyclif's remains dug up and burned; the sentence was executed in 1428 and the ashes tipped into the little river Swift. The men who ran Constance were not fools or monsters; they were the finest jurists and theologians of Christendom, acting to save the unity of an institution in visible peril. That is precisely what makes the meadow outside Constance one of the coldest places in this book. The long argument had rules, and the rules said that some arguers could be burned.

Which brings us, last, to the mechanism this whole era hands forward like a lit fuse: the indulgence, full-grown. Assemble the parts we have collected. From the penitential system: sin leaves a debt of temporal punishment, payable in this life or in purgatory. From the crusades: the church can remit that debt wholesale, and — via vow redemption — for money. From the theologians, formalized in Clement VI's bull *Unigenitus* (1343, the year before that pope faced the plague): the church administers a treasury of merit, the infinite surplus won by Christ and the saints, from which such remissions are drawn. From the shrines: grace attaches to places and visits, in quantified days and years of pardon. Add the jubilee: in 1300 Boniface VIII proclaimed a holy year — plenary indulgence for pilgrims to Rome — and the crowds were so vast that a chronicler describes two clerics standing at the altar of St. Paul's literally raking in the offerings day and night; Dante, who was probably there, put the crowd-control arrangements on the bridge of Castel Sant'Angelo into the *Inferno*. The interval was to be a century; demand shortened it to fifty years by 1350, thirty-three by 1390, twenty-five by the 1470s — the sacred discovering the logic of the subscription renewal. Pardoners — licensed and unlicensed — hawked letters of indulgence between jubilees; Chaucer, writing in the 1390s, could count on every reader recognizing his Pardoner, with his wallet of pardons hot from Rome and his pillowcase passed off as Our Lady's veil and pig's bones as saints' relics — satire that biting is evidence of a market that mature. And in 1476, Pope Sixtus IV took the step the logic had been straining toward for two centuries: an indulgence applicable to souls *already in purgatory*. The bereaved could now buy relief for their dead. Grief itself had a price list. The reformers' verse was already sitting in the Gospel, waiting: "My house shall be called the house of prayer; but ye have made it a den of thieves" (Matthew 21:13, KJV).

Every part of the machine had begun as an answer to a real pastoral question — how can penance be merciful? how can the living love the dead? — and every part had been ratified by careful men in good faith, one defensible step at a time. That is what makes the story instructive rather than merely lurid: institutions rarely corrupt by betraying their logic; they corrupt by completing it. By 1500 the completed logic was on sale in every market square in Europe, and the argument about it — Wyclif's argument, Hus's argument, paid for so far in exhumed ashes and a Bohemian meadow — had merely been postponed. One final image, and the fuse is laid. In Mainz, in 1454, a goldsmith named Johannes Gutenberg was perfecting a device of adjustable metal type, and among the very first dated products of his press — earlier than his famous Bible's completion — were printed indulgence certificates: standardized forms, blank spaces left for the buyer's name and the date, run off by the thousand for a fund-raising campaign against the Turks. The press's first commercial mass product was a receipt for grace. The machine that would broadcast the explosion was built, in part, to print the gunpowder. This book will strike the match in chapter 20.

## The Bridge Eastward

Stand back from the nine centuries and hear the chapter's epigraph again — *that they all may be one* — prayed over an altar that would be looted by its own communicants, a creed split by two words, a papacy split by two obediences, a Europe of one baptism that burned its arguers and its neighbors. And yet: the same centuries built the server farms that saved a civilization's memory, vaults that made stone weightless, roads that made kin at walking pace, universities where doubt became a degree requirement, and a woman in a cell in Norwich writing that all shall be well. The long argument was not a flaw in the system. It was the system — the kin-making technology running at continental scale, quarreling because it was alive, and paying for its unity in the coin of its edges. Now turn the map. While Latin Christendom argued at sword-point over who held Peter's keys, another teaching had been crossing the other half of the world — over the Pamirs and the South China Sea, through translation bureaus and merchant caravans and island monasteries — without a Clermont, without a legate's bull, without a single crusade. It conquered more of humanity than Christendom had ever seen, and it did so, by and large, without an army. How the Dharma did that is the next chapter's story.
