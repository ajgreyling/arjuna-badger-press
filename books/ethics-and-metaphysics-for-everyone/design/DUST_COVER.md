## Front Flap

*Physics Won't Hurt You* spent four hundred years and thirty chapters answering the question
"what's actually true." This is the book that was always going to have to come next, because it
turns out knowing what's true was somehow the easy part. Nobody has ever been executed for a bad
value judgment about *torque*. People have started wars over what's *right*, gone to their graves
over what's *real*, and built entire civilizations on metaphysical assumptions nobody in the room
had actually agreed to.

This is a plain-spoken, unashamedly funny tour of the two oldest and least cooperative branches of
philosophy — the one that tries to work out how you should live, and the one that tries to work out
what, if anything, is actually there while you're living it — written for the reader who has never
taken a philosophy class and would like to keep it that way while still getting to have the good
arguments. No jargon survives past its first plain-language handle. No thought experiment shows up
without a person attached to it: a trolley, a brain in a vat, a Chinese room, a ship that's been
repaired so many times nobody agrees it's still the same ship — and the very stubborn, very human
disagreements underneath every one of them.

It follows *Physics Won't Hurt You*'s own rule to the letter: mainstream consensus, live open
question, and honest speculation, labelled plainly and never confused for one another, all the way
out to the frontier where the two subjects turn out to be the same subject wearing different coats —
because it is very hard to argue about what you *should* do without first working out what's actually
*there* to do it to.

Physics told you what the universe is doing. This is the book about what you're supposed to do about
it, and whether "you" is even the right word for the thing doing the deciding.

## Back Cover

Ethics: is there a right answer, or just a lot of people who are very sure of themselves? Metaphysics:
is anything real, and if so, is it done being real yet? This book takes both questions exactly as
seriously as four thousand years of very smart, very disagreeable people have taken them — and not
one bit more solemnly than that.

**Inside:**
— Why "it depends" is sometimes the only honest answer, and why that's not a cop-out
— The trolley problem, and the friend of a friend who insists it happened to someone they know
— Free will, determinism, and the mess in between that most people actually live in
— What survives of "you" across a lifetime of replaced cells, changed minds, and forgotten Tuesdays
— The simulation argument's less famous cousin: does it even matter if it's true?
— A field guide to spotting a bad argument before it's finished being made

A companion volume to *Physics Won't Hurt You*. Read in either order. Argue about both.

## Author Bio (Back Flap)

Andries J. Greyling writes books instead of, or possibly in addition to, sleeping. He is the author
of *Physics Won't Hurt You (Well, Kind Of)*, a history of physics from falling apples to the edge of
everything, and has never met a plain question he didn't want to ask twice. He lives wherever there
is decent coffee and something worth arguing about.
