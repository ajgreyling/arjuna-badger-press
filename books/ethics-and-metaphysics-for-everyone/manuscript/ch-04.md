# Chapter 4 — The Greatest Number

Jeremy Bentham asked to be, and remains to this day, physically present at his own philosophy's
headquarters. His skeleton, dressed in his own clothes and topped with a wax head — the real one did
not survive the preservation process well — sits in a wooden cabinet at University College London,
where students still occasionally wheel him into faculty meetings as a joke that would, by all
accounts, have delighted him. It is a strange way to be remembered, and also, in its odd literal-
mindedness, a perfect illustration of the man's whole approach to ethics: reduce the fog, ask what
would actually maximize the good, and don't let sentiment or tradition talk you out of the answer
just because it's unusual.

Bentham's core idea, laid out most fully in his 1789 *An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and
Legislation*, is disarmingly simple to state and has proven almost endlessly complicated to actually
live by: the right action is whichever one produces the greatest happiness for the greatest number of
people affected by it. He called this the principle of utility, and he meant it with a rigor that
startled his contemporaries — happiness and suffering, in his accounting, were not vague poetic
notions but something closer to a measurable quantity, capable in principle of being added up,
compared, and traded off against each other, the way a chemist weighs reagents. He even sketched, half
seriously, a "felicific calculus" for scoring an action's pleasure against its pain across intensity,
duration, certainty, and reach — an idea that reads today as almost charmingly of its era, an
Enlightenment faith that anything worth knowing could eventually be counted.

His student and intellectual heir, John Stuart Mill, inherited the framework and spent much of his own
career sanding down its rougher edges. Mill's 1863 essay *Utilitarianism* is where the theory gets its
most famous refinement: not all pleasures, Mill argued against his own teacher, are created equal.
"It is better to be a human being dissatisfied than a pig satisfied," he wrote, "better to be Socrates
dissatisfied than a fool satisfied" — a direct concession that some satisfactions, the intellectual and
the creative among them, carry a kind of quality that raw quantity can't capture, however good you get
at counting. Mill also pushed the theory outward from individual acts toward general rules — arguing
that a society governed by broadly reliable rules like "keep your promises" and "don't steal" produces
more total happiness over time than one where everyone recalculates the utility of each individual
choice from scratch, a move that quietly borrows some of the stability Kant's system offers without
Kant's insistence that the rules bind absolutely, no exceptions, ever.

The theory's real power, and the reason it has never stopped shaping actual policy in the real world
— cost-benefit analysis, public health triage, the entire discipline of welfare economics all carry
its fingerprints — is that it takes suffering and wellbeing with total seriousness regardless of who
is experiencing them. A utilitarian calculation does not care whether the person whose happiness is at
stake is rich or poor, a citizen or a stranger, someone you love or someone you've never met. Bentham
himself extended the logic further than almost anyone in his century was willing to go, arguing openly
that the capacity to suffer, not the capacity to reason, was the relevant line for moral concern — a
position that put non-human animals inside the circle of ethical consideration two hundred years
before it became a mainstream academic argument, one this book will pick back up in Part Four.

And here is the problem, stated as plainly as the theory's own defenders eventually had to state it:
a calculation that only cares about the total sum can, in principle, justify almost anything done to
one person if the arithmetic for everyone else comes out high enough. Imagine a small, remote hospital
with one healthy visitor and five patients, each in need of a different organ to survive. A purely
utilitarian calculation — five lives saved against one life lost — appears, on the numbers alone, to
recommend the unthinkable: harvest the visitor. Almost nobody, utilitarians very much included, thinks
this is actually the right thing to do, and the case has become one of philosophy's most useful
stress tests precisely because it exposes the gap between a theory's clean arithmetic and the thing
Kant spent the previous chapter insisting could never be traded away — a person's standing as an end
in themselves, not a convenient supply of organs for someone else's better total.

Modern utilitarians have spent well over a century building responses to exactly this kind of case,
and the responses are genuinely serious, not dodges: rule utilitarianism argues that a society
permitting doctors to harvest unwilling patients would produce catastrophic fear and mistrust that
swamps any local benefit, so the *rule* against it maximizes utility even where the individual act
might not; others bite the bullet more carefully than the crude version of the thought experiment
allows, pointing out the case is stacked in ways real medicine never actually is. The argument has not
been resolved, and honest philosophers on both sides will tell you so directly.

What utilitarianism leaves behind, whichever way that specific argument eventually falls, is something
the ethics chapters ahead cannot do without: the insistence that consequences matter, that suffering
is real and comparable across different lives, and that an ethical theory which never has to check its
conclusions against actual outcomes in the actual world is, in its own way, just as much a form of
looking away as ignoring the arithmetic altogether. The next chapter takes both theories — Kant's
inviolable rules and Bentham and Mill's careful arithmetic — and drops them, on purpose, into the
single hardest, strangest thought experiment modern moral philosophy has ever built.
