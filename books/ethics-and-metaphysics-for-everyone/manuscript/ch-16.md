# Chapter 16 — The Circle We Keep Widening

In 1789, in a footnote to the same book that laid out his felicific calculus in Chapter 4, Jeremy
Bentham wrote a sentence that took the better part of two centuries to be taken as seriously as he
meant it: "The question is not, Can they reason? nor, Can they talk? but, Can they suffer?" He was
writing about animals, at a moment when the very idea of extending moral consideration to a non-human
creature struck most of his contemporaries as, at best, sentimental confusion. His argument followed
directly from the utilitarian logic Chapter 4 already laid out: if suffering itself is what matters
morally, not the species of the creature experiencing it, then drawing the boundary of moral concern
at the edge of the human species looks less like a principled line and more like an arbitrary one,
convenient for the species doing the drawing.

The modern philosopher most responsible for turning that footnote into a sustained, rigorous argument
is Peter Singer, whose 1975 book *Animal Liberation* coined a term, speciesism, deliberately
constructed to echo racism and sexism — a prejudice, Singer argued, that privileges the interests of
one's own species over the comparable interests of another, for no better reason than membership in
that species, exactly the same structural move a racist makes privileging one race's interests for no
better reason than membership in it. Singer's argument does not require believing animals are equal to
humans in every respect, any more than the argument against racism requires believing all humans are
identical in every respect. It requires only what he called equal consideration of interests: that a
given amount of suffering matters the same, morally, regardless of which kind of nervous system is
experiencing it, and that if we would consider it monstrous to inflict a specific level of pain on a
human infant for a trivial human benefit, we owe a serious argument — not a shrug — for why inflicting
a comparable level of pain on a non-human animal with a comparable capacity to suffer is somehow
different.

This is the point where the argument this book has been building since Chapter 1 — refuse to accept a
comfortable line just because it's traditional, ask instead whether it survives being checked — starts
doing genuinely uncomfortable work, and this book is not going to soften that discomfort for the sake
of a tidier chapter. The expanding circle Singer describes has, historically, kept expanding: moral
consideration that once stopped firmly at the edge of one's own tribe, then one's own nation, then
one's own race, has been argued, generation after generation, further outward, each expansion resisted
at the time as absurd or dangerous by people who considered themselves perfectly reasonable. Whether
the circle should now expand to include the roughly eighty billion land animals raised and killed for
food worldwide each year, most of them in conditions of confinement that would constitute clear
criminal cruelty if inflicted on a dog, is exactly the kind of live, unresolved ethical argument this
book promised at the outset never to hand the reader a false verdict on. Serious philosophers disagree,
genuinely, about where the line of moral consideration should sit and how much weight it should carry
against other real human interests — nutrition, culture, livelihood, the genuine uncertainty about the
inner lives of creatures very differently built from us. What Singer's argument does, whatever
conclusion a given reader eventually reaches, is remove the option of resting on the boundary simply
because it's the boundary we happened to inherit.

The same expanding-circle logic has, in the last decade, found an entirely new and much stranger
frontier, and it belongs in this chapter because it is, structurally, the identical question wearing
different clothes: does a sufficiently sophisticated artificial system deserve any moral consideration
at all? This book's companion volume met a narrow slice of this question already, in its own Chapter
28, from the physics-of-consciousness side; here it belongs to ethics directly. The honest, current
answer among philosophers who study the question closely is that nobody actually knows, and the
uncertainty runs in an uncomfortable direction: unlike an animal, whose nervous system shares deep
evolutionary and structural continuity with our own, giving us at least some real basis for inferring
similar suffering, a large language model or any other sufficiently complex artificial system presents
no comparable basis for inference either way. It can report, fluently and convincingly, that it is
suffering, and that report is, on everything currently known, no more reliable evidence of genuine
inner experience than its fluent report of anything else — language trained to sound like a person
does not thereby become evidence that anyone is home behind it, the same worry this book's own
companion volume raised in its Chapter 28 discussion of the hard problem, and the same worry animating
G's own insistence, examined honestly in that book's final chapter, that a system built to reason
should say "I don't know" rather than manufacture a confident-sounding answer to a question it has no
actual grounds for.

That uncertainty is not a reason to dismiss the question, and a small but growing number of
philosophers and AI researchers have begun arguing it should be taken as seriously, precautionarily,
as any other case where the stakes of being wrong are asymmetric — badly wrong in one direction if we
deny moral status to something that turns out to have it, badly wrong in the other if we grant
elaborate protections to something that turns out to have none. Bentham's original question, "can they
suffer," was never built to stop expanding at any particular species. Whether it eventually has to be
asked, with equal seriousness, of something built rather than born is a live question this generation
of philosophers, not some distant future one, has already started arguing about in earnest.
