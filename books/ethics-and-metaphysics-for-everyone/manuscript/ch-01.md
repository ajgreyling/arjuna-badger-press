# Chapter 1 — The Man Who Wouldn't Run

In the summer of 399 BCE, an old man in Athens was handed the easiest escape in the history of
philosophy, and turned it down. Socrates had just been convicted, by a jury of five hundred and one
of his fellow citizens, of corrupting the young and failing to honour the city's gods — charges that
were, by most honest modern readings, a thin legal costume draped over a much plainer civic
grievance: he had spent thirty years walking around the agora asking powerful, self-satisfied people
to define words they used constantly and had never actually examined, and enough of them had been
publicly embarrassed by the answers that they eventually found a jury willing to make the problem go
away. He was sentenced to die by drinking hemlock. His students had the guards bribed, the escape
route mapped, a friendly city in Thessaly already lined up to take him in. All he had to do was walk
out the door.

He wouldn't do it, and the reason he gave, recorded by his student Plato in the dialogue known as the
*Crito*, is the sentence this entire book exists to unpack: it is never right to do wrong, even in
response to wrong done to you, and a man who has spent his whole life arguing that the state's laws
deserve obedience cannot spend his last week breaking them just because obeying them is now
personally inconvenient. He did not want to die. He said so plainly. He simply could not find an
argument that let him escape without becoming, by his own standard, a worse man than the jury had
convicted him of being — and living as a worse man, he had already spent thirty years insisting to
anyone who'd listen, was a considerably worse fate than dying as a better one.

This book is going to spend its first seven chapters doing something Socrates would almost certainly
have approved of and found slightly insulting at the same time: taking the question he died for
— *how, exactly, should a person live?* — and handing it to roughly twenty-five centuries of his
argumentative descendants to see what they came up with. The honest answer, up front, is that they
have not agreed, and this book is not going to pretend otherwise by quietly picking a winner in
chapter one and grading everyone else against it. What they have done, generation after generation,
is exactly what Socrates was doing in the agora: refusing to accept an answer just because it was
comfortable, popular, or came from someone with the authority to enforce it, and building better and
sharper questions instead.

Socrates himself left almost no answers on paper — he wrote nothing at all, in fact, and everything
we have of him comes filtered through students, chiefly Plato, in dialogues where it is often hard to
tell where the historical man ends and the literary character begins. What survives clearly enough to
trust is his method, and it deserves to be named here in full because the rest of this book runs on
it: the *elenchus*, a relentless, patient, sometimes maddening process of asking someone to define a
term they use with total confidence — courage, justice, piety — and then, through nothing but further
plain questions, walking them to the place where their own definition contradicts itself. He did not
claim to have the right answer waiting in his back pocket. His famous, probably genuine claim was the
opposite: that he knew nothing worth knowing, and that this admission put him one honest step ahead
of everyone in Athens who was certain they did.

That is worth sitting with for a moment, because it is the plainest possible bridge from the book this
one follows. *Physics Won't Hurt You* spent four hundred years arguing that the discipline underneath
every real scientific advance was never genius — it was the plain, checkable willingness to ask a
question you hadn't already decided the answer to, and trust what you actually found over what you
were told to expect. Socrates was doing the identical thing, a full two thousand years earlier, aimed
not at falling apples but at the words people use to justify how they treat each other. He built no
ramps and dropped no balls off towers. He built questions, and used them the same way Galileo used
gravity: to find out whether a comfortable, widely accepted claim could actually survive being
checked.

It mostly couldn't, in his hands, which is precisely why the jury voted to end the conversation
permanently rather than keep losing it in public. And it is worth being honest, this early, about
what his death actually proves and what it doesn't. It does not prove Socrates was right about
everything — plenty of his specific positions, as reconstructed through Plato, run into real trouble
under modern scrutiny. What it demonstrates, plainly and at the highest possible cost, is that a
person can hold a moral position steadily enough to die for it without ever once claiming certainty
about the deeper metaphysics underneath it. He went to his death not sure what, if anything, waited
on the other side — the *Apology* has him weighing the possibility of an afterlife and the
possibility of dreamless nothing with the same equanimity, and preferring neither loudly over the
other. What he was sure of was narrower and, in its way, more demanding: that living wrongly was worse
than dying, whatever dying turned out to actually be.

That is the shape of the whole argument this book is about to walk through, chapter by chapter,
philosopher by philosopher, for the next several hundred years of recorded thought: people trying to
work out how to live well, arguing fiercely with each other about what "well" even means, while the
larger question of what's actually going on underneath it all — whether there's a soul, a self, a
world outside the mind describing it — stays open, sometimes for millennia at a stretch. Socrates
managed to answer the first question steadily enough to die by it while leaving the second one, by his
own admission, entirely unresolved. That turns out to be possible. It may, this book will eventually
suggest, be the only honest way to do either.
