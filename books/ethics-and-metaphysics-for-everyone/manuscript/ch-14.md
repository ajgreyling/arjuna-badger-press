# Chapter 14 — The Tree That Falls With No One Listening

If a tree falls in a forest and no one is around to hear it, does it make a sound? The question has
been flattened by overuse into a kind of stoner-dorm-room cliche, which is a shame, because the man
who actually forced philosophy to take it seriously, the Anglo-Irish bishop George Berkeley, meant it
as one of the most radical metaphysical claims of the entire eighteenth century, and defended it with
real philosophical rigor rather than mere provocation.

Berkeley's position, laid out in his 1710 *A Treatise Concerning the Principles of Human Knowledge*,
is usually summarized in three Latin words: *esse est percipi* — to be is to be perceived. He was not
merely making the modest, uncontroversial point that a falling tree makes vibrations in the air but
no *sound* — sound as a subjective auditory experience — without an ear present to convert those
vibrations into a heard sensation, a point any physicist would happily grant. Berkeley's claim was
enormously more radical: that the tree itself, the physical object, has no existence at all
independent of being perceived by some mind. Take away every observer — every ear, every eye, every
touching hand, and crucially, on Berkeley's own resolution of the puzzle, the mind of God as the
guarantor perceiving everything at all times, including the parts of the universe no human happens to
be looking at — and there is, on his account, simply nothing there. Not an unheard sound. Not an
unseen tree. Nothing, full stop, because for Berkeley matter itself, considered as something existing
independently of any mind perceiving it, was not merely unknown but strictly incoherent, a concept
that dissolves under examination the moment you try to say what "matter, entirely unperceived" could
even mean.

His argument for this startling conclusion runs, roughly, as follows. Everything you have ever known
about the physical world has come to you exclusively through your senses and your mind processing
them — you have never once had unmediated access to a "chair itself," only to the sight, feel, and
sound of a chair, all of which are, definitionally, perceptions occurring inside a mind. The idea of a
material object existing "behind" those perceptions, causing them but forever separate from any
possible experience of it, is, Berkeley argued, not a modest and sensible addition to what you
actually know — it's an unnecessary and ultimately unintelligible extra step, positing a category of
thing, "mind-independent matter," that nobody has ever encountered and nobody could, even in
principle, encounter, since the very act of encountering it would turn it into a perception. Simplify
the picture, Berkeley urged, and keep only what experience actually gives you: minds, and the ideas
— his word for perceptions of every kind — that occur within them. Take that step, and idealism, the
position that reality is fundamentally mental rather than material, stops looking like mysticism and
starts looking, on his telling, like simple economy of assumption.

Samuel Johnson's famous rebuttal — kicking a large stone and declaring "I refute it thus" — remains
one of the best-loved put-downs in the whole history of philosophy and, on sober reflection, does not
actually touch Berkeley's argument at all. Berkeley never denied that stones feel solid, resist being
kicked, and hurt a stubbed toe. He denied only that any of that requires the stone to exist as
mind-independent matter rather than as a very consistent, very reliable pattern of perceptions —
consistent and reliable, on his account, precisely because God perceives it continuously and
guarantees the pattern holds even when Johnson's own foot isn't currently in contact with it. Kicking
the stone harder proves the perceptions are vivid and consistent. It does nothing to prove they
require an unperceived material substrate underneath them.

Immanuel Kant, the same philosopher whose categorical imperative shaped Chapter 3, took a subtler
middle path between Berkeley's full idealism and the common-sense assumption that the physical world
exists exactly as it appears, independent of any mind. In his towering 1781 *Critique of Pure Reason*,
Kant drew a sharp line between what he called the phenomenal world — reality as it is actually
structured by, and therefore accessible to, human perception and reasoning, filtered through built-in
mental categories like space, time, and causality that the mind necessarily imposes on any experience
at all — and the noumenal world, the *thing-in-itself*, reality as it might exist entirely independent
of any mind's structuring of it. Kant's genuinely careful claim was not Berkeley's — he did not deny
that a mind-independent reality exists. He denied, more modestly and in some ways more disturbingly,
that human beings could ever know what it's actually like, because every single act of knowing
anything necessarily runs the raw material of experience through the mind's own built-in structuring
apparatus first. You cannot step outside your own perceiving to check whether the world matches it,
for the same reason you cannot take off a pair of glasses to check what your own eyesight looks like
without any glasses on, when the glasses themselves are the eyes.

Neither Berkeley's full idealism nor Kant's phenomena/noumena split has become anything close to a
settled consensus position among philosophers today, and both remain seriously argued minority
traditions rather than historical curiosities — contemporary versions of idealism have found real
defenders again in recent decades, partly through the panpsychism debate this book already met in
Chapter 12. What both men leave behind, independent of which specific solution eventually wins more
converts, is the same disciplined discomfort Plato's cave left in the chapter before this one: the
recognition that "the world as it obviously, straightforwardly is" turns out to rest on assumptions
that are considerably less obvious and considerably harder to check than they feel, every ordinary
morning, simply getting out of bed.
