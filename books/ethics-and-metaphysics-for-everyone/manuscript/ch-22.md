# Chapter 22 — How to Become Human

Confucius — Kongzi, "Master Kong," born around 551 BCE in the state of Lu during a period of chronic
warfare and collapsing political order that later Chinese historiography would name the Spring and
Autumn period — never wrote a systematic treatise. What survives under his name is the *Analects*, a
collection of short, often terse sayings and dialogues compiled by his students after his death,
closer in form to Chapter 1's Socrates than to Chapter 2's Aristotle, and for a similar reason: both
men taught in conversation, trusting that a live exchange revealed more than a finished doctrine
could. What Confucius was teaching, across those scattered fragments, turns out to sit remarkably
close to the territory Chapter 2 already opened with Aristotle's golden mean — and remarkably far from
it in one crucial, load-bearing respect worth examining closely.

The shared ground first. Confucius, like Aristotle roughly two centuries later on the far side of the
Eurasian landmass, was not primarily interested in handing down universal rules to be applied
mechanically to any situation. He was interested in *ren* — a term usually translated "benevolence,"
"humaneness," or simply "goodness," but closer in actual use to something like the full realization of
what a human being is capable of becoming, cultivated through practice rather than possessed from
birth. Ritual propriety, or *li* — the accumulated, inherited forms of proper conduct governing
everything from state ceremony to how a person addresses an elder — was not, for Confucius, empty
formality to be discarded in favour of authentic feeling. It was closer to what Chapter 2 called
virtue's slow cultivation through habituated practice: performing the forms sincerely and repeatedly,
Confucius taught, is how a person's raw, unruly nature is gradually reshaped into genuine goodness,
the outer form and the inner feeling eventually growing to fit each other the way a skilled musician's
fingers stop needing to consciously find the correct string.

Two of Confucius's most influential successors pulled that shared foundation in sharply different
directions, in an argument that echoes forward into every later debate about human nature this book
has touched. Mencius, writing in the fourth century BCE, argued that human beings are born with
genuine moral "sprouts" — an innate capacity for compassion, shame, courtesy, and a sense of right and
wrong, evidenced, in his famous example, by the immediate, unwilled alarm anyone feels on seeing a
child about to fall into a well, an impulse that arises before any calculation of self-interest or
reputation has time to occur. Moral cultivation, on Mencius's account, is essentially horticultural:
these sprouts exist in everyone and need only the right conditions — study, reflection, a decent
community — to grow into full virtue, the way a seed needs water and sunlight rather than a wholly
external design. Xunzi, writing a generation or two later, argued the opposite starting premise just
as carefully: human nature, left to itself, is genuinely bad — driven by appetite, envy, and
competition for scarce resources — and goodness is not something latent waiting to unfold, but
something actively imposed from outside, through ritual, teachers, and law, the way a warped piece of
wood must be forcibly straightened against a press rather than simply left to grow correctly on its
own.

That disagreement — is moral goodness discovered by clearing away obstruction, or built by active
external correction against a naturally resistant material — is, structurally, the same fault line
running underneath this book's own Chapter 3 and Chapter 4: Kant's confidence that a rational agent
already carries the categorical imperative's logic within them, waiting to be recognized, against
Bentham's colder, more engineering-minded confidence that good outcomes have to be actively calculated
and constructed rather than trusted to arise on their own. Confucian philosophy had the argument first,
in different vocabulary, centuries before either European name entered it.

What neither Mencius nor Xunzi ever questioned, and what marks the clearest genuine departure from the
whole Western line this book walked in its first seven chapters, is the assumption Chapter 20 already
introduced from a different tradition entirely: that personhood is inherently and inescapably
relational, not an individual achievement pursued in isolation and only secondarily brought into
contact with others. Confucian ethics is built almost entirely around the "five relationships" —
ruler and subject, parent and child, husband and wife, elder and younger sibling, friend and friend —
and *ren* is not achieved by a solitary individual reasoning alone toward a universal law the way
Kant's categorical imperative asks a person to do, silently, in their own head, checking a maxim
against a rule that would hold regardless of who else exists. It is achieved, and can only be
achieved, inside the actual, specific web of obligations a person is born into and grows through — a
vision of the good life as something inherently, structurally social all the way down, which this
book's next chapter is about to test against one of the oldest, hardest-won practical philosophies for
staying good when the actual society around you is falling apart.
