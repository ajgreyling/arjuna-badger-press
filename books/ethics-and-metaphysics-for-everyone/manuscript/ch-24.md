# Chapter 24 — One More Question, Differently Asked

In 1942, in a France under occupation, Albert Camus opened an essay called *The Myth of Sisyphus* with
a sentence that has lost none of its force in the eight decades since: "There is but one truly serious
philosophical problem, and that is suicide. Judging whether life is or is not worth living amounts to
answering the fundamental question of philosophy." He was not writing a suicide note disguised as
philosophy. He was naming, as plainly as this book has tried to name everything else in it, the actual
stakes underneath every chapter that came before this one. Camus's own answer runs through the figure
of Sisyphus, condemned by the Greek gods to push a boulder up a mountain for eternity, watching it roll
back down every single time, forever. The situation is, on the face of it, the purest possible image
of meaninglessness — endless effort, no final accomplishment, no escape. Camus's famous conclusion is
that we must imagine Sisyphus happy, not because the boulder secretly means something after all, but
because meaning was never going to be handed down from outside the situation in the first place. The
struggle itself, fully inhabited, owned, and pushed into without illusion, is enough to fill a human
heart — his phrase — even with no cosmic guarantee waiting at the summit.

That is one answer to the problem this book has spent twenty-three chapters quietly building toward
without ever naming outright: if the self turns out to be a looser, more provisional thing than it
feels like from the inside, if free will turns out to be harder to locate than the felt sensation of
choosing suggests, if the physical world itself might rest on foundations no experiment can currently
reach — what, then, is any of it for? Camus's answer is one honest response to that question, and this
book owes the reader the same discipline it has tried to keep throughout: it is not the only one, and
it is not obviously correct. Other serious thinkers have located meaning in relationships, in
Aristotle's *eudaimonia* built up through decades of practiced virtue, in Kant's picture of dignity
honoured rather than exploited, in Parfit's late, hard-won comfort at finding his own ego less
absolute than he'd assumed — a comfort, this book's own global detour showed, that a much older
tradition had already reached by a different road entirely. None of them agree with each other
completely. All of them share something this whole book has tried to model rather than merely
describe: they kept asking the question honestly rather than settling for the first comfortable answer
that came pre-packaged, and they built their answer out of what could actually survive scrutiny, not
out of what felt best to believe.

That is, this book would like to insist in its own final pages, the exact same discipline its
companion volume spent thirty chapters describing from physics's side of the desk. *Physics Won't
Hurt You* closed with a nineteen-year-old timing a chandelier against his own pulse, refusing to trust
his own eye's easy assumption without checking, and argued that the same small refusal — Newton's,
Faraday's, Rutherford's, Marie Curie's, Vera Rubin's — was the actual engine underneath four centuries
of getting the physical world right. This book has spent twenty-three chapters showing the identical
refusal running through an entirely different territory, on every continent it touched: Socrates
refusing to accept that a comfortable legal fiction excused him from asking what justice actually
required; Rawls refusing to accept that a rule is fair merely because the people writing it happen to
benefit from it; Singer refusing to accept that distance alone dissolves an obligation; Parfit refusing
to accept that a familiar word like "you" was as solid as it felt, once he'd actually pressed on it;
Nagarjuna refusing to accept that anything, examined closely enough, keeps the independent existence it
seemed to have from a distance; Epictetus, born into slavery with almost nothing else left to refuse,
refusing to hand over the one thing that was ever actually his to govern. Physics checks its claims
against what
nature does when you actually run the experiment. Ethics and metaphysics have no equivalent
laboratory, and this book has tried, at every turn, to be honest about that harder condition rather
than pretend a philosophical argument settles a question the way a measurement does. What they share is
the refusal to stop at the first plausible-sounding answer — the insistence on running the argument
again, from the other side, against your own best objections, before you're willing to call anything
settled.

Nothing in this book has handed its reader a verdict on how to live, what survives of the self across
a lifetime, or whether anything is real in the way it feels real every ordinary morning. That was
never the promise. The promise, the same one its companion volume made and tried to keep across four
hundred years of physics, was narrower and, in the end, more useful: an honest map of where serious
thinkers actually stand, which arguments have genuinely been settled by the sheer weight of scrutiny,
and which remain — after twenty-five centuries of some of the most careful minds in human history
working the problem — still wide open, not from any lack of trying, but because the questions
themselves are exactly as hard as Socrates thought they were on the last afternoon of his life. He died
without an answer to what waited on the other side of the hemlock, and lived, up to the very last
plain sentence he spoke to his students, by an answer he'd worked out to the only question he
considered settled enough to act on. That turned out to be enough. It may, honestly, be the only
version of enough that was ever actually on offer.

Go and ask the next question.
