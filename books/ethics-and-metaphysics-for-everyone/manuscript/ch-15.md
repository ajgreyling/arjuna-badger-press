# Chapter 15 — Are We Living in Someone Else's Computer?

In 2003, the Swedish philosopher Nick Bostrom published a short paper with a title so plain it almost
undersells the argument inside it: "Are You Living in a Computer Simulation?" It is worth stating
clearly, before anything else, that Bostrom's actual argument is not the thing popular culture usually
reduces it to. He was not claiming we probably are living in a simulation. He was making a narrower,
stranger, and in some ways more rigorous claim: that at least one of three specific propositions must
be true, though we currently have no reliable way of knowing which.

The argument runs like this. Assume, first, that it's technologically possible for an advanced
civilization to eventually run extremely detailed simulations of conscious beings — minds
indistinguishable, from the inside, from biological ones — on sufficiently powerful computers, the
same way this book's companion volume, in its own Chapter 26, described physicists taking that
possibility seriously as a live speculative frontier rather than science fiction. Assume, second, that
such a civilization, once capable of running these simulations, would have some reason to run a very
large number of them — for research, entertainment, ancestor-simulation, or simply because they could.
Given those two assumptions, Bostrom's trilemma follows with real logical force: either almost every
civilization at our level of technological development goes extinct before reaching the capability to
run such simulations, or almost every civilization capable of running them chooses, for some
consistent reason, not to, or — and this is the branch that captured the public imagination — the
number of simulated conscious minds vastly outnumbers the number of original, biological ones, in
which case any given mind, reasoning about its own likely situation with no other information to go
on, should assign a significant probability to being one of the simulated majority rather than one of
the rare biological originals.

Notice what the argument actually establishes and what it doesn't. It does not establish that we are
in a simulation. It establishes a disjunction — at least one of the three options must hold — and
leaves entirely open which one. A reader convinced that conscious digital minds are technologically
impossible, or that any sufficiently advanced civilization would find running them unethical and
therefore refrain, is not disagreeing with Bostrom's logic; they are simply picking a different branch
of his own trilemma, which the argument was built to accommodate from the start. This is, honestly,
one of the most careful and most frequently misrepresented arguments in recent philosophy, and this
book owes it the correction plainly: it is a conditional, not a prophecy.

It is also, on inspection, a close cousin of arguments this book has already met from two very
different directions. Descartes, in his 1641 *Meditations*, ran a version of the same fundamental
worry three and a half centuries earlier, without any computers to imagine: how do you know, he asked,
that an evil demon of surpassing power isn't feeding you a complete, seamless illusion of an external
world, indistinguishable from the real thing, at every single moment? Berkeley's idealism from the
previous chapter, though built for entirely different reasons, arrives at a structurally similar place
by another route — a reality that is, at bottom, patterns of experience sustained by a mind, rather
than free-standing matter underneath them. The simulation argument is the same worry again, wearing
its most modern possible clothing: not a demon, not God sustaining perception, but a server somewhere,
running a physics engine.

And here this book has to be as honest about the simulation argument as its companion volume was about
the identical question approached from physics: nothing about it is currently testable in any way
that would let an experiment settle it. Proposals exist — physicists have speculated, only half
seriously, about whether a discrete, pixel-like granularity to spacetime at extremely small scales
might show up as a detectable artifact, a kind of cosmic-ray "resolution limit" betraying an
underlying computational substrate — but no such signature has ever been found, and most physicists
who take the proposals seriously enough to publish on them are equally clear that a null result would
prove nothing, since a sufficiently sophisticated simulation could presumably be built without leaving
any such seam. The hypothesis, as it currently stands, does not make a prediction anyone knows how to
check, which places it, by this book's own repeated standard and by *Physics Won't Hurt You*'s Chapter
26 verdict on the identical question, squarely in the category of serious, taken-seriously-by-real-
thinkers speculation rather than live scientific hypothesis, let alone settled fact.

What the argument leaves behind, whatever the underlying truth turns out to be, is worth taking more
seriously than the hypothesis itself: a demonstration of exactly how little separates our own
supposedly solid, biological reality from Plato's shadows on the cave wall, once you actually follow
the logic rather than simply trusting how solid the floor feels underfoot. Descartes eventually
answered his own demon-worry by finding one thing that survived even total doubt — the fact of his own
doubting itself, *cogito ergo sum*, I think, therefore I am, at minimum, something that is currently
thinking. That may be the one piece of ground firm enough to stand on regardless of which branch of
Bostrom's trilemma turns out to be true, biological or simulated: whatever is reading this sentence
right now is, at the very least, a thing capable of wondering whether it's real. The next chapter takes
that thin but stubborn foothold and asks what obligations it creates toward every other thing that
might be standing on the same ground.
