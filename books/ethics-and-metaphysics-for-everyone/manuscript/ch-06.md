# Chapter 6 — The Veil of Ignorance

Imagine you have been handed total power to design a society from scratch — its laws, its economy,
who owns what, who gets educated and how, who gets cared for when they're sick or old or simply born
unlucky. There is exactly one catch. Before the design goes live, you will be dropped into that
society at a completely random position: any class, any race, any gender, any level of talent, health,
or luck. You do not know, while you're designing, whether you will land as the society's most
comfortable member or its most desperate. Design it now.

That is the thought experiment the American philosopher John Rawls built, and refined for over two
decades, into the single most influential work of political philosophy the twentieth century
produced: his 1971 *A Theory of Justice*. He called the imagined starting condition the "original
position," and the specific device that keeps you honest inside it the "veil of ignorance" — a veil
that hides from you every fact about yourself that might otherwise bias the rules in your own favour.
No knowing you'll be born wealthy, so no rigging the tax code to protect wealth you don't yet know
you'll have. No knowing you'll be born healthy, so no designing a healthcare system that quietly
assumes everyone starts from your own good luck.

Rawls's central claim is that a person reasoning honestly from behind that veil, genuinely unsure
where they'll land, would not gamble on a society organized purely around maximizing the average or
the total, the way the utilitarian arithmetic from two chapters back might recommend. A rational
person facing the possibility of landing at the very bottom would instead reason more cautiously,
something close to the way a careful person reasons about a decision they can't undo and can't afford
to lose badly on. Out of that reasoning, Rawls derived two principles he argued any such person would
choose. The first: each person is entitled to the most extensive set of basic liberties compatible
with a similar set of liberties for everyone else — speech, conscience, political participation, the
liberties a modern reader would recognize as basic civil rights, and Rawls placed this principle
first, meaning it could not be traded away for economic gains no matter how large. The second,
subtler principle governs inequality directly: social and economic inequalities are only justified if
they work out to the greatest benefit of the least advantaged members of society, and if the
positions attached to those inequalities are genuinely open to everyone under conditions of fair
equality of opportunity.

That second principle, which Rawls called the "difference principle," is doing something quietly
radical, and it's worth stating plainly rather than letting the careful academic language soften it:
Rawls is not arguing that all inequality is unjust. A world with some inequality can be more just than
a perfectly flat one, if — and only if — that specific inequality genuinely improves the position of
the people at the bottom, the way a surgeon's higher pay might be justified if it's what it actually
takes to get enough skilled surgeons operating on everyone, including the poorest patient in the
queue. What the difference principle rules out is inequality that simply accumulates at the top
because those already ahead were positioned to grab it, with no corresponding benefit trickling down
to the person who drew the worst hand in life's lottery. It is, in effect, an argument that a truly
fair society is not one designed by the winners looking backward and congratulating themselves on
having deserved their winnings, but one designed by someone who genuinely doesn't yet know if they'll
be a winner at all.

The theory has drawn serious, sustained criticism from more than one direction since 1971, and this
book owes the reader the honest shape of that argument rather than a tidy resolution. The libertarian
philosopher Robert Nozick, in his own 1974 reply *Anarchy, State, and Utopia*, argued that Rawls's
framework focuses entirely on the fairness of a distribution's end result and pays too little
attention to whether the process that produced it was fair — if you acquire your wealth through free,
voluntary exchanges nobody was coerced into, Nozick argued, the resulting pattern is just, however
unequal it looks, and redistributing it to satisfy someone else's preferred pattern is itself a kind
of injustice, a violation of the very liberty Rawls's own first principle claims to protect. Others
have pushed from the opposite direction, arguing the veil of ignorance smuggles in a very particular,
historically Western, risk-averse notion of rational choice, and that a person reasoning from a
different cultural starting point about family, community, or shared identity might not choose
Rawls's individualist framework at all.

What has proven durable, across every one of those criticisms, is the device itself, independent of
whichever specific principles you think it ultimately generates: the simple, powerful discipline of
asking, before judging any rule as fair, whether you would still call it fair if you didn't know in
advance which side of it you'd end up standing on. It is, in its own way, a close cousin of the
categorical imperative's universalizing test from two chapters back, run not through logical
consistency but through calculated uncertainty about your own luck — and it has become, in the decades
since, one of the most commonly borrowed tools in political philosophy, law, and public policy for
checking whether a proposed rule is actually just, or merely convenient for whoever happens to be
writing it.
