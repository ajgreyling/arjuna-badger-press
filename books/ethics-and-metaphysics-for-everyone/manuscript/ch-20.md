# Chapter 20 — Being Is Relation

Chapter 8 spent its whole length inside a very particular, very old assumption, one so deeply built
into the English language that it barely announces itself as an assumption at all: that a person is,
first and fundamentally, a separate thing — a self, sealed inside a body, whose relationships to other
people are additional facts layered on afterward, the way accessories get added to a chassis already
complete without them. Locke's memory chains, Parfit's teleporter, even the ship of Theseus itself, all
took that starting picture for granted and then asked hard questions about what holds the separate
thing together over time. A substantial family of African philosophical traditions asks a different
and, on reflection, genuinely destabilizing question: what if the separate thing was never there to
begin with?

The clearest, most widely cited compression of the alternative comes from the isiZulu and isiXhosa
concept of *ubuntu*, popularly rendered in the phrase *umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu* — a person is a
person through other persons. The Kenyan philosopher John Mbiti, in his influential 1969 study
*African Religions and Philosophy*, gave the idea its most quoted formulation for an international
audience: "I am, because we are; and since we are, therefore I am." That is not a warm sentiment
about the value of community layered on top of an already-complete individual self, the way a Western
reader's first instinct tends to hear it. It is a stronger, structural metaphysical claim: personhood
itself, on this account, is not a starting condition a human being simply has by virtue of being born
with a functioning brain. It is achieved, developed, and continuously sustained through relationships
— through duties recognized, kinship honoured, and a community's recognition actually extended — in a
way that makes "person" closer to an earned, relational status than a fixed biological fact.

That claim has been argued over with real rigor inside African philosophy itself, and this book owes
the reader that internal argument rather than a single flattened position labeled "the African view."
The Nigerian philosopher Ifeanyi Menkiti pressed the relational claim about as far as it can go,
arguing that personhood is something an individual acquires more fully over a lifetime, through
proper socialization and the fulfillment of community-defined roles — meaning, on his account, that a
newborn or a person who has failed those obligations genuinely possesses less full personhood than an
elder who has lived rightly within the community's expectations. The Ghanaian philosopher Kwame
Gyekye, in direct and sustained response, argued this goes too far: a human being, Gyekye maintained,
does possess certain basic rights and an irreducible core of individuality simply by virtue of being
human, prior to and independent of any community's recognition, even as that individuality is still
substantially shaped and completed by communal relationships. The Menkiti-Gyekye debate has run for
decades now, and it maps onto a real and unresolved tension this book has circled since Chapter 6:
how much of what a person is owed, and how much of what a person even is, can be settled by the
community around them, and how much has to stand independent of it.

What both sides of that debate share, and what marks the sharpest departure from Chapter 8's whole
framing, is the refusal to treat relation as an optional extra bolted onto an already-complete self.
The South African philosopher Mogobe Ramose has argued that *ubuntu* names not merely an ethical
ideal but the actual ontological starting point African philosophy in this tradition works from:
being itself is becoming, and becoming is inescapably relational, so that asking "what survives of a
person across time," the exact question Locke's memory chains and Parfit's teleporter were built to
answer, is already a slightly wrong question if it's asked about an isolated individual rather than
about a person-in-relation whose very continuity is sustained by the community remembering,
recognizing, and holding them across time, not solely by their own internal chain of memories.

This carries a real, practical ethical weight that this book's own Part Four already gestured toward
without naming its source: Archbishop Desmond Tutu, drawing explicitly on *ubuntu* as chair of South
Africa's Truth and Reconciliation Commission in the 1990s, argued that a wrongdoer's own humanity, and
the whole community's, is diminished by an act of harm — meaning genuine restoration has to repair the
relational fabric itself, not merely punish an isolated individual for an isolated act, a framework
that shaped restorative rather than purely punitive justice in a truth commission handling some of the
gravest wrongs of the twentieth century. It is, in its own way, a live, real-world demonstration of
the same idea Chapter 6's Rawls was reaching for from a completely different direction — that justice
has to be checked against something larger than any single actor's isolated interest — arrived at
here not through an imagined veil of ignorance, but through a conviction, argued and lived rather than
merely hypothesized, that no self was ever really isolated enough to need the veil in the first place.
