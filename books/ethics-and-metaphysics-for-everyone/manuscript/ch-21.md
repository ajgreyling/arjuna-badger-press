# Chapter 21 — Emptiness Is Not Nothing

Derek Parfit, whose teleporter thought experiment closed out Chapter 9 of this book, added a short,
striking passage near the end of *Reasons and Persons* that this book held back until now, because it
belongs here more than it belonged there. Having argued his way to the conclusion that personal
identity is not the deep, unified fact it feels like from the inside, Parfit wrote that he was not the
first to arrive at this destination — that a tradition already existed which had reasoned its way to
much the same place, roughly twenty-five centuries earlier, and had built an entire practical
discipline around living well once you actually believed it. "Buddha would have agreed," Parfit wrote,
"but he was writing over two thousand years ago, to a different audience."

That audience was hearing the doctrine of *anatta* — usually translated "not-self" or "no-self" — one
of the core teachings attributed to the historical Buddha, Siddhartha Gautama, in roughly the fifth
century BCE. The claim is not, as it is sometimes lazily summarized, that nothing exists at all. It is
narrower and considerably sharper: that what ordinary experience presents as a single, unified,
continuous self — an unchanging "I" sitting behind the stream of thoughts, sensations, and memories,
owning them the way a person owns a house — does not survive careful examination. Early Buddhist
analysis breaks the apparent self down into five aggregates: physical form, sensation, perception,
mental formations, and consciousness itself, none of which, examined individually, contains anything
that looks like a permanent, independent self. What a person actually is, on this account, is a
continuously changing process — closer to a flame passed from candle to candle, each moment's flame
causally dependent on the one before it without being strictly identical to it, than to a single fixed
thing persisting unchanged through time.

That is, point for point, the same reductionist destination Parfit reached through analytic philosophy
and a science-fiction teleporter rather than through meditation and centuries of philosophical debate
— and the convergence is worth taking seriously precisely because the two traditions arrived by such
completely different roads. Where they part company is in what follows next. Parfit, as Chapter 9
described, found the conclusion philosophically bracing and personally consoling, a way of caring less
about his own eventual death. Buddhist philosophy built an entire soteriology on the same insight,
because the doctrine of *anatta* connects directly to what the tradition treats as the central
practical problem of a human life: *dukkha*, usually translated "suffering" but closer in range to
"unsatisfactoriness" — the persistent friction of a life lived as though clinging to a fixed self and
fixed possessions could ever be secure, when both the self doing the clinging and the things being
clung to are, on this analysis, in constant flux. Release from that suffering, on the classical
account, comes not from acquiring a more stable self but from seeing clearly that the demand for one
was based on a misunderstanding in the first place.

The philosopher most responsible for pushing this insight into its sharpest metaphysical form was
Nagarjuna, writing in India around the second century CE, whose central concept of *sunyata* —
usually translated "emptiness" — extends the no-self doctrine from persons to everything whatsoever.
Things are "empty," in Nagarjuna's technical sense, not because they don't exist, but because they
lack *svabhava* — inherent, independent existence, a nature they would still have even considered in
total isolation from everything that gives rise to them. A chariot exists, usably and really, but only
as a pattern of dependently arising wheels, axle, and frame, none of which is "the chariot" on its
own, and none of which exists independent of the causes and conditions — the tree that grew the wood,
the craftsman who assembled it — that brought it about. Nagarjuna's famous, deliberately
paradox-flavoured formula holds that emptiness itself is not a separate, more real substance
underneath ordinary things, replacing them with a truer nothing. Emptiness and dependent origination
are, on his account, ultimately the same claim seen from two angles: to be empty of inherent
existence just is to exist relationally, dependently, in the same web of arising causes and
conditions Chapter 20's African relational traditions were describing from an entirely different
direction, arriving at a strikingly similar refusal of the sealed, self-sufficient individual thing.

This book is not going to flatten twenty-five centuries and dozens of distinct schools —
Theravada, Madhyamaka, Yogacara, Zen, and many more, each with genuine internal disagreements — into
a single tidy "Buddhist philosophy," any more than Chapter 3 flattened the whole of Western ethics into
a single voice. What survives, worth carrying forward into the rest of this book, is the demonstration
that the reductionist conclusion Chapter 9 reached about the self is not merely one clever modern
philosopher's isolated argument, vulnerable to being dismissed as an academic curiosity. It is a
conclusion serious, careful thinkers have reached more than once, by more than one method, in more
than one civilization, and built entire disciplined ways of living around — which counts, by this
book's own repeated standard for what makes an idea worth taking seriously, as exactly the kind of
independent confirmation a single argument, however elegant, can never quite provide on its own.
