# *ETHICS AND METAPHYSICS FOR EVERYONE* — Story Bible

> **A standalone popular-philosophy companion to *Physics Won't Hurt You*.** Same house voice,
> same discipline, a different pair of questions: not "what's true" but "what should I do about
> it" and "what, if anything, is actually there while I'm doing it." An **original narrative
> synthesis** of documented philosophical history — Bryson/Sapiens register, character-driven,
> funny, unafraid of technical material handled simply.

> **Tone (binding, the author's charge):** *wonder first, jokes earned, humility at the edges.*
> Ethics does not get a tidier treatment than metaphysics just because it feels more urgent, and
> metaphysics does not get waved off as academic just because nobody's life depends on the answer
> today. Every position gets a person and a scene before it gets an argument. Every live
> philosophical debate is labelled honestly: a rough working consensus among philosophers who
> study it (rare, and never confused with proof), a genuinely open and actively argued question,
> or a minority/speculative position taken seriously by some serious thinkers but not settled.

## 0. The shape of the book

**Part One — What Should I Do?** Socrates and the trial that founded the whole subject, Aristotle
and virtue, Kant and duty, Bentham/Mill and utility, the trolley problem and modern experimental
ethics, Rawls and justice, and a bridge chapter on the ethics of belief itself (Clifford vs James)
that hands the reader over into Part Two.

**Part Two — Am I Even Real?** Personal identity across time (the ship of Theseus, Locke's memory
theory), Parfit on what actually matters in survival, determinism and Laplace's demon,
Libet's experiments and the compatibilism/incompatibilism fight over free will, and the hard
problem of consciousness.

**Part Three — Is Anything Real?** Plato's cave, Berkeley's idealism, Kant's noumena/phenomena
split, and the simulation argument — treated as *Physics Won't Hurt You* Chapter 26's philosophical
twin, arrived at from a completely different direction and cross-referenced rather than repeated.

**Part Four — Where the Two Arguments Meet.** The moral status of animals and machines (the
expanding circle, and whether a sufficiently complex system could ever be owed anything), and
effective altruism and obligation at a distance.

**Part Five — The Same Questions, Asked Elsewhere.** A deliberate step outside the Athens-to-
Konigsberg line the rest of the book largely walks: Egyptian *ma'at* (Ch. 19), African relational
personhood / *ubuntu* and the Menkiti-Gyekye dispute (Ch. 20), Buddhist *anatta* and Nagarjuna's
emptiness read directly against Parfit's own acknowledged parallel (Ch. 21), Confucian *ren* read
against Aristotle's virtue (Ch. 22), and Stoicism's dichotomy of control read against the free-will
chapters (Ch. 23). Explicitly partial, not a full global survey — the honest map in Ch. 99 says so.

**Closing chapter (24).** Ties the whole book back to *Physics Won't Hurt You*'s own thesis — the
discipline of checking a claim against the world rather than trusting how it feels — applied this
time to values instead of facts, and now to a genuinely global cast rather than a single tradition's.

## 5. Provenance note

`MAXIMAL_ATLAS.md`, in this same canon/ folder, is an earlier, much larger (52-chapter,
100-130k-word) planning document for a maximal global version of this book, titled *How on Earth to
Be*. It is explicitly self-labelled "quarry, not canon" and does not override this document. Part
Five above was folded in from it (2026-08-30) as the highest-value, most novel material the quarry
offered, curated rather than adopted wholesale — the remaining ~47 chapters of that atlas (Mesopotamia,
Zoroastrianism, Hebrew prophetic ethics, Vedic/Jain thought, Islamic and Jewish philosophy, the
Hellenistic schools beyond Stoicism, Indigenous relational ontologies, and the full run through
20th-century and contemporary philosophy) remain unfolded, reserved as real material for a possible
future expanded edition rather than this companion volume.

## 1. What this book invents

Scene-setting, staged dialogue, and character colour, reconstructed from the documented record the
same way *Physics Won't Hurt You* handles its own cast — kept true to what's actually known, clearly
flagged wherever a famous anecdote (Socrates' last words, Diogenes and his lamp) is probably or
certainly apocryphal.

## 2. Register

One narrating voice throughout, continuous with *Physics Won't Hurt You*: warm, funny, digressive in
the Bryson mode, willing to admit what remains genuinely unresolved. A reader who never took a
philosophy class should finish able to name the actual disagreement in the trolley problem, not just
recite that it exists.

## 3. Guardrails

- No philosophical position presented as the field's settled answer when it isn't. Ethics
  especially: this book does not tell the reader what is right. It tells the reader, honestly, what
  the live disagreements actually are, and gives every serious side real argumentative weight.
- Cross-reference rather than duplicate *Physics Won't Hurt You*'s own material: the simulation
  argument (its Chapter 26), the hard problem of consciousness (its Chapter 28), determinism and
  time (its Chapter 24), quantum indeterminacy and free will (its Chapter 12).
- Thought experiments always get a plain-language handle and a person attached before the abstract
  version. No jargon survives past its first plain restatement.

## 4. Companion relationship to *Physics Won't Hurt You*

Same imprint (House of Greyling), same author, same "Per Ardua ad Magnum" mark, same dedication
register. Readable in either order. Neither book assumes the other has been read, but both reward it.
