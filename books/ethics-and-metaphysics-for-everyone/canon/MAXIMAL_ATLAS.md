# *HOW ON EARTH TO BE* — The Maximal Intellectual Atlas

> **Working subtitle:** *A Short History of Reality and What to Do About It*  
> **Status:** quarry, not canon. This deliberately contains too much. The finished book should be
> cut from this abundance, not attempt to carry every stone. Nothing in this document silently
> overrides `STORY_BIBLE.md`; title, thesis and structure become binding only when deliberately
> promoted into the story bible.  
> **Research warning:** this is an architecture and discovery map, not a publishable fact sheet.

## 1. The book in one breath

We are animals who woke up inside a world we did not make, equipped with minds we do not fully
understand, surrounded by other creatures whose inner lives we can never directly enter, and forced
to decide how to treat one another before the evidence about any of it is complete. We invented
gods, souls, atoms, duties, virtues, rights, nations, markets, laboratories and machines that speak.
Each invention—or discovery; the distinction is part of the argument—changed both what we thought
was real and whom we believed mattered. This is the story of those two questions growing up
together:

> **What is here? What, given that, should we do?**

The popular hook is philosophy without the lecture hall. The deeper hook is that metaphysics is
never merely decorative. A belief about what a person *is* becomes a law about who may be owned. A
belief about whether animals can suffer becomes a dinner. A belief about free will becomes a prison.
A belief about whether a machine can feel may become the first moral catastrophe involving a kind of
being humans manufactured before deciding whether it counted.

### The promise to the reader

By the final page, a reader who has never studied philosophy should be able to:

- see the hidden picture of reality underneath an ordinary moral claim;
- recognize the strongest versions of virtue ethics, duty, consequentialism, care, rights,
  contractualism, capabilities and moral pluralism;
- understand why consciousness, identity, causation, time and free will are practical moral
  problems rather than parlour games;
- distinguish a documented fact, a philosophical argument, a scientific inference and a beautiful
  speculation;
- disagree more precisely and live with uncertainty less cheaply;
- leave with no universal algorithm, but with a much better instrument panel.

### The true shape of the present draft

The existing seven chapters are the beginning of a lucid **26,000-word primer**. They are not yet the
large, species-scale book implied by *Sapiens* and *A Short History of Nearly Everything*. That larger
book is likely **100,000–130,000 words**, globally braided, archaeologically grounded, scientifically
serious and driven by a positive argument rather than a sequence of fair summaries. This atlas maps
that larger book. The seven chapters are reusable ore, not wasted labour.

## 2. The governing argument

### The braided thesis

1. **Every ethic contains a metaphysics.** Before we can say what we owe a being, we have already
   assumed something about what that being is: soul, citizen, rational agent, relation, organism,
   centre of experience, data pattern, property or kin.
2. **Every metaphysics eventually produces an ethic.** A universe of divine command, karmic
   consequence, purposeless matter, interdependent processes or simulated minds does not leave life
   looking quite the same.
3. **Morality is neither simply found nor simply invented.** Human moral worlds are built, but not
   from nothing. Pain, dependence, attachment, scarcity, embodiment, reciprocity, ecological limits
   and death push back. A bridge is constructed; gravity is not. Its being constructed does not make
   falling through it imaginary.
4. **A person is more plausibly a living process than a sealed substance.** We persist through
   change, memory, body, recognition and relationship. This weakens fantasies of absolute autonomy
   and strengthens the moral importance of care.
5. **Agency comes in degrees.** The choice is not between a magical, uncaused soul and a helpless
   meat machine. Responsiveness, reflection, inhibition, learning and answerability are real
   capacities even in a caused world.
6. **Conscious experience is the moral alarm bell.** We do not yet know why experience exists or
   exactly where it begins. That uncertainty is a reason for calibrated caution, not permission to
   ignore possible suffering.
7. **Power is philosophy with machinery attached.** Ideas become dangerous or liberating when
   encoded in courts, churches, borders, property systems, prisons, hospitals, markets, platforms
   and models.
8. **Uncertainty does not cancel responsibility.** We have always had to act before metaphysics was
   settled. The honest goal is not certainty but proportionate belief, reversible action where
   possible, attention to the vulnerable and responsibility for consequences.

### Recommended philosophical landing

The book should not end as a neutral catalogue. Its most defensible landing is **fallibilist,
process-relational naturalism joined to constrained moral pluralism**:

- there is a reality that does not depend on our opinion, though all access to it is mediated by
  bodies, language, culture and instruments;
- minds and selves are real patterns and processes even if they are not indivisible substances;
- suffering, agency, attachment, truthfulness, justice and ecological dependence create genuine
  moral constraints, but no single quantity captures all of them;
- moral judgment is disciplined triangulation, not vibes and not a universal calculator;
- traditions can disclose moral knowledge without being infallible or interchangeable;
- meaning can be made and still be real. A promise is made. So is a home. Neither is therefore fake.

### What the book must refuse

- the tidy rise-of-the-West story;
- the claim that science proved morality, free will, God, the self or consciousness away;
- the claim that every tradition was secretly saying the same thing;
- the opposite claim that traditions are sealed, incomparable worlds;
- the idea that evolution explains moral obligation merely by explaining moral behaviour;
- the fantasy that philosophical systems caused history by themselves, without institutions,
  material interests and force;
- the comfort of treating uncertainty as neutrality while someone else bears the cost.

## 3. The recurring machinery

Each chapter runs on the same six-beat engine:

1. **A person, object or event.** A marked stone, a grave, a courtroom, a meal, a slave ship, a
   microscope, a patient, a prison door, a chimpanzee refusing cucumber, a computer terminal.
2. **The ordinary question.** Is she still the same person? Why is this punishment deserved? Did
   the animal know? May the machine be switched off?
3. **The hidden metaphysics.** What is a self, cause, choice, mind, species, property, future or
   fact?
4. **The ethical receipt.** Who gains standing? Who loses it? Who pays if the picture is wrong?
5. **The strongest objection.** No straw opponents; no school leaves without its best critic.
6. **The residue.** Not a summary, but the piece of the problem the next worldview inherits.

### Five tests applied repeatedly

- **The stranger test:** what does the system owe someone outside family, tribe, faith or nation?
- **The powerless test:** what protects the person who cannot bargain, reciprocate or perform
  rational independence?
- **The animal test:** where does moral standing begin if speech and abstract reason are absent?
- **The power test:** what happens when a ruler, corporation or algorithm gets to apply the idea?
- **The Tuesday test:** what does this change about an ordinary choice when nobody is watching?

### Recurring visual and narrative devices

- **The census of souls:** at each historical turn, list who fully counts—citizen, woman, slave,
  stranger, child, disabled person, animal, ancestor, river, future person, machine.
- **The metaphysical receipt:** a small end-of-chapter reckoning of the real-world costs created by
  a picture of reality.
- **The honesty marker:** `attested record` / `scholarly reconstruction` / `live dispute` / `thought
  experiment` / `speculation`.
- **One troublesome object:** the same repaired axe or inherited cup passes through identity,
  property, memory, value and care chapters.
- **The empty chair:** every moral theory is asked who is missing from the room in which its
  universals were announced.
- **The hand on the lever:** return from spectacular dilemmas to the quieter institutional choices
  that place people on tracks in the first place.

## 4. The maximal chapter quarry

This is deliberately a **forty-chapter quarry**, not the final table of contents. A trade-length book
should probably fuse it into twenty-two to twenty-six chapters. Each entry names an opening scene,
the reality question, the moral consequence and the cast available to carry it.

### Prologue — Nine Red Lines

At Blombos Cave, a human hand drew a cross-hatched design in ochre tens of thousands of years before
writing. We do not know what it meant or whether it meant anything in the way that sentence assumes.
Begin in that uncertainty: at some point, reality acquired a second storey made of marks, memories,
rules and invisible beings. Human beings began living in worlds composed partly of matter and partly
of meaning.

The prologue then jumps to the present: a language model produces the sentence *I am afraid*. A mark
once raised the question of meaning; now a machine-made sentence raises the question of a mind. The
whole book lies between those two uncertain artefacts.

## Part I — The Animal That Built a Second World

### 1. Something It Is Like

Open not with a philosopher but with anaesthesia: a person is present, then apparently nowhere, then
present again. What disappeared? Introduce consciousness as the one fact each reader knows directly
and can never display directly to anyone else. Thomas Nagel, the hard problem, global-workspace and
higher-order approaches appear later; here the question is born.

### 2. The Corpse That Would Not Leave

Possible burials, funerary treatment, grief in humans and other animals, ancestors and afterlives.
Handle archaeological interpretation with brakes: bodies in pits do not by themselves prove a
doctrine of the soul. The ethical hinge is care for someone who can no longer reciprocate. Death may
be where metaphysics and morality first became impossible to separate.

### 3. The Fairness Animal

Food sharing, reciprocal altruism, kin selection, punishment, reputation, empathy, inequity
responses and cultural learning. Darwin, Kropotkin, Hamilton, Trivers, de Waal, Hrdy, Boehm,
Tomasello and Henrich. Refuse the easy leap from an evolutionary explanation of behaviour to a
justification of it. Nature made cooperation and cruelty available.

### 4. The Stranger at the Fire

Kin, guest, enemy, trade partner, marriage exchange, hospitality, scapegoating and the expanding or
contracting moral circle. The stranger is the book's recurring examiner because nearly every moral
system is generous at home. The serious test begins at the edge of belonging.

### 5. The Word That Made a World

Language, naming, taboo, promise, counterfactual, number, category and law. A rock can become a
border, a shell can become money, a sound can become a marriage and a sentence can become treason.
Durkheim, Mauss, Wittgenstein and social ontology wait in the wings. Constructed does not mean
imaginary; it means maintained by coordinated minds and often enforced on bodies.

### 6. The Person Before the Mirror

Development of self-recognition, autobiographical memory, theory of mind, shame and narrative
identity. The mirror test is useful and badly overpromoted; failure does not establish absence of a
self. The chapter asks whether personhood is a natural kind, a social achievement, a legal status or
all three at once.

## Part II — When the Universe Gave Instructions

### 7. The Heart and the Feather

Enter through Egyptian conceptions of *ma'at*: truth, balance, justice and cosmic order braided
together. Ptahhotep and funerary texts become openings, not proof of a single timeless Egyptian
philosophy. Ethics begins here as alignment with a world already morally shaped.

### 8. The Law in Stone

Hammurabi's stele and older Mesopotamian law collections: order advertised publicly, hierarchy
preserved explicitly, rulers claiming divine warrant. Law can constrain arbitrary power while also
freezing unequal worth into categories. Ask whether writing makes justice more accountable or merely
more durable.

### 9. The Lie That Damaged the Cosmos

Zoroastrian truth and falsehood, moral choice, judgment and cosmic struggle; influence on later
traditions must be described carefully rather than asserted in a straight line. The metaphysical
world is a battlefield in which ordinary truth-telling has cosmic weight.

### 10. The God Who Was Asked for Reasons

Hebrew prophetic justice, covenant, the suffering of Job, Ecclesiastes' refusal of tidy reward, the
Akedah and divine-command dilemmas. Amos asks what worship is worth without justice. Job asks whether
power is an answer. Euthyphro will later ask whether goodness is good because a god commands it or
commanded because it is good.

### 11. The Self That Might Be the Trap

Vedic sacrifice, Upanishadic inquiry, *atman* and *brahman*, karma and rebirth; Yajnavalkya and
Gargi; Jain *ahimsa* and many-sidedness; Buddhist dependent origination, suffering and *anatta*.
Do not flatten Indian traditions into one mysticism. Their disagreement about whether the deepest
self is universal or empty is one of the book's grand metaphysical collisions, with ethical
consequences for attachment, violence and liberation.

### 12. How to Become Human

Confucian ritual, cultivated virtue and relational personhood; Mencius on moral sprouts; Xunzi on
shaping unruly nature; Mozi's impartial care; Daoist suspicion of forced order; Legalist statecraft.
The chapter asks whether goodness is recovered, trained, universally extended or ruined by the very
institutions meant to impose it.

### 13. The Men Who Turned Wonder into an Argument

The Presocratics replace some genealogies of gods with proposed principles—water, change, being,
atoms—without inaugurating reason from nothing. Socrates relocates the question into the examined
life; Plato gives reality a perfect upper floor; Aristotle brings form into living things and virtue
into practice. The so-called Greek miracle is presented as contact, borrowing, argument and
institution, not immaculate European conception.

## Part III — Rival Ways of Being Human

### 14. The Good Life in a Bad Empire

Epicureans, Stoics, Cynics and Skeptics answer a practical question: what can remain free when the
world is not? Epicurus distinguishes simple pleasure from indulgence; Epictetus separates what is up
to us from what is not; Pyrrhonian suspension promises tranquillity; Diogenes supplies wonderful
stories, carefully labelled when apocryphal.

### 15. The Soul Acquires an Eternity

Plotinus, Paul, Augustine and early Christian struggles over body, will, sin, grace, love and the
person before God. Augustine makes inwardness into a continent and wrestles with evil as privation.
The ethical gift is radical equality of souls; the historical receipt includes institutions that
often failed or refused to embody it.

### 16. Reason Gets a House

Translation and original work across Abbasid intellectual worlds; al-Kindi, al-Farabi, Avicenna,
al-Ghazali, Averroes; Jewish thought through Maimonides; arguments about creation, necessity,
causation, intellect, revelation and the good life. Refuse the phrase “Islam preserved Greek
thought” unless immediately expanded: scholars translated, transformed, criticized and created.

### 17. Being Is Relation

African traditions enter as philosophy, not folklore and not a late corrective chapter. Ethiopian
reason through Zera Yacob and Walda Heywat; Akan personhood and the Menkiti–Gyekye dispute; Yoruba
knowledge and character; Ubuntu as a family of lived and argued traditions, not one slogan. Wiredu,
Oluwole, Ramose and Masolo help expose the Western habit of imagining a person first and relations
second.

### 18. The Land Is Not Scenery

Relational ontologies in diverse Indigenous traditions: land as ancestor, law, relation and
responsibility rather than inert property. Use named peoples, texts and living scholars—never a
composite mystical “Indigenous worldview.” Vine Deloria Jr., Linda Tuhiwai Smith, Robin Wall
Kimmerer, Kyle Powys Whyte and others can open questions of sovereignty, extraction and reciprocal
obligation.

### 19. Emptiness Is Not Nothing

Nagarjuna's dependent arising and emptiness, later Buddhist debates on mind, compassion and
non-attachment. The chapter dismantles the common error that emptiness means nihilism: things lack
independent essence precisely because they exist relationally. This becomes an unexpected bridge to
process thought and ecology without pretending they are identical.

## Part IV — The Great Unhousing

### 20. The Earth Moves and Heaven Does Not Fall

Copernicus, Galileo, Bacon, telescopes and the reordering of authority. The scientific revolution
does not simply replace faith with fact; it changes who may arbitrate disagreement and which
instruments count as witnesses. Ethics of inquiry begins to matter because methods distribute
credibility.

### 21. The Mind Locks Itself in a Room

Descartes strips doubt down to the thinking subject and splits mental from extended substance.
Princess Elisabeth of Bohemia immediately asks the devastating question: how does an immaterial mind
move a material body? The isolated modern self is born alongside a problem it cannot solve.

### 22. Three Universes and a Billiard Table

Spinoza's one substance, Leibniz's monads, Newtonian law, Berkeley's idealism and Hume's assault on
necessary causation. A billiard ball seems simple until asked what we actually perceive: one event,
then another, plus expectation. The moral hinge is Hume's is–ought gap and the role of sentiment.

### 23. The Lawgiver in the Head

Kant tries to rescue knowledge, freedom and morality by locating the conditions of experience and
the moral law in rational agency. The categorical imperative and dignity are given their full
strength; the murderer-at-the-door problem and Kant's racial hierarchy receive equal honesty.
Universality is powerful. So is asking who the universalizer imagined as fully human.

### 24. Freedom, Equality—and Whose Body?

Social contract traditions meet Wollstonecraft, de Gouges, Equiano, Douglass, Truth, the Haitian
Revolution and later Mills's racial contract. The chapter's subject is not hypocrisy as gossip but
the engineering problem of universal language built atop restricted personhood.

### 25. Happiness Gets a Calculator

Bentham, Mill, Harriet Taylor Mill and Sidgwick; utility, impartiality, higher pleasures, rules and
the demandingness problem. Bentham's question about animals—can they suffer?—becomes a fuse laid for
later. The utility monster, experience machine and organ-harvest cases show why arithmetic cannot be
the only instrument even when consequences cannot be ignored.

### 26. History Learns to Want Something

Hegelian development, recognition and master–slave relations; Marx on material life, alienation,
ideology and class. Ideas are placed back inside labour, ownership and production. The chapter asks
whether history has a direction or whether direction is what victors paint on contingency.

### 27. God Dies; Responsibility Does Not

Kierkegaard's leap and singular responsibility; Schopenhauer's will and compassion; Nietzsche's
genealogy, ressentiment and creation of values; Dostoevsky as a moral laboratory rather than a
supplier of slogans. The collapse of transcendent guarantee does not end morality. It makes every
borrowed value report for questioning.

### 28. The Moral Ape

Darwin dissolves the clean wall between human and animal without deriving ethics from natural
selection. Social Darwinism is exposed as ideology wearing borrowed biology; Kropotkin's mutual aid
complicates the cartoon of nature as only combat. Evolution explains why we have moral equipment,
not which use of it is right.

## Part V — The Century That Distrusted Foundations

### 29. Truth Is What Survives the Road

Peirce, James and Dewey: pragmatism as disciplined inquiry and consequences, not “whatever works for
me.” William James answers Clifford on when commitment cannot wait for proof. Truth remains answerable
to experience and community over time.

### 30. Experience Before Explanation

Husserl, phenomenology, Bergson and embodied experience; later Heidegger's being-in-the-world,
handled without laundering his politics. The world is not first encountered as neutral objects by a
floating spectator. We arrive involved, skilled, vulnerable and already among meanings.

### 31. The Self Has a Basement

Freud's divided mind, trauma, habit and the limits of introspection; move carefully between cultural
importance and empirical survival of specific Freudian claims. Fanon shows psyche and social world
inside one another: domination can inhabit the body image and the field of perception.

### 32. The Fly in the Language Bottle

Early and later Wittgenstein, logical positivism, ordinary-language philosophy and the suspicion
that some metaphysical problems are grammatical knots. Language can reveal confusion, but declaring
a problem meaningless does not make the pain or wonder that produced it disappear.

### 33. Condemned to Choose

Sartre, Beauvoir and Camus: freedom, bad faith, ambiguity, absurdity and solidarity without cosmic
permission. Beauvoir prevents existential freedom from becoming a solitary man's costume: our
freedom is entangled with the freedom and material situation of others.

### 34. Evil Gets an Office

Arendt on thoughtlessness, bureaucracy, totalitarianism and contested “banality”; Milgram with
methodological and interpretive care; the Stanford prison experiment chiefly as a warning about how
seductive bad evidence becomes myth. Systems can distribute harm until nobody experiences
themselves as the author.

### 35. The View from the Body They Named

Du Bois's double consciousness, Beauvoir on woman as Other, Fanon on racialized embodiment,
disability critique, Césaire, Said, Crenshaw and standpoint epistemology. The claim is not that truth
reduces to identity. It is that social position changes which machinery becomes visible and whose
testimony institutions are trained to discount.

### 36. Care Enters the Room

Gilligan, Noddings, Tronto, Kittay and Murdoch; dependency and attention as central rather than
embarrassing exceptions to autonomous adulthood. The infant, patient, disabled person and elder
reveal that the self-sufficient rational contractor was always a temporary achievement supported by
somebody else's work.

### 37. Justice Designs a Society

Rawls's original position and difference principle; Nozick's entitlement critique; Sen and
Nussbaum's capabilities; communitarian and feminist objections; Iris Marion Young on structure.
The veil of ignorance is kept as a brilliant instrument, not treated as a vending machine for one
inevitable political programme.

### 38. Are Values in the Furniture?

Metaethics without anaesthetic: Moore's open question, Ayer's emotivism, Mackie's error theory,
realism, constructivism, expressivism, reasons and contractualism. The ordinary question is whether
“cruelty is wrong” reports something, expresses something, builds something or commits us to
something—and whether the practical difference is as large as the labels suggest.

## Part VI — The Laboratory Turns Inward

### 39. Your Brain Before You

Laplace's demon, physical determinism, quantum indeterminacy, Libet and later timing studies, all
with the brakes on. Randomness is not freedom; neural preparation is not a courtroom acquittal for
every action. Compatibilism asks whether freedom means being uncaused or being able to act from one's
reasons without coercion.

### 40. The Self Comes Apart

Locke's prince and cobbler, amnesia, dementia, split-brain studies, teleporters, Parfit and reductionist
identity. If strict identity is not what matters, survival may be continuity of memory, character,
intention and relation. Ethics changes when responsibility and care must follow a fading or branching
person.

### 41. Mary Leaves the Black-and-White Room

Nagel's bat, Jackson's knowledge argument, Chalmers's zombies, Dennett's deflation, Churchland,
global workspace, higher-order theories, predictive processing and integrated-information proposals.
The reader gets an honest map: empirical theories of access and function are advancing; why any of
it feels like something remains genuinely disputed.

### 42. The Animal Looks Back

Bentham to Darwin to contemporary animal cognition: pain, play, planning, grief, selfhood and
sentience across species. Singer, Regan, Nussbaum, ecofeminist and relational approaches disagree
about why animals matter. Avoid turning one charismatic ape into a proxy for beings whose minds are
less familiar but whose pain may be no less real.

### 43. The Child Builds a Moral World

Piaget, Kohlberg, Turiel, attachment, prosocial development, infant studies and their replication
debates. Children are neither blank slates nor tiny saints. They arrive with biases and capacities
that culture trains, redirects and sometimes weaponizes.

### 44. The Trolley Enters the Scanner

Foot, Thomson, Greene, Haidt, experimental philosophy, WEIRD samples and cross-cultural variation.
Thought experiments are instruments, not verdicts. Self-driving cars do not literally solve trolley
problems; institutions make slower choices about acceptable risk long before any split-second swerve.

## Part VII — Power Outruns the Instruction Manual

### 45. The Planet Enters the Moral Circle

Leopold's land ethic, Carson, Naess, ecofeminism, environmental justice, Indigenous sovereignty,
climate science and the difficulty of valuing systems rather than only individuals. Is a river a
resource, a living system, a legal person, a relation or several of these under different useful
descriptions?

### 46. The Child in the Pond Is Far Away

Singer's demanding argument, global poverty, distance, identifiable victims, effective altruism,
institutional critique and the danger of turning justice into affluent personal optimization.
Numbers matter; so do power, dignity, local knowledge and who gets to define effectiveness.

### 47. The Body Is Not Raw Material

Medical ethics, consent, reproductive autonomy, disability, enhancement, transplantation, triage,
public health and the limits of the four-principles framework. The body is organism, self, property,
gift, dependency and political territory depending on who is speaking.

### 48. The Prison Door and the Caused Man

Retribution, deterrence, rehabilitation, incapacitation, restorative and transformative justice.
Sapolsky-style scepticism about free will meets the need to protect people and take victims
seriously. Responsibility may survive as answerability and social repair even if metaphysical desert
shrinks.

### 49. The Machine That Said “I”

Turing's imitation game, Wiener on control, Weizenbaum's ELIZA, Searle's Chinese room, contemporary
language models and the distinction between fluent self-report and evidence of experience. Avoid
both reflexive dismissal and marketing-driven enchantment. The first ethical question is not “is it
human?” but “what evidence would change our treatment?”

### 50. The Bias We Automated

Algorithms as frozen institutional choices; data as historical residue; classification, surveillance,
labour, opacity and scale. Gebru, Buolamwini, Bender, Crawford, Noble and Benjamin show that AI ethics
is not only about future conscious machines. Existing systems already redistribute opportunity and
harm among conscious people.

### 51. The People Who Do Not Exist Yet

Population ethics, Parfit's non-identity problem, the repugnant conclusion, climate obligations,
discounting and intergenerational justice. Future people cannot bargain with us, praise us or vote
us out. They are the stranger test with time added.

### 52. The Last Invention Is Still Made by Institutions

Alignment, control, concentration of power, labour displacement, autonomous weapons, epistemic
pollution and catastrophic-risk arguments. Bostrom, Russell, Ord, MacAskill, critics of longtermism
and scholars of present injustice belong in the same room. Do not let speculative extinction erase
documented harm; do not let documented harm make genuine tail risk unspeakable.

### Epilogue — The Cup of Water

Return from the cosmic scale to one person giving water to another without first settling whether
free will, God, moral realism or personal identity exists in the form philosophers hoped. The act
does not solve ethics. It demonstrates the condition under which ethics has always operated: partial
knowledge, real need, limited time.

The last claim should be earned, not announced as revelation:

> You do not need a final theory of everything before you can stop making the world worse. You need
> to look carefully at what is here, admit what you do not know, and become answerable for what your
> picture of reality permits you to do.

## 5. The cast constellation

This is not a “great minds” pantheon. People enter because they sharpened, embodied, transmitted,
criticized or exposed an idea; traditions, institutions, anonymous authors, translators, craftspeople,
patients and experimental subjects must remain visible. Dates below belong in the eventual research
ledger, not this quarry.

### Origins, anthropology and moral evolution

- **Charles Darwin** — continuity between human and animal; moral sentiments as evolved capacities.
- **Alfred Russel Wallace** — co-discoverer of natural selection; a useful complication because his
  later metaphysical views diverged from Darwin's.
- **Peter Kropotkin** — mutual aid as an evolutionary force; antidote to caricatures of nature as
  unbroken competition.
- **W. D. Hamilton** — inclusive fitness and kin selection.
- **Robert Trivers** — reciprocal altruism and parent–offspring conflict.
- **Frans de Waal** — empathy, reconciliation and inequity responses in primates; evidence, not proof
  that non-human animals possess human moral systems.
- **Sarah Blaffer Hrdy** — cooperative breeding, alloparenting and the evolution of human empathy.
- **Christopher Boehm** — egalitarian sanction and coalition against would-be dominators.
- **Michael Tomasello** — shared intentionality, joint attention and cooperation.
- **Richard Wrangham** — aggression, self-domestication and the troubling coexistence of reactive
  gentleness with planned violence.
- **Joseph Henrich** — cultural evolution, institutions and the warning that WEIRD psychology is not
  humanity's default.
- **Elinor Ostrom** — communities can govern commons without collapsing into either privatization or
  central command.
- **Émile Durkheim** — social facts, ritual and collective representations.
- **Marcel Mauss** — the gift as obligation, relation and power rather than free-floating generosity.
- **Mary Douglas** — purity, danger and classification as social order.
- **Pascal Boyer / Ara Norenzayan** — cognitive and cultural accounts of religious representation
  and large-scale cooperation; neither account settles whether religious claims are true.

### Egypt, Mesopotamia, Iran and Hebrew traditions

- **Ptahhotep** — wisdom, restraint, listening and ordered conduct within hierarchy.
- **The authors and transmitters of *ma'at*** — truth, justice, balance and cosmic-social order.
- **Ur-Nammu, Lipit-Ishtar and Hammurabi** — law codes as public order, royal legitimacy and ranked
  personhood.
- **Zarathustra** — truth versus the lie, moral choice and cosmic consequence; historical dating and
  downstream influence require careful handling.
- **Amos** — justice over ritual performance.
- **The authors of Job** — innocent suffering and the refusal of easy theodicy.
- **The voice of Ecclesiastes** — impermanence, limits and moral life without reliable earthly
  accounting.
- **Rabbinic traditions** — law as continuing argument rather than a dead list; disagreement as a
  form of fidelity.

### South Asian traditions

- **Gargi Vachaknavi** — metaphysical interrogation in the *Brihadaranyaka Upanishad*; a woman in the
  record asking what reality is woven upon.
- **Yajnavalkya** — self, ultimate reality and the neti-neti discipline of unsaying.
- **Mahavira** — nonviolence, many-sided truth and severe attention to living beings.
- **The Buddha** — dependent origination, suffering, impermanence, no permanent self and a practical
  path rather than speculative indulgence.
- **Ashoka** — the experiment of making moral concern and restraint part of imperial statecraft,
  with all the ambiguities that implies.
- **Nagarjuna** — emptiness as lack of independent essence; the middle way between eternal substance
  and nihilism.
- **Vasubandhu** — mind, perception and Buddhist analyses of experience.
- **Shankara** — nondual *Advaita* and the identity of self with ultimate reality.
- **Ramanuja** — qualified nondualism, relation, devotion and a less dissolving account of persons.
- **Kautilya** — statecraft, order, interest and the unsentimental machinery of rule.
- **Rabindranath Tagore** — humanism, education, relation and critique of aggressive nationalism.
- **B. R. Ambedkar** — caste as structural domination; dignity, constitutionalism and Buddhism as
  emancipatory reconstruction.

## 6. Concept atlas

### Metaphysics: what sort of thing is here?

- **Being and non-being** — whether “nothing” can be thought without becoming something thought.
- **Substance** — a thing that exists in itself; the durable bearer of properties.
- **Process** — becoming, activity and relation as more basic than static thinghood.
- **Event** — an occurrence rather than an object as the basic unit of reality.
- **Identity through change** — what permits something altered to remain the same thing.
- **Essence** — what a thing must be to be the kind of thing it is.
- **Existence** — that something is, distinguished from what it is.
- **Universals and particulars** — whether redness, justice or species exist beyond individual cases.
- **Nominalism** — general categories as names or practices rather than extra entities.
- **Realism** — mind-independent reality, with distinct versions for objects, science, mathematics
  and morality.
- **Idealism** — mind, experience or idea as metaphysically basic.
- **Materialism / physicalism** — everything is physical or depends wholly on the physical; define
  “physical” rather than using it as magic solvent.
- **Dualism** — mind and matter as fundamentally distinct.
- **Neutral monism** — mind and matter as aspects of something more basic.
- **Panpsychism** — experience-like properties as fundamental and widespread; the combination problem
  is the waiting bill.
- **Emergence** — novel patterns and powers arising from organization; weak versus strong versions.
- **Reduction** — explaining a phenomenon wholly in lower-level terms.
- **Supervenience** — no higher-level change without some lower-level change.
- **Grounding** — what holds in virtue of what.
- **Causation** — production, dependence, regularity, counterfactual difference or intervention.
- **Determinism** — a complete state plus laws fixes later states.
- **Indeterminism** — more than one future compatible with state and law; not automatically freedom.
- **Modality** — possibility, necessity and possible worlds.
- **Time** — presentism, eternalism, growing block, lived duration and thermodynamic arrow.
- **Space and relation** — container versus relations among things.
- **Personal identity** — body, brain, memory, soul, narrative, continuity or relation.
- **Self** — substance, bundle, model, process, social achievement or useful fiction.
- **Consciousness** — subjective experience, access, wakefulness and self-consciousness must not be
  collapsed into one word.
- **Intentionality** — the “aboutness” of thought.
- **Other minds** — inference from behaviour, structure, analogy and report.
- **Free will** — sourcehood, alternatives, reasons-responsiveness, absence of coercion or ultimate
  desert.
- **God / ultimate reality** — creator, ground, person, one substance, emptiness, order or category
  error; traditions must not be forced into one slot.
- **Purpose and teleology** — built-in ends, evolved functions, chosen projects and retrospective
  narrative.
- **Social ontology** — money, borders, offices, marriages, corporations, races and states as real
  because coordinated practice makes them causally powerful.
- **Simulation** — substrate uncertainty; ethical continuity may survive even if ontology changes.

- **Utilitarianism** — welfare impartially aggregated; act, rule, preference and negative variants.
- **Deontology** — duties, constraints and respect not reducible to totals.
- **Kantian ethics** — universalizable maxims, autonomy and persons as ends.
- **Natural law** — goods and norms read from human nature and practical reason.
- **Divine command** — obligation grounded in divine will, facing the Euthyphro question.
- **Contractarianism** — rules as mutually advantageous agreement.
- **Contractualism** — principles justifiable to each person.
- **Care ethics** — relation, dependency, attention and maintenance.
- **Ubuntu and relational ethics** — personhood and flourishing through right relation, internally

- **Confucian role ethics** — cultivated conduct through roles, ritual and humane relation.
- **Buddhist ethics** — suffering, intention, compassion, non-attachment and skilful means.

### The bridges where metaphysics becomes ethics

- **Consciousness:** if a being can suffer, its inner life creates a claim.
- **Identity:** who receives a promise, punishment, inheritance or apology across time?
- **Agency:** what kind of control makes praise, blame and consent meaningful?
- **Causation:** who or what produced a harm in a distributed system?
- **Relation:** are obligations added between completed individuals or partly constitutive of them?

- **Species:** is biological membership morally decisive or a proxy for other properties?
- **Scale:** do moral intuitions built for villages survive states, markets, climate and AI?
- **Power:** who gets to declare the ontology that law will enforce?

## 10. Argument constellations

### Is morality objective?

**For:** convergence on some harms; reasons that appear independent of desire; the possibility of
moral error; criticism across societies; suffering's stubborn badness from the inside.  
**Against:** deep disagreement; evolutionary shaping; queerness or metaphysical strangeness of moral
facts; explanatory redundancy; dependence on forms of life.  
**Bridge:** objectivity need not mean values floating like particles. Constructed practices can be
answerable to realities they did not construct.

### Is there a self?

**For substance:** continuity, ownership of experience, responsibility, first-person unity.  
**For process/bundle/model:** constant change, distributed neural function, memory failure,
dependent origination, split and branching cases.  
**Bridge:** “not an indivisible pearl” does not entail “not real.” A hurricane is a pattern and will
still remove your roof.

### Do we have free will?

**Libertarian:** genuine alternatives and sourcehood require more than causal determination.  
**Hard incompatibilist:** neither determinism nor randomness gives ultimate desert.  
**Compatibilist:** freedom is reasons-responsive action, reflection and absence of coercion inside
the causal order.  
**Revisionist landing:** shrink vengeance, keep protection, cultivation, answerability and repair.

### What makes a being matter?

- soul;
- divine creation;
- species membership;
- rationality;
- autonomy;
- language;
- sentience;
- capacity to flourish;
- relationships;
- being a subject-of-a-life;
- vulnerability;
- membership in a living community;
- potentiality;
- narrative recognition.

No candidate is cost-free. Rationality excludes infants and some disabled humans. Sentience may
include far more animals and future machines than custom permits. Relation can abandon the isolated.
Species alone risks arbitrary membership privilege. The book should make the reader feel each bill.

### Can one moral theory win?

Virtue sees character but can inherit a corrupt community. Duty protects dignity but can become
rigid. Consequence forces attention to suffering but can consume individuals in totals. Care sees
dependency but risks partiality and gendered burden. Rights establish floors but can hide material
incapacity. Capabilities see lived possibility but require a contested list. Ubuntu sees relation but
can be abused to silence dissent in the name of community.

The recommended instrument panel asks:

1. What happens to welfare and suffering?
2. Whose agency, consent or dignity is used?
3. What relationships and dependencies are sustained or betrayed?
4. Is the distribution just, especially for the least powerful?
5. What kind of character and institution does repetition create?
6. What uncertainty remains, and is the harm reversible?

This is not a formula. It is disciplined refusal to look through only one window.

## 13. Counterarguments the book must invite inside

- The braided thesis may overstate how much explicit metaphysics ordinary moral life needs; people
  often act from habit, emotion and institution without a theory of persons.
- Process-relational language can become fashionable fog unless tied to cases with real predictive or
  ethical difference.
- Moral pluralism can become sophisticated indecision. The book must show how judgment still closes
  around an action.
- Sentience-first ethics can neglect dignity, relation, ecology and beings whose sentience is
  uncertain.
- “Made but real” may fail to distinguish oppressive conventions from valid norms; the needed test is
  answerability to suffering, agency, reciprocity, evidence and justifiability to those burdened.
- A species-scale story can reproduce the simplifications it criticizes. Local scholars and primary
  traditions must be able to say “that is not what this meant here.”
- The global survey risks false equivalence between argument traditions with very different genres
  and goals.
- A focus on named thinkers risks mistaking literate archives for all thought.
- A focus on ideas risks underplaying force, material interest, ecology and accident.
- A confident ending may smuggle in the author's preferred morality after promising honest
  uncertainty. The landing must show its reasons and remaining costs.

## 14. Research and truth architecture

Every consequential claim enters a ledger with:

- exact claim;
- claim type: fact / interpretation / argument / reconstruction / anecdote / speculation;
- primary source where possible;
- best modern scholarly source;
- significant dissent;
- confidence level;
- wording ceiling—what the evidence permits the prose to say;
- ethical or sensitivity note;
- chapter and paragraph destination.

### Evidence labels in prose

- **Documented:** text, artefact, dated record or reproducible result supports the claim.
- **Best reconstruction:** evidence is incomplete but specialists have a defensible leading account.
- **Disputed:** serious alternatives remain live and materially change the story.
- **Thought experiment:** constructed to test intuitions, not evidence that the world behaves so.
- **Speculative:** coherent possibility without sufficient confirming evidence.

## 16. Opening seed

About seventy-three thousand years ago, in a cave above the Indian Ocean, someone took a piece of
ochre and drew nine red lines on a flake of stone.

The lines are not spectacular. No antelope gallops across them. No stick hunter raises a spear. They
form a small cross-hatched pattern which, to the untrained eye, resembles the work of a person trying
to discover whether a pen still works. There were no pens. There was also nobody writing down why
the lines were made, which has left modern scholars in the familiar position of knowing a great deal
about the mineral and almost nothing about the Tuesday.

Perhaps the mark was decoration. Perhaps it identified a person or a group. Perhaps it was practice,
counting, ritual or an idle movement of the hand. It may have meant something. It may be we who cannot
look at deliberate lines without manufacturing a meaning for them.

That uncertainty is the proper place to begin.

Long before a human being wrote the word *real*, human beings were already living in at least two
worlds. One contained stone, tide, blood, hunger, leopards and the inconvenient tendency of gravity
to continue working during moments of spiritual importance. The other contained promises, ancestors,
boundaries, debts, names, duties and things a mark could mean when no mark had meant them before.
The first world could break your bones. The second could decide whether anyone was obliged to carry
you home.

We have been trying to work out the relationship between those worlds ever since.

## 17. Ending seed

The book ends at human scale. No final revelation, no philosopher crowned, no universe caught
delivering its instructions.

A person is thirsty. Another person has water.

Between them stand four thousand years of argument about souls, selves, gods, consequences, duties,
rights, relations, freedom, causation and whether either person is a temporary arrangement of matter
mistaking itself for a noun. Nearly all of those questions remain open.

The cup still changes hands.

That is not proof of a moral universe. It is evidence of a moral animal: fallible, dangerous,
dependent, capable of looking at another centre of need and allowing that reality to interrupt its
own.

How on earth should we be here?

Carefully. Curiously. Answerably. Together, when we can manage it.

And when we cannot manage all of that, begin with the water.
