# Foreword

### *by Russell Cevic* †

Let me tell you the secret of the adventure novel, since I spent a long and disgracefully enjoyable life keeping it. The secret is that it must never, for one single page, be embarrassed about what it is. The moment a thriller starts apologising — starts reaching for respectability, starts wanting to be Literature when it grows up — it dies, and it dies bored, which is the only unforgivable way for a thriller to go.

This book is not embarrassed. It knows exactly what it is, and what it is is a *chase* — a clean, propulsive, set-piece-driven hunt across real and magnificent ground for a thing worth dying over — and it pursues that thing with a grin and a full tank and no brakes, and I loved it without reservation, which at my age and mileage is not a thing I say often.

Here is what the author gets right that nine out of ten can't. He understands that *velocity is a moral position.* He never wastes your time. He never stops the truck to admire his own research, even though — and this is the part that delighted me — the research is plainly enormous and completely real. The ancient sites are real. The engineering is real. The gold is real, and it is not what you think it is, and I will say no more. He has done the homework that the lazy skip, and then he has done the thing the homework-doers usually can't, which is to wear it lightly, to bury it inside the chase so that you are *learning the truth at sixty miles an hour* and mistaking it for fun.

And it *is* fun. People forget that this is allowed. They forget that a book can be true and rigorous and still, primarily, a tremendous good time — that you can put real archaeology and real danger and a genuinely clever puzzle into a story whose first duty, whose *holy* duty, is to make a tired person on an aeroplane miss their meal because they could not put it down. This book honours that duty. It is generous. It wants you to have a good time, and it has gone to enormous trouble to make sure you do.

The hero is the right kind of hero for this — not a superman, but a person who can *read the made thing*, who walks into a five-thousand-year-old machine and treats its builders as colleagues rather than mysteries, and is therefore the only one in the room who knows which wire to cut. I have built a lot of heroes. This is a good one. I'd have put them on my own crew without an interview.

A word on the set-pieces, since they are my own first love. They are excellent. They are *engineered* — each one a clean little machine with a problem, a cost, and a key, handed off to the next like a baton. That is how it is done. That is the whole craft, right there, and the author has it cold, and I tipped my hat more than once.

So here is my advice, and it is the only advice this kind of book ever needs. Don't read it sitting up straight. Don't read it with a pencil. Read it the way it was built to be read — fast, greedy, grinning, and slightly too late into the night. The gold is waiting. The chase is on. And the man who wrote it has done the one thing I always cared about above every other: he kept his foot down.

Go.

*— Russell Cevic*
