# Style Guide — *The Patient Sky*

> **Binding contract.** See [`STORY_BIBLE.md`](STORY_BIBLE.md) for the documented frame and the
> six-part shape this book must never contradict.

## The register

One narrator, plain and funny, talking to a smart reader who has never read a cuneiform tablet or
cast a chart. Bryson/Sapiens mode: enter every chapter through a person, an object, or a scene, not
a definition. Let the humor come from real detail (Ptolemy's quietly fudged data, Kepler's furious
letters about his mother's witchcraft trial, a Babylonian scribe's very human complaint about a
cloudy week) rather than from mocking the practice of astrology itself.

- **Concrete picture before abstraction.** Every idea — an omen tablet, the ecliptic, axial
  precession, a double-blind study protocol — gets a plain, physical image the reader can hold
  before any technical term does the same work.
- **The digression is a feature.** A short, clearly-marked tangent (why the week has seven days,
  what "quintessence" meant to a medieval physician) is in the Bryson spirit — keep it tight, never
  let it eat the chapter's spine.
- **People first, ideas second — but always both.** No scribe, astrologer, or astronomer appears
  only as a name attached to a fact; no chapter is only a character sketch with no history in it.
- **Part Five is the one place the book stops being sympathetic-first.** Every earlier part can and
  should explain why astrology felt convincing and useful. Part Five reports what was actually
  tested and what was actually found, plainly, once, without hedging it back toward balance.
- **No living person, named or identifiable, anywhere in the book.** Modern astrology is discussed
  as an industry and a psychological phenomenon (Chapter 23), never through a named practitioner,
  columnist, or company.
- **No borrowed sentences.** Nothing here is copied from Bryson, Harari, or any existing popular
  history of astrology or astronomy.

## Machine-tell taboos

No "in conclusion." No essay-style summary sentences closing a scene. No stacking three hedges in a
row. Let a short, plain sentence land a big idea and then get out of the way.
