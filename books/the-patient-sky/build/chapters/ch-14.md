# Chapter 14 — A Subject Taught Next to Medicine

By the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, a student training to become a physician at a leading
European university — Bologna and Padua in Italy stand out in the surviving record as particularly
well documented on this point, though the pattern was far from confined to those two cities — was
expected, as a formal requirement of the medical curriculum itself, to study astrology, at a level
of technical rigor comparable to the anatomy and pharmacology he studied alongside it. This was not
an elective curiosity tucked into the margins of a medical degree. At the University of Bologna,
astrology held the status of a formally endowed academic chair for a period spanning several
centuries, with named, salaried professors lecturing on the subject as an integral part of the
university's arts and medical faculties, producing generations of physicians for whom the melothesia
Chapter 12 described and the broader technical apparatus this book has traced since Babylon were not
optional supplementary knowledge, but core professional training, examined and credentialed the same
way any other required subject was.

The institutional logic behind this arrangement followed directly, and reasonably, from everything
this book's Part Three has already established: if celestial influences genuinely shaped bodily
health, the timing of disease, and the safety of medical intervention — a premise that, as Chapters
11 and 12 described, rested on real, if ultimately mistaken, continuity with Hippocratic and Galenic
medical theory — then a physician who had not studied astrology was, by the standards of his own
era's medical science, working with an incomplete diagnostic toolkit, no more defensible than a
modern physician refusing to learn pharmacology. Medieval and early Renaissance university
statutes and curricula treat the subject with exactly this kind of unremarkable, workaday
seriousness: astrology textbooks assigned alongside anatomical and pharmacological ones, examinations
covering planetary theory alongside humoral theory, degrees granted to students who had
demonstrated competence in both.

Outside the university, astrology's institutional presence in medieval and early modern European
life extended well beyond medicine, into a role this book has already documented in ancient Rome
and Assyria and now sees recur, largely unchanged in its basic shape, across an entirely different
civilization roughly a thousand years later: the court astrologer, retained by rulers and wealthy
patrons for advice on matters of state, war, and personal fortune. Guido Bonatti, an Italian
astrologer active through much of the thirteenth century, offers a particularly well-documented
example — he served, across his career, as a formal advisor to several Italian noble houses and
city-states, reportedly consulted before military campaigns and major political decisions, and his
own technical treatise, the *Liber Astronomiae*, compiled late in his life, became one of the most
widely copied and cited European astrological textbooks of the following two centuries, drawing
extensively on the Ptolemaic and Islamic Golden Age material Chapters 8 and 13 already traced. The
pattern this book documented in Esarhaddon's Assyria in Chapter 4 — a ruler retaining a trained,
professionally credentialed sky-reader as a standing advisor on questions of real political
consequence — had, by Bonatti's century, been running continuously, across an unbroken if
geographically wandering institutional lineage, for close to two thousand years.

The free-will objection this chapter is about to describe received its most influential and most
carefully qualified treatment from Thomas Aquinas, the thirteenth-century theologian whose synthesis
of Aristotelian philosophy and Christian doctrine shaped Catholic thought for centuries afterward.
Aquinas's own position, laid out across several of his major theological works, drew a careful
distinction between the stars' plausible influence over the body and physical passions — a claim he
was willing to grant real weight, consistent with the Hippocratic and Galenic medical tradition
Chapters 11 and 12 already described — and any claim that the stars could directly determine the
rational will or a specific individual moral choice, which he rejected outright as incompatible
with human free will and therefore with Christian moral responsibility. This Thomistic compromise,
stars inclining the body without compelling the soul, became something close to the standard
official theological position across late medieval and early modern Catholic Europe, giving
astrology's university-taught, medically grounded core a stable doctrinal home it would otherwise
have struggled to secure against the Church's own deepest theological commitments.

It would be a mistake, though, to read this long institutional persistence as evidence that nobody
in medieval or Renaissance Europe ever seriously doubted astrology's claims. Real, sustained
theological and philosophical criticism of astrology existed throughout this entire period, and it
came from serious, well-credentialed thinkers rather than fringe skeptics. The most influential
version of this critique centered on a specific theological objection: if the stars genuinely
determined human character and fate with the completeness astrology's own more deterministic
practitioners sometimes claimed, then human free will — a doctrine central to Christian theology,
and to the moral accountability the Church's entire penitential system depended on — would be an
illusion, and a person could not fairly be held responsible for sins the stars had, in effect,
compelled. This objection, raised in various forms by theologians across the medieval period,
produced a durable, widely accepted compromise position, one the more careful astrological
practitioners of the era, including Bonatti and later figures this book will meet, generally
accepted and worked within: the stars inclined, but did not compel. A birth chart might indicate a
strong natural tendency toward a particular temperament or a particular kind of trouble, but a
person of sufficient will and virtue, guided by reason and by Christian moral discipline, retained
the genuine capacity to resist that inclination. Astrology, on this compromise, was not
fatalism. It was closer to what a modern reader might recognize as risk assessment — useful,
real, worth consulting, but never fully deterministic of the outcome.

This theologically negotiated, institutionally secure position — astrology as required medical
curriculum, retained court practice, and philosophically qualified but broadly accepted natural
science — held remarkably stable across the medieval period and into the opening of the European
Renaissance, resilient enough to absorb real doctrinal criticism without losing its university
chairs or its royal patrons. The specific channel through which this Islamic Golden Age material reached Latin Europe deserves
its own brief mention, because it was neither gradual nor accidental. Twelfth-century Toledo, newly
under Christian rule after centuries as a center of Muslim Spanish learning, became host to a
sustained, organized translation effort, with scholars including Gerard of Cremona working
systematically through the Arabic scientific and astrological corpus, rendering Ptolemy, al-Battani,
and a wide range of other authors into Latin for the first time. Gerard alone is credited with
translating more than seventy works across his career, an output that single-handedly reshaped the
available scientific curriculum at the emerging European universities Chapter 14 has already
described, arriving in Bologna and its peer institutions within a generation or two of its
completion — the specific, traceable mechanism by which the House of Wisdom's four centuries of
labor finally became required reading for a European medical student.

What finally began pulling it apart was not, in the end, a theological
argument at all. It was a specific, individual astronomer, employed for most of his working life in
the very court-astrologer role this chapter has just described, who discovered — while, by his own
account, still fully believing some real version of astrology's underlying premise — a mathematical
fact about planetary motion so much more precise, and so much more strange, than anything the
Ptolemaic tradition had prepared anyone to expect, that it would end up, a few generations later,
quietly dissolving the very institutional marriage between astronomy and astrology that had kept
Guido Bonatti and every scholar before him gainfully, respectably employed.

Dante Alighieri, writing his *Divine Comedy* in the early fourteenth century, only a couple of
generations after Bonatti's own lifetime, placed Bonatti himself among the diviners and false
prophets condemned to Hell's eighth circle, his head twisted backward on his body as punishment for
having tried, in life, to see too far forward — a striking literary judgment from one of medieval
Europe's greatest poets, and a useful reminder that even at astrology's most institutionally secure
moment, the discipline's confident predictive claims could still draw real, pointed condemnation
from serious contemporary thinkers working from the same broadly shared Christian theological
framework Aquinas had used to grant astrology its qualified doctrinal home in the first place.

Dante's own verdict did nothing to dislodge astrology from the university curriculum in his own
century or the two that followed it — poetry and institutional practice, this book's story has
already shown more than once, do not always move at the same speed. But it stands, alongside the
Enlightenment critiques Chapter 18 covers directly, as evidence that the qualified doctrinal peace
this chapter has described was never a settled, uncontested consensus. It was a working compromise,
argued over by serious minds in every generation that inherited it, right up until the century that
finally let the argument lapse rather than resolving it.

That compromise's real durability is worth one final measure before this chapter closes: it held,
in broadly recognizable form, across close to five centuries of European intellectual life, from its
clearest thirteenth-century Thomistic statement through the very astronomer this book's next chapter
turns to, still working, comfortably, within its terms.
