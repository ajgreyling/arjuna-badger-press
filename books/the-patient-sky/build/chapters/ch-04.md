# Chapter 4 — The King's Weather Report

In the ruins of Nineveh, in the nineteenth century, archaeologists digging through the collapsed
library of the Assyrian king Ashurbanipal found something closer to an office's filed correspondence
than to a temple's sacred archive: hundreds of individual letters and reports, written on clay by
named scholars, addressed to named kings, discussing the sky in the same brisk, practical register a
modern advisor might use discussing the markets. *"On the sixth day the moon and the sun were seen
together. This is bad. The king should perform the appropriate ritual."* *"Venus has become visible
in the east. This means the harvest will be good, but the king's enemies should still be watched."*
*"A cloudy sky prevented observation last night. We will report again when the sky clears."*

That last kind of entry — the honest admission that nothing could be observed, filed and sent
anyway, rather than invented or quietly skipped — turns up often enough in this correspondence to be
worth pausing on, because it is a small, unglamorous piece of evidence for something this book has
been arguing since Chapter 1: the people running this system, at least the ones whose actual working
correspondence survives, were operating something closer to a functioning intelligence service than
a theater of confident prophecy. They reported uncertainty when they had it. They disagreed with
each other in writing, sometimes sharply, over how to interpret a given omen or which of two
competing traditions took precedence. They cited precedent, the way a modern lawyer cites case law,
reaching back into the archive Chapter 2 described to justify a present-day reading against past
recorded outcomes.

The correspondence dates mostly from the reigns of two seventh-century BCE Assyrian kings, Esarhaddon
and his son Ashurbanipal, and the scholars writing it were not fringe figures. They held an official
court position — a rotating group of senior astrologer-scribes, physicians, exorcists, and diviners,
collectively serving as the king's standing panel of experts on how to read reality and respond to
it correctly. Astrology, in this system, was never a solo discipline practiced in isolation. It sat
inside a broader apparatus of state divination that also included reading the internal organs of
sacrificial sheep, interpreting dreams, and watching for unusual animal behavior — all of it treated
as different channels tuned to the same underlying signal, cross-checked against each other the way
a modern analyst might triangulate several independent intelligence sources before briefing a head
of state. A scholar who reported a bad omen from the sky and then found it contradicted by a
favorable liver reading did not simply pick the answer he preferred. He wrote to the king and said
so, laying out the conflict and, where he could, an argument for which signal should take
precedence.

This entire body of correspondence survives today only because of a separate, much later act of
patient recovery, one that mirrors the archaeological rediscovery Chapter 1 already described for
cuneiform itself. The palace library that housed it was excavated in the 1840s and 1850s by a small
group of British and French archaeologists, Austen Henry Layard prominent among them, working at the
mounds of ancient Nineveh under conditions that would horrify a modern excavator — tunnels dug
straight through collapsed palace rooms, tablets shipped back to London in bulk crates with little
systematic record of exactly where in the ruin each one had originally lain. It would take
decades more of subsequent cataloguing, and the twentieth century's full decipherment of Akkadian,
before scholars could actually read what had been recovered, and a further, more recent, wave of
careful modern edition — much of it published only in the last several decades, under a project
titled State Archives of Assyria — before this specific correspondence between king and court
astrologer became fully accessible to historians in translation. The scholars who wrote these
letters could not have imagined that their private professional correspondence, filed and mostly
forgotten within a generation of their own deaths, would still be read, argued over, and translated
into a language that did not yet exist, two thousand six hundred years later.

Esarhaddon, in particular, appears in his own surviving correspondence as a king who took this
apparatus extremely seriously and, by the standards of the age, somewhat anxiously. He inherited the
throne after a violent succession crisis — his own brothers had murdered his father, the earlier king
Sennacherib — and the letters from his reign show a ruler who consulted his astrological and
divinatory scholars constantly, on questions ranging from military campaigns to his own health to
whether a particular building project should proceed on a given day. This was not eccentric
personal superstition layered on top of ordinary governance. It was, functionally, how governance
happened. A king who ignored the sky's reported omens without at least performing the correct
ritual counter-measures was, in the worldview shared by everyone in that court, taking a real and
foolish risk with the security of his kingdom — not a metaphorical risk, an actual one, no different
in kind from ignoring a report of enemy troop movements on the border.

The astrologers themselves were not infallible oracles exempt from consequence. The Assyrian court
maintained genuine competition and hierarchy among its scholars, and a report that turned out to be
badly wrong, or a scholar who was caught concealing an unfavorable observation, could and did damage
a career. Several surviving letters show scholars anxiously defending their own competence against
rivals, or pleading for the king's continued favor after an apparently failed prediction — evidence
of a working professional environment with real stakes, real reputational risk, and real
disagreement, rather than a closed priesthood reciting fixed dogma to a credulous audience. It reads,
across the distance of twenty-six centuries, uncannily like any other expert advisory body a modern
reader might recognize: cautious about overclaiming, defensive under criticism, genuinely divided on
hard cases, and only ever as good as the framework it was working within.

It is worth being honest, at this point in the book, about exactly where that framework was strong
and where it was not. The Assyrian court astrologers were, by any measure available to their century,
excellent observational astronomers — their eclipse records are precise enough that modern
astronomers have used them to help refine estimates of the very gradual slowing of Earth's own
rotation over the past three thousand years, a genuinely remarkable afterlife for a seventh-century
BCE clay tablet. What they were not, and could not have been, was correct about the underlying causal
claim: that a lunar eclipse, an event with a purely mechanical astronomical explanation these
scholars had no access to, was actually communicating divine intent about the king's personal safety.
The observations were real. The interpretive framework built on top of them was not. This is a
distinction the rest of this book will keep drawing, at every century it passes through, because
collapsing the two into a single verdict — either "the ancients were brilliant scientists" or "the
ancients were credulous fools" — flattens exactly the thing that makes this history worth telling.
They were neither. They were extremely good at one half of the job, and had no way, yet, of knowing
they had gotten the other half wrong.

The scholarly network behind this correspondence was not confined to Nineveh itself. Surviving
letters show court astrologers writing in from Babylon, Uruk, Nippur, and several other cities
across the empire, each apparently expected to report independently on the same celestial events
their colleagues elsewhere were also observing — a genuinely distributed reporting system, allowing
the king's central advisors to cross-check a given omen's occurrence and interpretation against
multiple independent observers before committing to a course of action, in much the same spirit a
modern intelligence service corroborates a single report against several separate sources before
briefing a decision-maker. Disagreements between cities over how to weigh a given sign do turn up in
the record, and the correspondence shows scholars in the capital sometimes explicitly favoring one
provincial report's interpretation over another's, or requesting clarification when two cities'
observations conflicted — evidence of a working, hierarchical but genuinely deliberative process,
rather than a single, unquestioned oracle handing down verdicts from the palace alone.

What this seventh-century BCE correspondence establishes, more than any single tablet from the omen
series in Chapter 2, is the sheer institutional weight the whole enterprise had achieved by the time
Babylon and Assyria were at their height. This was not folk belief practiced at the margins. It was
core state infrastructure, staffed by trained professionals, embedded in the same palace bureaucracy
that managed tax collection and military logistics, consulted on decisions of real consequence by
kings who had every incentive in the world to want it to actually work. Within another two centuries
of this correspondence, that same institutional machinery — the archive, the professional class, the
mathematical habits of tracking planetary motion with real precision — would produce the single
biggest innovation in the system's three-thousand-year history: a fully mathematicized zodiac, and
with it, the beginning of astronomy as a discipline that could, eventually, be checked against
itself and found wanting on the very claim it had been built to serve.

Ashurbanipal himself, the king whose collapsed library preserved so much of this material, is worth
a brief closing word, because his own relationship to the scholarly tradition this chapter has
described went beyond mere royal patronage. Surviving inscriptions have him boasting, in terms
unusually personal for a Mesopotamian royal text, of his own literacy and his own study of the
scribal arts, including the omen literature his court astrologers consulted on his behalf — a king,
by his own account, who could in some measure read the reports his own scholars sent him rather
than relying entirely on their summary and interpretation. Whether that boast reflects genuine
personal scholarship or standard royal self-praise is not fully resolvable from the evidence alone,
but it is a fitting note to end this chapter on regardless: a ruler who wanted history to remember
him not only as the sky's most attentive customer, but as someone who had, at least in part, learned
to read the reports himself.
