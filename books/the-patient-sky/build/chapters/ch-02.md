# Chapter 2 — Seventy Tablets of If-Then

If you want to understand the actual intellectual shape of Babylonian astrology — not the modern
newspaper-horoscope descendant, but the real thing, as practiced by the people who invented it — the
single most useful document is not a horoscope at all. It is a weather-and-disaster manual roughly
seventy tablets long, compiled and recopied over something like six hundred years, containing
somewhere north of seven thousand individual entries, every single one of which follows the exact
same grammatical structure: if this happens in the sky, then that will happen on the ground.

The Babylonians called it *Enūma Anu Enlil* — "When the gods Anu and Enlil," the opening words of
its first tablet, doing the job a title page does today. Scholars now generally agree the series
reached something like its final compiled form by around 1000 BCE, drawing on omen traditions that
were already centuries old by then, and it went on being copied, studied, and added to for another
five hundred years after that, right into the period when Babylon fell under first Persian and then
Greek rule. It is, by any reasonable measure, one of the longest-running research programs in human
history, conducted by generation after generation of scribes who never met each other and were, as
far as the evidence shows, entirely sincere about the project.

The individual entries are short, blunt, and almost comically specific. *If the moon is surrounded by
a halo and a planet stands within it: a king will die of a snakebite.* *If Venus disappears in the
west and stays absent for three days: there will be a massacre in the west.* *If Mars approaches the
star Antares: enemy troops will besiege the city.* Read a hundred of these in a row and a pattern
emerges that has nothing to do with astrology as such and everything to do with how a
pre-statistical civilization organizes anxiety: eclipses mean danger to the king, an unusually
bright Venus means agricultural abundance, an unfamiliar planetary conjunction means war is coming
from whatever direction the omen pointed to. The logic is conditional, exhaustive, and — this is the
part worth noticing — entirely impersonal. Almost none of these seventy tablets say anything about
an individual person's fate. They are addressed, without exception, to the state: the king, the
harvest, the army, the city. Individual horoscopy, the version of astrology a modern reader would
recognize, does not exist yet. It will not exist for another six or seven hundred years, and when it
finally appears, it will not appear in Babylon at all — a detail Chapter 7 picks up in full.

What did exist, and what *Enūma Anu Enlil* systematized, was a genuinely comprehensive division of
the visible sky into jurisdictions. Babylonian cosmology split the heavens into three parallel bands
running east to west — the "Path of Anu" along the celestial equator, the "Path of Enlil" to the
north, and the "Path of Ea" to the south, each one named for and associated with one of the three
chief gods of the Mesopotamian pantheon. Every star and constellation the scribes tracked was
assigned to one of these three paths, and the path a given celestial event occurred in shifted the
meaning of the omen — the same lunar halo carried different implications depending on which of the
three divine jurisdictions it appeared in. This is not decoration. It is an actual organizing
structure, applied with real consistency across seventy tablets and several centuries of scribal
practice, and it is the direct ancestor of something Chapter 5 will spend a full chapter on: the
zodiac itself, which is, at bottom, a later, more mathematically refined version of exactly this
same instinct — carve the sky into named jurisdictions, and track what moves through each one.

The people compiling and consulting this material were not amateur mystics working alone. By the
first millennium BCE, celestial divination in Mesopotamia had become a formal scribal specialty with
its own professional title — the *ṭupšar Enūma Anu Enlil*, literally "scribe of the *Enūma Anu
Enlil*" — trained through years of apprenticeship in reading, copying, and interpreting the omen
series, attached to royal courts and major temples, and expected to combine the received textual
tradition with fresh, ongoing observation. This was, in the fullest sense available to the ancient
world, professional expertise: a body of accumulated written knowledge, a formal credentialing path,
and a working population of practitioners who took the material seriously enough to argue with each
other about how to apply it. The Neo-Assyrian royal archives, uncovered in the ruins of Nineveh in
the nineteenth century, preserve hundreds of actual letters and reports these scholars sent to their
kings — a body of correspondence detailed and human enough that Chapter 4 gives it a full chapter of
its own.

The individual scribe behind any given tablet is, in almost every case, anonymous to modern
readers — a professional working within a shared, centuries-old textual tradition rather than an
author claiming individual credit, in keeping with a broader Mesopotamian scribal culture that
generally valued faithful transmission of received material over personal innovation. But the
training such a scribe underwent is well documented from surviving school texts: years of
apprenticeship, beginning with basic cuneiform sign-lists and working up through progressively more
technical material, culminating, for a scribe specializing in celestial divination, in direct
supervised copying and study of the omen series itself, alongside instruction in the mathematical
astronomy needed to actually track planetary and lunar positions in the first place. A separate,
related genre of text, sometimes called the diviner's manual, survives from roughly the same period
and lays out, in the scribes' own words, an explicit statement of method — instructing the reader on
how to correctly identify a given celestial phenomenon, cross-reference it against the omen series,
and weigh conflicting signs against each other, a level of methodological self-consciousness that
sits at real odds with any picture of this material as unreflective superstition simply repeated
by rote.

It is tempting, reading a list of omens as strange as "if a fox enters the palace, the palace will
be abandoned" alongside genuine, verifiable astronomical entries about eclipse timing, to sort the
material into a "real astronomy" pile and a "superstitious nonsense" pile and treat them as two
separate projects that happened to share an archive. The Babylonians themselves drew no such line.
To the scribes who compiled *Enūma Anu Enlil*, a fox in the palace and a lunar eclipse were the same
category of event: a legible sign, embedded in the observable world, that a careful, trained reader
could learn to interpret correctly if enough prior cases had been recorded and compared. That is not
a distinction between science and superstition. It is a single, unified theory of how the universe
communicates information, applied without discrimination to every strange thing that happened,
whether it happened in the sky or in the palace kitchen.

What made the sky-based omens different in the long run — different enough to eventually spin off
an entire separate discipline with its own Nobel Prizes twenty-five centuries later — was not that
anyone at the time thought them more reliable than the fox. It was that the sky, unlike a fox in a
palace, is regular. It repeats. An eclipse can be checked against a calendar and, eventually,
predicted in advance rather than merely recorded after the fact. Somewhere in the long process of
compiling and recompiling *Enūma Anu Enlil* over six centuries, the Babylonian scribal tradition
began noticing this regularity and building mathematical tools to track it more precisely than
observation alone allowed — a shift in emphasis, from *what does this omen mean* to *when exactly
will the next one occur*, that would eventually produce, out of the same institutions and often the
same individual scribes, both the fully mathematicized zodiac of Chapter 5 and the beginnings of
predictive planetary astronomy in its own right. That shift shows up most clearly in a genre of text that grew directly alongside the omen series
itself: the so-called goal-year texts, compilations that gathered together, for a single specific
year, every relevant prior astronomical observation needed to predict that year's planetary and
lunar behavior in advance, organized not by omen meaning but by the underlying periodicities the
scribes had noticed repeating — the roughly eighteen-year cycle governing eclipse recurrence, for
instance, a regularity precise enough that a scribe working from a sufficiently deep archive could,
in principle, predict an eclipse's approximate timing years before it happened, without needing to
understand anything at all about why eclipses actually occur. This is mathematical astronomy in a
genuinely recognizable modern sense, built entirely out of pattern-recognition applied to a long
enough archive rather than out of any correct physical model of the solar system — and it is worth
noting that a civilization can arrive at real, usable predictive power this way, purely empirically,
centuries before anyone works out the underlying mechanism generating the pattern in the first
place. A modern reader trying to picture the physical scale of this archive should imagine not a single
neat manuscript but a genuinely large, distributed library — tablets copied and recopied across
multiple cities, held in temple and palace collections that did not always agree with each other on
minor wording, and cross-referenced by scribes working from partial or locally variant copies of a
tradition too large for any one collection to hold complete. Modern editions of *Enūma Anu Enlil*,
assembled by twentieth- and twenty-first-century Assyriologists piecing together fragments recovered
from a dozen different excavation sites across Iraq, remain in places incomplete for exactly this
reason — a testament to how genuinely vast the original undertaking was, and a reminder that this
book's own account of it, however much detail it has offered, is still working from a partial,
painstakingly reassembled fraction of what once existed in full.

The fox omens went nowhere. The eclipse omens,
followed far enough and precisely enough, went everywhere.
