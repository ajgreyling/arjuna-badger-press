# Chapter 17 — Newton, the Last of the Magicians

In 1936, a Cambridge economist and collector named John Maynard Keynes bought a large trove of
Isaac Newton's private papers at auction — manuscripts that had been quietly passed down through
Newton's family and largely ignored by scholars for two centuries, on the reasonable but, it turned
out, badly mistaken assumption that they held nothing of real interest beyond what the *Principia*
and the *Opticks* had already made public. Reading through them, Keynes discovered that the man
credited with founding modern mathematical physics had spent a genuinely enormous portion of his
working life — by later scholarly estimate, considerably more time and paper than he ever devoted to
the physics this book's readers would recognize his name for — on alchemy, and on a private, highly
technical project of biblical chronology and numerology, attempting to calculate exact dates for
events like the construction of Solomon's Temple and the prophesied end of the world using methods
that, to a modern reader, look uncomfortably similar in spirit to the interpretive apparatus this
book has spent sixteen chapters describing. Keynes's verdict on what he had found, delivered in a
1946 essay written for the tercentenary of Newton's birth, has become one of the most quoted lines
in the entire history of science, and it belongs, without qualification, in this book: "Newton was
not the first of the age of reason. He was the last of the magicians."

The line is worth unpacking rather than simply admired, because its precise meaning is easy to
misread. Keynes was not claiming Newton secretly practiced astrology in the specific sense this
book has traced from Babylon onward — the alchemical and numerological material Keynes found is a
related but genuinely distinct tradition, concerned with transmuting matter and decoding hidden
patterns in scripture rather than casting horoscopes. But it belongs to the same broader
intellectual world this book has been describing all along: a conviction, shared across the entire
span from the Babylonian omen-scribes of Chapter 2 through Newton himself, that the visible,
physical universe was saturated with hidden correspondences and encoded meaning, accessible to a
sufficiently disciplined and learned investigator willing to do the patient, painstaking work of
decoding it. Newton approached alchemy and biblical numerology with exactly the same relentless,
obsessive rigor he brought to calculus and universal gravitation — meticulous experimental notes,
careful cross-referencing of sources, years of unglamorous, repetitive labor — because to Newton
himself, there was no clean methodological line separating the two projects. Both were, in his own
mind, disciplined attempts to read a universe he was certain had been deliberately, intelligibly
designed by a rational God, and that certainty, more than any specific finding, was the actual
throughline of his entire intellectual life.

The sheer scale of Newton's private, unpublished alchemical labor is worth stating in concrete
terms, because it is easy to underestimate from a modern vantage point that knows him only as a
physicist. Newton left behind, in his own hand, well over a million words of alchemical notes,
translations, and experimental records — copied recipes, careful transcriptions of earlier
alchemical authorities, and detailed logs of his own furnace experiments conducted in private rooms
at Cambridge, pursued alongside, not instead of, the mathematical and physical work for which he is
remembered today. He also devoted years to close, technical study of the Book of Revelation and the
Book of Daniel, attempting to extract precise chronological and prophetic information from their
symbolic language using methods that, in their basic logic — treating an ancient text's obscure
imagery as an encoded message a sufficiently rigorous reader could decode — bear a real family
resemblance to the interpretive habits this book's earlier chapters described astrologers applying
to the sky itself. None of this work was published in Newton's lifetime; he kept it largely private,
aware, by most historians' reading of the evidence, that it sat uneasily beside his growing public
reputation as the era's foremost natural philosopher.

What Newton's *Principia*, published in 1687, actually did to this book's story was not to reject
the old, encoded-universe worldview outright. It was to supply, for the very first time in the
entire history this book has traced, a single, unified, mathematically precise physical mechanism —
universal gravitation — capable of explaining planetary motion, falling apples, ocean tides, and
comets, all with the same handful of equations, without needing to invoke a planet's supposed
character, a god's active will, or a Ptolemaic radiated warmth to do any of the explanatory work.
This is the genuinely decisive break, more consequential in the long run than Galileo's telescope
from the previous chapter, because it did not merely add awkward new bodies to an already strained
system — it replaced the system's entire causal foundation. Every account of planetary influence
this book has covered, from Babylonian planetary gods through Ptolemy's radiated heat and moisture
through Kepler's harmonic resonance, had needed some story about *why* a distant body should affect
events on Earth. Newton's gravity supplied a mechanism precise enough to predict the exact timing
of a lunar eclipse or the exact return date of a comet to within days, using nothing but mass and
distance — and that same mechanism, run honestly and consistently, gave a clear, quantifiable answer
to whether a planet's gravitational pull could meaningfully affect a human body at the moment of
birth. It could not, not at any magnitude worth taking seriously; the numbers, once anyone bothered
to actually calculate them using Newton's own equations, came out too small by many orders of
magnitude, smaller even than the gravitational pull, as Chapter 12 already noted for the moon
specifically, exerted by another person standing nearby.

Newton himself, as far as the surviving record shows, never used his own mechanics to run that
specific calculation against astrology, and this book will not claim he did. He held complicated,
sometimes contradictory views across his life about various occult and alchemical traditions, and
there is no clean documentary evidence of him personally denouncing astrology as such. What matters
for this book's argument is not Newton's private opinion on the subject, but the objective,
structural consequence of the physics he actually published: a universe now explicable, in its most
fundamental celestial mechanics, by mass, distance, and a single force law, with no remaining
theoretical room for a planet's supposed character or a birth moment's supposed cosmic significance
to do any real causal work. The mechanism Ptolemy had proposed, and Kepler had modified, had just
been replaced by a mechanism precise enough to be checked — and once checked honestly, on its own
terms, it offered astrology no support at all.

It is worth being precise about one further point, because Newton's own biography is sometimes
enlisted, inaccurately, in service of a tidier story than the evidence supports. Newton did not
personally renounce or publicly campaign against astrology, and there is no strong documentary
basis for the popular anecdote, repeated in some later retellings, that he once silenced an
astrology-defending colleague with a crushing one-line rebuttal about the *Principia*'s
implications; that story, wherever it originates, does not appear in Newton's own contemporaneous
correspondence and this book will not repeat it as established fact. What can be stated with real
confidence is narrower and, this book would argue, more interesting: Newton built the physics that
removed astrology's plausible mechanism, while spending a large fraction of his own private
intellectual life pursuing a closely related, equally encoded-universe project of his own. The
irony belongs to the historical record. The clean morality tale belongs to later storytellers.

This is why Newton anchors this book's account of the actual split between astronomy and astrology
more firmly than any single figure before him, including Galileo. Not because Newton set out to end
astrology — the evidence does not support that specific claim — but because the physics he built,
almost as an afterthought to his own private alchemical and theological obsessions, quietly removed
the one thing every previous chapter's practitioners, from Babylonian priest to Renaissance court
astrologer, had been able to supply without difficulty: a plausible mechanism. Kepler had still
believed, sincerely, in some real physical resonance behind planetary aspects. After Newton, that
belief had nowhere left to stand on physical grounds alone — and the institutions that had spent a
thousand years teaching astrology as serious natural science, Chapter 18 will show, took roughly one
more century to formally notice.

Newton's own copy of the *Tetrabiblos*, along with a considerable astrological and alchemical
library, survives among the papers Keynes purchased in 1936 and later donated to Cambridge, physical
evidence that Newton owned and, by the marginal annotations in his own hand, actually engaged with
astrology's central technical text directly, rather than simply inheriting a vague cultural
familiarity with the subject secondhand. Whatever Newton privately concluded about the *Tetrabiblos*'s
claims — and his own marginal notes on the subject are sparse and inconclusive enough that this book
will not claim more certainty than they support — the plain fact of the volume's presence on his
own shelf, annotated in his own hand, is a fitting closing image for this chapter: the physicist who
would end astrology's plausible mechanism kept its founding textbook close enough to write in the
margins.
