# Chapter 16 — The Telescope Changes the Argument

On the night of January 7, 1610, Galileo Galilei pointed a telescope he had built himself, with
optics he had ground and polished by hand, at the planet Jupiter, and saw three small points of
light lined up close beside it that had no business being there on any star chart he owned. Within a
few more nights of careful observation, a fourth point appeared, and all four were visibly moving,
night after night, in a pattern that made unmistakable sense only one way: they were orbiting
Jupiter itself, the way the moon orbited Earth. Galileo had discovered what are still called, in his
honor, the Galilean moons, and in doing so he had found the first celestial bodies in recorded
history that demonstrably did not orbit Earth — a direct, observational blow against the geocentric
cosmology Ptolemy's *Almagest* had anchored for close to fifteen hundred years, and a direct, if less
immediately obvious, blow against something else this book has spent its first fifteen chapters
building: the tidy, closed, seven-body roster of sun, moon, and five visible planets that every
version of astrology since Babylon, as Chapter 3 described, had organized its entire interpretive
system around.

The telescope, over the following months and years, kept finding things the existing system had no
place for. Galileo observed the phases of Venus, showing conclusively that Venus orbited the sun
rather than Earth, further undermining the geocentric picture. He observed sunspots, dark blemishes
moving across the sun's own face, contradicting a long-standing philosophical assumption, inherited
from Aristotle and never seriously questioned, that celestial bodies beyond the moon were perfect
and unchanging. He observed that the Milky Way, long assumed to be some kind of diffuse celestial
glow, resolved under magnification into countless individual stars too faint to see with the naked
eye — evidence that the visible sky, the entire observational basis every prior chapter of this book
had worked from, was radically incomplete, a small, bright fraction of a much larger, much stranger
universe that had simply never been accessible to any of the Babylonian, Egyptian, Greek, Islamic,
or medieval European astronomers this book has covered so far.

None of these discoveries disproved astrology directly, in the sense of a controlled experiment
specifically designed to test its predictive claims — that kind of test, as Part Five of this book
will describe, would not arrive for another three and a half centuries. What the telescope did,
more corrosively, was undermine the closed, finite architecture the entire astrological tradition
had been built on since Babylon. A system whose interpretive grammar rested on exactly seven
named, individually characterized celestial bodies — a fixed, complete, divinely or physically
meaningful roster, as Chapter 3 described — had no ready mechanism for absorbing four newly
discovered moons around Jupiter, let alone the eventual discovery, over a century and a half later,
of an entirely new planet, Uranus, in 1781, which the tradition would scramble to retrofit
meaning onto only after the fact, in a move later astrologers themselves have candidly
acknowledged looks considerably more like improvised accommodation than confirmed prediction.
Astronomy, by contrast, absorbed each new discovery cleanly, because astronomy's actual working
claims — how bodies move, what forces govern them — never depended on the total number of bodies
being fixed or complete in the first place.

Galileo published his initial telescopic findings in March 1610 in a short book titled *Sidereus
Nuncius*, "The Starry Messenger," and dedicated it, in a calculated piece of career strategy, to
Cosimo II de' Medici, the Grand Duke of Tuscany — naming the four newly discovered moons of Jupiter,
in that dedication, the "Medicean Stars," in direct, flattering honor of his prospective patron's
family. The dedication worked: Galileo secured a position at the Medici court in Florence shortly
afterward, a testament to how thoroughly astronomical patronage in this period still ran through the
same court networks that funded the astrological forecasting this chapter has already described
Galileo himself providing. The book caused genuine, immediate sensation across learned Europe,
precisely because its claims were checkable in principle by anyone with access to a comparable
instrument, and because several of Galileo's own contemporaries, initially skeptical, were able to
build or acquire their own telescopes within a matter of months and confirm the same four moons for
themselves — an unusually fast, unusually public replication, by the standards of an era with no
scientific journals in the modern sense, that did a great deal to establish the new instrument's
credibility.

It is worth pausing here on a detail that complicates any tidy story of Galileo as astrology's
straightforward antagonist, because the actual historical record refuses to cooperate with that
simpler version. Galileo, across a career spent making precisely the observations this chapter has
just described, also cast horoscopes — for wealthy patrons, including members of the Medici family
he depended on for his own court position in Florence, and, in a detail modern historians have
documented from his own surviving papers, for his own two daughters and, on at least one occasion,
for himself. This was not, for a working astronomer of Galileo's generation, a strange or
hypocritical sideline any more than it had been for Kepler in the previous chapter. Casting
horoscopes remained, well into the seventeenth century, a routine, income-generating professional
service any competent mathematical astronomer was expected to be able to provide, and Galileo
provided it without, as far as his surviving correspondence shows, any apparent sense that doing so
sat in tension with the telescopic discoveries that were, in the same years, dismantling the
cosmological picture astrology had always assumed. The break between the two disciplines, when it
finally came, would not come from any individual astronomer's personal crisis of conscience. It
would come, more slowly and more institutionally, from the cumulative weight of discoveries like
Galileo's making the old, closed system increasingly difficult for the broader learned community to
take at face value — a process the next two chapters trace to its conclusion.

It is worth adding that Galileo's later, far more famous confrontation with the Catholic Church, the
1633 trial that ended in his forced recantation of the Copernican, sun-centered model, was never
framed by his accusers as a dispute over astrology at all — it was a dispute over cosmology and
biblical interpretation, a separate strand of this book's story that belongs more properly to the
history of astronomy than to the history this book is telling. The two disputes are easy to conflate
from a modern distance, since both involve Galileo, both involve the heavens, and both involve
institutional authority pushing back against new observational claims. But astrology itself was
never on trial in 1633, and the Church's own theologians, as Chapter 14 already described, had
already worked out a stable doctrinal accommodation with astrology generations earlier. What the
Church could not accommodate was Earth's motion, not a birth chart.

What Galileo's telescope actually broke, in the end, was not a specific astrological prediction. It
was the founding assumption, unquestioned since Babylon, that the visible sky was the whole sky —
that seven bodies, tracked and interpreted with sufficient care, constituted a complete and closed
system worth building an entire theory of human fate around. Once that assumption cracked, every
subsequent astronomical discovery — Uranus in 1781, Neptune in 1846, the asteroid belt, the sheer,
continuing expansion of what "the sky" actually contained — widened the gap a little further between
a discipline, astronomy, that welcomed an open-ended universe as simply more to study, and a
discipline, astrology, whose entire interpretive architecture had been built, from its very first
Babylonian tablet, around the comforting premise that the sky was finite, nameable, and complete.

It is worth noting, as a coda to this chapter, that Galileo's own moons still carry, in a small and
entirely secular way, an echo of the naming convention Chapter 3 traced back to Babylonian planetary
theology. When later astronomers formally settled on individual names for the four Galilean moons —
Io, Europa, Ganymede, and Callisto — they drew, as tradition by then required, on figures from
Greek mythology associated with Jupiter's own Greek counterpart, Zeus. The habit of naming a
celestial discovery after a mythological figure tied to the presiding god of that region of sky, a
habit this book's very first chapters described as sincere theology, had by the seventeenth century
become pure convention — retained for its familiarity and its classical prestige, entirely emptied
of the literal belief that had originally motivated it.

The same pattern has held for every major body discovered since. Uranus, William Herschel's 1781
find, very nearly broke the naming convention entirely — Herschel himself proposed naming it after
his royal patron, King George III, and the name "Georgium Sidus" saw real use for several decades
before international astronomical convention settled, instead, on the mythological name still used
today. By the time Neptune was discovered in 1846, the mythological convention was fully restored
and never seriously challenged again — a small, telling sign of how completely the old naming
habit had outlived, by two centuries, any belief in the gods it once named.

This book has now traced that naming habit across four thousand years, from Marduk's literal
identity as Jupiter in Chapter 3 to a purely decorative classical reference attached to a moon of
Jupiter in Galileo's own century, and it is worth marking the distance covered: the same impulse
that once named a living god has become, by this chapter's end, a naming convention nobody involved
believes in even slightly, and everybody involved still, for reasons of pure tradition, keeps.
