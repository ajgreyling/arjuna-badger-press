# Chapter 15 — Kepler's Day Job

In 1601, Johannes Kepler was appointed Imperial Mathematician to Rudolf II, Holy Roman Emperor,
succeeding to the post on the death of his own mentor and sometime rival, the great observational
astronomer Tycho Brahe. The title sounds, to a modern reader, like a purely scientific appointment,
and in part it was — Kepler inherited, along with the position, Tycho's extraordinary archive of
naked-eye planetary observations, the most precise ever compiled before the telescope, and it was
against that exact archive that Kepler would spend the following decade grinding out the discovery
this book most associates with his name: that planets do not move in the perfect circles Ptolemy,
Copernicus, and every serious astronomer before him had assumed for close to two thousand years, but
in ellipses, a correction so mathematically unglamorous compared to the circle's ancient philosophical
prestige that Kepler himself later described the years he spent resisting his own data, trying every
possible circular and oval variant before finally, reluctantly, accepting the ellipse, as something
close to a personal ordeal.

But the actual job description attached to Imperial Mathematician, in 1601, was not confined to
astronomical calculation. It explicitly included producing annual astrological forecasts —
predictions of the coming year's weather, political events, and general fortunes, cast for the
emperor's court and, in practice, sold more widely as printed almanacs to a paying public — along
with casting individual horoscopes for the emperor and other wealthy patrons on request. This was
not an embarrassing sideline Kepler tolerated to keep his real astronomical research funded. It was,
structurally and financially, the actual core of what the position paid for. Kepler's astronomical
work, including the years of ellipse-fitting labor that would eventually produce his first two laws
of planetary motion, published in 1609 in a work whose full title translates as *New Astronomy,
Based upon Causes, or Celestial Physics*, was substantially subsidized by fees earned casting
horoscopes and selling astrological almanacs — a fact Kepler stated about his own working life with
real, unembarrassed directness, in a line from his writing that has survived in close to its original
form and needs no invented dressing up: astrology, he said, was astronomy's foolish daughter, but a
daughter whose earnings kept her wise mother fed.

Kepler's own family circumstances give this chapter an additional, harder-edged dimension worth
including rather than smoothing over. In 1615, Kepler's own mother, Katharina Kepler, was formally
accused of witchcraft in their home town in the Duchy of Württemberg, a charge that dragged on for
roughly six years and carried, under the law of the time and place, a genuine risk of execution.
Kepler, by then established as a respected imperial mathematician, devoted enormous personal and
financial effort to his mother's legal defense, eventually traveling to argue the case in person and
helping secure her release without torture or execution, though only after a prolonged, exhausting
ordeal that shadowed the final years of his own scientific work. The episode is a useful, sobering
reminder of the genuinely dangerous world this book's astrologer-astronomers actually worked in: the
same broad cultural conviction that the visible world was saturated with hidden, readable
significance — the conviction this entire book has traced from Babylon onward, and the conviction
that funded Kepler's own household through the astrological forecasts this chapter has already
described — could, applied with hostile intent by a suspicious neighbor rather than a careful
astronomer, just as easily turn lethal.

That line is worth sitting with, because it captures something more interesting than simple
mercenary cynicism. Kepler's actual, considered position on astrology, laid out most fully in a 1601
treatise whose Latin title translates as *On the More Certain Fundamentals of Astrology*, was neither
a wholesale endorsement of the Ptolemaic tradition he had inherited nor a wholesale rejection of the
underlying premise that the sky might genuinely influence earthly events. Kepler dismissed, with
real and specific scorn, large parts of conventional astrological practice — the elaborate,
centuries-accumulated system of houses, the fine-grained personality-trait assignments to individual
planets and signs, and above all the practice of casting detailed, confident predictions about a
specific individual's personal fate, which he regarded as having outrun any evidence that could
possibly support it. What he did not dismiss was a narrower, more restrained claim: that planetary
configurations, particularly the geometric angles or "aspects" formed between planets as seen from
Earth, might produce genuine, physically real effects on terrestrial weather and, through weather
and through some vaguer resonant harmony he never fully specified, on human mood and character in
aggregate. Kepler's own cosmology, developed across his career, was deeply invested in the idea
that the universe was structured by mathematical and harmonic relationships — his 1619 work
*Harmonices Mundi*, "The Harmony of the World," argues at length that the ratios between planetary
orbital speeds echo the mathematical ratios underlying musical harmony — and his qualified defense
of astrology's aspects grew directly out of that same conviction: not that a distant planet radiated
mystical personal fate onto an individual, but that the geometric relationships between moving
bodies in a mathematically harmonious cosmos might produce real, if modest and largely
weather-and-mood-level, resonant effects here on Earth.

This is, by any honest reading, a genuinely intermediate position, neither the full Ptolemaic system
Chapter 8 described nor anything close to the modern rejection Part Five of this book will document
in detail, and it is worth resisting the temptation, common in popular retellings of Kepler's life,
to simplify it in either direction — to cast him either as a closet true believer embarrassingly out
of step with his own revolutionary astronomy, or as a cynical operator who privately scorned
astrology entirely and only pretended otherwise for money. Both simplifications flatten a more
interesting and more historically honest truth: Kepler was working, as every figure in this book's
first fourteen chapters had worked before him, at the actual edge of what his era's evidence could
support, applying real scientific caution to a tradition he had inherited, discarding the parts he
judged unjustified while retaining a narrower core he genuinely believed, on the mathematical and
physical grounds available to him, might still hold up.

Kepler's predecessor and one-time employer, Tycho Brahe, deserves a brief word here too, because his
own relationship with astrology sharpens the picture this chapter is drawing. Tycho, whose
naked-eye observational precision remained unmatched until the telescope arrived, was himself a
committed practicing astrologer, casting a widely circulated forecast predicting significant
political upheaval in Europe tied to a rare planetary conjunction observed in 1583 — a forecast
later readers found eerily suggestive in light of the Thirty Years' War that began some decades
afterward, though this book will not claim that later coincidence as confirming evidence any more
than it claimed Thrasyllus's prediction for Tiberius as confirmed in Chapter 10. What matters for
this chapter is narrower and more solid: the single most precise observational astronomer of the
pre-telescopic age, whose data Kepler would spend a decade mining for the ellipse, saw no more
contradiction between his instruments and his horoscopes than Kepler himself would after him.

What makes Kepler's chapter in this book's story the true hinge point of Part Three, more than any
individual claim he did or did not endorse, is the sheer, uncomfortable proximity it establishes
between astrology's last serious institutional respectability and modern astronomy's actual
founding revolution. The same man, in the same decade, using the same observational archive,
discovered the elliptical orbits that would make the Copernican system finally, mathematically
work — the single most important astronomical breakthrough of the early modern period — while
drawing a substantial share of his own household income casting the very horoscopes his
astronomical discoveries would eventually help render intellectually untenable. He did not live to
see that consequence fully play out; astrology would remain a going academic and court concern for
decades after his death in 1630. But the tools that would finally force the separation this book's
Part Four covers in full — sharper instruments, more precise mathematics, and a physical theory of
planetary motion that no longer needed a planet's supposed character to explain its behavior — were,
by the time Kepler died, already substantially in place, built in no small part by Kepler's own
reluctant, decade-long surrender to an ellipse he had not wanted to find.

Kepler's third and final law of planetary motion, published a decade after the first two, in the
same 1619 *Harmonices Mundi* that laid out his harmonic defense of astrology's aspects, relates a
planet's orbital period to its distance from the sun with a mathematical precision that would, less
than a century later, hand Newton the exact empirical relationship his own law of universal
gravitation needed to explain. It is a small, quietly remarkable fact that the specific mathematical
tool most directly responsible for dismantling astrology's plausible physical mechanism, in Chapter
17's account, was forged by a working astrologer, in the same book, in the same argumentative breath,
as his own defense of that mechanism's basic premise.

Kepler died in 1630, in Regensburg, traveling to collect a debt owed him from years of unpaid
salary as imperial mathematician — a fittingly unglamorous end for a life spent serving emperors who
valued his astrology enough to employ him and his astronomy too little to pay him reliably for it.
His own epitaph, which he composed himself, reads, in translation: "I measured the skies, now the
shadows I measure." The astrology that paid his bills earned no mention at all.
