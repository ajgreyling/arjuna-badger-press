# What's Real, What's Open, and What's Not Supported

*A note from the author, and an invitation.*

This book has tried, at every chapter, to say plainly which kind of ground it was standing on. This
final section collects that discipline in one place, the same way this shelf's *Physics Won't Hurt
You* closes with its own version of the same accounting.

## What's real (documented history and settled science)

The Babylonian omen tablets, the *Enūma Anu Enlil* series, and the Neo-Assyrian royal astrological
correspondence are real, excavated, and substantially deciphered primary sources (Chapters 1-4). The
zodiac's invention in Babylon, as a mathematical coordinate system rather than a literal star map,
is documented in the surviving tablet record, including the earliest known individual horoscope from
around 410 BCE (Chapter 5). The transmission of Babylonian astrology to the Greek world via figures
including Berossus, its fusion with Egyptian decanal tradition and Greek philosophy into the
individual Hellenistic horoscope, and Ptolemy's *Tetrabiblos* and *Almagest* as companion works by
the same author are all documented history (Chapters 6-9). Rome's repeated, documented cycle of
expelling and privately consulting astrologers, including the well-attested Tiberius-Thrasyllus
relationship, is drawn from ancient Roman historians (Chapter 10). Medical astrology's real
institutional presence in medieval European university medical curricula, including Bologna's
astrology chair, is documented in surviving university records (Chapters 11-14). Kepler's actual
employment as Imperial Mathematician, his documented income from astrological almanacs and
horoscopes, and his qualified, genuinely complicated published views on astrology are drawn from his
own surviving writings (Chapter 15). Galileo's telescopic discoveries and his own documented practice
of casting horoscopes, Newton's documented alchemical and biblical-numerological writings (the
Keynes quote is real and precisely sourced), and the documented institutional retreat of astrology
from European universities and academies across the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries are all
settled historical record (Chapters 16-18). The astronomical fact of axial precession, discovered by
Hipparchus and entirely uncontroversial in modern astronomy, is settled science (Chapter 21). The
Barnum/Forer effect and the broader psychological literature on cold reading, confirmation bias, and
illusory correlation are well-replicated findings in mainstream cognitive and social psychology
(Chapter 22). R. H. Naylor's 1930 newspaper column and its role in launching the modern sun-sign
horoscope format are documented media history (Chapter 23).

## What's a live, honestly open question

Michel Gauquelin's original "Mars effect" data and the specific methodological reasons later,
tighter-controlled replications failed to confirm it remain a genuinely contested episode in the
history of parapsychological and statistical research, and this book has reported that contest
honestly rather than flattening it into either "proven" or "debunked" (Chapter 20). The precise
historical mechanism and degree of direct transmission between Hellenistic and Vedic astrology,
though real and documented in broad outline, includes specific technical details still actively
studied and debated by historians of Indian astronomy (Chapter 25). The exact felt boundary and
starting date of any "Age of Aquarius" is, by its own astrological tradition's internal logic, not a
settled or agreed figure even among astrologers (Chapter 23).

## What's specifically not supported

Controlled, peer-reviewed research — most notably Shawn Carlson's 1985 double-blind study in
*Nature*, designed in direct collaboration with practicing astrologers, and the broader body of
meta-analytic research that has followed it — has not found evidence that natal astrology predicts
personality or life outcomes at a rate better than chance (Chapter 20). The popular folk belief that
a birth-date "sign" corresponds to the sun's actual current position among the physical
constellations is astronomically false for the great majority of Western tropical astrology, due to
the accumulated effect of axial precession since the zodiac was first fixed (Chapter 21). No named
Hippocrates quote endorsing astrology as a medical requirement appears in the genuinely early
Hippocratic Corpus; the widely circulated version is very likely a later, misattributed addition
(Chapter 11). The popular anecdote of Newton silencing a colleague's defense of astrology with a
single crushing rebuttal about the *Principia* does not appear in Newton's own contemporaneous
papers or correspondence and is not repeated here as established fact (Chapter 17).

## A word on the name

No living person, named or identifiable, appears anywhere in this book, in any role. Modern
astrology's commercial persistence is discussed at the level of the industry and the psychology
behind it, never through a named practitioner, columnist, company, or product. This is a deliberate
choice, held consistently from this book's first page to its last, and readers looking for a
verdict on any specific modern astrologer, app, or public figure will not find one here — this book
is a history, not a review.

## Sources, in brief

This book draws on the published work of historians of Mesopotamian, Egyptian, Hellenistic,
Islamic, and medieval science and astrology, on the primary ancient and medieval texts named across
its chapters (the *Enūma Anu Enlil* series, the Neo-Assyrian scholarly correspondence, Ptolemy's
*Almagest* and *Tetrabiblos*, Kepler's own astrological and astronomical writings among them), and
on the peer-reviewed modern research named directly in Chapters 20 through 22. It does not reproduce
any other popular history's prose, argument structure, or specific framing — the synthesis, and any
errors within it, are the author's own.
