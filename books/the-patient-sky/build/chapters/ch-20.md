# Chapter 20 — Testing the Chart

For roughly three thousand years, from the Babylonian omen-scribes of Chapter 2 to Kepler casting
horoscopes to pay his household bills in Chapter 15, nobody practicing astrology had access to the
one tool that could have actually settled, on evidence rather than accumulated impression, whether
the discipline's central claim was true: a controlled study, designed in advance to rule out
coincidence, selective memory, and the observer's own hopes for the outcome. That tool did not exist,
as a formal method, until the modern era, and it was not seriously turned on astrology's actual
predictive claims until the second half of the twentieth century. This chapter tells that story
directly, and it is the one chapter in this book where the register this book has otherwise kept
sympathetic throughout — taking every earlier era's astrologers seriously as people doing careful
work with the tools and the evidence available to them — gives way to a plainer, more direct
question: given the tools finally available, what did the tests actually find.

The most famous and most contested of these tests came first, and it did not originate as an attempt
to debunk astrology at all. Michel Gauquelin, a French psychologist and statistician, spent much of
the 1950s and 1960s conducting large-scale statistical studies correlating birth data against
professional and personal outcomes, initially expecting, by his own account, to find nothing and
thereby confirm astrology's lack of validity. Instead, in data drawn from several thousand European
sports champions, he reported a statistically notable correlation between the position of Mars in
the sky at the moment of birth and eventual success in athletics — a finding that came to be known,
across the following decades of controversy it generated, as the "Mars effect." Gauquelin's own
methodology was genuinely careful by the standards of his era, and the finding was not simply waved
away; it prompted a series of serious attempted replications by researchers specifically motivated to
check it, including groups affiliated with organizations skeptical of astrology's broader claims.
Those replication attempts, conducted through the 1970s and into the 1980s under progressively
tightened controls — larger and more carefully defined comparison samples, corrections for known
biases in how "champion" status itself had originally been selected and recorded — produced results
that did not hold up to the original finding's strength, and by the 1990s the consensus among
researchers who had examined the question closely, including some who had initially taken
Gauquelin's result seriously, was that the effect was best explained by methodological artifacts in
the original sample selection rather than any genuine astronomical influence on athletic ability.
This book reports that history in full, rather than either the flat "it was debunked" version or the
flat "it was proven" version sometimes offered in less careful popular accounts, because the actual
Gauquelin episode is a genuinely useful case study in exactly the kind of careful, iterative,
self-correcting process controlled science is supposed to run — and, in this instance, did run,
across several independent research groups and several decades, arriving in the end at a
non-confirmation.

The most direct and most widely cited single test of astrology's central personal claim came in
1985, when Shawn Carlson, then a physics graduate student, published a study in the scientific
journal *Nature* — a genuinely rigorous, peer-reviewed venue, chosen deliberately to hold the test
to the highest standard available — designed with input from professional astrologers themselves on
how to make the test fair to their own discipline's actual claims. The design was straightforward
and, crucially, double-blind: subjects completed a standard, well-validated personality assessment,
the California Psychological Inventory, and professional astrologers were given each subject's full
natal chart along with three personality profiles — the subject's real one, mixed in with two others
chosen at random — and asked to identify which profile actually matched the chart. If astrology's
central claim held even loosely true, astrologers with real professional experience should have been
able to make this match at a rate meaningfully better than the one-in-three chance a random guess
would produce. Across the full study, the participating astrologers — experienced practitioners, not
skeptics of their own field, and confident enough beforehand to predict they would succeed at a rate
well above chance — matched charts to profiles correctly almost exactly as often as random guessing
would predict, and no better. Carlson's own conclusion, stated plainly in the published paper, was
that the study represented a genuine, rigorous test of natal astrology's central claim under
conditions astrologers themselves had helped design as fair, and that the claim had failed the test.

It is worth describing the Carlson study's actual design in a little more detail, because its
fairness to astrology is precisely what gives its negative result real force. Carlson recruited
practicing astrologers through the National Council for Geocosmic Research, a genuine professional
astrological organization, specifically so the participants would be experienced practitioners
rather than skeptics chosen to make the test easy to fail. The astrologers themselves reviewed and
approved the study's protocol in advance, including the specific personality inventory used and the
method for presenting each subject's chart, and they predicted beforehand, with real confidence,
that they would succeed at matching charts to profiles at a rate of at least fifty percent — well
above the one-in-three chance baseline. The actual result, across all trials, came in at almost
exactly the chance rate the null hypothesis predicted, a gap between the astrologers' own confident
prediction and their actual performance that Carlson's paper highlighted as itself a notable
finding, independent of the headline result.

The Carlson study is not, and this book will not pretend it is, the only relevant piece of evidence.
It sits within a considerably larger body of research, including meta-analyses conducted by
researchers such as Geoffrey Dean and Ivan Kelly reviewing decades of accumulated studies across
multiple countries and methodologies, that has, with real consistency across a large number of
independent tests, failed to find evidence supporting astrology's specific predictive claims about
personality, compatibility, or life events based on birth chart data. This is not the same as a
claim that astrology has been proven impossible in some absolute logical sense — controlled research
of this kind establishes an absence of demonstrated effect within the conditions actually tested,
not a metaphysical impossibility, and this book will hold that distinction carefully rather than
overstate it. But it is a genuinely strong, repeatedly replicated result, arrived at by researchers
across several decades and several countries, using methods designed in direct collaboration with
practicing astrologers to give the discipline's own claims the fairest possible hearing available.
After three thousand years of patient, careful, and often genuinely rigorous observation, the
specific claim that a birth chart predicts a life, tested at last under the one set of conditions
capable of actually answering the question, did not hold up.

It is worth adding that this negative result has itself been checked and re-checked in the decades
since Carlson's original 1985 paper. Later researchers have run comparable tests on more specific
astrological claims — whether sun signs correlate with measurable personality traits on standard
psychological inventories, whether astrologers can outperform chance at matching charts to
professions, whether synastry, the astrological analysis of romantic compatibility between two
charts, predicts actual relationship outcomes better than random pairing — and the accumulated
pattern across this broader body of research has remained consistent with Carlson's own finding
rather than overturning it. This book reports that consistency because it matters for how much
weight the original result deserves: a single failed test, however well designed, always leaves room
for the possibility of an unlucky sample or an overlooked flaw. A consistent pattern of failed tests,
run by different researchers, using different methods, across four decades, is a considerably harder
result to explain away as a fluke.

It is worth closing this chapter by naming what a negative result of this kind does and does not
mean for the individual astrologer's own sincerity, because the two questions are genuinely separate
and this book does not want to collapse them. A practitioner who has spent years developing real
skill at reading a chart, noticing patterns, and offering a client thoughtful, attentive
observations about their life may well be doing something of real value in that consulting room —
listening carefully, asking good questions, offering a structured occasion for reflection a client
might not otherwise take time for — even though the specific astronomical mechanism they invoke to
explain that value has not, on the evidence this chapter has just laid out, been shown to be doing
any of the actual work.

That distinction — between a practice's felt personal value to a client and its underlying causal
claim's actual truth — is not a loophole this book is offering astrology to escape through. It is a
distinction that applies with equal force to any consultation involving careful, attentive listening
from a trained practitioner, astrological or otherwise, and this book raises it here only to keep
its own verdict precise: Chapter 20 has established what the controlled evidence shows about the
mechanism. It has not, and does not need to, pass judgment on every individual human interaction
that happens to take place inside an astrological framework.
