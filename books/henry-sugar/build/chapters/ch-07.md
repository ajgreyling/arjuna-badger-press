# The Yogi in the Tree

I had been to Hardwar before, but not like this — not with a question in me sharp enough to draw blood. The pilgrims came down to the river in their thousands, and the bathing ghats steamed in the morning, and the priests sang the Ganga down out of the mountains as they had sung her for a thousand years. I walked among them and did not bathe. I was looking for a man, and the man I was looking for did not want to be found.

His name, I had been told, was Banerjee. He lived somewhere in the forest above the town, where the trees came down close to the foothills and the heat sat thick in the green. They said he had been training his whole life. They said he never performed and never taught and never spoke of what he could do, and that this was precisely how I would know him from the frauds — because the real ones, the ones who had paid the full price in years, were forbidden to make a show of any of it. The men who let you watch were the men who had nothing to show.

I had spent four months looking for a real teacher. I had found a great many performers. I had stood in the back of theatres and watched men I knew to be working tricks I myself had worked, and I had felt sick with it, because I had wanted so badly to find one thing that was not a trick. So when a sadhu at the riverside told me there was a true yogi in the forest, and that the yogi would chase me away if I came near, I took the warning as the first encouraging thing anyone had said to me in months.

I went up into the trees before dawn and I hid.

There was a clearing where the old man came at first light. I climbed a peepal and lay along a branch with the leaves between me and the open ground, and I waited, and the light came grey and then gold through the canopy, and the insects started, and at last he came. He was thin and very old and he wore almost nothing. He sat down cross-legged in the middle of the clearing with his back very straight and his hands open on his knees, and he closed his eyes.

For a long time nothing happened. His breathing changed. I could not hear it from where I lay but I could see it in the rise of his ribs — long and slow and very even, drawn down deep and held and let go, a count I could not measure but could feel was deliberate, the way you feel the metre of a thing before you can name it. The morning warmed. A film of sweat came up on his shoulders and his scalp and ran in a single line down his spine, and still he did not move, and I began to think I had wasted a climb on an old man at his prayers.

Then the old man rose off the ground.

I want to set it down exactly as it happened to me, in the order it happened, because the order is the only honest thing I have. First I saw a gap. There was earth where his folded legs had been, and then there was air. He did not lurch up or float like a thing on a string. He was simply, between one breath and the next, a hand's width above the place where he had been sitting, and he stayed there, perfectly still, his back straight and his eyes shut and that single line of sweat running down it, as though the ground had only ever been a courtesy he had decided to do without.

I did not believe it. That is the thing I must say. I had spent my life teaching my eyes to lie to other people, and now my own eyes were telling me a thing I could not afford to believe, and the conjurer in me went hunting at once for the wire and the false floor and the angle of the light — and there was no wire, there was no floor, there was only the old man and the air and the morning coming up gold through the leaves.

And I made a sound.

I do not know what the sound was. A breath, a word, something that came out of me before I could stop it. The leaves shook. And the old man's eyes opened.

He came down — I did not see how, only that the earth had him again — and he was on his feet before I had got my own legs under me, and he was looking up into the tree straight at the place where I lay as though there had never been a single leaf between us. His face had gone from the stillness of the river into something I can only call fury. It was the fury of a man who has had a thing stolen from him that he has guarded for sixty years. He shook his fists and he stamped and he cursed me up and down in three languages, and the word he kept coming back to was *thief* — thief, thief, who told you, who sent you, how long have you been watching — and I have never since read the tale of the little man who tore himself in two with rage at being named without thinking of Banerjee under that tree, white with anger, screaming up into the leaves.

I came down. I did not run. I stood in front of him with my hands open the way he had held his on his knees, and I told him the truth — that I had spent my life inside trickery and was sick to death of it; that I had come to find the one thing that was not a trick; and that I had found it, and would he please, please teach me.

He told me to go to hell. He told me I would learn nothing, that the price was a lifetime and I had the face of a man who wanted a fortune by Friday. He was right about that, though I did not yet know how right. He turned his back on me and walked into the trees, and I let him go.

And then I came back the next morning. And the morning after that.

I will not pretend it was noble. I had been a performer; I knew how to make myself a fixture, how to wear a man down by simply being there, day on day, until refusing me cost him more effort than the refusing was worth. But there was more to it than craft. I had seen the thing. I had seen a man set aside the floor of the world as though it were optional, and I could no more have walked away from that than a starving man can walk away from bread he can smell. I came every day for two weeks. I sat at the edge of his clearing where he could see me and I said nothing and I asked for nothing. He cursed me the first days and ignored me the rest. I brought him a little food. I brought him water from the river. I learned to sit with my back straight and my breath long and even, copying him without permission, and I think it was that — the back, the breath, the sheer animal stubbornness of it — that finally moved something in him.

On the fourteenth morning he did not curse me. He looked at me for a long time, the way you might look at a stray that has decided your doorway is its home, and at last he said that he himself could not teach me — that what he did he had no gift for giving away — but that there was a man, three days' walk to the north, who took students. A teaching yogi. A rare thing, he said, and a dangerous one, because a man who learns the inner sight for the wrong reasons learns also a debt he will one day have to pay. He looked at me very hard when he said it, and I understood that he was looking at the fortune-by-Friday in my face, and I had the grace at least to say nothing.

He gave me the name. He gave me the way. And then he said the thing I have carried with me ever since, the thing that has more weight in it now than it did when I was young and hungry and quick.

He said: *It will take you three years. Not three years if you are clever. Three years if you are clean, and longer if you are not. There is no quick door. The door is the three years.*

I thanked him. He waved me off into the trees as he had waved me off the first day, but it was a different gesture now, the kind you make to send a man toward a thing rather than away from yourself.

I went north. I found the teaching yogi where Banerjee had said he would be. And on a morning much like the morning I had hidden in the peepal and seen an old man set the ground aside, I sat down in front of my teacher with my back straight and my hands open and my breath drawn long and even, and the three years began.
