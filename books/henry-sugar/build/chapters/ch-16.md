# What a Novelist Would Do

Let me step out from behind the curtain for a moment, because here is where I am tempted to lie to you.

I have been telling this story straight. I have given you Henry as he was — the capped teeth, the marked deck at Monte Carlo, the motto about the mild rebuke and the onerous task — and I have not softened him, because softening him would have been a kind of lie too. But now we arrive at the night after his first big win, and I, who am writing this, feel the old machinery of the trade begin to turn under my hands. The machinery that wants a *shape*. The machinery that wants the story to behave.

So let me show you what a competent novelist would do here. Not a great one — a great one might do something stranger — but a competent one, a craftsman who knows where the satisfaction lives in a reader's chest and how to press it.

He would kill Henry.

Not crudely. Not with a car or a bad oyster. He would kill him with the gift itself, because that is where the moral lives and a craftsman always finds the moral and puts it where the reader will trip over it. Remember that the yogis — the real ones, in Imhrat Khan's account, the ones who could levitate and walk the firepit and read a book pressed to the back of the skull — were forbidden to perform in public. Forbidden absolutely. The faculty was given to be turned inward, toward what Imhrat's teacher called the long work, and a man who took it to a card table was a man who had stolen something sacred and was spending it on chips.

A competent novelist loves a rule like that. A rule like that is a loaded gun on the mantelpiece, and the trade says: fire it.

So picture the flat. Mayfair, the small hours, the £850,000 still in two banded bricks on the dresser where Henry had dropped them, money he had not counted because counting it would have meant pretending it mattered. Picture him standing in front of the full-length mirror in the bedroom — the one he'd had fitted because a man should be able to see the whole of himself when he dressed — and picture him doing, idly, the thing the training had taught him to do without effort now: turning the inner sense on.

In Imhrat's report the inner sense was described as something close to touch. Not eyes behind the eyes — Kuda Bux, when the doctors of the London council sealed dough into his sockets in 1935 and layered the bandages and still he read aloud from a book and threaded a needle, did not claim eyes. He claimed his *skin* saw. That the surface of the body was an instrument, that perception was not a thing locked behind the face but spread across the whole envelope of him, and could be trained to reach. The novelist would have read that. The novelist would have it ready.

And so in the novelist's version Henry turns the inner sense on himself in the glass, the way you might run a hand down your own arm in the dark, and for the first time the instrument finds not a card, not a wall, not the street below — but the inside of him. His own chest. He sees, the way Imhrat learned to see, *through*. The architecture of himself: the long muscle of the heart still working, the wet rooms of the lungs, the dark traffic of the blood. And there — the novelist's hand does not even tremble, this is the easiest sentence he will write all year — there, lodged in a vein behind the breastbone, a clot. Small. Patient. Loosening.

The yogi's law had been broken. The gift had been spent on greed, and the gift, being older and larger than Henry, collects what it is owed. In the novelist's version Henry watches his own blood carry the clot toward the chambers of his heart with the same calm, four-second attention he had trained on the backs of playing cards, and he understands, in the last clear second, exactly what is killing him and exactly why, and he cannot look away, because looking away is the one thing the inner sense will not let him do.

They would find him in the morning. The money untouched. The mirror clean. A man dead of perfectly natural causes — a coroner would write *embolism*, a common thing, a thing that fells common men in their flats every night of the year — and only the reader would know that nothing about it was natural at all. The reader would close the chapter with that cold pleasure that good craft delivers: *of course. It had to be that. The story told me so from the start.*

It is a good ending. I want you to feel how good it is. The rule fires. The vanity that fueled the training — Henry visualising his own face in the candle flame, not a beloved's, the monotropic self-love that made him learn so fast — that same vanity becomes the instrument of his death, because a man who turns the whole apparatus of his perception inward on *himself* will, eventually, see the one thing none of us is meant to watch. Greed in, greed out. The gift turned the wrong way kills the one who turns it. There is a thermocouple's precision to how the pieces lock.

And it is a lie.

This story is not fiction. The only untrue things in it are the names — and I will keep my word about those to the end. But Henry Sugar did not die that night, or that year, of a clot he watched arrive. There was no mirror revelation. He did not break the yogi's law and have the law come for him, because the law, if it is a law at all, does not appear to work the way a novelist needs it to. The presentiment researchers — Radin and the others who pooled the studies, all those bodies wired to sensors that twitched a beat or two *before* a random image flashed, skin and heart anticipating what no nerve could yet know — none of them ever reported a man reading his own death in advance and being unable to stop it. That is not on the record. That is the trade, dressing a moral up as physics and hoping you won't check.

What actually happened was duller and stranger and, I think, much harder to bear.

Henry woke late. The money was still on the dresser, both bricks of it, and he looked at them the way you look at a coat you bought drunk — with a faint, specific embarrassment. He had expected, you understand, to feel triumphant. He had spent three years and a stolen dossier and ten months of nothing-nothing-nothing before the first card ever turned, and at the end of all of it he had walked into a private room off St James's and taken £850,000 from men he despised, and the whole point — the entire freight of the want that had carried him there — had been the moment of *winning*. He had been certain the moment would fill him.

It did not fill him. It is the oldest thing in the world and Imhrat's teacher had said it plainly somewhere in the report, in that documentary hush the nested pages all had: *nothing satisfies if you can have as much as you want.* Henry had read the sentence and skipped it, because it was not a card-table sentence, and now it sat in him like the clot the novelist never gave him.

He stood at the window — Curzon Street below him, wet, ordinary, a man walking a dog, a taxi idling — and the inner sense was still on, faintly, the way a radio left low keeps murmuring. And he found, looking down at the dull live street, that the training had changed something in him that the winning never could. Imhrat had warned of it without warning, in his own way: that the long work alters the one who does it whether he means it to or not, that you cannot spend three years learning to feel the field of attention reach past your own skin — the thing Sheldrake's people argued in their staring studies, the prickle on the neck, the sense of being looked at that the body insists on before any sound or shadow — you cannot train that and come out the same man who sat down. The instrument, once tuned, hears more than cards. It hears the room. It hears, faintly, the people in the room, whether you want it to or not.

So there was no death. There was a rich man at a window with eight hundred and fifty thousand pounds he did not want, feeling something move in his chest that was not a clot — that was, God help him, the first stirring of a wish to give it away. He did not have the shape of it yet. He did not know about the balcony, or the policeman who would shout at him, or John Winston in Geneva, or Max, or the twenty years. He only knew, standing there, that the want had been a lie and the hole was still a hole and the money on the dresser was the wrong size for it entirely.

I could have killed him. The craft is sitting right here in my hands, asking me to. But the man was real, and what was happening to him that morning was real, and it was better than the ending the trade wanted — because the ending the trade wanted was clean, and what actually came next was not clean at all. It was a grown man learning, far too late and at enormous cost, what he was for.

He put his coat on. He picked up one of the banded bricks, and then, after a moment, the second.
