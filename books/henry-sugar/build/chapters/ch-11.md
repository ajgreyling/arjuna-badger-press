# Mild Rebuke

Henry closed the dossier on the last page, which described the death of a man in a Bombay bed, and sat for a long time without moving.

The library had gone dark around him. Somewhere below, the poker had broken up — he could hear the men in the hall now, the heavy good-nights, the slam of car doors, the rain still going at the windows in its patient way. They had not missed him. He understood, sitting there with the dead godfather's Moleskine in his lap, that no one in that house had thought of Henry Sugar once in three hours.

He did not care. He had stopped caring at the page where Imhrat Khan learned to read the underside of a playing card.

That was the thing he kept returning to, the way a man's tongue keeps returning to a chipped tooth. Not the bicycle through traffic. Not the needle threaded blind in front of doctors. The cards. The cards were the whole point, and the cards had been sitting in this report for the better part of a century like money left in a coat.

He read that section again under the desk lamp. *Three years*, the man had needed. Three years of staring at a candle in a cell, of breathing in counts, of feeling the heat rise behind the eyes until something in the body learned what the eyes could no longer be trusted to do. And then the cards. Then any room with cards in it was an open book.

Henry was a member of ten private rooms in London. He had been to the others — Monte Carlo, the floating game off Antibes, the back table at a club in Geneva where the buy-in was a number you said quietly. He had cheated in most of them, in the small, lazy, deniable ways a man cheats when he has already decided that incurring a mild rebuke is always preferable to performing an onerous task. A marked deck at a charity tournament once, which he had won without ever touching the marks, because the marks had been a kind of insurance and the winning had come easy anyway.

This was different. This was not a marked deck. This was a man who *was* the marking. Who walked into the room already knowing.

He did the arithmetic the way other men daydream. A good private table turned over real money in a night. If a man could see every hand before it was dealt face up — not guess, not count, *see* — then there was no ceiling. There was only how much he was willing to win before someone noticed, and the answer to that, with Max's old trade of faces and disguises somewhere at the edge of his thinking, was that no one would ever notice the same man twice.

Three years was a long time. Henry had never spent three years on anything. He had never spent three weeks on anything.

But three years against an open vault for the rest of his life — that was not an onerous task. That was the best investment he had ever seen described.

---

He did not give the Moleskine back.

There was no one to give it back to. The godfather was dead; the estate was being cleared by a firm in Cirencester who would not know a yogi's medical report from a gas bill. Henry slid the blue notebook into the inside pocket of his jacket, where it sat against his ribs all the way down to breakfast, and he was aware the whole time that this was a small theft, and that small theft was exactly the sort of thing he was good at, and that the man whose life he had just read had spent three years learning not to be the sort of thing Henry was.

He drove back to London before the others were up. The rain followed him most of the way down the M40 and then gave out somewhere past the Chilterns, and by the time he reached the flat the sky had that washed, exhausted look it gets after a storm has worn itself down.

The flat was on the right floor of the right building. He went through it now with a new eye, the way you walk through rooms you are about to use for something other than what they were built for. The master suite was the obvious cell. It had a heavy door, a lock that worked, no view worth keeping the curtains open for. He told the woman who came in three mornings a week that he did not want to be disturbed in there, ever, under any circumstances, and she said *of course, Mr Sugar* in the tone of someone who had stopped being surprised by him years ago.

He needed a candle. He remembered that the way you remember a single instruction from a long document — *concentrate the conscious mind upon the object of the flame.* He thought briefly about lighting one of the tea-lights left in a drawer from some dinner, then dismissed it. A tea-light was a thing that drowned in its own wax in an hour. He went out and bought a pillar candle, white, fat as a fist, the kind that would burn for a week of evenings, and he set it on a low table in the middle of the bedroom floor and pulled a chair up to it and shut the door.

He had read the instructions so many times he could have recited them. Sit so the flame is level with the eyes. Breathe in a count, hold, breathe out a longer count. Let the body grow heavy. Look at the flame, and only the flame, until the flame is the only thing in the world, and then — and this was the part the report dwelt on — until you can hold the picture of the flame behind your closed eyes as clearly as you held it open.

He had, somewhere in the silt at the bottom of his attention, half a memory of a documentary he had not really watched, in which someone with a calm American voice had talked about exactly this — breath and heat and elevated feeling, ordinary people training the body to do things the body was supposed not to do, hours and weeks of it, the boring repetition that rewired you. He had thought, at the time, that it was the sort of thing sad people did instead of having a life. He had changed the channel.

He lit the candle with a kitchen match and sat down and began.

---

For a while he simply looked at the flame and felt faintly ridiculous, a grown man in good shoes sitting on the floor of his own bedroom staring at a candle while the city went about its evening below him.

Then he forgot to feel ridiculous, because looking at a flame is harder than it sounds. The flame would not stay one thing. It bent and recovered and threw off a thin black thread of smoke and pulled itself back to a point. His eyes watered and he blinked and lost it and found it again. He counted his breath the way the report said and lost count and started over.

He thought about the cards. He thought about the room in Geneva. He pulled himself back to the flame.

He thought about a sum of money, and then the shape of the sum, and then the look on the face of the man across the green baize when the night went a way the man could not understand. He pulled himself back to the flame.

His back began to ache between the shoulders. A dull warmth gathered behind his eyes, which he noted with a flicker of something — *was that it, was that the beginning of it* — before he understood it was only the strain of not blinking, the ordinary protest of a body asked to do an unaccustomed thing. He closed his eyes. There was a smear of orange where the flame had been, and it slid away when he tried to hold it, the way a name slides away the moment you reach for it, and then there was nothing.

Nothing behind his eyes. Nothing in the room but the small wet sound of the candle and the traffic far down on the street.

He sat with the nothing for what felt like a great while and was, when he looked, eleven minutes.

He had expected — he was honest enough, even now, to admit he had expected — that being a one-in-a-million sort of man, the kind of man things came easy to, it would simply begin. That he would sit down and feel the gift recognise him.

He blew the candle out, and the smoke went up in its thread, and he sat in the dark and felt the precise size of the task he had agreed to.
