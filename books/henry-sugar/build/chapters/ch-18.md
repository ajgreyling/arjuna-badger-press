# Twenty Years

What followed I can tell you only in the shape of an arc, because that is how Henry himself came to see it — not as nights but as a single long curve, the way you remember a journey by its bends and not its yards.

He began in London and went out from there. Within eighteen months he was a member of casinos in seven cities, and within five years there was scarcely a serious private room in the world where Henry Sugar's name, or one of the names he travelled under, did not appear on a quiet list. He never stayed. That was the first rule he made, sitting in the flat the morning after Curzon Street, working it out with a fountain pen on a sheet of hotel paper as though it were a problem in logistics, which it was. Win modestly. Win at several tables across several nights. Never break a house. Never be remembered as the man who could not lose.

The second rule was Max.

Max Engleman was a make-up artist, and the best Henry had ever seen, and Henry found him the way he found everything useful — by paying for it, generously, and then discovering that the man behind the price was someone he could not afford to lose. Max could change the architecture of a face. Not a costume — Henry could buy a wig anywhere — but the deep grammar of a man: the set of the jaw built out a centimetre with spirit gum and cotton, the cheeks aged ten years and then, a week later, in another city, made young again, foreign, ordinary, forgettable. He worked on Henry for two and sometimes three hours before a night out, and the man who walked into the casino was not the man who would walk in the following month two thousand miles away, and the surveillance rooms with their photographs and their good memories never put the faces together.

For thirteen years Max travelled with him. He was the only person on earth who knew, in full, what Henry did and how, and he never told a soul, and when I asked him at the end why he had kept the secret so completely he said only that he had loved him, and that there was nothing more to it than that. *I loved him*, Max said. *That is the whole of it. There is no other reason.*

The money went to Switzerland.

John Winston was a chartered accountant whose father had served Henry's father, and the arrangement Henry made with him was elegant in the way that only legitimate machinery can be. Henry won money that no court could prove he had stolen — he had touched nothing, marked nothing, palmed nothing; he had simply looked at the backs of cards and known their faces, and there is no statute against knowing. The winnings, once won, were Henry's to give. So he gave them. They flowed into accounts in Geneva and out again into a charitable trust that Winston administered, and from the trust came the orphanages.

There were twenty-one of them in the end, scattered across the world — and they were not cheques posted to existing institutions but places built from the ground, with Winston flying out to break the soil and Henry never coming at all, because Henry had decided that his name would touch none of it. He never visited a single one. He gave somewhere over a hundred and forty million pounds across those twenty years, and he saw, with his own eyes, none of what it bought. He thought this was the correct way to do it. I am inclined to agree with him.

I want to pause here, because there is a thing about those twenty years that the curve of the story hides, and it is this: Henry was happier than he had ever been, and he could not have told you why.

He thought, at first, that the sight had simply made him good at a thing, the way training makes a man good at any thing, and that the satisfaction was the satisfaction of mastery. But mastery palls. He had felt it pall already, that first hollow morning with the money on the dresser. What did not pall was the gift moving outward. He noticed, somewhere around the fifth year, that on the nights after he had cabled Winston a fresh sum he slept like a child, and that the cards came to him faster and easier than ever — as though the same faculty that reached out to read a hidden face grew stronger when it was pointed, even a little, away from himself.

He did not have a vocabulary for this and he distrusted the vocabularies on offer. He had read, by then, the things one reads — the field arguments, the lab papers, the careful contested claims that attention is not sealed inside the skull but reaches, somehow, and that a mind turned outward toward another's wellbeing is doing something measurable rather than merely sentimental. He had read that a day-old chick, imprinted on a small machine driven by a random generator, would draw that machine across its pen against the odds, simply by wanting it near — and that the wanting bent the randomness, and that nobody had a mechanism for it and the effect was small and the skeptics had their case and the thing would not go away. He thought, sometimes, about that chick. He thought: if a chick can lean on a random world by wanting, then perhaps a man who has spent twenty years routing every penny he wins toward children he will never meet is leaning on the world too, and the world is leaning back, and that is why he sleeps. He never said this aloud. It was not the sort of thing Henry Sugar said aloud. But he believed it, in the body, where he had learned to believe everything.

The disguises grew more elaborate. The sums grew larger. Winston's trust acquired the dull magnificence of a real institution, with auditors and a board that did not know whose money it was. Max aged honestly while making Henry age fraudulently. And the years went by in the strange compressed way that good years do, so that Henry woke one morning genuinely surprised to find that he was sixty-three and that the curve was nearly run.

He died in his flat, of an embolism, on a winter afternoon, alone, with no drama and no mirror and nothing seen through his own chest — quietly, the way the body sometimes simply stops. The cleaner found him. The doctor came and wrote the ordinary word on the ordinary form. There was no scandal because there had never been anything to find. A wealthy bachelor, no children, a clean estate, a charitable trust in Switzerland administered by a Mr Winston of Geneva.

And it was Mr Winston, when the news reached him, who sat for a long time at his desk above the lake, and then drew the telephone toward him, and lifted the receiver, and began — very deliberately, with the care of a man who has kept a secret for two decades and has decided, at last, that the time has come to give it away properly — to dial.
