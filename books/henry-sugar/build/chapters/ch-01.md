# The Sort of Person He Was

This is a true story. I have changed almost nothing, and what little I have changed I will tell you about plainly before the end, because the man at the centre of it deserves at least that much honesty from me. His name was not Henry Sugar. I have given him that name because the real one belongs to a family that still trades under it, and because a promise was made on his behalf to a Swiss accountant who, years later, would sit across a table from me with a folder in his lap and ask me to write the thing down. I agreed. This is the writing-down. The only untrue things in it are the names.

So. Henry Sugar.

He was forty-one years old, and he had never in his life done a single day's work for money. He did not need to. The money came to him the way weather comes — without effort, without gratitude, an inherited climate. There was a family office in a discreet building off Berkeley Square, and there were men in that office whose entire careers consisted of making certain that Henry's capital did quietly each year what capital does, so that Henry himself never had to think about it, or indeed about anything that resembled a task. He had a flat in Kensington with a view of a private garden he had a key to and never walked in. He had a driver. He had, in winter, a seasonal arrangement of weeks in the Caribbean and weeks in Monaco, always with the same cohort of people who were as rich as he was and as bored as he was and who would have been astonished, all of them, to learn that anyone considered this a problem.

He was greedy. I want to put that down at the start without softening it, because the softening, if it comes, must be earned later and not assumed now. He was greedy in the particular way of a man who already has more than he could spend in three lifetimes and who nevertheless feels, when he hears that another man has made a great deal of money quickly, a small hot pinch of resentment somewhere behind the sternum. He wanted more. Not for anything. Just more.

He was vain. He had his teeth capped — all of them, top and bottom, a uniform porcelain white that cost the price of a small car. He had a man in Mayfair who cut his hair and a different man who came to the flat to do his skin, and a tailor in a street I will not name who kept Henry's measurements in a leather book and altered nothing without a second fitting. There had been a mole, once, at the corner of his jaw; it was gone now, removed by a surgeon in Harley Street who specialised in such removals for men who could not bear a single unconsidered thing upon their own faces. Henry studied himself in mirrors the way other men study the financial pages — frequently, with attention, looking for the smallest movement.

And he was slothful, which in a poor man is a misfortune and in Henry was a comedy. He had a motto. He used it often, and his friends found it enormously funny, and he delivered it always with the same lazy half-smile, as though he were handing you a small gift. *It is better,* he would say, *to incur a mild rebuke than to perform an onerous task.* That was the whole of his philosophy. If a thing could be left undone with no worse consequence than someone being a little cross with you, then leaving it undone was, to Henry, self-evidently the correct course. He had never built anything. He had never made anything. He had never, so far as I can establish, finished anything that he had not been compelled to finish by the threat of genuine and immediate discomfort.

You will think I am being hard on him. I am being accurate. There is a difference, and the difference will matter to you later.

Now I should tell you about the cards, because the cards are where the rot showed, and because what happened to Henry later happened *through* the cards, so that they are, in a manner of speaking, the hinge of the whole thing.

Henry gambled. Not in the public rooms where tourists lose forty pounds and feel daring about it, but in private games — six or eight men at a table in a borrowed villa, the buy-ins large enough that the losing of them would have ruined a normal person and merely irritated everyone present. And Henry, who could afford to lose any amount, could not bear to lose at all. So he cheated.

I will give you one example, because one is enough to show you the cast of his mind.

There had been, the previous spring, a charity tournament in Monte Carlo — one of those evenings where the rich gather to be photographed donating to children they will never meet, and where the actual play is incidental to the champagne. Henry had arranged, through a man who arranged such things for a fee, for a particular deck to be in particular hands at a particular table. The deck was marked. Not crudely — there were no scratches a pit boss might catch — but read by anyone who knew the pattern, the way a fluent reader takes a whole word at a glance without sounding out the letters. And here is the detail I want you to keep, because it tells you everything. Henry did not touch the marks himself. He never had to. He had taught two other players the pattern and split the take with them afterward in cash, in a hotel room, with the curtains drawn — so that if anyone were ever caught, it would not be Henry, who had merely sat at the table looking bored and beautiful and winning, by what appeared to be luck, rather more than his share. He won eleven thousand euros that night from men who could spare it, at an event raising money for an orphanage, and he gave none of it back, and he did not think about the orphanage at all, not once, not even as a passing flicker. The orphanage will come round again, much later, in a way Henry could not have imagined. I mention it now only so that you will recognise it when it returns.

That, then, is the sort of person he was. Greedy. Vain. Idle. Dishonest in the small, careful, deniable ways available to a man with money and patience and no particular use for his own days. Forty-one years old and unmarried — and the *unmarried* matters too, though I cannot yet tell you why; there was a reason, and the reason has a name, and the name will be at the end of this with a sentence beside it that I find I cannot read aloud without stopping. But that is the end. We are at the beginning.

I have been honest with you about him because I have to be. If I make him better than he was, then the thing that happened to him means nothing, and the thing that happened to him is the only reason I am writing at all. A man who is already good and then becomes good is not a story. Henry was not good. Henry would have laughed at the very idea of becoming good, and offered you his motto, and signalled to a waiter.

And now that you have a rough idea of the sort of person Henry Sugar was, I can begin my story.
