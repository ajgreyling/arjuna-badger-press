# The Only Untrue Things

The telephone call came on a wet Thursday in November, a little after nine in the morning, while I was standing in my kitchen waiting for the kettle and thinking about nothing in particular.

A man's voice, precise, faintly accented, the consonants filed clean the way the Swiss file them.

"Is that the writer?" he said. "The one who writes the stories?"

I said it was.

"My name is John Winston," he said. "I am — I was — an accountant. I have something I should like you to write. It concerns a man who has died. A man I worked for, for the better part of thirty years, whose money I moved around the world and whose name almost nobody alive knows correctly." A pause. "I am told you do not flinch at things that sound impossible."

I told him I had spent a good portion of my working life sitting in rooms with people who had seen things they could not afterwards make anyone believe. I told him I had read declassified files about a government that paid men to shut their eyes and describe submarines under construction in the White Sea, and that one of those men, when he wrote his memoir, had spent half of it describing how *boring* it was, how full of doubt and false starts and sessions that produced nothing but garbage — and that this, the boredom, was the detail that had convinced me. Liars do not invent tedium.

There was a silence on the line that I have learned to recognise. It is the silence of a man deciding he has reached the right person.

"Then I will tell you," he said, "and you will come to Geneva, and I will give you the papers."

---

I went. There is a room above the lake, in an apartment that smells of cigars and cedarwood, where an old man keeps thirty years of accounts in folders the colour of dried blood. There is a ledger in there that should not exist — a record of more money than most countries' charities see in a generation, all of it won at the same crooked game, all of it given away to children who never learned the name of the man who paid for their beds.

Winston showed me photographs. Casinos in cities I will not list, because to list them would be to undo the work of a make-up artist named Max Engleman, who for thirteen years built a different face for his friend in a different hotel bathroom before every long night at the tables. Henry going in fat, going in bald, going in with a moustache and a limp and an American accent. Never twice the same. Never caught.

"He could have been caught a hundred times," Winston said. "He chose not to be. There is a difference, with him, between *could not* and *chose not*. You will find that difference everywhere in the papers."

I asked him the question I had been holding since the kettle.

"The cards," I said. "Did you ever see it. With your own eyes."

The old man looked at the lake for a while.

"Once," he said. "He sat across from me at this table and named the suit and the number of nine cards I had shuffled myself and laid face down. He did not touch them. He did not lean. He breathed slowly, the way the swimmers do before they go under, and a heat came into his face — I could see it, the colour rising — and then he simply *said* them, one after another, no faster than a man reading a list off a wall." Winston turned from the window. "When he was younger he could not have done it. He told me there were years, at the beginning, of nothing. Heat behind the eyes and an ache in the base of the spine and nothing. He said the body learned it before the mind agreed to believe it had. He said that was the part nobody who hasn't done it can understand — that the *knowing* arrives a half-second ahead of the looking, the way your skin sometimes prickles before the door opens, before there is anything there to make it prickle."

I did not lecture him on the meta-analyses, the eighty-some experiments pooled and re-pooled by careful people, the small stubborn finding that the pulse and the sweat-glands shift *before* the random thing happens and not after. I have learned not to do that to a man who has seen the thing itself. The graph is for people who haven't.

---

Before I left, Max Engleman came up to the apartment. A small, neat, grieving man with brushes still in his coat pocket out of habit.

He talked for an hour about faces. About how a good prosthetic ages a man twenty years and a bad one ages him five and looks like a costume. About how Henry never once complained, never once flinched at the spirit gum or the long sittings, how he would close his eyes in the chair and go somewhere — Max could always tell when he had gone — and come back calm.

At the door, Max took my hand in both of his.

"Write that he was kind to me," he said. "Write that he never married, and write that I do not think it was because he was cold. I worked beside that man for thirteen years. I loved him. You can put that in your book if you like. I don't mind who reads it."

---

So here is what I am obliged to tell you, now that you have read the whole of it.

The man's name was not Henry Sugar. The casino in which he first won eight hundred and fifty thousand pounds in a single sitting is not called what I have called it, and stands on a street I have moved by half a mile. John Winston is not John Winston, though he asked me, when it came to it, to keep the name I had been using, because he had grown fond of being a character. Max is Max. He asked for nothing to be changed. He said a man who has spent his life building false faces is entitled to keep his own.

Everything else is true. The boring years are true. The heat behind the eyes is true, and the ache at the base of the spine, and the months of nothing, and the card that turned over in under four seconds while a pillar candle burned in a locked room. The orphanages are true and you could walk into one tomorrow and lie down on a bed he paid for and never know it.

The only untrue things in this book are the names — and a single street, moved to protect a building.

And one thing more, which is not a lie but a confession: the document Henry found in that library, the dead man's report on a man who could see without his eyes — you have just read its grandchild. He read his way into a larger world. You read your way into his. I wrote it down because an old man above a lake asked me to, and because I, too, once read something that did not flinch when it should have, and could not afterwards make the world small again.

I don't recommend it. It ruins you for the advertised size of things.

But the kettle's boiled, and there it is, and now you know.
