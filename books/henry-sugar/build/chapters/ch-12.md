# Nothing, Nothing

The first thing he learned was that nothing happened.

He had expected — though he would not have admitted to expecting anything so naïve — that there would be a current, some early flicker, a faint warm hint that the thing was real and reachable and his. The dossier had promised it, after a fashion. The yogi had felt heat behind his eyes in the first weeks. Imhrat had described a softening at the edges of sight within a month.

Henry lit the pillar candle, sat with his spine against the cold radiator, fixed his gaze on the flame, and felt absolutely nothing for forty minutes. Then his knees ached and he stood up and poured a drink.

He tried again the next morning. Nothing. And the morning after that.

He had read somewhere — one of those late documentaries he half-watched, the kind that filled the small hours when sleep would not come — about laboratories where people sat in shielded rooms and tried to nudge the output of a machine that spat out random numbers. A bell curve that should have stayed symmetrical, leaning, fractionally, when a person concentrated. The presenter had said the effect was tiny. Vanishingly small. But it was there, the man said, across hundreds of thousands of trials, present in the aggregate where it could never be seen in a single run.

That was the part Henry held onto in the second week, when the flame told him nothing and his eyes streamed and the headache arrived. *In the aggregate.* No single sitting would announce itself. You could not look for the result in the result. You had to trust the protocol — the blind, dull, repeating protocol — and let the numbers gather behind your back where you weren't allowed to watch.

He did not believe it, exactly. But he behaved as though he did, which the dossier suggested was the same thing for practical purposes.

---

The headaches were the first real proof of anything at all, and they were proof of nothing but effort.

They came in from behind, a band tightening across the back of the skull, then climbing forward to settle over the right eye. By the third week they had taken up residence. He took pills. He learned which ones did nothing and which dulled the worst of it without putting him to sleep. He kept on sitting.

Imhrat's account had warned of this without dwelling on it — the yogi had told him to expect discomfort and to ignore it, that the discomfort was the body objecting to being asked to do something the culture had told it for forty years was impossible. Henry, reading that line again at two in the morning with a flannel pressed to his eye, found it almost funny. His body objected to everything. It objected to early rising and late drinking and being made to sit still on a hard floor. Why should this be different.

He counted breaths because the dossier said to count breaths. In, hold, out — a slow cadence, longer than felt natural, until the longness itself became the natural thing and the room seemed to settle around it. On the good days a kind of heat would gather low in his back, at the base of the spine, a dull warmth like sitting too near a fire. The dossier had a word for the place. Henry did not use the word. He noted the heat, the way you'd note a symptom, and he kept counting.

Nothing came of the heat. He logged it anyway, in a notebook he kept beside the candle — date, duration, sensations, result. The result column read *nil* for the better part of two months.

---

He had built the room like a cell. The master suite of the Kensington flat — the largest, with the absurd marble bathroom and the view he had bought it for and never looked at — he had stripped of everything that wasn't necessary. He pulled the bed apart and stacked it in the spare room. He took down the pictures. He drew the heavy curtains and left them drawn, so that the only light was the flame, and the flame was the only thing in the world worth looking at, which was the point.

His cleaner asked, carefully, whether everything was all right. He said he was redecorating. She did not believe him and she did not press, which was one of the things he paid her for.

He told no one else. There was no one else, really, to tell — his cohort were in the islands, then in the Alps, sending him photographs of themselves looking expensive in the snow, and he replied to none of them, and after a while they stopped. A man at the retreat back in the autumn had mentioned, laughing, a fellow who'd worked for the Americans as a *psychic spy*, who claimed he could draw buildings on the far side of the planet from a numbered envelope. Everyone at the table had laughed. Henry had laughed too. He thought of it now, in the dark with the candle, and did not laugh. The man — McMoneagle, was it? — had apparently written it all down: the doubt, the years of producing garbage, the fear that he was fooling himself, and then the day a drawing matched something it had no business matching. The shape of the learning, the man insisted, was the shape of any skill. Boredom. Failure. Failure. Then proof.

Henry had no envelopes and no buildings. He had a flame and a headache and a notebook that said *nil*. But he had the shape. He clung to the shape.

---

Somewhere in the eighth week the candle changed.

Not dramatically. He almost missed it. He had been sitting his usual forty minutes, eyes on the flame, breath slowed, when he became aware that he was no longer *looking at* the flame so much as the flame was simply present, the way a sound is present in a quiet room. The distinction was slippery and he could not hold it. But it returned the next day, and the day after, and he wrote *flame — present, not looked-at* in the notebook and did not know what it meant.

The dossier said this was the threshold. It said the eyes were learning to step aside.

In the ninth week he could keep the flame in mind with his eyes shut. For a few seconds at first — the after-image, anyone could do that, it meant nothing. But the after-image faded and the flame stayed, behind his eyes, in a place that had no name in the language he used for ordinary seeing. It guttered when his attention guttered. It steadied when he steadied. It was not memory and it was not the eye. It was something doing the work the eye usually did, without the eye.

He sat very still and did not let himself want it, because wanting it made it go.

---

At ten months he could outline an object across the room with his eyes shut.

It was a small thing. It was the largest thing that had ever happened to him.

He had placed an apple on the dresser, a few feet away, because the dossier suggested a simple shape with clean edges. He sat in his usual place, ran the breath down to its slow length, brought the flame up behind his eyes, and then — gently, the way you'd coax a wary animal — turned that inner attention toward the dresser. For a long while there was nothing, the familiar nothing, the patient nothing he had learned to wait inside.

Then there was an edge.

Not a picture. Not the apple in colour and gloss as the eye would have it. An edge — a roundness, a sense of where the thing stopped and the air began, a presence at the dresser that pressed faintly back against his attention the way a wall presses against an outstretched hand in the dark. He held it. It held. He opened his eyes to check, and the apple sat exactly where the edge had said it sat, and the breath went out of him in something that was almost a laugh and almost not.

He shut his eyes and found it again. The edge was still there. It was there because the apple was there — he tested this, badly, by moving the apple to the windowsill while keeping his eyes shut, and reaching for the edge, and finding it had moved. The attention had not gone to where he remembered the apple. It had gone to where the apple now was.

He sat in the dark for a long time after that, not practising, just sitting, the candle steady in front of him and the slow breath going in and out.

It was not cards. He understood that clearly, with no inflation. He could not have told you the suit of anything, could not have read a word, could not have done a tenth of what the yogi had done or a hundredth of what he himself would need to do at a table. There were years between here and there. The dossier said three. He believed it now, which he had not believed before.

But there was an edge at the windowsill, and his eyes were shut, and the edge was true.

He wrote one word in the notebook, in the column that had said *nil* for the better part of a year.

Then he blew out the candle and lit it again, and began.
