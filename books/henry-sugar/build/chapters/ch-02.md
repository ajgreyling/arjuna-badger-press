# The Blue Folder

The house was in the Cotswolds, all honey-coloured stone and mullioned windows, and it belonged to a fund called Arundell Macro, which used it three times a year to remind its principals that they were the sort of men who owned houses like this. The official word for the weekend was *offsite*. There would be a session on Sunday morning about thematic allocation and a man flown in from Zurich to talk about gold. The rest of it was meant to be leisure, which for these particular men meant drinking very good wine and discussing the price of things they already owned.

Henry was not in the fund. He was in it the way a barnacle is in a ship — attached, carried along, contributing nothing and consuming what passed. His money was in it, parked there by a family office in a structure he did not fully understand and had never tried to, and the parking earned him invitations to weekends like this. He came because the alternative was Monaco in the off-season, which was worse.

By Saturday afternoon the storm had closed over the valley like a lid. It had been forecast all week — a thing rolling up from the Atlantic — and it arrived on schedule and stayed, hammering the leaded windows and turning the gravel drive into a black river. The shooting was off. The walk to the village was off. Eleven men in cashmere stood about the great room watching rain come down the chimney and hiss on the fire, and the boredom in the place became a kind of pressure, a weather of its own.

Somebody produced cards.

This pleased Henry more than he let it show. He was very good with cards. He understood them the way a mechanic understands an engine, which is to say without sentiment, as a system of pressures and tells and probabilities that could be tilted if you knew where to lean. He had once won a charity tournament in Monte Carlo using a deck he had arranged to have marked, and the lovely thing — the thing that still gave him pleasure to remember — was that on the night he had not needed to read a single mark. The knowledge that he *could* had been enough to loosen him, and he had won clean, on nerve, and walked off with a sportsman's silver salver and the warm regard of everyone present.

But there were six seats at the table and seven men who wanted them. The host had decreed a private game — proper stakes, a closed table, none of the kitchen-supper canasta you might play with wives. Six in. Two principals, a hedge man down from Edinburgh, a fellow who did something with shipping, the Zurich gold man who turned out to play like a butcher, and the host himself.

"Sit out the first level, Henry," the host said, not unkindly, dealing already. "We'll rotate you in."

They would not rotate him in. Henry knew the geometry of these things. The chips would settle, the men would dig in, and by midnight nobody would be standing up from a winning seat to make room for the spare. He stood behind the chair for a polite ninety seconds, watched the gold man overplay a marginal hand on stubbornness alone, felt the old itch to lean into the table and tilt it — and then, because it was better to drift than to sulk, he went looking for somewhere to be that was not here.

The house was large and the storm had pushed everyone into two rooms, so the rest of it stood dark and cold and his. He went down a corridor of staring portraits and found, at the end of it, a library.

It was a real one — not the decorative kind with sets of Trollope bought by the yard. The house had belonged, before Arundell Macro bought it furnished and entire, to a man named Edward Voss, who had been Henry's godfather in the loose modern sense: a friend of his late father's, a presence at certain Christmases, a writer of generous and impersonal cheques. Voss had died eighteen months ago and the estate had been wound up and the house sold, books and all, and nobody at the fund had ever opened a single one of them. Henry hadn't known the library was here. He found it the way you find money in an old coat.

He put on a lamp. The rain ran down the black glass in sheets. The shelves went up past the reach of the light, and the books on them were the books of a man who had actually read — pale spines, foxed, annotated, the disordered library of a curious mind. Henry was not curious in that way. He read thrillers, and he read them on aeroplanes, and he forgot them on landing. But there is a thing that happens to even an incurious man alone in a dead man's library on a wild night, and it happened to Henry: a small, slow pull toward the shelves, as if the room expected something of him.

The folder was lying flat on top of a row of books, where a reader leaves a thing he means to come back to.

It was blue. A Moleskine, the good kind, soft-cornered with use, a black elastic gone slack around it. Tucked into the band was a single sheet of printer paper, folded once — Henry drew it out and unfolded it and found it was the cover sheet of a PDF, printed off and creased, the header showing a filename and a string of estate-archive metadata: *scanned, E. Voss collection, item 114, originated 1934.* Below that, reproduced from whatever yellowed typescript the scanner had eaten, was a title.

> *A Report on Certain Demonstrations by Imhrat Khan — the Man Who Could See Without His Eyes.*
> *Prepared at Bombay, 1934. J. R. Cartwright, M.B., B.S.*

Henry read it twice. The phrasing had the flat, careful weight of an old medical document, a man choosing words he could defend later in front of colleagues who would want to laugh at him. *See without his eyes.* Not *blindfold tricks.* Not *the celebrated illusionist.* A claim, made plainly, by a man with letters after his name, in a year when no one was writing for the internet.

Something stirred at the very back of Henry's attention — a documentary half-watched on a flight, a man with a beard saying the body knew things before the mind did, a graph of skin response that ticked upward a few seconds before a random picture appeared on a screen. He had not believed it. He had not disbelieved it either. He had ordered another drink and let it slide off him the way everything slid off him. There had been a podcast, too, someone calling someone else a psychic spy and the whole table at some dinner laughing. Filed and forgotten. Now the file opened a crack of its own accord.

He should, he knew, put the thing back and go and watch the gold man lose money on stubbornness. That was the Henry-shaped thing to do. But the storm had nailed the doors shut on the evening, and the library was warm, and there was a particular pleasure — he recognised it as the same pleasure he took in cards — in holding a closed system and feeling for where it could be opened.

He sat down in Edward Voss's reading chair, under Edward Voss's lamp, and opened the blue folder.

The Moleskine, it turned out, was a fair copy — Voss's own neat hand had transcribed the typescript, page after page, the better to study it, the way Henry's mother had once copied out recipes she loved. The first page was headed the same as the printout. Then, beneath, the report began.

*I am a surgeon,* it said, *and I do not believe in miracles. I write this account precisely because I cannot make it stop being true.*

The rain came on. Down the corridor, faintly, a man laughed and chips clicked into a pot Henry was not in. He did not hear them. He had gone somewhere else — down into the dead man's careful blue handwriting, into a hospital lounge in Bombay in the hot season of 1934, where a tired surgeon named Cartwright was about to be interrupted by a stranger with an impossible request.
