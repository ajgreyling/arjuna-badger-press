# Dough and Bandages

I had assisted at a hundred small deceptions in my years of practice — the malingerer with his borrowed limp, the hysteric whose paralysis dissolved the moment she believed herself unobserved — and I confess that I went into the matter expecting to expose one more. Imhrat Khan had told us he could see without his eyes. Very well. The only honest answer to such a claim is not argument but procedure. You do not debate a man who says he can see through dough. You seal his eyes with dough and watch what he does next.

We chose the dressing room behind the small theatre on Hornby Road, three of us — myself, Dr Marshall, and a younger registrar named Mitra whose hands did not shake and whose scepticism, unlike my own, had not yet curdled into curiosity. It was Marshall who insisted on the protocol, and I was glad of him. Left alone I might have grown sentimental. Marshall was a man who counted things.

I had read, that spring, an account of the tests run on a performer in London — the Council for Psychical Investigation, gentlemen of standing, who had taken a blindfolded man and packed his eye-sockets with dough and bound the whole of his head and still found him able to read. I had thought it credulous nonsense when I read it. Now I found myself reproducing their method almost gesture for gesture, and the irony was not lost on me. A sceptic borrows the sceptic's tools. It is only the result he refuses to borrow in advance.

We began with the dough.

Mitra had brought a quantity of soft wheat flour worked into a stiff paste, the kind a baker would reject as overhandled. Khan sat very still in the chair while I pressed it into the hollows of his eyes. I want to be exact, because exactness is the only thing of value I can offer. I filled each socket until the lids were buried and the dough stood proud of the bone. I worked it into the lashes. I asked him twice whether he could feel light, and twice he said no, and I believed him, because there was nothing of the showman in his face — only a patience that I have since come to think of as the patience of a man who has done a difficult thing so many times it has stopped being difficult.

Over the dough we laid pads of cotton wool. Over the cotton, surgical gauze. Marshall then took a roll of bandage and wound it round and round the upper half of the skull, ear to ear and crown to jaw, layer upon layer, until I counted seven turns and lost count, and Khan's head was a smooth white ovoid with a mouth. Mitra sealed the edges with a flour-and-water glue along the cheekbones, so that no slit remained between bandage and skin through which the most determined eye might have peered along the side of the nose. That trick — the glance down the cheek, the imperfect seal — is the oldest in the blindfold act, and we had closed it.

I stood back. The man before us could not have seen the sun.

"Now," said Khan, and his voice came muffled from inside the wrappings, perfectly calm, "give me something to read."

Mitra opened a book at random and held it before the bandaged face. Khan turned his head a fraction, as a man does who is bringing a thing into focus, though there was nothing in him left to focus. He read the page aloud. Not haltingly. Not in the manner of a memoriser reciting. He read it the way you or I read — with the small stumbles of live attention, pausing at a word, correcting himself once, his finger nowhere near the line. Marshall checked the text against the sound and looked at me, and I have never in my life seen a man's face do quite what Marshall's did then. He had counted everything. There was nothing left to count.

But the dressing room was not enough. A blindfold read in a small bright room proves only that the room is small. Khan knew this better than we did. It was he who proposed the street.

I will not pretend I was easy about it.

He asked for a bicycle, and we found him one, and outside on Hornby Road the morning traffic was the ordinary chaos of that city — bullock carts and motors and bicycles and the press of people who fill any road in Bombay until it seems no machine could move through them at speed without killing someone. The bandages were still on. The dough was still in his eyes. Mitra had checked the seal a second time and pronounced it whole.

Khan mounted the bicycle and rode.

I followed in a hired car, and I tell you I followed with my heart in my mouth, because a man with his eyes packed full of paste has no business in such a road, and I was certain I would see him under a cart inside fifty yards. He was not under a cart. He wove. He leaned the machine to pass a stationary lorry, straightened, swung wide of a knot of pedestrians who had not even seen him coming, rang the bell once at a dog and corrected for it, and rode on through that murderous current as smoothly as a man riding an empty lane at dawn. He did not graze a soul. He did not slow except where slowing was sense. When he stopped at last and put a foot down he was not even breathing hard, and I sat in the car with my hands cold on my knees and understood that I had crossed a line in myself from which there is no comfortable way back.

It was Marshall, that evening, who wanted the needle.

We had returned to the theatre, and he had thought of a thing the street could not give us — a task too fine for any residual peeping along the nose, a task that needed not the gross shape of a road but the threading of one small eye with one thin thread. He produced a sewing needle and a length of cotton from his own coat, where I suspect he had carried them all day for exactly this. We bandaged Khan again — fresh dough, fresh gauze, the same seven turns and more, Mitra glued and Marshall counted — and we put the needle in his fingers and the thread in his other hand, and we said nothing about how it ought to be done.

He held the needle up. He turned it once, finding the eye not by looking, for there was nothing to look with, but by some address of attention I have no language for. He wet the thread between his lips. And he passed it through the eye of the needle on the first attempt, cleanly, and drew it taut, and held it out to Marshall, who took it without a word.

You will understand that a man does not thread a needle by accident. The street might be argued — a blind man learns a known road, a sceptic might say. The needle could not be argued. The needle was new. The eye of it was a thing he had never seen and, in any honest sense of the word, did not see now.

I asked him then how it was done. I asked, I think, more humbly than I had ever asked anything of a patient, because the ordinary relation of doctor and demonstrating subject had reversed itself in my chest. He sat with the bandages still on, the dough still in his eyes, and considered the question for a while in the patient way I have described.

"I will tell you," he said at last. "But not standing here, and not in pieces. It took me a great many years, and I would not have you think it a trick learned in an afternoon. Come and eat with me tonight. I will tell you all of it — how I learned, and from whom, and what it cost. And when you have heard it, you may write it down, if you still wish to."

I said that I wished to very much.

"Then you must hear the whole," he said. "Or none of it will be true."

We unwound the bandages. Mitra peeled the dough from his eyes, and Khan blinked in the lamplight like any man returning to ordinary sight, and rubbed at the lids, and looked at the three of us with two perfectly good and perfectly normal eyes — the same eyes that had been buried under glue and gauze an hour before, and that had not, by any account I could give to a colleague or to myself, done one particle of the seeing.

He named the hour for dinner. I wrote it in my notebook, beneath the count of the bandages and the page he had read, and I found that my hand, which had been steady all through the dough and the needle, was no longer quite steady at all.
