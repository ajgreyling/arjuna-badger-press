# The Hand That Read Alice

It was three years and some months after Banerjee first chased him through the trees that Imhrat Khan sat down on the floor of a closed room in Calcutta and let a stranger bind his eyes.

The stranger was a doctor named Marshall, a friend of mine through the Bengal medical circuit, and it is from his notes, copied into the report I now hold, that the next part comes. Marshall had been a sceptic of the bullying kind — the kind who arrives already composing the paragraph in which he exposes you. He had brought his own materials. This is worth dwelling on, because it is the whole of the proof: a man who wished to catch Imhrat out was given every instrument with which to do it, and could not.

First the dough. Marshall worked it himself from flour and water until it was a stiff grey paste, and he pressed a thick wad of it over each closed eye, moulding it down hard into the orbit so that not the thinnest channel of light could survive at the side of the nose. Imhrat sat without complaint while it set. Then a thick bandage, wound twice around the head and knotted at the back. Then a second bandage. Marshall noted the layers in the margin the way one notes a dosage. Two of cloth, beneath it the sealed dough, beneath that the lids themselves.

This was not invention. Harry Price and the University of London people had done very nearly the same thing in 1935 to a man called Kuda Bux — dough kneaded into the sockets, the head wrapped, witnesses watching the wrapping — and Kuda Bux had read and walked and threaded a needle while sealed. Marshall knew the Price tests. He had read the published account. He was, in his own mind, repeating an experiment that had already failed once to find its trick, and he meant to find it this time.

"Now," said Marshall. "Read."

He had brought a book at random from his own shelf that morning, told no one which, and he held it open now before the bound face. It was a worn edition of *Alice's Adventures in Wonderland*, and he had opened it to a page somewhere in the middle, near the trial, where the cards stand up in court.

Imhrat did not lean forward. He did not turn his head toward the book in the small hunting way that a man does when he is trying to catch a gleam under a blindfold. He simply lifted his right hand, palm down, fingers loose, and held it perhaps a foot above the open page, the way you might hold a hand over a stove to learn whether it has been lit.

And then he began to read aloud.

Not quickly. The first words came slow and shaped, as though he were copying them out of the air with his mouth. Then a little quicker. He read of the King and the jury and the tarts that the Knave of Hearts was said to have stolen, and where Marshall's own eyes ran ahead down the page Imhrat's voice followed, line by line, no word skipped, no word guessed wrong. When the doctor turned the leaf, Imhrat waited the half-second it takes paper to settle and then went on into the new page without being told it had been turned.

Marshall's note here loses its hard edge. He writes that he moved the book. He lifted it silently and set it down two feet to the left, on the floorboards, without a word — and Imhrat's hand drifted after it through the dark like a compass needle finding north, and the reading did not stop.

What I want to be careful about — and Cartwright is careful about it too, later, in the part of the report that made me steal the whole thing — is the *hand*. Imhrat did not say he saw the page with his face. He said it later, at the dinner, in his own words: that the eyes are only one instrument of the sense of sight, and not even the best of them. He said the whole skin learns to look, if it is taught patiently enough, and that the palm of the hand, being the most awake part of the skin, learns soonest. He held his hand over the words because the words came to him as the heat of a flame comes to the hand — as a thing felt, sorted, and only then named.

Marshall did not believe in this. Marshall wrote down what happened anyway, because what happened was a man with two bandages and a finger's depth of dried dough sealed over both eyes reading a book the man himself had chosen that morning, in a sealed room, with his hand.

There is a school of thought — I will only put it in the frame, not in Imhrat's room — that the skin does prickle to things before the eye confirms them. There have been experiments, since, where a body's own electrics shift a few seconds *before* a screen shows it a picture it cannot have predicted: the gut going tight ahead of the shock. Mossbridge and Radin and Utts gathered up a heap of these and counted them and found the effect, small and stubborn, more often than chance allowed. I mention it only because Marshall, who would have hated the comparison, kept reaching in his notes for the same idea without a name for it. *He answered before he could have known,* he wrote once, and then crossed it out, because it sounded like the thing he had come to disprove.

Then came the cards.

It was Marshall's own deck, and he shuffled it himself, hard, three times, the way a man shuffles when he wants the order to be no one's. He fanned it face down on the floor in front of Imhrat. He did not announce the test. He simply set the deck down and waited to see whether the man would understand.

Imhrat passed his open hand over the fanned backs, slow, an inch above them, the way he had passed it over Alice. And he named them.

Not all at once. He took the first card — the doctor's note specifies he never turned it over, never lifted it, only laid two fingers lightly upon its painted back — and he said, *the seven of clubs.* Marshall turned it. The seven of clubs. Another. *The queen of diamonds.* The queen of diamonds. Another, and another, down the fanned row, and where Marshall expected the long stutter of a guesser hedging his odds there was only the same shaped slowness as the reading, the words arriving with the small delay of a thing felt through the back of a card and then spoken — heat sorted into a name.

I will not pretend the doctor's hand was steady when he wrote the column of results. It was not. He recorded the misses too, in honesty — there were two in the first deck, both face cards, both corrected by Imhrat himself a moment later as though the picture had come into focus a beat late. But a man does not name fifty pasteboards through their backs by accident, and Marshall, who had built the whole afternoon to catch him, sat on the floor of his own sealed room and could not say how it was done.

This is the moment the report stops being a curiosity and becomes a thing somebody might want. I felt it when I read it — the lift, the cold opening of a door — and I think Cartwright felt it too, because his own marginal hand grows careless here, the letters running together. *If this can be taught,* Cartwright wrote, *and Khan says it can be taught, then we must — we must understand it, we must record the method, we must give it to the men who have lost their eyes and to the surgeons who would restore them.* The publish-fever. The same fever the doctors always catch when the miracle is sitting in the room and breathing: the certainty that it belongs now to medicine, to the journals, to the good of mankind, and that all that remains is to write it down properly and present it.

They never did get to give it to the blind. But that comes later, and I am getting ahead of the page.

What Cartwright saw, and set down, and could not stop seeing, was a man reading a sealed book with his hand and naming a stranger's shuffled deck through the painted backs of the cards. He saw it as a door to surgery, to charity, to a paper that would carry his name into the long memory of his profession.

Henry Sugar, sitting on the floor of a dead man's library with the blue folder open across his knees, saw it differently.

He read the column of named cards twice. *The seven of clubs. The queen of diamonds.* He read the doctor's shaking hand, the line crossed out, the fanned deck laid face down on a Calcutta floor in 1934, a hand passing an inch above the backs.

And he lifted his eyes from the page.

The rain was still going at the window. Somewhere below him, faint through the floor, the others were still at their game — six men around a table, cards face down, money in the middle, the soft clatter of chips that meant the night had a long way left to run.

Henry looked at the door. Then he looked back down at the page, at the place where it said the thing could be taught, and at the figure beside it: three years.

He did not think about the blind. He did not think about surgery, or charity, or the long memory of any profession. He thought about a table downstairs, and the painted backs of cards, and how very much could be made to lie behind them.

He picked the folder up off his knees and held it, the way a man holds a thing he has already decided to keep.
