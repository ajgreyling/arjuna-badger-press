# The Fire They Said Would Burn

In the third year my teacher sent me away.

He did this without warning, the way he did most things. One morning, before the light had properly arrived, he told me there was a fire-walking ceremony four days' walk to the south, in a town whose name I will not write, and that I should attend it. He did not say why. He said only that a man who wished to understand his own body should occasionally watch what other bodies were willing to do.

I asked whether he would come. He said the question did not deserve an answer, and went back to his breathing.

So I walked.

The fire-walking took place in the forecourt of a temple, on the night of a festival whose particulars I have forgotten. A pit had been dug perhaps four paces long and two wide, and into it, since the previous afternoon, men had been feeding wood and charcoal. By the time I arrived the wood was gone and what remained was a long bed of embers — not flame, but worse than flame, a low orange continent that breathed. You could feel its heat from twenty feet. It pressed against the face like a hand.

I have since read that men with proper instruments have measured such beds. The surface of glowing coals in a fire-walk has been recorded at well over four hundred degrees on the centigrade scale — hot enough to char dry timber on contact, hot enough that the priests raking it level wore cloth wound thick around their hands and still stepped back, wincing, every few seconds. A European gentleman with a notebook had measured exactly this, in London, years before I was born, at a test arranged by a society of careful sceptics; they had bandaged and examined and weighed every doubt, and the coals had still read above four hundred degrees, and the man who walked them had still come away with feet that the doctors could find nothing wrong with. I did not know that account then. I only knew the heat against my face, and the smell, which was the smell of something that wants to consume you.

The priests walked first. They walked quickly, four short strides, their faces fixed on a point above the far end of the pit, and they came off the other side and were embraced by their families and showed their soles to anyone who asked, and the soles were unmarked. A man in the crowd called out that it was a trick of the wet — that they had soaked their feet in something. The priest nearest him simply walked back across the coals to where the heckler stood, and held out his feet, and said: *Then come and soak yours, and walk.*

The heckler did not.

I watched perhaps a dozen men cross. Some hurried. Some, the older ones, walked as though crossing a courtyard, unhurried, almost bored, and these interested me most, because boredom is not a thing the body counterfeits. You cannot pretend to be calm in front of a fire. The fire knows. Your heart knows. I had spent two years learning to read my own heart, and I could read theirs in the set of their shoulders, and theirs were quiet.

Then the priest who had answered the heckler turned to the crowd and asked, as the priests always ask, whether any visitor wished to cross.

I should tell you that everything in my training to that point had been interior. Breath counted to a cadence until the count fell away. A flame held in the eye until the eye stopped reporting flame and reported only the small steady pressure of attention itself. Heat that began behind the eyes and travelled, over the boring weeks, down the spine, gathering at the base of it like water finding a low place. My teacher called these things by the old names, the seven centres, but he forbade me to think of them as pictures or wheels or coloured lights. *They are sensations,* he said, *and the moment you make them into a diagram you have stopped feeling them and started decorating them.* So I knew them only as I knew hunger: as a place in the body that asked for something.

None of this had yet been tested against the world. A man can convince himself of a great deal in a quiet room. The fire is not a quiet room.

I raised my hand.

The priest looked at me a moment — at my soft hands, my city sandals already off and held in one fist — and then he stood aside, which is its own kind of answer. He did not warn me. He did not encourage me. He had measured something in my face and decided not to interfere with it.

I will not pretend I felt nothing. My heart had begun to climb the instant I lifted my hand, and I let it climb, and then I did the thing I had been practising for two years without knowing it was practice for this: I gathered the breath, slow, and on the long out-breath I let the count fall away, and I put my eye on the point above the far end of the pit, the way the priests had, and I did not look at the coals at all.

The heat came up through the soles of my feet at the first step. I felt it — let no one tell you the body feels nothing; it felt everything, the texture of the embers shifting under my weight, the small live grit of them — but the heat arrived as information and not as alarm. There is a difference, and the difference is the whole of it. The body that walks the fire is not a body that has stopped feeling. It is a body whose centre has decided, before the foot is raised, that it will not flinch.

Four strides. Five. The far edge. Cool stone.

A boy poured water over my feet from a brass vessel and the water hissed, and I sat, and I turned my own soles up to look at them as the priests had, expecting — I do not know what I expected. Blisters. Black. The honest punishment of physics.

There was nothing. A faint warmth, already fading. The skin was the skin I had been born with.

I want to be careful here, because this is the smallest of the things I learned to do, and I have seen men make a whole religion out of less. The fire-walk is not the great matter. Better men than I have explained that bare skin crossing embers quickly does not have time to take in the heat the way a notebook expects it to, that the coal is a poor conductor, that the contact is brief — and perhaps all of that is true, and perhaps it accounts for the priests entirely, and I will not argue with the man who measured four hundred degrees and came away with a clean explanation. He may keep his explanation. It costs me nothing.

What it could not explain, sitting there with my cooling feet in my hands, was the thing that had happened *inside* me on the first step — the click of a switch I had been told existed and had never been permitted to believe in. For two years I had practised in a room, on faith, against nothing. Now I had practised against the fire, which lies to no one, and the fire had let me pass.

It was not that the fire had not burned me. It was that I had not been afraid, and the not-being-afraid had been a deliberate thing, made in the body, on the out-breath, the way a man makes a fist.

I sat a long time in the cool of the temple wall while the festival went on around me. The priests did not congratulate me. One of them, the old one who walked as though bored, glanced at me once and gave a very small nod, the nod of a tradesman recognising another tradesman's work, and that was all.

The next morning I began the four days' walk back. I did not hurry. I had something to carry now that I had not carried south, and I did not want to spill it.

My teacher was sitting where I had left him, as though he had not moved in eight days, which I have come to believe was very nearly the case.

He did not ask whether I had walked the fire. He could see that I had — not on my feet, which were unmarked, but somewhere in how I sat down in front of him.

"Now," he said, "you will believe me about the candle."

And he was right. I did.
