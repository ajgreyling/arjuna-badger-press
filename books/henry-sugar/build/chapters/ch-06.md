# All Trickery

My name is Imhrat Khan, and I was born in Akhnur, in the state of Kashmir, in the year 1905. I tell you this not because it matters where a man begins, but because the doctor who is writing down my words has asked me to leave nothing out, and I have promised him that I will not.

I came to the magic by way of hunger. My father died when I was thirteen, and there was no money in the house, and a man called Professor Moor came through our district with a travelling show. He needed a boy with quick hands and a face that would not give the game away. He looked at me for a long time, the way a butcher looks at a carcass to see what cuts it will yield, and then he said I would do.

What I learned in the three years I worked for Professor Moor was that there is no magic in magic. There is only the audience, and the way an audience wishes to be fooled. Every trick I was taught was a lie built carefully, the way a mason builds a wall — one small deception laid upon another until the whole thing stood up and looked like a marvel.

I will tell you how it is done, because I want you to understand the disgust that came over me.

A man appears to read a sealed message. There is no reading. The envelope is switched, or the writing is taken by a strip of carbon under the table, or the assistant — which was me — has crept behind the curtain and read the thing over the spectator's shoulder while he wrote it, and passed the words forward in a code of coughs. A woman appears to float in the air above the stage. She does not float. She lies upon a steel arm painted the colour of the black velvet behind her, and the magician passes a hoop along her body in a curve that the eye cannot follow, so that the eye reports *no support* when in truth the support is everywhere. A man swallows fire. He does not swallow it. He has wetted his mouth, and he tips his head, and the flame goes out against his own breath before it can do him harm.

All of it. Every last gesture of it. Trickery.

I want to be very plain about this, because it is the hinge on which my whole life turned. The people who came to Professor Moor's tent came because they wished to see the laws of the world broken. They paid their few annas to feel, for one hour, that the cage of ordinary cause and effect had a door in it. And we gave them nothing of the kind. We gave them a wall of lies dressed up as a door. They went home satisfied and cheated, and the worst of it was that they did not know they had been cheated, and so they never asked the only question worth asking, which is: *if all of this is false, is there anything that is true?*

For a long time I did not ask it either. I learned my craft. My hands became very good. By the time I was sixteen I could palm a coin so that a man might shake my open hand and not find it, and I could make a deck of cards do almost anything I told it to. I was proud of these things. Pride is the last refuge of a man who suspects he is doing something worthless.

Then one night, in a town whose name I have forgotten, a thing happened that broke the pride in me.

We had a man come to the show who was clearly poor, poorer than the rest, and he had brought with him a child who was sick. The child's eyes were wrong — clouded, the way a fish's eye clouds after a day in the market. The man had heard, somewhere on the road, that Professor Moor could do wonders. And he stood at the edge of the crowd through the whole performance, and at the end he came forward and held the child up and asked, in a low voice that I have never been able to forget, whether the professor could make the boy see.

And Professor Moor, who was a kind enough man in his way, told him the truth. He said: *I am sorry, friend. What I do is not real. I cannot help your son.*

The man's face. I will not try to describe the man's face. But I understood, watching it, that I had spent three years of my life manufacturing exactly the hope that had brought this man across half a province with a blind child in his arms — and that I had nothing at the bottom of it to give him. We were sellers of an empty box, painted to look full.

I left Professor Moor not long after that. And I made a vow, which I have kept, that I would not perform another trick as long as I lived unless behind the trick there stood a thing that was *true* — a real power, not a lie laid on a lie.

But where does a man go to find a real power?

I will be honest with you, doctor, because you have asked me to be. My first reasons were not pure. I did not set out to see without my eyes in order to give sight to blind children. I set out because I had seen how a crowd loves a marvel, and I thought that if I possessed a marvel that was *genuine* — one that no rival could expose, because there was nothing to expose — then I would be the greatest performer in all of India. I would be rich. I would be famous from Peshawar to Madras. The vanity that had made me proud of my palming now made me hungry for something larger to be proud of. I do not hide this from you. A man does not always begin a great road for a great reason. Sometimes the reason is small and the road is long enough to enlarge him.

So I went looking for the yogis.

This was harder than I had imagined, and for a reason that surprised me. I had thought a true power, if it existed, would announce itself, would set up its tent beside ours and draw the larger crowd. It does not. I learned, in the years of my searching, that the genuine men — the ones who could in truth do the things that Professor Moor only counterfeited — were forbidden by their own discipline to show those things in public. It is a law among them, as strict as any law in any temple. A power gained through years of self-mastery is not to be spent for money, or for the pleasure of a gaping crowd, or for the vanity of the man who has it. The moment it is shown for gain, it is held to corrupt the man who shows it. The very thing I wanted it *for* was the thing I was forbidden to do with it.

Do you see the trap I had walked into? I had fled the false magic because it was false, and gone in search of the true — and the true would not perform. The men who could not really do it, did it nightly under canvas. The men who really could, would not be seen.

For a while this nearly broke me. I went from town to town and from holy man to holy man, and most of them were the same painted boxes I had left behind — fakirs with hidden wires, with concealed pockets, with sleights I knew better than they did, because I had been a professional and they were amateurs. I unmasked a dozen of them in my heart without saying a word, and each unmasking made the hunger in me sharper, not duller. Because every false one I found was a proof that somewhere, by the law of the thing, there had to be a true one. A counterfeit is a confession that real coin exists.

I came at last to a decision, and I made it standing on a road outside Hardwar with my whole life in a cloth bag over my shoulder. I said to myself: *Imhrat, you will find a man who can do this thing in truth, and you will make him teach you, or you will give it up altogether and go back to coins and cards and be honest about being a liar.*

There would be no more middle ground. No more travelling from fakir to fakir collecting disappointments. A real teacher, or nothing.

I did not know, when I made that vow on the road, that I was three days' walk from a man sitting under a tree by the river — and that what I would see him do would settle the question forever.
