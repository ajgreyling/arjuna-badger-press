# CANON — King Lion and the Birds Who Stole the Sky

- **Book id:** how-king-lion
- **File slug:** kinglion
- **Shelf:** Children's Library — read-aloud picture books, ages 4–8
- **Tradition:** original African pourquoi ("how it came to be") tale. The author's own composed
  story; the text and phrasing are original, the folk-motif (rooted, sky-gazing, nectar-weeping
  birds = the bird-of-paradise / strelitzia) is the heritage being honoured.
- **Sister tale (CROSS-REFERENCE):** `bird-of-paradise-flower` — the companion pourquoi about the
  same flower, read from the flower's side. *King Lion* is the same story told from the sky's side:
  why the proud birds can no longer reach it. Shelve the two together.

## Spine (one sentence)
When proud, greedy birds steal the farmers' maize and the frightened villagers begin to blame and
kill every animal for it, King Lion judges the true thieves — they will never fly again, and stand
rooted forever as flowers that lift their bright heads to the sky they lost and weep.

## The story under the story
Pride and greed don't only cost *you*. When you take what isn't yours, the innocent around you pay
first — the animals who never touched the maize are the ones the fear comes for. And consequence,
even *just* consequence, can carry real sorrow: the birds are not monsters, and their punishment is
genuinely sad. We hold this in the house's all-sides spirit — indict the pride and the fear, not the
creatures. The child learns: say sorry before the cost lands on someone else; and that being right
about a punishment does not make it happy.

## Cast
- **King Lion** — old, slow-eyed, fair. Not a roaring tyrant; a tired, just king who would rather
  warn than punish. His sorrow at the end is the heart of the book.
- **The Birds** — bright, loud, beautiful, proud. Orange-crowned, blue-throated, sharp-beaked. Not
  villains: dazzling and thoughtless, sure the sky is theirs and the maize is theirs too. Their
  beauty becomes the flower's beauty.
- **The Farmers / Villagers** — working people of the veld; not caricatures. Tired, afraid, growing
  angry as the harvest vanishes. Their fear is the dangerous force, and it is honest fear.
- **The Animals** — duiker, hare, tortoise, the small ones — innocent, hunted by mistake. The
  collateral. Seen but not blamed.
- **{{CHILD}}** — the watching human child-hero, small in the tall grass, who sees the whole
  judgement and carries the lesson home. Named with the literal token everywhere.

## The refrain (a child can say it back)
> *Look up, little one, look up at the sky —*
> *and remember the birds who forgot how to fly.*

Recurs across the spreads; lands hardest at the rooting and at the end. Early spreads may use it as a
gentle invitation (looking up at birds in flight); the final spreads turn it (looking up and never
reaching). Same words, turned by the story.

## Spread plan (13 spreads)
1. Wide veld at dawn; {{CHILD}} in the maize; birds wheeling overhead — refrain as invitation.
2. The proud birds described — bright, loud, sure the sky belongs to them.
3. The thieving begins — birds tearing into the golden maize, day after day.
4. Empty fields; hungry, frightened farmers; the harvest gone.
5. The fear turns to blame — villagers begin to fear and hunt *every* animal.
6. The innocent suffer — duiker and hare and tortoise hiding, hunted for a crime not theirs.
7. King Lion hears of it; summons every bird to the great old tree.
8. The reprimand — "Stop, or you bring ruin on all of us." A warning, not yet a sentence.
9. The birds, too proud, laugh and steal again the very next morning.
10. The judgement — King Lion, sorrowful, passes the sentence: never fly again.
11. The rooting — their feet sink into the ground; wings stiffen; they cannot rise.
12. The reveal — rooted birds lift bright heads to the sky, weeping drops of nectar: they ARE the
    bird-of-paradise flowers. {{CHILD}} watching.
13. Close — {{CHILD}} carries it home; the turned refrain; "— THE END —".

## Art direction
Warm painterly storybook tradition — soft gouache/watercolour light, golden-hour African veld, big
skies. Dignified and specific: open grassland, maize fields, marula/acacia, kraal fences and round
homes in the distance — never generic "jungle", never caricature. The birds rendered so their
crests and throats clearly become the orange-and-blue of the strelitzia bloom at the reveal. King
Lion warm and weighty, kind-eyed. {{CHILD}} small, brown-skinned, barefoot, real and tender. Landscape
3:2, full-bleed, no text in image. Sorrow handled gently — the rooting is sad, not frightening; no
gore in the "hunting" spreads (fear and hiding, shown softly).

## Heritage / source note
Honours the southern-African pourquoi tradition that explains the natural world through the choices
of animals — here the *Strelitzia reginae* (crane flower / bird-of-paradise), whose bloom truly does
look like a bright bird with a crested head, lifting from a beak-shaped sheath, beaded with nectar.
The flower's resemblance to a poised, grounded bird is the real-world seed of this invented tale. No
sacred or initiation matter is used; the magic is the gentle pourquoi turn of bird-into-flower.
Companion to `bird-of-paradise-flower`.
