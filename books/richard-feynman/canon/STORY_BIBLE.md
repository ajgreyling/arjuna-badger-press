# *THE MAN WHO DREW THE UNIVERSE* — Story Bible

> **A standalone narrative biography.** An **original narrative account of a documented life:**
> Richard Phillips Feynman (1918-1988), physicist — Far Rockaway, MIT, Princeton, the Manhattan
> Project at Los Alamos, Cornell, Caltech, the 1965 Nobel Prize in Physics (shared with Julian
> Schwinger and Sin-Itiro Tomonaga), the 1986 Rogers Commission on the *Challenger* disaster, and
> the popular books, lectures, and persona that followed. Not part of any other shelf in this
> library — its own world, its own canon. Shares only the house voice: grounded, sensory, unafraid
> of technical material handled in plain language, and a hard commitment to telling a real life
> straight rather than varnishing it.

> **Tone (binding, the author's charge):** *curiosity as the load-bearing virtue; the showmanship
> never allowed to stand in for the whole man; the cost of the life told as honestly as the wonder
> of it.* This is not a hagiography and not a takedown. Arline's death, the postwar depression, two
> marriages that did not last, and the vanity that came with fame are on the page. So is the real
> thing underneath all of it: a mind that would not stop asking how things actually work.

## 0. The true story (DOCUMENTED — the bedrock)

Verified, real, and the frame every chapter is built inside:

- **Born** 11 May 1918, Far Rockaway, Queens, New York, to Melville Feynman (a uniform salesman
  who taught his son to distrust naming things without understanding them) and Lucille Phillips
  Feynman. Younger sister **Joan Feynman**, later herself a solar physicist. An infant brother,
  Henry Phillips, died young.
- **MIT** (undergraduate, physics, 1935-1939), then **Princeton** (PhD 1942, under **John
  Archibald Wheeler**) — the Wheeler-Feynman absorber theory and the early path-integral thinking
  come out of this period.
- **Arline Greenbaum**, his childhood sweetheart from Far Rockaway, diagnosed with tuberculosis
  before their marriage. They married in **1942** anyway, on the way to Los Alamos, in a roadside
  ceremony with witnesses signed off a bus. She lived in a sanatorium near the project. She died
  **16 June 1945**, weeks before the Trinity test. Feynman was at her bedside.
- **The Manhattan Project**, Los Alamos, 1943-1945: youngest group leader in the Theoretical
  Division under **Hans Bethe**; safecracking as a hobby and running joke; documented friendships
  with Bethe, Oppenheimer, Bohr (Feynman was one of the few junior physicists who could argue
  with Bohr, because he wasn't intimidated enough to just agree); drumming; watched the **Trinity**
  test through a truck windshield instead of the issued dark glass, reasoning (correctly) that the
  glass filtered the dangerous light and the windshield would too.
- **Postwar collapse**: real, documented burnout and a period Feynman later described as feeling
  he had lost the ability to do physics. Cornell (1945-1950), the famous **wobbling-plate
  incident** in the Cornell cafeteria — watching a thrown plate wobble and spin, working out the
  relation between the two out of pure play, and tracing the thread from that back into serious
  work on quantum electrodynamics.
- **QED and the diagrams**: the **Feynman diagram**, developed roughly 1948, presented at the
  **Shelter Island (1947) and Pocono (1948) conferences**; the postwar reformulation of quantum
  electrodynamics developed independently and in different mathematical language by Feynman,
  **Julian Schwinger**, and **Sin-Itiro Tomonaga**, later shown equivalent by **Freeman Dyson**.
- **Caltech**, from 1950 until his death — his professional home for the rest of his life.
  Documented as the place he most wanted to be for the freedom and the students, having turned
  down more prestigious postings.
- **Second marriage**, to Mary Louise Bell, 1952-1956, brief and unhappy, ending in divorce.
- **Third marriage**, to **Gweneth Howarth**, 1960 until his death; documented as the happy,
  steadying one. Children Carl (adopted, b. 1962) and Michelle (adopted, b. 1968).
- **The Feynman Lectures on Physics** (with Robert Leighton and Matthew Sands), based on his
  1961-1963 Caltech introductory lectures — became a landmark physics text, famously more
  treasured by working physicists than by the undergraduates who first sat through it.
- **1965 Nobel Prize in Physics**, shared with Schwinger and Tomonaga, "for their fundamental
  work in quantum electrodynamics." Documented ambivalence about the publicity that came with it.
- **The showman years, documented and real**: bongo drums; painting under the pseudonym "Ofey";
  safecracking at Los Alamos and later; the 1981-82 BBC *Horizon* interview ("The Pleasure of
  Finding Things Out"); the pursuit (with Ralph Leighton) of a diplomatic path to Tuva, never
  completed before his death; *Surely You're Joking, Mr. Feynman!* (1985) and *What Do You Care
  What Other People Think?* (1988), both compiled by Ralph Leighton from taped conversations.
- **The parton model** (1969) and early theoretical seeding of ideas later foundational to
  quantum computing, from his 1981-82 talks on simulating physics with computers; the 1959
  Caltech talk **"There's Plenty of Room at the Bottom,"** later claimed as a founding document
  of nanotechnology.
- **The Rogers Commission, 1986**: appointed to investigate the *Challenger* disaster; the
  documented O-ring/ice-water demonstration at a televised hearing; his dissenting personal
  appendix to the official report, printed after resistance from the commission's chair.
- **Illness and death**: two rare forms of cancer (liposarcoma, first diagnosed and operated on
  in 1978; further operations through the 1980s); died **15 February 1988**, in Los Angeles.
  Documented near-final words, reported by those present, expressing frustration at how boring
  dying was — reported in more than one direct account, treated here with care rather than as a
  punchline.

## 1. What this book invents

Scene connective tissue, staged dialogue, and interior monologue where the documented record
gives the fact but not the sentence — kept **true to the documented frame**, never contradicting
it, and never borrowing a sentence from Feynman's own books or from any single biography. Where
two documented accounts of the same event differ in detail, the version used is noted as an
authorial choice, not asserted as the only truth. Technical explanations of the physics are the
author's own plain-language versions, not textbook reproductions.

## 2. POV and register

Close-third on Feynman throughout, with brief close-third excursions into Arline's and Gweneth's
points of view where the record supports it (letters, later interviews with those who knew them).
One register: warm, exact, occasionally very funny because the man was, but never letting the
funny carry a scene that is actually about grief, fear, or cost.

## 3. Guardrails

No romanticising the bomb. No flattening Arline into a saintly deathbed prop — she is written as
a person with her own wit and her own fear. No treating the two failed-then-happy marriages as a
punchline. The Challenger chapter does not turn NASA management into cartoon villains; the
documented institutional pressure is treated as systemic, not as a single person's malice.
