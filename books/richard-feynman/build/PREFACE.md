# A Note on the True Story

*The man in this book was real. So was the desk, the drum, the safe, the diagrams, and the woman
he married before he had any right to expect she'd live to see him try.*

---

Richard Phillips Feynman was born in Far Rockaway, Queens, on 11 May 1918, and died in Los
Angeles on 15 February 1988. In between he became, by wide agreement among the people qualified to
judge such things, one of the great physicists of the twentieth century — and, almost
independently of that, one of its more magnetic personalities, a fact he found alternately useful
and exhausting.

He helped build the atomic bomb at Los Alamos while his young wife lay dying of tuberculosis in a
sanatorium down the road, and he sat with her when she died. He came out of the war a wreck and
spent years thinking he had lost the thing that made him a physicist, until a thrown cafeteria
plate, wobbling on its way across a room, put him back in the game — not because the plate
mattered, but because he let himself be curious about it anyway, with no one grading the answer.
The play led back to serious work: a new way of doing quantum electrodynamics, built out of little
pictures that a technician could learn to draw and use without first mastering the abstract
mathematics underneath them. Those pictures now carry his name. They are still how physicists
think about how particles interact, in classrooms and papers written decades after he stopped
being alive to argue about them.

He shared a Nobel Prize for that work in 1965, and treated the ceremony with the same suspicion he
treated most honors — not because he didn't want to be right, but because being right had never
needed a medal to be true. He taught undergraduates at Caltech for the rest of his career, in
lectures so good and so strange that they were compiled into a textbook working physicists still
keep on their shelves, decades after the freshmen who first heard them mostly moved on to easier
careers. He cracked safes at Los Alamos as a running joke on wartime security. He played the
bongos, badly and joyfully. He painted under a pseudonym he thought no one would ever connect to
him. In 1986 he sat on the commission investigating the *Challenger* disaster and, when he was
tired of being managed by people more interested in the report's politics than in the report's
truth, dropped a piece of rubber O-ring into a glass of ice water on live television and let
everyone watch it lose its resilience in real time.

He was, by his own insistence and by the testimony of nearly everyone who knew him, driven by one
thing above all others: the pleasure of finding things out. This book is an attempt to tell that
life straight — the wonder of it and the cost of it both, without flattening either one into the
other.

This is an **original narrative account of those documented events** — the history is public
record; Feynman's own sentences, wherever he wrote or spoke them, are nowhere borrowed here.
