# Chapter 11 — Gell-Mann

Murray Gell-Mann arrived at Caltech in 1955, a few years after Feynman, and the two of them spent
the better part of three decades sharing a hallway, a physics department, and a rivalry that
neither man ever quite finished being honest about in public. Gell-Mann was, by every account of
colleagues who watched the two of them work, Feynman's equal in raw ability and his near-opposite
in temperament: formally erudite, fluent in more languages than physics required of him, precise
almost to the point of pedantry about terminology and attribution, visibly irritated by what he
regarded as Feynman's showman's carelessness about getting the history and the credit exactly
right in the stories he told. Feynman, for his part, found Gell-Mann's formality faintly
exhausting and occasionally very funny, in the specific way that a man who trusts his own picture
of a problem over any book's account of it finds someone else's insistence on the book's account
faintly exhausting — and yet the two of them, across the late 1950s, produced together one of the
more important pieces of theoretical work either man did after quantum electrodynamics.

The problem was the weak nuclear force — the interaction responsible for radioactive beta decay,
among other processes, and one whose mathematical description in the mid-1950s remained a
patchwork of competing, only partially satisfying theories. Following the 1956 discovery, by
Chien-Shiung Wu and collaborators, that the weak interaction violated a symmetry called parity —
that nature, in this one specific interaction, could tell left from right, a possibility most
physicists had assumed on aesthetic grounds alone was forbidden — the field was left with a
confirmed, startling experimental fact and no settled theory that explained it cleanly. Feynman
and Gell-Mann, working together at Caltech through 1957, proposed a theory — now generally called
the V-A theory of the weak interaction — that got the mathematics right in a way competing
proposals from other groups did not quite manage, predicting behavior that further experiments
would go on to confirm.

Feynman arrived at the same central conclusion Gell-Mann had by a route entirely his own — working
it out, by his later account, over a stretch of illness that kept him confined to bed, running the
competing experimental data through his head from memory rather than from the published papers he
did not have in front of him, and finding that only one simple form of the interaction actually fit
all of it cleanly, even though it required assuming that at least one respected recent experimental
result then in print had to be wrong. He was right, and the courage of that particular bet — trusting
a clean theoretical picture over a specific piece of published experimental data — became, in later
retellings by physicists who admired the story, one of the clearest single illustrations of the
nerve his method actually required: it is one thing to distrust an authority in the abstract, and
another to publish a theory that only works if a named colleague's careful, published measurement
turns out to be mistaken.

The collaboration itself, by the account of physicists who watched it happen, was less a smooth
meeting of minds than a running argument that happened to be productive — the two men circling the
same blackboard, Gell-Mann insisting on mathematical completeness and correct attribution to the
existing literature at every step, Feynman wanting to push forward on the physical intuition and
worry about the formal bookkeeping afterward, each one, by turns, exasperated by and quietly
grateful for the other's opposite instinct. Neither man, in later decades, entirely agreed in
public about how the credit for the theory's key insight ought to be divided, and the disagreement
— genuinely felt on both sides, and never quite resolved into a tidy joint account — became, itself,
a kind of standing joke and a kind of real, low-grade wound in a friendship that lasted the rest of
Feynman's life.

The two men also disagreed, sharply and semi-publicly, about how a physicist ought to comport
himself in front of a general audience — a disagreement that ran well past the weak-interaction
paper and coloured the whole of their thirty years down the same Caltech corridor. Gell-Mann
regarded showmanship as, at best, a distraction from the work and, at worst, a subtle
misrepresentation of how physics actually got done, and made no secret of his opinion that his
colleague's bongo-playing, safecracking public image did the field's dignity no particular favours.
Feynman, unmoved, kept right on doing all of it, on the theory that a physicist's dignity was never
actually at stake in a public lecture — only the honesty of the physics was, and he had never once
let the drumming change a single line of a derivation.

Students who took courses from both men across the 1960s and 1970s learned, without needing to be
told directly, that the two professors represented genuinely different working philosophies rather
than a simple difference in personality: Gell-Mann built outward from symmetry and mathematical
elegance, trusting that a sufficiently beautiful structure was likely to be true even before the
experimental confirmation arrived, while Feynman kept insisting on starting from a concrete physical
picture and letting the elegance, if any turned up, arrive afterward as a bonus rather than a guide.
Both approaches produced real physics; watching the two methods argue with each other in adjoining
offices, for three decades, was itself an education a great many Caltech graduate students later
described as more valuable than either professor's formal lectures.

What kept the two of them in the same department for three decades, through disagreements about
credit and temperament and — more seriously, in Gell-Mann's later telling — a genuine and
occasionally wounding difference in how visibly each man was willing to work, was a mutual, if
grudging, respect that neither ever fully suppressed even at its most irritated. Gell-Mann went on,
in the years following their collaboration, to develop the quark model that gave the parton
picture Feynman would later build on its deeper theoretical footing — the two men's separate,
occasionally competing bodies of work turning out, over the following decade, to fit together into
a single, coherent picture of how matter is actually built, even as the two men who produced the
pieces continued to needle each other about method and manners in the corridor between their
offices.

Gell-Mann, asked in later years and after Feynman's death to summarize the relationship, gave an
answer that managed to be both an old grievance and an unmistakable tribute in the same breath: that
Feynman spent a great deal of energy cultivating a public myth of himself as an folksy, unpretentious
outsider who arrived at deep truths by pure native cleverness, when in fact he was, underneath the
performance, one of the most disciplined and hard-working physicists Gell-Mann had ever known — a
complaint that reads, on the page, less like a takedown than like the closest thing to an
epitaph one exacting, precise colleague could offer another: he never once mistook the showman for
the whole man, because he had spent thirty years watching what the showman actually did at the
blackboard when nobody outside the department was looking.
