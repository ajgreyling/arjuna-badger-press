# Chapter 10 — Going West

By 1950 Richard Feynman had his choice of nearly any physics department in the country and turned
most of the grandest offers down for reasons that had less to do with prestige than with a
specific, almost physical need he had identified in himself since the Cornell recovery: freedom
from the wrong kind of pressure, and proximity to a climate and a culture where he could actually
be curious without an institution constantly reminding him how important he was supposed to be.
The California Institute of Technology made him an offer that included, alongside a professorship,
an unusual and pointed sweetener: a paid sabbatical in Brazil before he even had to start teaching,
time to travel, to learn Portuguese, to samba badly and enthusiastically at Carnival, to lecture at
a physics institute in Rio, and to simply not be Richard Feynman, Nobel-track genius, for a while
before settling into the job that would occupy him for the rest of his life. He accepted.

Rio de Janeiro, when he arrived, gave him something Cornell's grey winters and Princeton's tweed
had never offered: a culture with no particular reverence for the mystique of a visiting American
physicist and an enormous, unembarrassed appetite for pleasure taken seriously, which suited him
completely. He lectured at the Centro Brasileiro de Pesquisas Físicas, worked with a small, eager
group of local physicists and students, and spent his evenings doing what he had done at Los Alamos
with the bongos and would do for the rest of his life with anything that caught his hands and his
attention: learning to actually play, rather than merely appreciate, the percussion at the centre of
the culture around him. He took up the frigideira, a small metal frying-pan-shaped instrument
central to samba rhythm sections, practiced with the same patient, walk-it-around discipline he
brought to a locked safe, and by the following year's Carnival was good enough to march and play
with a real samba school through the streets of Rio — not as a tolerated foreign curiosity but as a
working member of the percussion line, judged, as everyone in the line was judged, on whether he
kept the beat.

The sabbatical was not only pleasure. Sitting in on Brazilian physics classrooms, he watched
students who could recite definitions and pass examinations with startling fluency freeze
completely the moment a question asked them to apply the same material to a situation phrased even
slightly differently from the textbook's own wording — students, in other words, who had learned
the vocabulary of physics without ever being taught to see the mechanism the vocabulary was
supposed to describe. He raised the problem, bluntly and specifically, with the Brazilian
faculty who had invited him, and diagnosed it, in terms he would return to and sharpen over the
following decades, as a system built for confident recitation rather than actual understanding — an
early, first-hand version of the exact diagnosis he would later generalize, at a Caltech
commencement podium in 1974, into the phrase *cargo cult science*: an elaborate, sincerely
performed imitation of learning, with the one ingredient that made the original method work — real,
checkable contact with how the world actually behaves — missing from the middle of it.

He came home from South America in the autumn of 1951 having spent the better part of a year
deliberately, almost defiantly, not doing very much conventional physics at all — a second, gentler
version of the same lesson the wobbling plate had taught him at Cornell: that the work went better,
and stayed more honestly his own, when it was not the only thing he let himself be.

Caltech in the early 1950s was smaller and less encumbered by bureaucracy than the East Coast
institutions that had courted him, a school built around a culture of rigorous, hands-on science
that suited his temperament closely, and it would remain his professional home, without a serious
attempt to leave it again, for the rest of his career. He found in Pasadena something he had not
quite had since his father's attic: a place where a mind that insisted on rebuilding every fact
from the ground up was not merely tolerated but was, in fact, the local house style.

His office door became, within a couple of years of his arrival, something close to an informal
consulting service for the rest of the department: graduate students and even senior faculty from
fields well outside his own would knock with a problem they had been stuck on for weeks, describe
it to him in plain terms, and watch him pace the small room, ask two or three pointed questions
about what was actually happening physically rather than what the stuck equation said, and hand back
either the missing piece of the answer or a clear diagnosis of exactly where their own reasoning had
gone soft — a service he offered for free, out of the same unglamorous pleasure that had once made
him take apart a stranger's radio, and that built him a reputation across Caltech, well before any
Nobel committee weighed in, as the single most useful mind on campus to be stuck in front of.

His personal life in these years did not follow the same steadying trajectory as his career. In
1952 he married Mary Louise Bell, a woman he had met and courted with the same restless energy he
brought to everything, and the marriage, from very early on, strained against a mismatch that
neither party's affection for the other seemed able to close: Mary Lou wanted a husband present in
the ordinary domestic and social sense — evenings that belonged to the marriage, attention that
was not perpetually half-claimed by an equation half-finished in the back of his mind — and
Feynman, whatever his intentions, could not or would not fully deliver it. He worked, by his own
later account, in the same all-consuming way he always had, carrying half-solved problems around
in his head through dinners and social evenings in a manner that read, from the outside and
eventually from across the breakfast table, less like devotion to his craft and more like a
standing absence dressed up as a man in the room.

The marriage produced real unhappiness on both sides rather than a single dramatic rupture — small
grievances compounding across four years, arguments about attention and presence that neither of
them seemed able to resolve into either genuine partnership or a clean, early separation. Mary Lou
would later describe, in accounts given after the divorce, a husband who solved differential
equations at the dinner table and appeared to regard emotional conversation as a problem with no
clean derivation, and therefore, on some level, not worth the same rigor he brought to physics — an
account Feynman himself did not, in his more candid later reflections, entirely dispute. They
divorced in 1956, in proceedings that, by some later retellings, included Mary Lou's own testimony
citing his habit of working out mathematics in his head during moments she needed him present for
something else entirely as a formal grievance — a detail that reads, with the distance of decades,
less like a joke about a genius's quirks and more like the plain, ordinary cost such a mind can
impose on the people who share a house with it.

He spent the years immediately following the divorce doing what he had always done with an
unresolved difficulty: he kept working, kept teaching, kept building the reputation at Caltech that
would, within another decade, make him one of the most recognizable physicists alive. Colleagues
from the period describe a man who had become, by his mid-thirties, genuinely brilliant at
lecturing — able to take an idea that had taken him years of hard, private struggle to understand
and deliver it to a room of undergraduates as though it were the most obvious and delightful thing
in the world, a gift for translation that had its roots in the same impulse that once made him
explain a *Tyrannosaurus*'s skull to a boy standing at a window frame: a fact was not worth knowing
until you could make someone else see it too.

It was into this period — settled at Caltech, freshly and unhappily divorced, carrying a growing
public reputation he had not entirely asked for — that a quieter, steadier kind of companionship
would eventually arrive, though not for a few more years yet, and not from anywhere near Pasadena.
