# Chapter 2 — Joan

His sister was born in 1927, nine years after him, into a household that had already, by then, more
or less finished shaping the elder child into the particular kind of person he was going to be. Joan
Feynman grew up in the same Far Rockaway house, under the same encyclopedia shelf, and wanted,
almost from the moment she was old enough to want anything specific, the same thing her brother had:
to understand how the world actually worked, rather than simply to be told.

It did not go smoothly at first, and the reason it did not go smoothly came, uncomfortably, from
him. Their mother, Lucille, held the ordinary view of her generation about what a girl's mind was
and wasn't suited for, and voiced it plainly enough, more than once, that women's brains were not
built for grasping science the way men's were — a belief she is reported to have stated to her
young daughter directly, and one that a boy already deep into his own scientific education did not,
in his early adolescence, have the maturity or the motive to contradict. By Joan's own later
recollections, given decades afterward once she had a doctorate and a career in solar physics that
would eventually see her honoured by NASA, her older brother's silence in the face of their
mother's dismissal — and, worse, moments in which the same casual scepticism came out of his own
mouth — did real damage to a young girl's confidence at exactly the age it mattered most. It was
not, on his part, considered cruelty; it was the ordinary, unexamined weather of the household he
had grown up breathing, passed along without much reflection, the one significant instance in his
life of failing to apply his own stated principle — walk the fact around and check it yourself
before you accept it — to a belief that was sitting right in his own family's kitchen.

What changed it, by Joan's own account, was a night in the New Jersey sky, sometime after Richard
had left for Princeton and come home again on a visit, when the aurora borealis was visible far
enough south to reach the family's view, an unusual and striking event for the latitude. He woke his
much younger sister and took her outside to see it, and rather than simply pointing at the lights
and naming them, he did what their father had always done for him: he made her look closer, asked
her what she noticed about the way the colours moved and separated, and told her, with the same
plain seriousness he brought to every real question, that nobody yet properly understood what
caused the aurora to behave exactly this way — that it was, in fact, an open scientific problem,
not a settled fact in a textbook, and that there was no reason on earth a determined mind couldn't
be the one to work it out. It was not, by itself, an apology for the years of unexamined dismissal
that had come before it. It was something more useful than an apology: a correction, delivered the
only way he knew how to deliver anything that mattered, by handing her a real, unsolved, genuinely
interesting problem and treating her as someone capable of taking it seriously.

The aurora itself stayed a private thread between the two of them for the rest of both their lives —
a running joke and a running argument in equal measure, since the actual physics of the phenomenon,
tied up in the behaviour of the solar wind striking the Earth's magnetic field, would not be properly
worked out by the wider field of geophysics for decades, and brother and sister spent that whole
stretch periodically comparing notes on the problem as equals rather than as teacher and student,
each of them, by their own later accounts, delighted whenever the other turned out to have thought
of something the first hadn't.

He gave her, not long after, a stack of his old college textbooks — genuine, difficult, adult
physics and astronomy texts, not a simplified or softened version prepared for a girl he suspected
of lesser capacity — and left her to work through them at her own pace, checking in periodically not
to grade her but to argue a point with her the exact way Bethe would later argue a point with him at
Los Alamos: at speed, without deference to her age, willing to be shown wrong. Joan later described
this stretch of her adolescence as the real beginning of her scientific life, the moment the family's
earlier scepticism about a girl's aptitude stopped being the operative fact in the house and her own
curiosity, finally given real material to work on, took over instead.

Her path to that doctorate was not, even with her brother's support secured, an easy one; she
encountered, across her own undergraduate and graduate years, a steady low hum of the same
scepticism her mother had once voiced outright, now dressed in the more polite institutional forms
a university could produce — advisors who steered women students toward less mathematically
demanding specialities, colleagues who assumed a question from her was a request for explanation
rather than a genuine scientific challenge. She met each instance, by her own account, with a
version of the same method her brother had modeled for her at the aurora: she did not argue the
general principle of whether women belonged in physics, a debate she considered a waste of the
finite time available for actually doing the work. She simply kept doing the work, well enough and
persistently enough that the specific objection in front of her, one at a time, ran out of room to
stand on.

She went on to earn a doctorate in physics from Syracuse University in 1958, and built a long,
distinguished career studying the aurora she had first properly looked at as a girl standing beside
her brother in a New Jersey yard — solar wind, geomagnetic storms, the interaction between the sun
and the Earth's magnetosphere, eventually rising to a senior research position at NASA's Jet
Propulsion Laboratory, the same institution whose parent university employed her brother a few
miles away. The two of them remained close for the rest of his life, comparing notes across
different corners of physics with the same unpretentious argumentative affection that had always
run the household, and Joan spoke, in later interviews given long after both her own career and her
brother's death, with a frank clarity about the whole arc: the early damage of an unexamined
prejudice absorbed uncritically from a well-meaning household, and the later, deliberate repair her
brother had made once he actually stopped to look at the belief the way he looked at everything
else — closely, honestly, and without the comfortable excuse of simply having always thought it
that way.
