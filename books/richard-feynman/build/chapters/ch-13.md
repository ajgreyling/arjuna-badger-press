# Chapter 13 — The Textbook Committee

In the early 1960s, with two young children of his own now old enough to be entering California's
public schools, Feynman accepted an appointment to the state's Curriculum Commission, the body
charged with reviewing mathematics and science textbooks before the state would approve them for
adoption in classrooms across California — a bureaucratic, unglamorous assignment that a physicist
of his stature had no obvious professional need to take on, and that he took on anyway for the
plainest possible reason: his own children were about to be handed these books, and he wanted to
know, firsthand and in detail, what was actually in them.

What he found, working through box after box of submitted textbooks with the same patient, one-page-
at-a-time discipline he had once brought to a locked safe, alarmed him in a way that surprised even
him. Book after book, he later reported, presented mathematics and science not as a living method
of finding things out but as a kind of ceremonial vocabulary — sets, terminology, elaborate
definitional scaffolding, introduced and drilled with tremendous formal rigor and almost no
connection to any actual, checkable fact about the world a child could go verify for themselves. He
found chapters that defined new abstract terms purely in order to define further abstract terms on
top of them, an entire apparent curriculum built, as he put it privately to colleagues at the time,
on words about words about words, with the concrete, walkable fact — the actual thing a formula was
supposed to be describing — nowhere in sight. It was, to him, the exact failure he would later name
publicly in the Cargo Cult Science address: all the outward form of rigorous instruction, none of
the underlying mechanism that made the rigor mean anything.

He tested his own reaction against his own standing rule before he trusted it, the same way he
tested any strong first impression: he picked a chapter at random from a book that had struck him
as particularly empty, worked the material himself from scratch as though he were the intended
student, and timed how long it took him, an experienced physicist, to reconstruct the actual
reasoning the chapter's exercises assumed a twelve-year-old would simply absorb from the page. More
than once, he could not do it either — not because the underlying mathematics was beyond him, but
because the book itself had never actually stated the reasoning in the first place, only the
vocabulary and the exercises built to drill it. A chapter no physicist could reconstruct from its
own printed content was not, by his standard, teaching anything, however confidently it was priced,
bound, and stamped with a state seal of approval.

One book on the commission's list submitted blank pages for several chapters, apparently a
production error at the printer that no other reviewer on the panel had caught, or had caught and
not thought worth mentioning; Feynman had, by his own account, actually read every single page of
every submitted volume rather than skimming the summaries and sample chapters most reviewers
worked from, and was the one member of the commission who could report, specifically and by page
number, that a supposedly reviewed, supposedly rigorous textbook contained sections that did not
exist. Another book he found genuinely and specifically wrong on the science — not merely dull, but
factually mistaken about a point of physics in a way that would, if left uncorrected, teach an
entire year's worth of California schoolchildren something false and be very hard to unteach later.

He kept a private numbering system running across the stack of submissions on his own desk at home,
grading each book not on the polish of its typesetting or the reputation of its publisher but on a
single blunt test he applied consistently to every chapter: could a genuinely curious child, working
only from what was printed on the page, actually reconstruct why a given fact was true, or did the
book simply assert the fact and move on, trusting memorization to do the rest of the work. Most
submissions, by his own private tally, failed that test more often than they passed it, and the
handful that passed consistently became, in his correspondence with fellow commissioners, the
standard he tried, with limited success, to hold the rest of the field to.

He argued his findings at the commission's meetings with the same blunt, ungraded honesty that had
unsettled the more procedure-minded members of the Rogers Commission two decades later — this being,
in its way, an early rehearsal for exactly that later, higher-stakes confrontation between his
insistence on the mechanism and an institution's preference for a smoother, less confrontational
process. Some of his fellow commissioners appreciated the rigor; others found him difficult, over-
literal, unwilling to simply defer to the publishers' sales representatives and the state's existing
adoption procedures the way the commission had always operated before. He grew frustrated, over the
course of his term, with a process that seemed built less to protect the actual quality of what
children were taught than to keep a large, established textbook industry moving smoothly through an
approval pipeline, and he eventually stepped back from the commission with his opinion of educational
bureaucracy considerably lower than it had been going in, and his opinion of the value of simply
reading every page yourself, rather than trusting a summary, considerably confirmed.

He did occasionally win a smaller, more concrete victory in the meetings themselves, and told those
stories, in later years, with considerably more relish than the general complaint about the
industry — a publisher's representative caught unable to answer a direct question about a formula
printed under the company's own name; a colleague on the commission persuaded, after initial
resistance, to reject a submission once Feynman simply read the offending page aloud rather than
summarizing his objection to it, letting the words condemn themselves. Those small wins, rare as
they were against the larger weight of an entrenched adoption process, were enough to keep him
showing up to meetings he otherwise found close to unbearable, on the theory that a battle worth
losing overall was still worth winning wherever it could actually be won.

The episode did not change California's textbook-adoption process in any lasting structural way, and
Feynman himself, telling the story in later years, told it mostly as a wry, slightly exasperated
comedy about institutional inertia rather than as one of his prouder professional achievements. But
it sat, in his own accounting of his life, on exactly the same shelf as the safecracking and the
ice-water demonstration and the wobbling plate: another instance of the same underlying refusal, this
time applied not to a bomb or a shuttle but to a stack of ordinary school textbooks his own children
were about to be handed — the conviction, tested and confirmed one more time, that an institution's
approval stamp was never, by itself, a substitute for actually opening the book and reading what was
on the page.
