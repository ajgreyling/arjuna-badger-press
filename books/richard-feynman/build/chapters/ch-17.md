# Chapter 17 — Room at the Bottom

Long before nanotechnology was a word anyone used, and decades before it became a serious field
with its own journals and conferences, Feynman stood in front of the American Physical Society at
Caltech in December 1959 and gave a talk that would not be widely recognized as prophetic until
long after most of the room had forgotten it. He titled it, with characteristic plainness, "There's
Plenty of Room at the Bottom," and the argument underneath the title was, at the time, closer to a
thought experiment than a research program: the laws of physics, as far as anyone could tell, placed
no fundamental barrier in the way of manipulating matter atom by atom — writing the entire
*Encyclopaedia Britannica* on the head of a pin was not science fiction, he argued, but simply a
matter of scale, and scale was a problem engineering could, in principle, eventually solve. He even
offered, half-serious, a standing cash prize of his own money for the first person to actually
build a working electric motor small enough to fit inside a cube a sixty-fourth of an inch on a
side — a prize a Caltech engineer claimed within the year, using conventional machining rather than
anything approaching the atomic manipulation Feynman had actually been describing, a small,
characteristic joke on him: the easy version of the challenge got solved almost immediately; the
genuinely hard version, building things atom by atom, took the rest of the century to even begin.

He walked the audience through the argument the same way he walked through every argument, from the
concrete case outward rather than from the abstraction inward. Information, he pointed out, could
in principle be written far more densely than any existing printing technology managed — the entire
contents of the world's great libraries, he calculated live in front of the room, could theoretically
be inscribed within a cube of material smaller than a speck of dust, if only someone worked out how
to write and read at the scale of individual atoms rather than the scale of ink droplets. He talked
about the possibility of building machines that built smaller machines that built smaller machines
still, a cascading miniaturization with no floor in physical law, only in engineering patience — and
about biology, almost as an aside, as proof the whole idea already worked: a living cell, he
reminded the room, was itself a factory operating at exactly the scale he was describing, storing
extraordinary amounts of information in the folded chemistry of a molecule far too small to see
with any optical microscope then in existence. It was vintage Feynman showmanship in service of a
genuinely serious point: rather than simply asserting that atomic-scale engineering was possible, he
built the audience a mental picture vivid enough that the possibility became, by the end of the
talk, difficult to un-see.

The talk sat mostly unnoticed for decades, filed away in physics folklore as an amusing curiosity
rather than a founding document, until researchers in the emerging field of nanotechnology began,
in the 1980s and after, digging back through the literature for intellectual ancestors and finding,
to their evident delight, that Feynman had laid out the basic case for their entire field a
generation before anyone had the tools to attempt it. He had done, in other words, exactly what he
had always done with an idle curiosity: walked a fact — that physics itself imposed no ban on tiny
machines — all the way around until he could see its shape clearly, published it because the
picture pleased him, and left the actual engineering for whoever came along later with the tools to
do it.

His research work in these same decades did not stand still while the showman persona grew louder
in public. In 1969 he introduced the **parton model**, a way of picturing the proton and neutron —
particles that, by the 1960s, experiments were increasingly showing to have internal structure —
as composed of smaller, point-like constituents that could be probed by firing high-energy
electrons at them and reading the scattering pattern the way his father had once read a
*Tyrannosaurus*'s skull against a window frame: not by naming the particle, but by working out,
concretely, what was actually inside it and how the pieces behaved when you hit them hard enough to
notice. The model gave experimentalists at Stanford's linear accelerator a concrete physical
picture to test their scattering data against, at a moment when the underlying theory of quarks —
which the partons would eventually be identified with — was still being worked out by others in
more abstract mathematical language. It was the diagrams all over again, in miniature: a
picture-first approach that let working physicists get useful answers before the fully rigorous
theory had entirely caught up to justify them.

Toward the end of his career, in a series of talks and informal seminars in 1981 and 1982, he took
up a still stranger question: could a computer, built from ordinary classical components, actually
simulate the full behaviour of quantum mechanical systems, with their strange superpositions and
probabilities, without running into an exponential wall of computational cost? His answer, worked
out with the same first-principles scepticism he brought to everything, was that classical
computers probably could not do it efficiently — but that a computer built out of quantum
mechanical components themselves, obeying the same strange rules the simulation was trying to
capture, plausibly could. The talks, later published, are now widely credited as among the founding
conceptual documents of quantum computing, a field that would not produce its first working
laboratory devices for another decade and would not become a serious commercial and academic
industry for several decades more — Feynman, once again, walking a fact around to its logical
edge and publishing the shape of it years before the tools existed to build what the shape
implied.

Alongside the frontier research ran the other half of his working life that never stopped: teaching,
and an increasingly public campaign, delivered in talks, essays, and a famous 1974 Caltech
commencement address, against what he called **cargo cult science** — research and reasoning that
wore the outward form of the scientific method, the white coats and the careful-sounding language,
without the one ingredient he considered non-negotiable: a genuine, rigorous willingness to try to
prove your own idea wrong before anyone else got the chance to. He drew the image, characteristically,
from something concrete rather than abstract — Pacific islanders in the years after the Second World
War who had watched military airstrips draw in cargo planes loaded with goods, and who, when the
war ended and the planes stopped coming, built their own replica airstrips and control towers out
of local materials, going through every outward motion of what they had observed, in the sincere
hope that the planes would come back, without any of it actually being connected to the mechanism
that had made the original airstrips work. Real science, he told the graduating class, required a
harder and lonelier discipline than merely following the outward form of experiment and publication:
it required a scientist to report the ways their own experiment could be wrong, the data that
didn't fit, the alternative explanations not yet ruled out — a kind of relentless self-honesty that
he considered the actual, load-bearing difference between science and its cargo-cult imitation, and
that he held himself to, in his own research and his own life, with a consistency that made the
speech land as autobiography as much as advice.
