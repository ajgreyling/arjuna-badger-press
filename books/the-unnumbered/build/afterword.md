# Afterword — What Is Real

This is a novel. Piet Buys never existed, and neither did the man he hunts. The country they move
through does.

I want to be precise about the line between those two things, because a crime novel that blurs it is
doing something dishonest to people who are still alive.

## The women

Deline Fortuin, Bernadine Klaasen, Neliswa Mgcini, Amoret Booysen, Sylvia Tafira, Rochelle, Mmapula
— every woman in this book is invented. So is every mother. Aunty Miems Fortuin is not anyone.

**The reason nobody looked for them is not invented.**

## The killers I did not use

There are real South African serial-murder cases in the record, and their offenders are alive and in
prison, and their victims are survived by mothers and sisters in Gauteng and the Cape right now. I
have not named any of them in the story and I have not put invented acts on a real person's name.
Naming them would have been a defamation exposure; more to the point, it would have handed those
men another book, and they have enough.

What I took from the record was **method**, and only method:

- **The fake job offer.** In a Gauteng series in 1994–95, the offender ran a shell organisation
  posing as an anti-abuse charity, walked through townships handing out official-looking forms, and
  offered women desk jobs that did not exist. He was convicted of 38 murders and 40 rapes. That is
  the most South African predation there is — the weaponising of unemployment — and it is the seed
  of this book's central mechanism. I changed the crucial thing: in *The Unnumbered*, **the work is
  real.** The farm exists, the wage exists, the crew exists. Only the destination is false, and only
  sometimes. Invent a farm and eventually somebody phones it. Nobody ever phones a real farm to ask
  whether a woman who was on nobody's list arrived somewhere she was never officially going.
- **The transport corridor.** Between 2005 and 2007 at least five women were murdered in Namibia,
  their remains found near the national road that runs the length of that country. Three were
  identified; two never were. One man was arrested and acquitted for lack of evidence; another was
  implicated after his suicide in 2008. **The case remains unsolved.** I took the shape of an
  unsolved series running across a border that no single police service owns. I did not take the
  offender's signature, and I did not solve his case in fiction, because it is not mine to solve.

## The investigators

Piet Buys is not a portrait of a real person. He owes his patience and his method of building a case
slowly, from paper and from confidence, to **Piet Byleveld**, the South African detective who died
in 2017 after thirty-eight years, and who worked the cases the previous section describes obliquely.
He owes his *profession* to **Dr Micki Pistorius**, who founded the SAPS Investigative Psychology
Unit in 1996 and was this country's first profiler, and to **Dr Gérard Labuschagne**, who ran that
unit for some fourteen and a half years after her.

None of the three is in this book. Byleveld was a detective, not a profiler, and worked in Gauteng,
not the Cape. Their work is the reason it was possible to imagine a man like Buys at all.

## The country

These are the load-bearing facts. Every one is sourced. Where I could not source a number, I have
not given one — which is Elsie Mtimkulu's rule in chapter eleven, and it is mine.

**Safety is a purchased commodity.** More than 2.6 million security officers are registered with
PSIRA, of whom roughly 586,000 are actively employed. The South African Police Service had a
personnel strength of 184,106 as at 31 March 2024. South Africa has the largest private security
industry per capita in the world — more active private officers than the police service and the
defence force combined. Nothing about Hendrik Buys's company is exaggerated for effect.

**There is no twenty-four-hour rule.** There never was. SAPS is explicit that a missing-person report
must be opened and investigated immediately, that the SAPS 55(A) is completed with the complainant
present, and that details go onto the National Bureau for Missing Persons system. The Portfolio
Committee on Police has had to issue a public statement about stations not adhering to that
directive. Aunty Miems was turned away three times in this novel, and every one of those refusals
was against standing instruction, and nobody broke a law, and nobody would be disciplined. That is
the quietest and most devastating fact I found while writing this.

**The FCS units** — Family Violence, Child Protection and Sexual Offences — were broken up in late
September 2006 and re-established in June 2010. Charmaine Adonis's career is built exactly on that
gap, because thousands of real detectives' careers were.

**The unidentified dead.** As at August 2024 there were roughly 3,186 unclaimed bodies in state
mortuaries nationally, some held for as long as six years. In 2024 the Western Cape conducted 289
pauper burials, 279 of them in the Cape Town metro. A body may be released for pauper burial after
thirty days. The national forensic DNA backlog has exceeded 140,000 cases. And the National Forensic
DNA Database supports **familial searching** — the technique that could match a mother's swab to an
unidentified daughter — but that capability is not aimed at the missing. It is legislated and
resourced for crime, and it works at that. There is no standing programme that takes swabs from the
families of missing persons and runs them against the unidentified dead.

Elsie Mtimkulu's argument in chapter eleven is the argument of this whole book. They are not
nameless and they are not un-numbered — every one of them has a case number, a DR number, a plot
number, a page in a book. They are simply never **counted**. There is no total anywhere that anyone
has to stand up and answer for, because nobody was ever given the job of adding them up.

That is the title.

**The labour system.** In 2012, of roughly 121,000 farm workers in the Western Cape, slightly more
than half were seasonal — mostly women — and employed through labour brokers. The industry has moved
steadily away from permanent on-farm workers toward off-farm seasonal and casual workers on
short-term contracts or none. Table grapes in the Cape run from vine preparation in August and
September through to packing in March and April. From 1 March 2026 the national minimum wage,
including for farm workers, is R30.23 an hour.

On the Orange River, at Aussenkehr in Namibia's Karas Region — immediately across the border — the
grapes ripen earlier, harvested from early November to the second week of January. A settlement of
around five thousand people becomes as many as twenty thousand for those nine weeks, and the
seasonal workforce comes down from the north of Namibia, a thousand kilometres and more. The living
conditions there have been documented repeatedly and critically by Namibian and South African
journalists.

And there is no filling station at Vioolsdrif. The last diesel in the Republic is at Steinkopf,
sixty-seven kilometres short of the bridge. The next pump is at Noordoewer, on the other side, where
the N7 stops being the N7.

**De Doorns, November 2009.** Between the 14th and the 17th, xenophobic violence in De Doorns
displaced around three thousand Zimbabwean farm workers — sixty-eight in the first wave at
Ekuphumleni, roughly three thousand in the second — who sheltered first at the police station and
then on the Hexvallei sports field. It was the only *rural* site of violence in the national wave of
2008 and 2009. A character in this book refers to it once, in passing, the way you refer to a year
rather than an event, and the book does not dramatise it, and that restraint is deliberate.

I will say plainly here what the novel refuses to say on the page: that violence came out of people
competing for six-week jobs inside a system built to make them compete. The machine is the villain
of this book. It is never the people.

## What is still owed

This draft has outstanding verification of its own, and I would rather print the list than pretend
there isn't one: whether a labour broker is legally obliged to retain a crew list, and for how long;
the real timeline of a mutual legal assistance request between South Africa and Namibia; whether a
South African fleet card transacts across that border and who a monthly debit would fall on; how
Namibia handles a missing-person report for a foreign national made by a foreign family; and what a
chain of custody held by a private company is actually worth in a South African court. If the answer
to that last one is *nothing*, the scene will change, and it will be a better scene.

Corrections are welcome and will be used.

— *A.J.G., July 2026*
