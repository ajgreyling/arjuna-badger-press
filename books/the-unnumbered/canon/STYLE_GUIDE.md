# STYLE_GUIDE — *The Unnumbered*

> The voice laws. Two of them are unique to this book and non-negotiable: **the bill** and **the
> register**. The rest is house.

## The narrating voice

**Charl narrates. First person, past tense.** The prose is hers: Cape Flats English with Afrikaans
running under it, dry, fast, funny where funny is the only available exit. She is an educated
detective, not a folk character — never write her as texture. If a sentence sounds like an author
doing an accent, it is wrong. `[sensitivity read gate before publication]`

Piet is only ever seen from outside. **We never enter his head.** This is a craft decision and a
moral one: the book refuses to perform his interiority for the reader's entertainment. What he is
paying is shown in his body, his silences, and his hands — and Charl, who has learned to read him,
tells us what she thinks it costs, and is sometimes wrong.

## Mandate #1 — the bill (binding)

**No scene shows the gift without showing the price.** Not "somewhere in the book" — in that scene
or the next.

- He reads the room; then he cannot be in it.
- He gets the answer; then he is silent in the car for forty minutes and Charl drives.
- He is brilliant on the Tuesday; on the Wednesday he does not answer his phone, and this is not a
  mystery beat, it is a Wednesday.
- Masking is shown as *labour*: the jacket, the rehearsed opening line, the count of how long he
  has to hold it, the collapse after.

**Forbidden:** the effortless savant. The rudeness played for laughs. The moment where his autism
conveniently supplies a fact the plot needs and costs nothing. Meltdown or shutdown used as a
third-act spectacle. Anyone calling him a genius approvingly. A cure arc. A romance that fixes him.

**Also forbidden:** the opposite failure — misery-porn, or a character who is only ever suffering.
He is good at his job, he likes his life more days than not, he has a friend downstairs, and he
swims in cold water because it is nice. Write a **person**.

## Mandate #2 — the register (binding)

Multilingual by default, never subtitled to death. Afrikaans, English and isiXhosa live in the same
paragraph the way they live in the same street.

- Untranslated Afrikaans/isiXhosa **where context carries it.** Trust the reader. No glossary
  parentheses mid-scene, no italicising the country's own languages as though they were foreign.
- A short glossary in the backmatter for the handful of terms that genuinely need one.
- Code-switching is characterisation: who switches to what, in front of whom, tells you the power
  in the room. Piet switches to Afrikaans when he is tired and to English when he is masking.

## The violence law

The book is about murdered women. Therefore:

- **The camera is on the living.** Bodies are described with the flat precision of a scene report or
  Elsie's voice — clinical, unlingering, respectful. Never eroticised, never aestheticised, never
  slow.
- **No victim POV, no final-moments scene.** Not once.
- **The grief is where the pages go.** Aunty Miems's kitchen gets more words than any crime scene in
  the book, and that is the whole aesthetic argument.
- **No killer POV.** He does not get chapters and he does not get a voice-over. See
  ADAPTATION_DOCTRINE § the Moriarty rule.

## The house non-negotiables

- **No alcohol** framing for any character modelled on real people close to the author; nobody is
  characterised through a drink. (House rule.)
- **Accessibility is the foundation**, not a theme. It shows up in *how the book is built* as well as
  what it says: Atkinson Hyperlegible in all set text, EPUB structural headings clean, alt text on
  every image, no meaning carried by colour alone.
- **Velocity.** No sag. If a passage is explaining instead of happening, cut it and build the scene
  that makes the reader deduce it.
- **Indict the machine, not the people.** Every time. Including the police, including the labour
  broker, including Hendrik.

## Chapter shape

- 2,500–4,000 words. ~30–34 chapters, four movements.
- Open in motion, close on a turn — a fact, not a cliffhanger gimmick.
- Scene, not summary. Weather is allowed to matter; the Cape in February is a character.
- Chapter headings: plain numbers. No cute case-file typography.
