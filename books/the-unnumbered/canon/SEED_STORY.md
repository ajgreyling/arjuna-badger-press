# SEED_STORY — *The Unnumbered*

> The book in one page, at four lengths. If a drafting decision conflicts with this file, this file
> wins.

## One line

A late-diagnosed autistic profiler and the ex-FCS detective who narrates him hunt a man harvesting
women off the Cape seasonal-labour routes — women no one reported, because they were already away
for work.

## One paragraph

Pieter Buys can't stop noticing, and he can't leave a noticed thing alone. It made him a police
profiler; it also makes a Saturday shopping centre unsurvivable, and it cost him a career in a unit
that has since been hollowed out anyway. Now he sells threat assessments to frightened wine farmers
from a room above a bicycle shop in Stellenbosch. When Charmaine Adonis brings him a woman from a
Rawsonville grape crew — gone eight months, reported three times, never entered as missing — he
finds the second one in an afternoon and the ninth by Friday. They are strung along the harvest
calendar and the labour routes north to the border like beads on a wire. Nobody has a docket. Nobody
has jurisdiction. Nobody is counting. And the one time Piet is certain, he is catastrophically
wrong, and a living man pays for it.

## The pitch (jacket copy draft)

> Eight months is a long time to be gone. In the Boland it is one season, and one season is not
> missing. She went for work.
>
> Pieter Buys used to profile serial murderers for a police unit that barely exists anymore. Now he
> writes risk assessments for wine estates and charges by the hour, because frightened rich people
> pay on time and dead poor women do not pay at all.
>
> Charmaine Adonis was fourteen years in FCS before her back and the service parted ways. She has a
> file nobody opened, a mother in Rawsonville who was told three times to wait, and a name.
>
> By Friday they have nine names. By the border they have a pattern that runs on the harvest
> calendar — six weeks of grapes, eight of citrus, a bakkie, a clipboard, and a country where a
> woman who leaves to work is not a woman who is gone.
>
> They will catch him. That is not the hard part. The hard part is the number.

## The thesis (say this to no one, build everything on it)

**Being findable is a purchased service.** The book's murderer is a man with a bakkie; the book's
antagonist is the arithmetic of who gets looked for. A crime novel set in this country that does not
know this is writing about a different country.

## The secondary thesis (the one that makes it *his* book)

The genius-detective is a lie about how a mind like this works, and it is a lie told almost entirely
by people who do not have one. The truth is less flattering and more interesting: **the gift and the
disability are the same organ.** You do not get the pattern without the four a.m. It is not a
superpower with a quirky downside. It is one thing, and it has a price, and he pays it every single
day, and he is good at his job anyway.

## Why the two of them

Neither can do it alone and both of them know it — no worship, no mystique. He has the pattern; she
has the door. The book proves this structurally: in Movement III she works the case *without* him,
in a kitchen, with tea, and gets further than he did.

## The ending, held from page one

They prove eleven. They believe more than thirty. The state confirms four. One mother gets a docket
number written on the back of a till slip because there is no form for it.

Nothing is fixed. Someone is holding a list.
