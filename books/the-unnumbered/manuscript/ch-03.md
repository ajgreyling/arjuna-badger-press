# Chapter 3

The house was in a street where every house had a wire fence and a gate, and none of the gates had a
sticker on them, and I noticed that I had noticed it, and I thought, *thanks, Piet*.

Aunty Miems Fortuin was sixty-one and about a metre and a half of her, and she had done her hair.
That is the detail I have never been able to get out. We had phoned at nine that morning, from the
office, forty minutes' notice, and she had done her hair and put out a cloth on the table and there
were biscuits from a shop, on a plate, arranged.

She had had eight months of nobody coming. So when somebody came, she did her hair.

"Kom in, kom in," she said. "Julle het gery. Sit."

Piet said, "Dankie, Mevrou Fortuin," and sat where she pointed, which was the chair with its back to
the window and the door in front of him, and I saw him take it and I saw him notice that he had been
given it rather than taken it, and something in his shoulders let go.

"Miems," she said. "Nobody says Mevrou Fortuin except the municipality."

"Miems," he said, and then he did not say anything else for two hours and eleven minutes.

---

I want to describe the kitchen and I am aware that this is the part where a certain kind of writer
starts doing poverty. So let me be careful, because Aunty Miems would take my head off.

It was a good kitchen. Small, hot, spotless, a fridge with a Vodacom magnet and a school photo of a
boy with his tie crooked, a pot cooking that she'd started before we arrived because we were coming
at eleven and eleven is near enough to lunch that you don't let people leave without eating. There
was a cross on the wall and a certificate from a church next to it in a frame, and on the counter a
Tupperware of dried apricots from a farm that she gave me half of when we left and that I ate in the
car and could not swallow properly.

She poured tea. She asked about the drive. She asked whether we'd come past De Doorns or over the
pass. She asked me where I was from and when I said Mitchells Plain she said *ja* in a particular
way, and I said *ja* back, and that was a whole conversation and it took two seconds.

Then she said, "So. Where must I start."

And I said, "Wherever you want, Aunty. I've got the whole day."

She looked at me for a second. And then she started, and she did not start with the ninth of
October. She started with Deline at seven years old and a bicycle with no chain on it, and I let her,
and Piet sat with his hands flat on his knees and did not move, and outside a dog was going on about
something and the pot ticked on the stove.

---

Deline Fortuin, twenty-six. The middle one. Two brothers, one in Worcester at a tyre place, one who
does not phone.

Matric, and this is a thing Miems said twice, in the same words both times: *sy het haar matriek
klaargemaak.* She finished it. She got the certificate. It is in the tin.

Then eight years of the thing that happens, which is that there is no work that is work, so you do
seasons. Grapes from December through to about March. Then whatever. Packhouse if you're lucky, and
you're lucky if you're known. She had done Ceres. She had done Wolseley. She had done one long
stretch in a packhouse in Wellington that she liked because it was inside and it was regular and the
supervisor was a woman.

"En sy stuur geld," said Miems. "Elke maand. Sonder dat ek vra."

Every month, without being asked. That went in my book with the date, because money is a record
even when nothing else is.

"How did she get the work?" I asked.

"Deur die mense."

"Which people?"

And Miems made a movement with her hand that I have seen my own mother make, and it means: the
arrangement, the way of things, the men who come with the bakkies. Not a person. A weather system.

"Was it always the same one?"

"Nee." She thought about it, and she was careful, and I want that on the record too, because when
this all came out afterwards there were people who said the families were confused, the families
couldn't give a straight story. She was not confused. She was being careful in a way that most
witnesses are not, because she had been over it three thousand times in the dark. "Verskillendes.
Maar hulle ken mekaar."

Different ones. But they know each other.

---

On the ninth of October Deline left for eight weeks. Not grapes — early citrus, up past Citrusdal,
that's what she said, and eight weeks would have brought her back the first week of December, in
time for the December work if she wanted it and in time for Christmas either way.

She phoned on the eleventh. Two days in. Said the place was fine, said the room was fine, said there
were four of them in it, said one of the girls was from Lesotho and could do hair. Miems told me
what Deline said about the hair for about four minutes and every word of it went in my book.

Then nothing.

Not "then a strange silence." Nothing is normal. Signal up there is signal. Data costs money. People
go quiet for two weeks at a time and it means nothing at all, and that is exactly, precisely the
gap this whole thing lives inside.

On the twenty-ninth she messaged. Two lines. One of the two screenshots the nephew printed. It said
*hi ma alles reg hier* and then a second one, eleven minutes later, that said *ek sal Vrydag bel.*

She did not phone on the Friday. Or the next one.

The first week of December came and Deline did not come home, and Miems told herself the season had
run long, which happens, which happens constantly, which is the most ordinary thing in the world.

Christmas came. Deline did not come home.

"En toe het ek geweet," she said.

Then I knew.

She did not cry when she said it. She said it the way you say a thing you have already said to
yourself so many times that it has worn smooth. She got up in the middle of it and stirred the pot,
because the pot needed stirring, and sat down again.

---

The three times.

**The first time** was the fourth of January. De Doorns station. She stood in the queue with her
sister-in-law and got to the counter and said her daughter had not come back from work.

She was asked how old the daughter was. Twenty-six.

She was asked whether they had had a fight.

She was asked whether the daughter had a boyfriend.

She was told that a grown woman is allowed to go where she likes, and that people come back after
the season, and that she must come back if the daughter is not home by the end of the month.

She said thank you. That is the part. She said thank you, and went home, and waited another
twenty-six days, because a man behind a counter had told her a thing with authority and she had been
raised to believe it.

**The second time** was the thirtieth of January, same station, different member. This time she had
the photograph with her. She was told that they cannot open a case for somebody who went for work,
and that she must go to the farm and ask the farm, and that if the farm says the person left, then
the person left.

She went to the farm. Two taxis and a lift. The farm office had a woman who was very nice to her and
who looked in a book and said there was no Fortuin, D. on the list for that season, and Miems said
that's not possible, and the woman said, well, sometimes they come through a contractor and then
they're not on our list, they're on his.

Whose?

The woman did not know. The woman was sorry.

**The third time** was the middle of March, at Worcester, because somebody had told her Worcester
was better. At Worcester a member listened to the whole story, which was more than she'd got before,
and then said that this was De Doorns's area and she must report it at De Doorns.

She did not go a fourth time.

I asked her why not, and she said — and this is the sentence, this is the one, out of two hours and
eleven minutes this is the one I still have —

"Ek het nie meer 'n storie gehad nie."

I didn't have a story anymore.

Because that's what it does. You go three times and every time you have to make it smaller to fit
across a counter, and by the third time you've said it so short and so fast and so apologetically
that you can hear how it sounds, and it sounds like nothing, and you start to believe the version
you've been forced to tell.

---

I told her the truth then. I had decided in the car that I would and I did it while I still had the
nerve.

I said: Aunty, there is no twenty-four-hour rule. There is no month rule. There is no rule that a
grown woman must be gone a certain time. The instruction is that they open it that day, the day you
walk in, and they take a form called a fifty-five A, and they fill it in with you standing there,
and they put her photograph on the system, and they give you the name of a detective and a number
you can phone. That is not me being kind to you. That is the standing instruction and it was the
standing instruction on the fourth of January.

She sat very still.

Then she said, "So hulle het gelieg."

And this is where I had to be careful, and I wasn't as careful as I should have been, and I've
thought about it since.

I said: No. I don't think anybody sat there deciding to lie to you. I think a tired person said a
thing that everybody says, that isn't true, and has never been true, and that thousands of people
believe including people wearing the uniform. Which is worse. Because a lie has somebody standing
behind it. This has nobody standing behind it at all.

"Dis nie beter nie," she said.

"No, Aunty. It's not better."

She got up and stirred the pot again for a long time.

---

Piet stood up at ten to two.

Two hours and eleven minutes, and the man had not said one word since *Miems*, and I could see what
it had cost him because I know where to look — the hands had come off the knees and gone flat on the
table about an hour in, which is his tell, and he had not touched the tea, and there was a fine
tremor going in his left leg that had been going for a while.

He said, "Mevrou Fortuin—"

"Miems."

"Miems." He stopped. Started again. "I am going to ask you for one thing and then we will go, and I
am sorry that it is a strange thing."

She waited.

"When Deline went — this time, in October — did anybody give her a piece of paper?"

Aunty Miems looked at him.

"A slip," he said. "Or a card. Or a number written down. Something from the man with the bakkie, or
from an office. It would be small. It would say a farm, or a week, or a number. It would not look
important."

And I watched a thing happen in that kitchen that I have thought about for two years since.

She got up. She went into the passage. We heard a cupboard, and a chair being moved, and then she
came back with a Quality Street tin, the old kind, and she put it on the table and took off the lid,
and inside was a funeral policy, an ID book, a birth certificate for a boy, three photographs, a
matric certificate with a green cover — *sy het haar matriek klaargemaak* — and a gold-coloured
watch that did not go.

And under all of it, folded twice, a piece of paper about the size of a till slip.

It had a cellphone number on it, and a date, and *CITRUS 8W*, and in the corner somebody had written
**C4** and circled it.

"Dit was in haar baadjie," said Miems. "Sy het dit gelos. Ek het dit gehou omdat dit hare was."

It was in her jacket. She left it behind. I kept it because it was hers.

Nobody had ever asked her for it. Not once, in eight months, at three counters, at one farm office.
Not because anybody was hiding anything. Because nobody had ever opened anything, and if you do not
open anything you do not ask for anything, and a piece of paper in a tin in a passage is not
evidence, it is a mother keeping her daughter's things.

Piet did not touch it either. He said, "May I photograph it?"

"Ja."

He photographed it, on the table, without picking it up, four times, from four angles, and then he
put his phone in his pocket and said, "Thank you. That is the only thing anybody has that is worth
anything, and you kept it for eight months, and you did not know that you were doing it."

Which was — I want to say clumsy, and it wasn't. She heard it exactly right. She put her hand flat
on the tin.

"Sal julle my bel?" she said. "Al is dit sleg nuus. Bel my."

"Yes," I said.

"Every Friday," said Piet, "whether there is news or not. That is easier than waiting for the phone
to ring."

He was right about that. He is right about that in a way that most people who are kinder than him
never work out, and I have used it a hundred times since with a hundred families, and I got it from
him, in that kitchen, at two o'clock in the afternoon.

---

She made us take food. Of course she did. She stood at the gate with her arms folded while I turned
the car, and she was still standing there when we came round the corner, and I looked in the mirror
and she had not moved.

We got to the R43 and Piet said, "C4."

"Crew four."

"Crew four." He was looking out of the window. "There is a crew one, two and three."

I did not answer, because he wasn't talking to me.

"And a crew five," he said.
