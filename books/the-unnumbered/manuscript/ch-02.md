# Chapter 2

Our office is one room above Riaan's bicycle shop, up a flight of green-painted stairs that smell
like rubber and degreaser all the way to the top, and I want to say something about that smell,
which is that it is the only building Piet Buys will walk into without preparing first.

I have watched him do it a hundred times. At the bottom of the stairs he is a man who has been out
in the world. By the landing he is not. Somewhere around the fourth step his shoulders come down
about two centimetres and stay there. I asked him about it once, early on, when I still asked
things, and he said, "It smells the same every day," and I said, "That's it?" and he said, "Yes,"
in a tone that suggested he could not understand why anybody would want more than that from a
building.

I have a lot of theories about Piet. That one I got right the first time and never improved on.

He went straight to the corner and started the kettle and did not say anything for eleven minutes,
which is normal for a Monday and was not the reason my heart was going.

The reason my heart was going was in my bag.

---

The room has two desks that don't match, a printer that we bought used and that we both hate, and
one wall that Piet keeps empty on purpose. Not empty like unfurnished. Empty like a rule. In three
years I have watched him take down a calendar, a poster from a bike race, and a framed photograph
of my daughter's matric dance that I put up without asking, and each time he moved it to the other
wall, carefully, without comment, and each time I understood a little more clearly that the man
needs one surface in his life where nothing is happening.

I have my back to that wall now, telling this, so you know how it is going to go.

The kettle clicked. He made the coffee — two, always two, one for him and one for whoever is in the
room, which is a piece of manners so consistent it took me a year to notice it was manners — and
put mine down on my desk on the coaster, which is a thing I don't use and he has never once
mentioned.

"The report went out at 06:40," he said. "Dirk's office acknowledged. He has not read page four."

"He's read page four."

"He acknowledged in four minutes. It is forty-one pages." He sat down. "He read page one and the
last page. He will phone on Thursday about page four and be angry with me on the phone in a way he
is currently angry with himself."

"You want to bet?"

"No," he said, honestly, and drank his coffee, and I put the file on his desk.

---

It was not a file. Let me be accurate, because this whole thing turns on paperwork and I am not
going to be sloppy about it in my own account.

It was a Checkers packet with a school exercise book in it, a page torn out of a diary, two printed
WhatsApp screenshots that a nephew had done at an internet café in Worcester, and a photograph, six
by four, of a woman in a red top standing next to a bakkie with her hand up against the sun.

There was no docket number on any of it, because there was no docket.

Piet did not touch it. He looked at it the way he looks at things, which is not the way it goes in
the films — there is no leaning in, no squinting, no picking up an object and turning it in the
light. He sat still with his hands on his knees and looked at a Checkers packet on his desk for
about forty seconds, and I watched the coffee stop being drunk.

"Whose?" he said.

"Her name is Deline Fortuin. She's twenty-six. She left Rawsonville on the ninth of last October
for eight weeks' work and she hasn't been home."

"Eight weeks is not eight months."

"No."

"Who is paying?"

"Nobody's paying, Piet."

He said nothing.

"Her mother's name is Wilhelmina Fortuin. Everyone calls her Aunty Miems. She reported it three
times."

His head came up. "Three."

"Three."

"To the same station?"

"Twice at De Doorns, once at Worcester."

"And the case number?"

And here it is, here is the sentence, and I have said it out loud maybe forty times since and it has
never once come out of my mouth easily.

"There isn't one. It was never opened."

---

Now. I want to describe what happened next properly, because people who have heard this story from
me before think they know, and what they think they know is wrong.

Nothing dramatic happened. He did not stand up. He did not sweep the desk. His face did not do
anything I could photograph and show you.

What happened is that he stopped.

He had been about to put the cup down, and the cup went down, and then his hand stayed on it, and
his eyes went to a point about a metre to the left of my head where the empty wall is, and he was
gone. Not upset. *Working.* And I sat there and drank my coffee and did not speak, because the one
thing I have learned about that state is that the cost of interrupting it is not measured in
seconds, it is measured in the rest of the day.

Ninety seconds. I counted, because I count things now; he's contagious.

Then he said, "Not lost."

"No."

"Lost is a thing that happens to a docket. There is no docket. So a person made a decision three
separate times, or three people each made it once." He was still looking at the wall. "Which is it?"

"I don't know."

"It matters."

"I know it matters."

"If it is three people it is a culture. If it is one person it is a man." He blinked, and came back
into the room, and looked at me for the first time since I put the packet down. "You know the
instruction."

"I know the instruction."

"Say it."

I put the cup down. "There's no twenty-four-hour rule. There never was. It's a myth. A missing
person is opened immediately and investigated immediately, and if you are the member behind that
counter you take the fifty-five A and you fill it in with the complainant standing there, whether
she came in at nine in the morning or two in the morning, whether the person is six or sixty, and
you give her your name and your cell number before she leaves. That's not a guideline. That's the
instruction. Parliament has had to send out a *statement* reminding stations that it is the
instruction."

"And she was told to wait."

"She was told to come back tomorrow. Three times. In eight months."

Piet looked at the packet again.

"Then nobody broke the law," he said.

"No."

"Nobody will be disciplined."

"No."

"And there is no record that she was ever there." He said it flatly, no emphasis anywhere in the
sentence, which is how you can tell with him. "The state does not know it was asked. That is worse
than refusing. A refusal leaves a mark."

---

Here is the thing I have to put in, because I said I'd be honest in this account and it is the part
that doesn't flatter me.

I had had that packet for four days.

Four days I carried a woman's mother's exercise book around in my bag, into a Shoprite, to my
sister's on the Saturday, to the estate on the Monday morning while we talked about a man stealing
wine on Tuesdays. And people will say, ag, Charl, you were being careful, you were checking it
first, you wanted to be sure before you took it to him.

I was sure on the first afternoon. Any FCS detective would have been sure by the second cup of tea.

What I was doing for four days was deciding whether to hand a man like Piet Buys a thing like that,
because I knew — I *knew*, the way you know the weather is coming before you see it — that once he
had it he would not be able to put it down. Not wouldn't. Couldn't. That is not a figure of speech
about a determined man. That is a description of how he is built. He will not sleep properly again
until it closes, and some of these never close, and I have seen what a thing that does not close
does to him and I did not want to be the person who handed him one.

I handed it to him anyway. Because Deline Fortuin was twenty-six and her mother had asked three
times, and I could carry the packet or I could carry that, and I have carried that one before and I
know what it costs and it costs more.

He can pay his bill. I have to pay mine either way.

---

"Do you want the background," I said, "or do you want to go?"

He was already standing. He does not do the thing where he announces it.

"Where does she live?"

"Rawsonville."

"Then we go now, and you talk," he said, "and I will not talk, because she has told this three times
to people who did not write it down, and if I ask her to start again from the beginning she will do
it, and it will cost her more than it costs us, and we will get less."

He took his jacket off the back of the chair, and then he stopped, and he put it back.

"Not the jacket," he said, mostly to himself. "Not in a kitchen."

And I want to be very clear that this is what people are talking about when they say he doesn't
understand people. He had the whole thing worked out before we were down the stairs: who talks, why
she must not be made to start again, and what a jacket says in a kitchen in Rawsonville about who is
sitting at the table and who is being interviewed.

He gets it wrong sometimes. God knows. I'm going to tell you about the time he got it wrong and a
man nearly died for it, and I'm not going to soften that.

But this idea people have, that he doesn't understand — no. He understands. He just can't do it and
be in a room at the same time.

We took the N1 out past Paarl with the mountains going brown-gold on the left and the trucks coming
down the pass at us out of the heat, and Piet did not say one word for fifty minutes, and I let him
have it, because he was going to need it.
