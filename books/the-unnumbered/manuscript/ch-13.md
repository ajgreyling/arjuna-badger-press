# Chapter 13

The tracking archive arrived at 15:52 that afternoon.

Ninety-one minutes from a man saying yes in a restaurant to four years of vehicle movement data
sitting on a hard drive on a desk above a bicycle shop in Stellenbosch, delivered by a courier in a
marked vehicle, signed for, with a compliance letter attached explaining the lawful basis on which
it was being shared with a contracted risk consultant, which we now apparently were.

Colonel Thandeka Nyathi, of the South African Police Service, had a vehicle available on the
eleventh.

I have never got over that. I don't think I'm supposed to.

---

The swabs took two days and they were the best two days of that whole year, and I did not expect
that and neither did Piet.

Here is what I thought it would be: six grieving women being asked to give up a piece of their own
body so that a machine could tell them their daughter was dead.

Here is what it was.

Aunty Miems sat at her own kitchen table with the cloth on it and the biscuits out, and I explained
it, and Piet stood by the door because a kitchen with a stranger doing a procedure in it needs one
fewer man in the middle of it than you think. I said: this is a cotton bud. I rub it inside your
cheek for four seconds. It doesn't hurt, it doesn't take blood, and it can't be used for anything
except comparing to Deline.

And I said the hard part, because I had promised myself I would say the hard part every single time
and never soften it: *this may never be run. There is no programme. There is no queue we're joining.
We are keeping these so that on the day somebody is willing to run them, we do not have to come back
to you and ask.*

Aunty Miems said, "Gee hier."

She opened her mouth like a woman at a dentist and I did four seconds inside her cheek and put the
bud in the tube and sealed it and she signed the form and Piet witnessed it and dated it.

And then she sat back and said, "So nou is ek in die ding."

Now I'm in the thing.

That's it. That is what it was. Not grief. *Participation.* For eight months she had been a woman
who phoned people. Now there was a tube with her name on it in a fridge and it could not be taken
away from her, and when the day came — if the day came — she had already done her part and done it
first.

Five out of six of them said some version of the same sentence. Grieta, who was not on our list and
whose daughter had had a case number for two years, held the tube afterwards and looked at it for a
while and said, "Dis meer as wat hulle in twee jaar gedoen het."

That's more than they did in two years.

It cost eleven rand and it took four seconds.

---

We had, by the end of that week, in a private office above a bicycle shop, the following.

Six DNA samples with consent forms, witnessed, dated, in a validated cold chain in a facility in
Century City owned by a listed company.

Four years of vehicle movement across two corridors.

Fuel-card transaction histories for fourteen registration numbers.

Eleven women, five new dockets, one provincial series reference, and a colonel who read her email.

And the State of the Republic of South Africa had: a series reference, and a vehicle available on
the eleventh.

I sat on the floor of the office on the Friday night after everybody had gone home and had a small,
private, entirely unproductive crisis about it.

Because I had spent fourteen years inside that state. I had done the FCS course. I had sat in a room
in 2006 and been told my unit was being decentralised and had watched every specialist I knew get
sent to a station to do house robberies, and I had sat in another room in 2010 and been told we were
being re-established, and had done four more years of it with two vehicles for eleven detectives.
And the whole time I believed — I actually believed — that the problem was money and that if the
money came the thing would work.

The money came. It went to Century City. It bought eleven thousand four hundred members and a screen
with eleven hundred dots on it and an average response of four minutes eleven, and it is *very
good*, and it does not attend to Deline Fortuin, because Deline Fortuin never had a contract.

That's not corruption. Nobody stole it. It was never in the state's hands to steal. It went straight
past.

---

Piet came back at about nine and found me on the floor and did not ask what was wrong, which is his
best quality.

He sat down against the other desk with the hard drive in his lap.

"Four years," he said.

"Ja."

"Every vehicle that his client base insured, every route, four years, timestamped to the second." He
was turning it over in his hands. "There is a very good chance that the answer to all of this is on
here, and has been on here for four years, and that nobody has ever had a reason to look."

"Is that supposed to make me feel better?"

"No," he said. "I have been sitting in a car park for forty minutes trying to decide whether to say
it to you at all."

"Say it."

"If we find him with this," said Piet, "then the reason he was not found before is not that it was
impossible. It is that the data was privately owned and the question was never asked by anybody who
could pay for it." He looked up. "Which means that the difference between eleven women and none was
a *purchase order*. Not a genius. Not a breakthrough. An invoice."

I put my head back against the desk.

"He's going to be so ordinary," I said. "Isn't he."

"Yes," said Piet. "I have never had any doubt about that."

---

I asked him, that night, the thing I had been circling since the lobby with the water feature.

"Why haven't you asked him before? Eleven years, Piet. He'd have given you anything."

And he thought about it long enough that I knew the answer was going to be an honest one.

"Because he would have given me anything," he said.

"That's not—"

"It is exactly the reason." He set the drive down. "If I can have it any time I want it, then every
case I do not solve becomes a case in which I chose not to ask my brother. And every case I do
solve, I have solved by being the man whose brother owns eleven thousand four hundred members." He
turned his hands over and looked at them, which he does. "Adonis, I am the profiler who could not
stay in a unit and cannot get through a Saturday in a shopping centre. The one thing I have is that
what I do, I do out of what is publicly available and what is in my head. If I take the helicopter
every time, then in eleven years I will be a man who phones his brother."

"You'd have found them faster."

"Yes."

"Some of them might—"

"Yes," said Piet. "I have thought about that every day since the fourteenth of March three years
ago, and I will be thinking about it on the day I die, and I would like now to talk about something
else, please."

So we talked about something else.

But I want it in the account, because two months later, in a different province, a version of that
exact reasoning is going to get a man beaten half to death in a street in Springbok, and when it
happens people are going to ask me whether there were any warnings.

There were. There was this one. He told me himself, on a floor, on a Friday night, in his own words:
*the one thing I have is what is in my head.*

You cannot audit what is in a man's head. That's the whole problem. And he had been alone in there
with a pencil for three years and four months.
