# Chapter 16

The fuel card went north.

That is the whole of this chapter and I could stop there, but the going north is the part where this
stopped being a case and became the eleven weeks that took my back out again and cost me my
daughter's prelim results evening, so I am going to do it properly.

Four years of diesel. Every shoulder window, the same shape: Vredendal, Klawer, Garies, Springbok.
And then, in five of the eight windows, one more transaction past Springbok — **Steinkopf**, which
is the last place you can buy diesel in the Republic, sixty-seven kilometres short of the line.

That on its own means nothing. Somebody filling up at Steinkopf is a man going to Steinkopf.

It was Piet who saw the other thing, and he saw it at about half past eleven at night, and he did
not phone me, he waited until the morning, which for him is a kind of restraint I did not know he
had.

In two of the eight windows, the next transaction on that card — the very next one, forty minutes
later — is at **Noordoewer**.

I had to look it up. I am South African and I have lived here my whole life and I had to look up
where Noordoewer is.

It is on the other side.

There is no filling station at Vioolsdrif. There has never been one. You come up the N7 through the
Richtersveld and the last pump in this country is at Steinkopf, and then there is nothing, and then
there is a bridge over the Orange River, and on the far side of that bridge the road stops being the
N7 and becomes the B1, and about four kilometres in there is a garage.

So a fleet card belonging to a close corporation that was deregistered two years ago, on a bakkie
registered to a man who died in 2021, bought diesel in the Republic of Namibia. Twice. In the
shoulder. Forty minutes after Steinkopf.

Nobody in this book ever had to say the word *border*. The card said it.

---

I took it to Nyathi on the Tuesday, and she did the thing I already knew she was going to do, and
she did it kindly.

"I can't."

"Colonel—"

"Adonis, listen to me properly, because I want you to be able to explain this to those mothers and I
don't want you explaining it wrong." She had the page in front of her. "My series is Western Cape. I
have a colleague in Northern Cape who has taken my call twice and been decent both times, and if I
send him this he will read it, and he will believe it, and he will put it on a pile with his own
nine columns on it. That is not obstruction. That's Tuesday."

"And the border?"

"The border is not police work. The border is a mutual legal assistance request, which is Justice,
not SAPS, and which goes to a national office, and which takes—" she stopped. "I was going to say
months."

"How long, Colonel?"

"I have been waiting two years and four months on one," she said, "for a matter in which we have a
body, a suspect, and a confession."

---

So that was that.

I want to be clear that Nyathi did not throw us out and did not tell us not to go. What she did was
lean back and look at the ceiling for a while and then say a sentence very carefully, in the manner
of a woman placing something on a table without touching it.

"There is nothing in law," she said, "that prevents a member of the public from travelling to
another country and asking members of the public in that country questions."

"No, Colonel."

"And if a member of the public happened to obtain, lawfully, from a member of the public, information
which they then handed to me—"

"That would just be a person giving information to the police."

"That is exactly what that would be," said Nyathi. "That happens forty times a day in this
building."

She sat forward.

"Now. Here is the other half, and this is not the fun half. If you go up there and you do this
badly, you will burn it. Not for you — for me. If a Namibian officer's first contact with this
matter is two South Africans arriving without notice and asking about a dead woman, then when I
eventually get my request through, in two years, the file will already have a note on it saying two
private investigators were poking around, and every single thing you touched will be worth nothing."
She held my eye. "I have seen a good case die of that. Do it properly. Talk to the families, not the
police. And Adonis — when you are up there, you are nobody. You have no authority whatsoever. I
cannot help you. If something happens to you I will find out about it from the news."

"Yes, Colonel."

"Say it back."

"We're nobody."

"You're nobody," she said.

---

Here is why we had to go, and it is not because of the fuel card.

The grapes on the Orange River come in before the Cape grapes do. That is not a coincidence of
climate, it is the whole basis of the industry up there — a hot river valley that ripens weeks
ahead, so the crews go north for that season and come south again for ours.

Which means that the six-to-nine days when the Cape has no work is *exactly* the six-to-nine days
when there is movement north — and, coming the other way, that a woman who leaves a Cape valley in
the shoulder and does not arrive anywhere has, as her most ordinary and most believable
explanation, the sentence: *sy's Oranjerivier toe.*

She's gone to the river.

And here is what I did not understand about the river until I stood in it, and what I had entirely
backwards on the drive up.

I thought: a border town, a labour camp, a lawless place where anybody can disappear.

It is not that. It is a settlement of about five thousand people on the bank of the Orange River
that becomes, for nine weeks from the beginning of November, a town of twenty thousand — and the
fifteen thousand who arrive do not come from our side. They come down from the north of Namibia, a
thousand kilometres and more, for the early grapes, and in the second week of January they go home
again and the place shrinks back to five thousand and the reed houses stand empty.

So a woman from Rawsonville who goes up there is not vanishing into chaos. She is arriving into a
place where she is the foreigner, where not one person knows her face, where everybody around her is
also from somewhere else and also leaving in January, and where the only census that has ever been
taken is of the town that exists in the off-season.

There is no province. There is no docket. There is no country, in the sense that matters — because
she is South African and she is in Namibia, and the state that might look for her has no standing
there, and the state that is there has no reason to know she was ever a person who came.

She is not hidden. She is simply not in anybody's arithmetic.

I heard Elsie Mtimkulu's voice saying it while I was looking at that river, and I had to go and sit
in the car.

Piet said it in one line at the kitchen table at my house at eleven at night while my daughter was
asleep upstairs.

"The window is a place," he said. "I had it wrong. It is not only when nobody expects to hear from
her. It is where nobody's records are."

---

I found four missing-persons appeals on Namibian community Facebook pages in two evenings. Four.
Two of them had South African names.

Piet found nine more in one night, because of course he did, and two of the nine were on a page for
a farm settlement on the river where the population in season is in the tens of thousands and out of
season is not.

We did not know what any of them meant. We still don't know what most of them meant. I want that in,
because when the number went public later, it went public as a number, and numbers do not carry the
words *we do not know what most of these mean*, and I have spent two years trying to put those words
back on.

---

I told Aunty Miems on the Friday.

"Ons gaan op," I said. "Noordkaap toe, en dan miskien oor die grens."

She was quiet.

"Aunty?"

"Hoe lank?"

"I don't know. Weeks."

"En sal jy nog Vrydae bel?"

And I said yes, and I meant it, and I did — from Springbok, from Kakamas, from a filling station at
Vioolsdrif with the wind taking the door out of my hand, from a room in Keetmanshoop with the phone
held up against the window for signal. Every Friday. Eleven of them.

Except one, and I will get to it.

---

We left on the Monday.

Riaan came out to the pavement in his apron and gave Piet a bicycle pump, which I did not understand
and did not ask about, and Piet put it in the boot without comment, and it is still in the boot of
that car today.

I drove first. Out through Malmesbury and up the N7 with the sun coming over on the right, past the
wheat and then out of the wheat, and by Piketberg the country had started doing the thing it does,
which is get bigger and emptier and older, and by Clanwilliam neither of us had spoken for an hour
and it was the good kind.

Somewhere past Vanrhynsdorp Piet said, "Adonis."

"Ja."

"If I become certain," he said, "will you tell me."

I took my eyes off the road for a second to look at him, and he was looking straight ahead with his
hands on his knees.

"Ja, Piet," I said. "I'll tell you."

I want that on the record too. He asked. Out of his own mouth, unprompted, four hundred kilometres
before it mattered, he asked me to do exactly the thing I then did not do.
