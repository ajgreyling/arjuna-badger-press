# Chapter 7

I had the map wrong for four days and so did he, and I want that in, because afterwards everybody
talked about it as though he had looked at nine names and seen it whole.

We had a map. Riaan came up with it — an old AA road map of the Western and Northern Cape, the paper
kind, with the fold lines gone furry — and he stood in the doorway with it under his arm and said,
"Ek het gehoor julle soek 'n kaart," and Piet said, "Thank you, Riaan," and Riaan said, "Bring dit
terug," and went back downstairs, and that was the entire transaction, and it is the only friendship
I have ever watched Piet Buys conduct without visible effort.

We put pins in it. Nine pins. And the nine pins made a shape that looked like something, which is
the most dangerous thing a map can do.

They went up the country. De Doorns, Worcester, Touwsrivier, then Citrusdal, then Clanwilliam, then
two in the Northern Cape near Kakamas, and one, right at the top, at Keimoes.

"It's the N7," I said. "It's the road."

"It is a road," said Piet.

"It's a corridor, Piet, that's — look at it. That's a straight line."

"It is a straight line because the country is a straight line." He was on the floor with his back
against the desk. "There are four roads north. Everything is on one of them. If I put pins in nine
weddings they would also make a corridor."

"So what, it means nothing?"

"It means what it says. It says that women who go for seasonal work travel on the roads that go to
seasonal work." He rubbed his eyes with the heels of his hands. "It is a map of the agriculture. I
have made a map of agriculture."

---

He was flat for two days after that. Not the Wednesday kind of flat — a duller thing, a man who has
been running on the certainty that the shape would arrive and has had it not arrive.

I did the ordinary work. Somebody has to invoice. Dirk phoned on the Thursday about page four, angry
in exactly the manner predicted, and I took it and did not tell Piet he'd been right because he
would not have enjoyed it.

On the Saturday I went out to Rawsonville and sat with Aunty Miems for two hours, off the clock, and
we did the roof and her grandson and a long thing about her sister-in-law, and on the way home I
stopped at the top of the pass and looked down at the valley in the late light, the whole of it, the
vines in their lines and the packhouses with their roofs going pink and the little town, and I
thought: eleven women in one crew. Sixteen people. Five crews. One page.

And I thought: how many crews are down there right now.

I phoned him from the top of the pass.

"It's not the road," I said.

"I know it is not the road."

"No, listen. It's not *where*. It's *when*."

There was a silence on the phone that went on long enough that I looked at the screen to see if it
had dropped.

"Say that again," he said.

"October ninth. Deline goes for eight weeks of citrus. That's the shoulder — grapes aren't started
yet, citrus is just going. Bernadine's the second of October. My third one, Neliswa, that's the
beginning of March, and the grape season's just ending, so she's between—"

"Say the word again."

"Between."

"Yes," he said. "Between. Get here."

---

I got there at twenty past eight on a Saturday night and he had already taken the pins out.

The map was on the floor and it was blank again and he had turned it over, and on the back of it, on
the white side, he had drawn a year. Just a long line across the paper with twelve marks on it.

Then he had put the nine women on it. Not by where. By when.

And they sat on it like teeth.

Not spread. Not random. They clustered in two places on that line, and both places were gaps: the
first week of October, when the citrus crews assemble and the grape crews have not; and the last
week of March into April, when the grapes finish and everybody who has been working since December
is suddenly, all at once, unemployed and mobile and on a road somewhere with cash in their pocket
and no fixed address until the next thing starts.

"The shoulder," he said.

"The shoulder."

"That is the window." He was crouched over it. "It is not a place. It is a condition. It is the six
to nine days in which a woman has left one arrangement and has not yet arrived at the next, and
during which *there is no one on earth who expects to hear from her*." He looked up at me. "Her
mother does not expect a phone call, because she is travelling. The old farm does not expect her,
because the contract is finished. The new farm does not expect her, because the crew has not been
called yet. The broker does not expect her, because he has not counted a head since the ninth of
October."

"And that's not eight days," I said. "That's—"

"That is eight days in which she vanishes, and then six weeks in which nobody is surprised, and then
a season in which people begin to wonder, and then a year in which people assume, and then she is
gone." He sat back on his heels. "The disappearance is eight days. The *silence* is engineered by
the calendar. He is not hiding anything. He does not have to hide anything. The system hides the
absence for him, for free, every year, twice a year, and it has been doing it since before he
started and it will do it after he stops."

I sat down on the floor.

"How long," I said.

"How long what?"

"How long has he been doing it? If the window's twice a year and nobody notices for a year—"

And Piet Buys did the thing he does about once every two months, which is that he did not answer a
question.

He got up, and went to the kettle, and boiled it, and made two cups, and put mine down on the coaster
I don't use.

"Piet."

"Nine is what I can defend," he said, with his back to me.

"That's not what I asked."

"No," he agreed.

---

I want to be fair to him about that night, because when it came out later I was angry in a way I am
not proud of, and some of that anger was really about being tired.

He was not lying to me. He has never lied to me. He answered exactly the question I should have
asked, which is: what can you defend? And nine was what he could defend. Nine had a name, a
comment, a last post, a date. Nine you could take to a colonel.

But I had asked how long, and he had heard how many, and he had already run it, days before, on the
back of an envelope — I found the envelope afterwards, in the file, because he never throws anything
away — and he had put it away in the drawer of his desk with the pages he does not put on the wall.

He was protecting me. Which is a kind thing to do and it is also, exactly and precisely, the thing
that goes wrong in the third act of this story.

We drank the coffee sitting on the floor with a map of a year between us, and he said, "On Monday we
go to the police," and I said, "Ja," and neither of us said the word out loud, and both of us knew
that on Monday somebody was going to have to.
