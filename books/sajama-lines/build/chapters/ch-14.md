# Chapter 14 — The Words Withheld

The frost had not lifted off the *paja brava* when Inés Mamani came for him, and Bashir was already awake, which was its own small embarrassment — awake because he had spent the night running her grandmother's Aymara through his head the way some people count sheep, turning the agglutinated verbs over, the way the language stacked a whole sentence into one long-jointed word: the going, the carrying, the saying, the for-whom, all welded into a single breath. He could not stop doing it. He had never been able to stop. The tin roof of the *estancia* ticked as the sun touched it, and somewhere a llama complained, and Bashir lay in his sleeping bag mouthing *sarañani*, *let us go*, and feeling the cold air saw at the back of his throat at four thousand metres.

She did not knock. There was no door to knock on, only a wool blanket hung across the opening, and she drew it aside and stood there in her *pollera* and two cardigans and a felt hat, and looked at him the way she had looked at him from the first hour — patiently, as at weather.

"*Jutam*," she said. Come.

He came.

Outside, the plain went on the way it always went on, pale ochre to the foot of the white cone, and the cone above it impossible, hung in a sky so deep at the top that it stopped being blue and became something with a violet in it that Bashir had no word for in any of his eleven languages, and he had checked. Mara would have a word for it in light. Naila would have a number. He had nothing, and for once the having-nothing did not feel like the loneliness it usually was. It felt like standing under something.

They walked. Not on a line — beside one. He had learned, in the days here, that you did not put your boots on the scraped track unless you were walking it for the walking, and this morning was not that. The line ran north of them, a pale ribbon a metre wide where the dark oxidised surface stones had been cleared away three thousand years deep, exposing the lighter subsoil, kept bright — he understood this now in his body and not just from the dossier — kept bright only because feet kept clearing it. Stop, and it greens over. The largest drawing on the earth, and it lives the way a fire lives: only while tended.

Below them, half a kilometre off and downwind, he could hear the mapping team's generator. A flat industrial buzz that did not belong to the plain. Dr. Renske Vos's people had their drone cases out in the grey light, the foam-cut Pelican boxes open like clams, and a man in a high-vis vest was planting a survey marker with a mallet, the *tock* of it carrying clean across the cold air the way every sound carried up here, wrong and too close. They had been refused the trailhead. They were mapping the approach to it instead, which was permitted, which was legal, and which Inés walked past every morning without turning her head, the way you walk past a dog that is barking behind a fence it cannot clear.

"They will make a beautiful map," Inés said. In Spanish, this — she chose Spanish when she wanted him to hear it as a thing said *at* the team and not *to* the land. "And the map will be correct."

"And it will show nothing," Bashir said.

She looked at him sideways. He had got that right, and they both knew he had got it from her and not from himself, and she let him keep it anyway, which was its own kind of gift.

---

She stopped him at a *wak'a* — a shrine, though "shrine" was a thieving word for it, too churchy. It was a low rock, weathered, a darker stone than the plain around it, with the line running straight to its foot and straight away again from its other side, dead north on a bearing that, Naila had confirmed two nights ago with her phone and a tripod and a great deal of muttering, did not waver more than a hand's width over the nearest two kilometres of broken ground, including a low ridge it had climbed rather than gone around.

*It refused to curve around the hill,* Naila had said, and there had been something in her voice Bashir recognised, because it was the voice he used when a language did something his ear could not yet model. *It would have been easier to go around. It is always easier to go around. They didn't.*

He could see the ridge from here. The line went over it like a seam in cloth, and you could not see the far side of the ridge from this rock, and you could not see this rock from the far side of the ridge, and there was no high ground anywhere that let you see both at once, and there never had been, and a society of separate walkers with no king and no instrument and no vantage had held that bearing arrow-straight for who-knew-how-long, and the published people, the honest ones who had spent careers on this, wrote down plainly that nobody fully understood how. Bashir had read that sentence in the dossier and felt the floor of it tilt — not toward anything, just open. Just *open.*

Inés set a small bundle down at the foot of the rock. Coca leaves, folded in a square of cloth. She did not unwrap it for him, and he did not look too hard, and that was the first of the morning's withholdings, and he let it be one.

Then she said: "I am going to say something. You will hear it. You will not write it. You will not say it again. You will not make it into another language."

The fish — and there was no fish, there was only Bashir, but somewhere in the back of his skull a voice that had been built over years of listening, a dry heckling voice that he privately thought of as the part of him that survived checkpoints, that voice said: *oh, here we go, the forbidden phrase, the magic words, very atmospheric, what's it pay—*

— and then she said it, and the voice shut up.

He could not have written it down if she had begged him to. That was the thing he had not expected. It was not that the words were hard; some of them he knew. It was that they came in an order, with a weight on certain syllables, with a pause where his ear told him no pause should grammatically go, and the pause *was* the meaning, the pause was load-bearing, and the whole thing was built to be said *while walking*, he could feel that — the rhythm of it was a gait, it was paced to feet on ground, it was a sentence you laid down a metre at a time, and the straightness of the line was not an achievement held *against* the terrain at all.

It was the fossil of the saying.

You said the words, in order, in the going, and the bearing held because the words held the bearing, because you could not say them and drift, the saying *was* the surveying, the naming-while-going kept the line true the way a melody keeps a singer on pitch without the singer ever seeing a stave. No vantage. No instrument. The instrument was the mouth and the feet and three thousand years of people who knew the words and walked them in the right order, and the map from the air showed none of it because the map from the air was the one view no maker had ever had or needed or wanted.

He stood very still at the foot of the rock and felt the whole architecture of it arrive in him at once, the way a language sometimes did — not learned, *received*, dropping into place with a click he felt in his sternum.

And then, half a second behind the wonder, the second thing arrived, and it was heavier.

He understood it. And he could not have it.

---

This was the part Bashir had spent his whole life on the wrong side of. His entire usable self was *reaching all the way in* — that was the gift, that was the thing checkpoints could not strip-search out of him, the thing that had kept a stateless boy alive across borders that wanted him to be no one: he learned the next mouth so completely that the room half-disarmed, that the guard heard his own grandmother's village in a stranger's vowels and softened. He carried conversations the way other people carried passports. He had never, not once, met a closed door he could not at least make smaller.

And here was a sentence he had heard — *heard*, taken in, felt the structure of — that he was not to carry.

Not a sentence he failed to understand. That happened, that was just work, that was the ordinary friction he loved. This was worse and better. He understood it well enough to know that understanding it more was not the point, that translation would not deepen it but *empty* it, that to render it into English or Arabic or Afrikaans or any of the tongues he wore like coats would be to lift the meaning out of the feet that made it and pin it to a page where it would die the way the line dies when the walking stops. The words were alive the way the line was alive: only in the use. Write them down and you'd have a corpse and call it preservation.

Down the slope, faint, the generator buzzed, and the mallet went *tock*, and a man read coordinates aloud to another man in flat Dutch-accented English, numbers, beautiful correct numbers, a map that would be right and show nothing.

Bashir thought, with a clarity that hurt: *they will never get this, because there is nothing here for an instrument to take. And neither will I. And the difference between me and them is the only thing that matters, and the difference is that I know to stop.*

He did not say any of this. Inés would not have wanted it said; saying it would have been its own small theft, dressing her withholding up in his words. He just stood, and let the loss be a loss — because it was one, he was honest enough not to pretend the door closing didn't cost him, the polyglot ache of being so close to a tongue and being told *not this far, friend, not you, not this* — and he let the respect be the larger thing, sitting on top of the loss like the cone sat on top of the plain.

Inés watched him take it. He understood, after, that this had been the whole test, and that it had not been a test of whether he could learn the phrase — she'd known he could — but of whether he could be told *no* and not push the door. Niamh had read this in her days ago and signed it to him across a cold breakfast, her hands quick and exact: *she's not deciding if you're clever. She decided that on day one. She's deciding if you'll stop.*

"You will not say it again," Inés said. Not a question.

"I will not say it again," Bashir said, in Aymara, the courtesy form, and got it right, and meant it more than he had ever meant anything in any language he owned.

She nodded once. And then — and this undid him more than the refusal had — she smiled, a small dry mountain smile, and switched into Spanish, and gave him a different thing, an *easier* thing, a thing he *could* carry, the way you give a child the safe knife.

"You can tell them what it is *for*," she said.

He waited.

"It is for walking," Inés said. "Write that. That one is true. That one you can take."

He almost laughed, because it was a door that opened by being shut, because she had handed him the answer to the only question his whole crew had crossed an ocean to ask and the answer was a closed gate with a sign on it that said the gate is the point. *It is for walking.* Grounded. Physical. A thing you do with your body in order, in the going, and the largest drawing on earth was just the long bright fossil of three thousand years of people doing it.

Below, the mallet stopped. The generator cut out — out of fuel, or someone had thought better of it — and in the sudden enormous silence of the plain the wind came over the ridge with the line in it, the *surazo*, cold and clean, and carried nothing he could write down.

Bashir turned the unwritten phrase over once more, privately, the last time he would let himself, feeling its gait, its load-bearing pause, the place where the bearing held. Then he set it down at the foot of the rock with the coca leaves, in his head, where it would stay, and walked back beside the line — never on it — toward the others, carrying the one true sentence she'd let him keep, and leaving the other one exactly where it lived.

In the feet. On the ground. In a language that was not his to lend.
