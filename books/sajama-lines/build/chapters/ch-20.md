# Chapter 20 — The Road Home

The Beast wouldn't start until the sun cleared the queñua, and Naila found she didn't mind waiting. Cold had got into the diesel overnight the way it got into everything here — the water in the jerry can skinned with ice, her own breath frozen to the inside of the canvas, the camera batteries dead until Mara warmed them against her stomach like eggs. The plain held the night long after the sky let go of it. Frost lay blue in the shadow of every tussock.

She had her notebook open on her knees. She had filled most of it.

There were the bearings she'd taken with the hand compass, page after page, and the corrections she'd worked back from them, and the long column of numbers where she'd tried — for nine days — to make the straightness do what straightness was supposed to do, which was *resolve*. A line is the shortest distance between two points. That is the whole of it. Give Naila two points and an afternoon and she would give you a line, and it would be true, because she had a horizon to lay it against and a level to swear by and the patience to walk it twice.

The line above the village had none of that and was straighter than hers.

She'd measured it. That was the part she kept coming back to, the part her hands believed before the rest of her did. She'd set the surveyor's app and the borrowed total station — the mapping company's, lent for an hour by a sheepish junior tech before his supervisor noticed — against a segment that ran two kilometres over a low rise and down the far side, a segment nobody alive could see end to end from any point on the ground. The deviation across that span was less than the width of the line itself. A metre, in two thousand. Held over a hill that hid one end from the other.

"You've read that page forty times," Bashir said. He was sitting on the running board with a tin mug going cold in his hands, watching the woodsmoke come up off the village in straight grey columns because there was no wind yet. There was never wind, at this hour. The *surazo* slept till afternoon.

"I keep waiting for it to be wrong," Naila said.

"Is it?"

"No." She closed the book on her thumb. "That's the problem. If it were wrong I could fix it."

---

They had agreed to leave, the night before, around the stove in the long adobe room, and the agreeing had not felt like a decision so much as a tide going out. Frik had said it first, in the gap between two tics, the way the truest things came out of him — *we should go before we start owning it.* Nobody argued. Niamh had watched the *yatiri's* face when the words were interpreted to her and then signed something short and flat to her terp, who relayed it without softening: *She already knew that. She's been waiting to see if you did.*

So: out-read again. Naila was getting used to the feeling, which was not a comfortable feeling and was not meant to be.

She had wanted to solve it. She could say that now, to the page if to no one else. She had come up the plain certain that the straightness was a *how* with an answer, a clever procedure lost to time that a careful enough mind could reverse-engineer — fire-cairns sighted at night, maybe, or a chain of walkers calling bearings, or the old beautiful trick of three stakes and a sightline repeated and repeated up the slope. She'd modelled every one of them. She'd built the cairn version in her notebook and walked the math up the ridge and watched the error accumulate, the way error always accumulates, a degree begetting a degree, until two kilometres out her best honest reconstruction wandered eleven metres off true.

Eleven metres. The real line wandered one.

"You want to know the thing I can't write down," she said.

Bashir tipped his head. He had a way of going still when someone was about to hand him a sentence that mattered — the polyglot's stillness, she thought, the ear clearing itself.

"It isn't that they were better than me," Naila said. "I could believe that. Better surveyors than me have existed. It's that they *weren't surveying.* Nobody was holding the whole line in their head. There was no whole line in anybody's head, ever, not once in three thousand years. It got built a few hundred metres at a time by people who'd never see the far end and never wanted to. And it's straighter than the thing I'd build *with* the far end."

"And that's—"

"That's not a how question." She looked at the rise above the village, at the pale scratch of the line going up it and over and not coming back. "I had the wrong question the whole way up the plain. I kept asking how they kept it straight. Wrong verb. They didn't *keep* it anything. The straight is just — what's left. After."

---

*After what,* the obvious follow-up, she did not say, because she had already learned where that question stopped being hers.

The *yatiri* came up from the village while the diesel was still too cold to turn over. She came on foot, the way she came everywhere, in the layered skirts and the hard small hat and the woven *aguayo* knotted at her shoulder, and she did not hurry, because nobody on this plain hurried; hurry cost air you didn't have. She stopped a few metres off and looked at the Beast and the half-loaded gear and the crew arranged around it in the postures of people who had decided to go but hadn't moved yet.

She said something to Bashir. Naila caught maybe one word in five now, after nine days — enough to hear the shape, not the sense.

Bashir answered her in the Aymara that had half-opened this whole door, the careful courteous register he'd worked at for years before any of them knew her, and the *yatiri* allowed herself the small upward pull at the corner of her mouth that was, on her, a whole conversation. She answered him. He went quiet for a moment, the way he'd gone quiet twice now this trip when a sentence came at him that he could carry but had decided not to hand on.

"She'll walk us to the road," he said. "Part of the way. There's a line that runs near it."

---

They went on foot the last stretch to where the Beast could meet them, the *yatiri* setting the pace, which was slow and even and not slow at all once you tried to match it uphill at four thousand metres with your lungs filing a complaint at every step. The line ran along their left hand for a while, the bright scrape of it holding to its bearing while the path they walked drifted and wandered the way paths do, finding the easy ground, going around the rock the line went over.

Mara walked with the camera down. She'd had it down for days. The drone had gone back in its case after the second morning and never come out — Naila had watched her decide that, watched the most sellable frame in the whole hemisphere offer itself, the god's-eye sweep up the line and over the ridge, the shot that would have run on every magazine in the world under the words *largest drawing on Earth* — and watched her put it away. The aerial view was the one that lied. They all knew it now. From above, the line was a line. From the feet, walking it, in order, it was — something the feet knew and the eye couldn't be told.

The *yatiri* walked beside the line and her lips moved. Not at them. Not for them. She was naming as she went — Naila could see that much, could see the rhythm of it even without the words, the saying-while-walking that Bashir had heard down to its grammar and had told them, once, carefully, what it *was* and not at all what it *said*. The naming held the bearing. The line was the fossil of the saying. You did not read it. You walked it and you said it and the straight was the residue of having said it right, in order, for three thousand years, and if you stopped saying it the ground would forget and green over and the largest thing humans ever drew would be gone in a generation of unwalked rain.

There was a fish in Naila's head by now — there was a fish in everyone's head by now, an editorial heckle that had attached itself to the crew somewhere around Book One and would not be dislodged — and it chose this exact reverent moment to observe, *sure, it's a sentence, it's a three-thousand-year-old sentence nobody will translate for us, that's not ominous at all, that's fine.*

She let it heckle. It wasn't wrong, exactly. It just wasn't the point, and the not-being-the-point was the point.

---

Where the path met the track, the Beast was waiting with Jakobus leaning against the warm bonnet, having got it started somehow, the way he got everything started somehow, off home ground always and never once in charge of it. He'd been ahead of them and apart from them the whole trip, the road and never the lead, deferring, handing the moment over, and he did the last of that here.

He looked at the line. He looked at the *yatiri*. He asked her the question — through Bashir, in the careful tongue, the one question the whole crew existed to ask of a made thing, the question that had cracked open a stone temple and a hill of buried silence and a book no one could read.

*What is it for.*

The *yatiri* looked at him for a while. The morning had warmed enough now that the frost was off the tussocks and the first thread of *surazo* was coming up off the salt flat to the south. She looked at the line running up the rise and over and not coming back, and she looked at the crew who had come a long way to ask, and she did not look annoyed and she did not look like a keeper of secrets, which was the thing Naila would remember — she looked like a woman being asked, kindly and from a great distance, to explain what walking was.

She said three words. Bashir didn't translate them right away. He stood with them in his mouth the way you'd stand with something fragile, and then he gave them over, plain, no decoration:

"It is for walking."

And Jakobus nodded, slow, like a man who'd just been handed the answer he came for — because he had, Naila understood, that *was* the answer, that was the function, grounded and physical and complete: a thing you walk, that exists in the walking, that the walking *is*. He went quiet then. He didn't reach for more. He read it into her hand and stepped back, and the moment belonged to the *yatiri*, where it had always belonged.

The fish in Naila's head said nothing at all.

---

The *yatiri* didn't come to the track. She stopped where the line did, where the path's drift and the line's bearing finally parted company for good, and she turned. Not a goodbye, exactly. She put her hand flat to her chest and inclined her head to Bashir, the courtesy returned, and she said one more thing that he carried but didn't pass on, and then she stepped off the easy path and onto the bright scrape of the line, and she went up the rise, naming as she walked, holding the bearing, and over the top of it, and she did not come back, because the line did not come back, because that was not what the line was for.

They watched her until the rise took her.

"Right," said Frik, eventually, to no one, in the gap. "Right. *Okay.*"

Mara had the camera up. Just for a second. She framed the empty rise and the line going over it and the white cone of Sajama presiding behind, and then she lowered it without pressing anything, and looked at the screen that held nothing, and put it in the case.

"You didn't take it," Naila said.

"No." Mara latched the case. "It's not on the rise. It went over with her."

---

They loaded the last of the gear in a silence that wasn't sad and wasn't easy. Naila got in beside Bashir. She had her notebook still open on her knee, the column of numbers, the eleven metres and the one metre and the long honest record of every way she'd tried to make the straightness be a thing she could own and take home and explain at a conference with a laser pointer.

She didn't close it. She left it open. She'd decided, somewhere on the walk, to leave that page open in the back matter exactly as it was — every failed model, every accumulating degree, the whole record of the not-solving — because the not-solving was the truest thing she'd measured up here, truer than the deviation, truer than the scale.

The Beast pulled onto the track and the plain began to slide past, ochre and bone and that violet zenith, and the line ran along their left hand for a while longer, holding its bearing while they took the easy road, until the track bent away around a hill the way roads do, around the thing the line went over.

"Question," said Bashir, watching it go.

"Go on."

"Nobody commissioned it. Nobody ever saw it whole. Three thousand years of separate feet, no king, no instrument, no hill to read it from." He paused. "So what told all of them to keep it straight?"

Naila looked at the line until the hill took it.

"She did," she said. "Just then. Walking up the rise. That's the whole answer, and we're not going to get it, and that's correct."

The fish, for once, agreed. The road home unspooled ahead of them, and behind them the plain forgot nothing, because somebody was still walking it.
