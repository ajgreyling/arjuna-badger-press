# Chapter 18 — The Handoff

The cold came up out of the ground before the light did.

Bashir had learned, by the fourth morning, that the Altiplano gave its dawn away in stages, like a man counting out coins he resents parting with. First the cone — Sajama, white and impossible, taking the sun an hour before anyone on the plain felt it. Then the high *queñua* on the flanks, the red bark going copper. Then, last and grudging, the floor of the world where the people actually stood, where the frost had welded the *paja brava* into little spears of glass that snapped underfoot.

He stood in that last cold with the others, and he kept his mouth shut, which for him was the hardest discipline there was.

There were eleven of them on the line. Doña Rosa at the head — the *yatiri*, though Bashir had stopped calling her that in his own head somewhere around the third day, because the word did less work than her hands did. Two of her grandsons. An old man named Mauricio who had walked this segment, he'd said, since before the road came. A woman with a child wrapped against her back, the child's face the only part of either of them not bound in cloth against the *surazo*. And the crew, strung out behind, told once and clearly where to stand: behind. Always behind.

Mara's camera hung at her chest, capped. She had capped it herself, last night, without being asked, and Bashir had watched her do it and said nothing about that either.

"*Kunapachakamasa,*" Rosa said. Not to them. To the line.

Bashir's ear caught it and held it the way it always did, helpless, parsing before he could stop it — *until when, for how long, the duration of a thing* — and then he made himself let it go. That was the discipline. Hear it; don't carry it off.

---

The official story is true. He kept reminding himself of that, because the truth of it was the only thing that kept the morning from feeling like trespass.

This was pilgrimage. The British filmmaker Tony Morrison had brought word of these lines to the outside world in the 1970s, and the men and women from the University of Pennsylvania had walked the *Tierra Sajama* survey across this district and written down what could be written, and what they had written was not *mystery* — it was *Aymara, theirs, alive.* A web of trails scored from the dark surface stones down to the pale earth beneath, threaded between *wak'as* and *chullpas* and the white chapels the practice had simply absorbed into itself, the way a river takes a new stone. Sixteen thousand kilometres of it. The largest thing human beings had ever drawn on the face of the planet, and Doña Rosa was about to walk a single kilometre of it because today was a day it was walked.

There was no riddle here. He'd believed that, arriving. He still believed it. He just no longer believed it was the *whole* of the thing, and the gap between those two beliefs was where he'd been living for a week.

Rosa stepped onto the line.

---

The thing about the straightness — and Naila had said this on the second day, flat, in the voice she used when a fact had personally offended her — is that it should not survive contact with this ground.

He could see what she meant now, watching the line run out ahead of them. It did not pick the easy way. There was a ridge maybe four hundred metres on, a low spine of broken volcanic rubble with a perfectly good saddle to the left of it where any reasonable trail would have gone, where his own feet wanted to go just looking at it. The line refused the saddle. The line went *up*, over the spine, dead-straight, and came down the far side on exactly the bearing it had held coming in, and you could see — even from here, even in this raking light — that it had been doing this for a very long time.

Naila had spent two days trying to break it. She'd had the drone up — before Rosa's people had quietly made it understood that the drone was a guest who had overstayed — and she'd laid the satellite tiles over the survey, and she had measured, on the ground, the deviation of a twenty-kilometre run, and she had come back to the *estancia* and sat down hard and said: *they didn't have a hill to read it from.*

That was the whole of it, in seven words. To sight a line straight, you climb something and look. You correct. There is no high place on this plain that lets you see twenty kilometres of your own intention; the curve of the world takes it, the ground heaves and the line drops into a ravine and out again and you, standing anywhere a human being can stand, cannot see whether it came out true. The published surveys said it without flinching, and Naila had quoted it to them like a charge sheet: *neither the purpose nor how the makers achieved such precision is completely understood.*

No king ordered it. No instrument sighted it. No vantage corrected it. It was made piecemeal, by people who did not coordinate across the three thousand years it took, and it came out arrow-straight over the hills.

*So how—* Naila had started, that night.

And Rosa, who had been listening from the doorway with her tea, who they hadn't known understood as much Spanish as she did, had said: *You keep asking* how *they did it.* And smiled, not unkindly, and gone to bed, and left the *how* lying on the table like a coin she'd declined to pick up.

---

They walked.

It was not, Bashir found, like walking. It was like being *kept on something*. Rosa walked at the head and she was *saying* — low, steady, a register he had no name for in any of the tongues he carried, somewhere between speech and counting and the thing a mother does over a fevered child. The grandsons answered at intervals. Mauricio answered. The words went down the line and along the line and Bashir, walking, finally understood the sentence the whole book had been trying to make him hear, and it arrived the way it was supposed to — not from above, not as a solution. In the body. In the order. In the feet.

The line was the bearing.

Not a path *to* a place. The saying *was* the path. Rosa was naming the ground as she crossed it — that rock, that turn the spring had made before the spring went dry, that place where the thing had happened that the word still held — and the naming had an order, and the order had a direction, and you could not say the next word from anywhere but the next step. You walked the sentence. The straightness wasn't engineering. The straightness was *grammar.* You held the bearing because the words came in that order and the words came in that order because that was where they were *true*, and a man trying to skirt the ridge would have had to say the wrong word in the wrong place, and so the line went over the ridge, because the sentence did.

No one had drawn this. It had been *said* across three thousand years, and the saying had worn itself into the earth.

He did not translate it. That was the discipline and he held it, and it cost him, and he let it cost him. Some sentences you don't carry off. He walked behind Mauricio with his throat full of words he would not say to anyone and felt, for once in his stateless life, like he was *in* a conversation instead of holding one up.

---

They came over the ridge. The rubble bit through his boots. His lungs made the high thin protest they made at any slope here, the air giving back about two-thirds of what it owed.

And on the far side, down on the saddle the line had refused — the easy saddle — were the others.

The mapping team. Four of them, around a tripod, in the good outdoor gear that the crew's funding could only dream of, the company name on the windbreakers in a font designed to look like discovery. Their drone was up. He could hear it before he saw it — the flat insect note that did not belong on this plain. They had a total station on the tripod, the laser kind that shoots a bearing and reads a prism, the kind of instrument that could lay a survey-grade line across this whole district in a week and never break a sweat.

They were trying to map the walk.

Bashir watched the lead surveyor — a decent enough man, he'd met him in La Paz, this was greed but it was *honest* greed, a man who genuinely believed he was *opening* something — watched him sight the total station along Rosa's line and read the bearing off and frown at it, and turn the instrument, and read it again. Because the instrument gave him a number. The number was correct. The number was useless. It told him where the line *went*. It could not tell him a single one of the words that put it there. He had the bearing and not the sentence. He had the largest drawing on Earth in his eyepiece, dead-straight, and not one syllable of what kept it straight, and his drone could hover over it for a thousand years and photograph it whole and never see the one thing about it that mattered, because the one thing about it had never been visible from the air.

You could not map a sentence by photographing the ground it had been said over.

The drone hung there, recording nothing.

Rosa did not look at them. She walked her line down off the ridge, said her word at the place the word went, and the team and their instrument might as well have been weather.

---

Jakobus was at the back. He almost always was.

He'd come the whole week the way he came everywhere — off his own ground, deferring, the weathered face that read a made thing for what it was *for* and then, every time, handed the reading to someone with more right to it than he had. He had not measured anything. He had not asked Rosa a single question that Bashir had heard. He had watched, and he had let Naila do the measuring and Bashir do the listening and Mara do the not-photographing, and he had carried the crew's two thermoses and never once made it about himself.

At the bottom of the ridge, where the line ran on flat and true toward a *chullpa* a kilometre off, Rosa stopped. The walking paused. The saying paused. The cold held them all.

And Jakobus stepped up — not ahead of the crew, level with them, hat in his scarred hands — and he asked her the question. The only question he ever asked. The one he'd asked of a wall in Anatolia and a script no one could read and a silence in a quiet town, the question he was built around, the question that was the whole reason any of them had crossed an ocean to stand frostbitten on the roof of the world.

"*¿Para qué es?*" he said. *What is it for.*

Rosa looked at him. Then past him, at the line — running off straight toward the tower, climbing the next rise it had no business climbing, holding its bearing toward Sajama the way everything here held toward Sajama.

"*Es para caminar,*" she said.

*It is for walking.*

And that was all.

Bashir had every language. He turned the four words over in three of them, looking for the second meaning, the door behind the door — and found that there wasn't one, or rather that there were a thousand, and that she had said the only one she was going to say, and that she had said it complete. Not *I don't know.* Not the closed face of a secret. The open face of an answer that was whole and was hers and stopped exactly where she meant it to stop, with everything still inside it.

She was telling him where to stop. That was the trust.

Jakobus heard it. Bashir watched him hear it. The old fixer who could look at a thing humans made and tell you the function buried in it — the water map, the almanac, the key — looked at Rosa, and looked at the line, and put his hat back on his head.

"*Sí,*" he said. *Yes.* And nothing else.

He did not push. He did not name the question he was declining to answer; he just declined it, the way you set down a thing that was never yours to pick up. The function was *walking*. The line was for the walking and the walking was the line and there was no level under it for an outsider to lift the lid off, because there was no lid, because it had never been a box. He had read the made thing all the way down to the bottom and the bottom said *keep walking*, and he believed her, and he stood out of the way.

Down on the saddle the total station read its useless true number again.

Rosa said her word. The grandsons answered. Mauricio answered. And the line gathered itself and went on toward the tower, toward the mountain, toward the next place the word went, the way it had been going since before there were nations to ignore it.

The crew stepped back.

Not away — back. Off the line, onto the dark surface stones where they were not walking and so had no business being. Naila first, her notebook closed in her cold hand, the measurement she could not finish let go of at last and, somehow, lighter for it. Then Mara, camera capped, hands open and empty, watching the frame she would never take walk away from her toward the *chullpa*. Then Niamh, who had read all week the difference between *won't say* and *can't say* and had refused, every single time, to push the door that was Rosa's to keep shut. Then Bashir, with the sentence still ringing in him, unspoken, kept.

They stood off the line, in the rising cold-bright light, and they watched the largest thing human beings ever made go on being made, one word and one footstep at a time, the way it had to, the way it always had.

The drone ran out of battery and came down on the saddle with a small mechanical sigh.

Nobody up on the line looked back.
