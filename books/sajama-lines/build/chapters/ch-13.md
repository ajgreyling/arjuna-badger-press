# Chapter 13 — The Practice Endures

The wak'a was a rock.

Mara made herself sit with that before she did anything else, because the camera in her hands wanted to make it something more, and the camera was always lying a little, even when she was telling the truth. A rock the size of a kneeling llama, dark and weathered, sitting where the line ran up to it and stopped — or didn't stop, you couldn't tell, the line went pale into the grass on the far side and kept going toward the next thing the eye couldn't find. Coca leaves at the base, a few of them, green going brown. A smear of fat, old, gone grey. The light at this hour was the kind she'd have crossed a continent for once: low, gold, raking, the *paja brava* lit from the side so every blade threw a shadow as long as a man.

She did not lift the camera.

Doña Filomena came up the line at the pace of someone who had walked it ten thousand times and would walk it ten thousand more, which is to say no pace at all, a pace that had nothing to do with arriving. Two others with her, older women, one carrying a small bundle wrapped in a striped *aguayo* the colour of a parrot. Bashir walked a respectful three steps behind, his hands loose at his sides, his whole body arranged into the posture of a man who has decided not to be useful.

That was new for him. Mara had watched him spend three weeks being useful in nine languages.

> *Oh good,* said the fish, from the flat rock where it had installed itself in a borrowed dish of meltwater, *we drove eleven hours up a mountain to watch a man practise not talking. Riveting. Tell the Academy.*

Naila was crouched at the line itself, not measuring. Her instruments were back in the truck. She had her hands flat on the scraped surface, palms down, the way you'd feel an animal's flank to know if it was breathing, and she was reading something off it through her skin that Mara couldn't see and didn't ask about. Niamh stood apart, where she always put herself, at the angle that let her see every face at once. Her interpreter, Sol, stood at her shoulder. Neither of them moved.

Filomena reached the rock. She did not perform anything. That was the first thing Mara's hands wanted to make a lie of — there was no *moment*, no gathering of stillness, no breath the camera could have caught and called *the ritual beginning*. The old woman simply arrived at the rock the way you arrive at a kitchen table, and crouched, and her knees cracked, and one of the other women laughed at the crack and Filomena said something that made all three of them laugh, and then she was unwrapping the parrot-coloured bundle and the laughing was still happening while she did it.

Bashir's mouth moved. Translating under his breath, to himself, because he couldn't not. Then he stopped himself doing even that and Mara saw the cost of it cross his face — the specific small grief of a man holding a door shut against his own hand.

The bundle held more coca, and a small bottle of something clear, and three little wrapped sweets in cellophane, the kind sold by the till in any shop on Earth.

> *Sweets,* the fish noted. *Three thousand years of unbroken sacred tradition and the offering is grape Chupa Chups. I love these people. No notes.*

The clear bottle was alcohol; Filomena tipped a little onto the ground, onto the rock, said the names — Mara couldn't follow but she could hear that they *were* names, the cadence of a list, this one and this one and this one, a roll-call spoken downward into the dirt and outward along the pale line and up, once, with a lift of the chin, at the white cone of the mountain that owned the whole sky behind them. The two other women answered. Not in unison. In the way you answer at a table when someone says a thing you also know.

This is what the books got right and could never get across, Mara thought. *Ritual pilgrimage footpaths linking sacred places, kept by the walking, three thousand years.* All true. The British filmmaker in the seventies, the university survey, the careful map of the network — true, every word, and dead on the page, because the page made it sound finished. A thing that had *happened.* Past tense, completed, available for study. And here was the thing happening, in present continuous, with grape sweets and bad knees and a joke Mara would never understand, and the gap between the page and the rock was the whole distance she'd come.

Sol leaned to Niamh's ear, then signed something low and fast. Niamh signed back, one hand. Mara had learned to read about four of their words by now and this one she knew: *now.* Niamh had said *now.* Meaning, Mara was fairly sure, *they are not remembering this. They are doing it.*

She got it then, in her chest before her head, which was the only place getting anything ever actually mattered.

It was not a re-enactment. There was no original it was a copy of. The women at the rock were not honouring the walkers who'd come before; they *were* the walkers, the same walkers, the practice didn't have a then and a now, it had only ever had a *while-you-go*. The line stayed bright because feet kept scraping it. The wak'a stayed a wak'a because someone kept arriving at it with sweets and saying the names. Stop, and the grass takes the line back in a season; stop, and the rock is just a rock. The thing was alive in the most literal and least sentimental way Mara had ever encountered: it was alive the way a fire is alive. It existed only as long as it was being done.

Her hands lifted the camera.

She didn't decide to. Twenty years of muscle decided for her — the light, God, the *light*, Filomena's profile against the cone, the brown leaves and the cellophane catching gold and the long shadows of the grass, and she had the frame before she'd thought it, the frame that wins things, the frame that ends up on the cover with her name small in the corner, the frame that says *I was let in.*

She brought the eyepiece almost to her eye.

Filomena did not look up. But the woman with the *aguayo* did. Not sharply. Not a flinch. She just — found Mara's face, across the rock, the way you find a draught in a room, and held it, with no expression Mara could put a word to, and waited.

And there it was: the thing she'd been refusing to learn the whole way up the mountain. The aerial shot of Nazca that made the hummingbird famous, the drone tile that had "solved" it — they'd been her instinct from the first day on the plain, *get above it, get the whole, get the god's-eye* — and they were a dead end that looked like a destination, because the makers never had them, because the meaning had never once been visible from above. And this was the same theft scaled down to one rock and three sweets. The frame would lift the moment out of the feet. It would take a thing that only existed in the doing and make it a thing that had been done. Past tense. Available for study. Hers.

> *Take it,* murmured the fish, very quietly, not heckling now. *Or don't. Nobody up here's going to stop you, kid. That's the whole problem, isn't it.*

She lowered the camera.

She did it slowly, so it would read as what it was — not a snatch back, not a flinch of guilt, just a man putting down a thing he'd decided not to use. The woman with the *aguayo* watched her do it. Then she did something Mara would carry: she gave the smallest nod, half an inch, and turned back to the rock, and the nod hadn't been thanks and it hadn't been permission, it had been the thing Niamh had been trying to teach all of them for weeks — the difference between *can't* and *won't*, read in a single tilt of the head. *Won't.* And the *won't*, once you saw it, was not a wall. It was a held bearing. A line that refused to curve around the easy thing.

Mara let the strap take the weight of the camera against her sternum and stood there with her empty hands and felt, for the first time since the war, a particular muscle in her chest unclench that she hadn't known was clenched — the one that turned everything she loved into a thing to be kept.

The ritual, if it was a ritual, was already winding down, or it had no edges, the same way it had no beginning. Filomena rewrapped the bottle. One of the sweets had gone — to the rock, into a crack, an offering; the other two went into a pocket, and that was right too, that was exactly the rightness of the whole thing, you gave one and you kept two, you didn't make a museum of your own grandmother's snacks. They were laughing again. Filomena said something over her shoulder to Bashir.

He answered. Carefully. A short phrase, the vowels of it placed like a man setting down eggs. Filomena's face opened. Not surprise — the door-half-opening thing he did, the courtesy in the real tongue that disarmed a room — but past that, into something warmer, an old woman finding that the stranger had bothered. She said a longer thing back, and Bashir's eyes shone, and he did not translate it.

Mara watched him not translate it and understood that he had been *trusted* not to. That somewhere in the longer thing was a sentence that was not theirs to carry off the mountain, and that Filomena had said it to him precisely *because* he was the one who'd know to leave it where it lay. The man whose entire gift was reaching all the way in had been let in exactly far enough to learn where the wall was, and the gift Filomena gave him was the wall itself, shown plainly, *here, this far, friend, and that's love, not insult.*

He nodded. He put his hand briefly over his own mouth. It looked like a man covering a smile. Mara knew it for what it was.

Jakobus was further down the line, where he always was — at the edge of the frame, off his own ground, the weathered shape of him reading the *land* the way the rest of them read faces. He'd come up the day before, said almost nothing, walked the line for a kilometre with his hands behind his back and that look he got at a made thing, the *what's-it-for* look, the look that had pulled apart a dozen ruins across three books. He had it now, watching the women at the rock. And then Mara saw him do the thing he did, the thing that was the whole reason the crew followed him onto cold plains: he let the look go. He turned it off, deliberately, the way you'd lower a weapon, and he found Filomena, and he didn't ask her anything. He just inclined his head, the small bow of a man handing something back that was never his to hold, and stepped further out of the frame, toward the truck, toward the road. Leaving the rock to the people whose rock it was.

The women started back down the line. Not toward anything Mara could see — toward the next node, the next pale thread running off into grass that read flat and was not flat, that climbed a ridge a kilometre off without bending one degree to do it, the arrow held straight over the broken back of the world by nothing anyone had ever found written down. No king had commissioned it. No surveyor had sighted it. No one had ever stood high enough to see it whole. And it was still being walked, this minute, away from her, three women and two sweets, the names spoken downward into the dirt as they went.

Naila stood up from the line. She had a smear of pale subsoil on her palms. She looked at it, then at the women dwindling along the bearing they were not measuring against anything but their own going, and Mara watched the engineer in her finally stop fighting the un-solvable and just — watch. The way you watch weather. The way you watch a fire you've decided not to put out and not to feed, only to sit beside, while it does the one thing it does.

> *Huh,* said the fish, and for once that was all it said.

Bashir came back along the line to where Mara stood. He looked at the camera lying dark against her chest. He looked at her face. He understood, the way he understood everything, sideways and through the words people didn't say.

"You didn't take it," he said.

"No."

He nodded. The wind came up off the plain, the cold edge of it, the *surazo* starting its evening business, and far down the line a woman laughed at something, and the sound carried impossibly far in the thin clean air, the way distance lied up here, the way the ridge an hour off was half a day and the laugh a kilometre away was right at your ear.

"Good," Bashir said. And then, with the specific loneliness of a man who had spent his life being the door everyone walked through and had just, today, been the one trusted to keep one shut: "She left me something to carry that I'm not going to give you."

"I know," Mara said.

He smiled. It was not a happy smile and it was not a sad one. It was the smile of someone who has finally been allowed to *belong* to something by being kept out of part of it, which was the only kind of belonging the door had ever offered him, and the first time it had ever felt like enough.

They stood and watched the line carry the women off it, away over the ground, the brightness of it renewing under their feet with every step, the largest drawing on Earth getting drawn again, right now, by three people with bad knees who had somewhere to be.

Mara left the lens cap on.
