# Chapter 11 — The Adversary’s Pitch

The room they'd taken was the new wing of the municipal building in Sajama village — concrete block, fresh paint, a roof that hadn't yet learned to leak. Niamh clocked it the way she clocked every room: the exits, the light, the faces and where they were pointed. Forty plastic chairs in rows. A projector throwing a rectangle of dust-haze onto a pull-down screen. At the back, a folding table with thermoses and a stack of paper cups, because even a corporate land grab knows you offer people coffee first.

The heating was a single gas blower roaring in the corner, and it made the air taste of butane and altitude. Niamh felt the roar more than heard it — a pressure on the skin of her face, a low confusion in the floor through her boots. She didn't have the words coming off the mouths yet. She had the bodies. The bodies were already telling her the whole story.

She found her interpreter — Tomás, hired in La Paz, patient, good hands — and set him where she could see him and the front of the room in one sweep. Then she settled in to read.

The man running the show was named Holloway. She'd learned that much from the lanyard and from Bashir, who'd shaken his hand at the door and come back with his jaw set in the particular way it set when a language had been *used* at him instead of spoken to him. Holloway was English, fifty-ish, weathered in the good-boots-and-fleece way of men who fly to high places and stand near things. He had the easy stillness of someone who had given this talk before. His hands stayed open. His weight stayed even. He believed himself, which was the dangerous kind.

He clicked the projector. The first slide came up and the room made the small collective intake that Niamh saw as shoulders rising an inch all down the rows.

An aerial. The plain from above, false-coloured, the lines leaping out of the grass like stitching pulled tight on a sleeve. From up there the network was legible the way a circuit board is legible — radial, deliberate, *enormous.* The officials in the front row leaned forward. A man from the department in La Paz, two from the regional tourism board, a woman with a press badge who had not stopped filming on her phone. Niamh watched the wonder land on them and saw it for what it was: the easy wonder. The kind you get from above. The kind that costs nothing and explains less.

*Sixteen thousand kilometres,* Tomás signed, lagging the English by a half-second, his fingers crisp. *He's saying it again. Largest. Single. Archaeological. Artwork. On Earth.*

It was true. That was the thing about it that made Niamh's chest tighten. Every figure on the slides was real. Holloway clicked through them like a man laying out evidence he was proud of, and he had every right to be proud of finding them, because the world had spent a hundred years staring at Nazca's hummingbird while the bigger thing — fifteen times bigger, never abandoned — sat up here in the cold barely looked at. Holloway hadn't lied about a single number. The numbers were a gift to anyone who'd listen.

It was everything *between* the numbers where the floor went thin.

She watched his mouth and let Tomás carry the words and read the rest off his body. *A scientific framework,* the hands said. *Mapping. Conservation. Sustainable access.* The slide turned to a clean schematic — proposed visitor trails, dotted in red, looping out from a planned interpretive centre, here, a polite asterisk on the plain a kilometre from the village. *Protecting the heritage,* Tomás relayed, *by formalising the experience.*

Niamh felt Mara go rigid two seats down. She didn't have to look. She knew the photographer's stillness by now — the held breath of someone watching a frame compose itself that she did not want to exist. The slide after showed an artist's render: a boardwalk. A viewing platform with a rail. Two cartoon tourists pointing at a line.

Holloway's hands opened wider. He was getting to the part he liked.

*And we believe,* Tomás signed, careful, neutral, *that we have begun to understand the method.*

A new slide. Survey lasers, a tripod, a GPS rover the colour of a traffic cone. A heat map of one line segment with deviation measured in centimetres. Holloway was saying — Tomás's fingers steady — that with modern instruments they could now reconstruct, with high confidence, *how it was likely done.* Sighting stakes. Fire-signals at night. Relay teams. A lost surveying tradition, recoverable, *explicable.*

And here, Niamh thought, here is where you have walked off the edge of the cliff with your nice open hands still spread, because you have done it with all your kit and you still made a—

She caught herself. She glanced sideways. Naila was sitting very upright at the end of the row, completely motionless except for one foot, which was tapping out a count against the concrete that Niamh could feel through the floor like a pulse. Naila had spent four days trying to lay a single straight kilometre with a laser level and a clear morning and a hilltop to stand on, and she had a notebook full of the centimetres by which she'd failed. The makers had no laser, no hill to stand on, no morning agreed in advance across three thousand years, and they'd held it for twenty kilometres over a ridge that *had no business being crossed.* Naila's foot kept counting. She wasn't going to say it here. This wasn't the room for it.

Holloway clicked again and the projector showed his last slide: the company logo, a website, and a single line of text Tomás rendered slowly, letting each word stand.

*Opening. The. Lines. To. The. World.*

The officials applauded. Light, polite, real. The press woman lowered her phone and raised her hand. Niamh watched the question form on Holloway's face before Tomás could catch the words — that small downward settle of relief men get when the question is the one they prepared for.

Then she stopped reading Holloway, because the room had changed temperature in a place the gas blower couldn't reach, and she turned to find the source.

Doña Lucía had not moved.

She was sitting where she'd been sitting since before the lights dimmed, third row from the back, on the aisle, in a brown skirt and a hat the colour of dried apricot, her hands folded over the bundle in her lap. *Yatiri.* The walker who out-read all of them, who'd taken four days to decide whether to let Bashir's careful Aymara even half-open the door. The crew had come to her village; she had come to the company's room. That was a thing Niamh filed and did not yet fully understand: she had *come.* She was here. She was watching it happen.

And she was perfectly, deliberately silent.

Niamh knew silence. It was her whole country. She had spent a life being talked over, talked around, talked *about* in the third person two feet from her own face, and she had learned the grammar of it the way Bashir learned verbs. There is the silence of someone who has nothing to say. There is the silence of someone who hasn't been given room. And there is the third kind, the rarest, the one nobody who's never lived inside silence can read at all — the silence of someone who has everything to say and is *choosing,* second by second, with total control, not to give it to you.

Doña Lucía was doing the third one. She was doing it with her whole body. Her hands were still. Her chin was level. Her eyes were on the screen where the boardwalk render still glowed, and she was not unsettled by it, and she was not afraid of it, and she was not — this was the part Niamh felt go cold and clean through her — she was not going to correct it.

That was the threat. Niamh saw it then, fully, the way you see the shape of a room when the light finally finds the corner. Holloway wasn't going to bulldoze anything. He had open hands. He was going to build a boardwalk and a rail and an interpretive centre, and he was going to put up a *sign,* and the sign was going to say *how it was likely done* — sighting stakes, fire-signals, relay teams, a lost surveying tradition, recoverable, explicable, *solved* — and the sign would be wrong, and it would be *plausible,* and it would be in three languages, and it would be there in laminated print every dawn that Doña Lucía's people came to walk, and within a generation the children would read the sign before they walked the line, and the line would still be there, bright, scraped, held arrow-straight over the ridge, and it would mean what the sign said it meant.

He wasn't going to take the ground. He was going to take the *answer.* And the people in the front row, the decent ones, the tourism board and the department, were applauding because the answer he was offering was tidy, and tidy is what officials are for.

Niamh's hands wanted to move. She put them flat on her thighs.

The press woman's question came. Tomás caught it. *She is asking* — his face went carefully blank, the interpreter's discipline — *she is asking whether the local community supports the project.*

Holloway smiled. He did not look at Doña Lucía. He gestured, generously, toward the back rows, toward the handful of Aymara who'd come, and said something Tomás gave to Niamh in pieces: *deep consultation* — *partnership* — *the community's heritage at the heart of everything we do.* His open hands swept the room and rested, for a beat, palm-up, in the general direction of the people he was speaking for without speaking *to.*

Niamh watched the swept hand pass over Doña Lucía like a searchlight over a stone.

And Doña Lucía, who had been asked nothing, who had been gestured at as a backdrop to a yes, did the smallest thing Niamh had ever watched a person do.

She looked, for one second, directly at Niamh.

Not at Holloway. Not at the press. At the Deaf woman in the row across the aisle who had been reading the room all morning, who could not hear a word of the pitch and had therefore heard none of the velvet on it. One second. A flat, level, unhurried look, the look of one person who lives in silence acknowledging another, and in it Niamh read — not a word, not a code, nothing she could carry out of the room and translate, just the plain fact of it — *I see what he's doing. You see it too. Watch what I do about it.*

Then Doña Lucía stood up.

The plastic chair scraped. Heads turned. Holloway's hands paused mid-sweep. The press phone came up.

She did not go to the front. She did not take the floor. She gathered her bundle against her hip, settled her hat, and walked — unhurried, the high-plain walk, the walk of someone who has crossed this floor and floors like it for longer than the building has stood — toward the door at the back. As she passed the other Aymara in the rear rows, one of the men rose. Then a woman with a baby on her back. Then two more. No signal Niamh could catch. No word. They simply stood, the way water finds its level, and turned, and went out into the white hammering light of the plain, and the door let in a slab of cold and the roar of the *surazo* and then swung shut.

The front rows sat in the new quiet. The tourism board glanced at one another. The department man's smile had nowhere to go. Holloway, to his credit, kept his open hands open, though Niamh saw the recalculation move behind his face like cloud-shadow over the grass — saw him understand, just enough, that the yes he'd gestured at had stood up and walked out before he could photograph it.

Tomás's hands were still. There was nothing to translate. That was the whole point.

Niamh looked at the empty chairs in the back rows. She looked at the screen, where the boardwalk still glowed, two cartoon tourists pointing at a line nobody in this room could read. She thought about the difference between *doesn't know* and *won't say,* the difference she'd been reading off faces her entire life, and she thought: *they know exactly what it's for. They have always known. And they have just decided, with their feet, in front of the press and the department and the man with the lasers, that knowing it is not the same as handing it over.*

Holloway said something. Tomás began, automatically, to render it, then stopped, and let his hands fall.

Outside, through the block wall, faint enough that Niamh felt it more as a rhythm in the floor than anything else, the small group of walkers had not gone far. They had simply moved off across the plain, toward the lines, in the direction they always went — and they were going on foot, in order, the way you keep a thing alive, the way you keep it *yours,* by doing the one thing a boardwalk and a laser and a laminated sign can never do for you.

They were walking.
