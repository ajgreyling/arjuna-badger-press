# Chapter 10 — The Map That Fails

The generator in the back room of the *alojamiento* had a stutter to it, a missed beat every nine seconds or so, and by the second hour Bashir had stopped hearing it as a fault and started hearing it as a word — *ka, ka-ka, ka* — the way he heard everything, parsing the cough of a machine for grammar it didn't have. He pressed two fingers to the bridge of his nose and let the syllable dissolve.

On the trestle table, Naila had laid the plain out flat.

Six A1 sheets of satellite tile, edge-matched and weighted at the corners with whatever the room offered — a tin mug, a fist-sized rock with a chalky underside, the LENS's lens cap, a folded glove. Above the paper, propped on a crate, the drone footage looped on the laptop: Sajama rising white and indifferent off the left of frame, and below it the plain combed with lines, fine as the grain in old wood, running away to the violet edge of the sky.

"That's it," Naila said. "That's the whole god's-eye."

It was beautiful. Bashir would give it that, would give it everything. From four thousand metres up — borrowed altitude, borrowed by a quadcopter that cost more than the village's only truck — the lines did the thing the famous Peruvian glyphs did in every coffee-table book on the planet. They resolved. They became a thing you could *see*. Threads radiating from a dark knot that was a *wak'a*, a shrine, a place where something had once happened; threads crossing other threads; a whole written page of plain, three thousand years in the keeping.

"Read it to me," Mara said. She wasn't looking at the paper. She was looking at the loop, at the mountain, with the specific stillness she had when a frame was good and she was deciding whether good was a sin.

"That's the problem." Naila put one blunt finger on a line where it met the ridge marked in faint topographic brown. "There's nothing to read. There's a line. It goes from here" — the finger walked — "to here, twenty kilometres, and it crosses the ridge at the steepest place instead of going round, and I can tell you to the half-degree how straight it is, and it tells me *nothing*. It's a sentence with the letters but no language."

---

Here is the official story, and it is true, and Bashir had spent enough years at enough borders to know how rare it was to be able to say *true* without a caveat snagging in your teeth.

In the 1970s a British filmmaker named Tony Morrison came across the lines and brought word of them out to the world that catalogues such things, and decades later a university project out of Pennsylvania surveyed them properly, and the answer they returned was clean and good: these are pilgrimage paths. The Aymara have walked them for three thousand years and walk them still, scraping the dark oxidised stone aside to bare the pale ground beneath, line after line linking the shrines and the funerary towers and the villages and, after the Spanish came, the little white chapels that the practice simply absorbed without surrendering anything older. Not a monument. A landscape that is alive because it is in use. Stop walking it and it greens over and is gone.

Sixteen thousand kilometres of it. Naila had done the arithmetic on the back of a fuel receipt their first night and then sat with the receipt in her hand longer than the sum warranted. Three times the width of the continental United States, scored by foot. Twenty-two and a half thousand square kilometres of patterned ground — fifteen Nazcas, give or take — very likely the largest single thing human beings ever drew on the face of the Earth.

And the most-photographed lines on the planet were eight hundred kilometres north in Peru, where they let you fly over in a Cessna for a hundred dollars and feel the airsickness of awe.

"The famous one is the decoy," Mara had said, that first night, turning the receipt over in the lamplight. Nobody had argued.

---

"Bashir." Naila tapped the laptop's spacebar; the loop froze with the line halfway up the ridge. "Tell me what they could see when they made it."

"From the ground?"

"From anywhere. There's no tower. There's no scaffold anybody's ever found. There's no hill in the right place to stand on and check the back-bearing — and even if there were, the line goes *over the next ridge* where you'd lose sight of where you started." She wasn't excited. She never sounded excited; she sounded the way a level sounds when the bubble finds centre. "So. No instrument. No vantage. No one in charge — it's not a king's road, it's a thing a thousand separate people kept up over a hundred generations. And it holds to under a degree for twenty kilometres over broken ground."

"That's the published thing," Bashir said. "Word for word, nearly. *Neither the purpose nor how the makers achieved such precision is completely understood.*"

"Not understood by *them*," said a voice from the doorway, and it was not warm.

---

The adversary did not look like an adversary. Bashir had learned to distrust the part of himself that wanted villains to have a tell. Dr. Esteban Olmedo had clean hands — literally clean, soft, a man whose work happened on screens — and an open, tired face, and a way of standing slightly stooped as though apologising in advance for the size of his funding. His outfit was called something with *Heritage* in it. They had drones better than the crew's, surveyors with total stations, and a press release already drafted, Niamh had read it off a tablet over a man's shoulder in the one café — drafted, and gentle, and devastating: *the mystery of the Sajama Lines, explained at last.*

"May I?" He came to the table without waiting for the answer, which was its own kind of answer. He looked at the loop, at the frozen line on the ridge, and he smiled, and the smile was real. He loved this. That was the worst of it. "You see it too. The radial geometry. Ceque logic — like Cusco, the shrine-lines out from a centre, only scaled up across a whole district. We're modelling the whole network. Every node, every bearing. We'll have it solved by the dry season."

"Solved," Naila said.

"The straightness." Olmedo spread his soft hands over the paper as if warming them at it. "People want to make it a riddle. It isn't. Give people a fixed start, a fixed target they can see across the flat, and a rope, and an afternoon, and they'll lay you a straight line. Repeat it for three thousand years and refresh the easy stretches and—" He shrugged, fond, dismissive. "The hills are the only interesting part, and the hills are just persistence. We'll demonstrate it. We have a slot booked on the eastern segment. Total station, dGPS, the full kit. We'll walk a line ourselves, in a day, and put the *how* to bed, and then the tourists can come and the lines can be properly *valued*."

*Valued.* Bashir heard the word go in like a splinter. He watched Niamh, across the room, who was watching Olmedo's mouth with the flat attention she gave to people who talked too much, and he saw her file something — the small tightening at the corner of the man's eye when he said *valued*, the thing the man himself didn't know he'd shown.

"And the people who walk it now?" Bashir asked, in Spanish, mildly.

"Are welcome to keep walking it," Olmedo said, and meant it, and that was the splinter going deeper. "We're not taking anything. We're *explaining* it. There's a difference."

---

When he had gone — the soft handshake, the genuine warmth, the slot booked on the eastern segment like a dentist's appointment — the room held the generator's stutter for a while.

"He's not wrong about the rope," Naila said at last. She said it the way she said hard things, flatly, against herself. "On the flat. Two people, a sightline, you can do a kilometre dead straight with nothing. I could do it. The fish could probably do it."

From the floor, where it sat in the cooler with the last of the good water, Hecklefish offered its opinion. *"I could ABSOLUTELY do it. I have an excellent eye. I'm basically a level with a personality."*

"It's the ridge," Naila went on, not dignifying that. "The over-the-hill bit. You lose the back-sight the second you crest. And then the next stretch on the far side has to *match* — has to be the same bearing, picked up by somebody who maybe wasn't there yesterday, maybe wasn't born yet, with no one to phone, no plan in a drawer, no map." She put her hand flat on the satellite tile, on the line, on the very thing she'd been hired by her own brain to measure. "And the map doesn't help. *This* doesn't help. They never had this. This is the one tool that makes it look easy and it's the one tool that proves nothing, because the people who made it never once saw it."

"That's the joke," Mara said quietly.

"It's not a joke." Naila looked up. There was something raw in it, just for a second, the thing she didn't show — the pattern that wouldn't resolve, sitting in front of her, refusing to be a problem she could close. "I'm the one who's supposed to model it. I've got the tiles and the drone and the lidar offset and I can give you the straightness to three decimals and I cannot tell you *how a person standing on the ground did it.* I keep doing the thing he's doing. I keep looking at it from above."

---

Bashir went out for the air, which was a thing you did here, because the air was the whole argument.

Outside, the cold came down the way it did the instant the sun dropped behind Sajama — not a chill, a subtraction, the heat of the day simply removed from the world in the space of a breath. The *paja brava* hissed in the *surazo*. Up the slope, surreal in the last light, the highest forest on Earth held its red-barked, gnarled little stand of *queñua*, trees that had no business at this altitude and grew here anyway, stubborn as the lines.

An old woman was coming down the trail from the *chullpas* with two others, walking unhurried, walking the way they all walked here — economical, lungs banked against the thin air, a gait that knew exactly how much each step cost and spent not one breath more. The *yatiri*. She had not given the crew much. She had given Bashir something the others hadn't gotten, which was a door left on the latch, and she had given it to him not because his Aymara was good — it was passable, walker's courtesy, *kamisaraki*, the small right words in the right order — but because he had used the right words in the right order and then *stopped*, and let the silence be hers.

She stopped now, a few metres off, and looked at him, and then past him at the doorway with its rectangle of generator-light and the laptop glow inside.

She said something. Bashir caught it — caught the shape of it before the sense, the way he always did, the grammar arriving a half-beat ahead of the meaning. She nodded at the lit room, at the paper he knew was spread on the table in there, the whole plain flattened and weighted under a tin mug and a rock.

*"Ukax janiw uñjañ kunäkiti,"* she said. And then, because she was kind, or because she wanted him to carry it back exactly, she said it slower.

He turned it over. *That thing is not a looking-thing.* Not — *that's not a picture.* Closer: *that is not for looking.* The verb sat where the verb sat in Aymara, the way it always undid him a little, the action holding the centre of the sentence and the thing itself orbiting it.

He could have argued. He could have said *we know, we're not trying to look, we're trying to understand.* But that was the same sentence Olmedo had used with his clean soft hands. *We're not taking anything. We're explaining it.*

"*Jisa,*" Bashir said instead. Yes. And then, carefully, in her language, with the small right words: "Then how is it for?"

The two younger walkers had stopped a little up the slope. The wind moved the grass. The old woman looked at him for long enough that he understood he had asked the question slightly wrong — not wrongly enough to close the door, but wrongly, the *how* where some other word should sit, a wrongness she registered the way Niamh registered the splinter in Olmedo's eye.

She didn't correct him. She started walking again, down past him toward the village, and as she passed she said it once more, almost to herself, the way you'd say a thing a child needed to find out by doing rather than being told —

*"Sarañatakiw."*

And then she was gone down the trail, the cold filling in behind her, and Bashir stood holding the word in his mouth like a stone with a chalky underside, turning it, because it would not lie flat the way the satellite tile did. *Saraña.* To walk, to go. *-taki*, for. *-w*, the affirmative, the *it-is-so*.

*It is for going.*

No. Not quite. The map of the word failed the same way the map of the plain failed. The meaning wasn't *in* it, laid out to be read from above. It was in the verb, in the doing, in the part you only got by—

He went back inside, where Naila still had both hands flat on the line she could not solve, and the loop still hung frozen with the line halfway up the ridge, and the generator said *ka, ka-ka, ka*, and nobody had moved.

"We've been doing it wrong," he said.

Mara lowered the camera she hadn't quite been raising. "All of us?"

"All of us." He looked at the paper, all six beautiful weighted sheets of it, the largest thing humans ever drew, flattened and lit and as silent as the day Nazca went quiet. "We brought the one tool the makers never had and we're standing here wondering why it won't tell us anything."

*"So what's the right tool?"* the fish said, from the cooler. *"And before you say 'feelings,' I want you to know I'm prepared to leave."*

"No," Bashir said. He picked up the rock off the corner of the satellite tile, and the paper curled, and the plain went back to being a flat thing that meant nothing without feet on it. "Not feelings."

He weighed the stone in his hand.

"She didn't say it's for looking at. She said it's for walking."
