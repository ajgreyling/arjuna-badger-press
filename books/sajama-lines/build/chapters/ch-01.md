# Chapter 1 — The Overhead View

The man at the El Alto checkpoint had three languages in his mouth and Bashir gave him the wrong one first, on purpose, to watch which one he reached for to correct it. *Castellano* with the official's stiffness, then a slide of Aymara under it — *kamisaraki* — and the man's shoulders came down a centimetre, and the gate came up.

"You did that thing again," Naila said, from the back seat, not looking up from her tablet. "Where you make a friend out of a barrier."

"He was never a barrier. He was a man being paid to be one."

"That's what I said."

The road out of El Alto runs flat and brutal across the top of the world. La Paz had been a bowl of brick and breath below the airport, every street tilted, every breath short — Bashir had felt it the moment the plane doors opened, the way the air went thin and businesslike and declined to fully arrive in his chest. Four thousand metres and climbing. The cold here was not weather. It was a fact about the place that the place did not intend to argue.

Mara rode up front with the driver, camera off, which for Mara meant only that the camera was resting. She watched the *Altiplano* unspool the way she watched a room she didn't trust yet — corners first. The plain went on past anything Bashir's eye knew how to size. Pale ochre. Bone. Bunchgrass in tufts that the wind worked over like it was looking for something it had dropped.

"That's it," Naila said. "Top right of the windscreen. That's Sajama."

It stood alone. A white cone with a plume of cloud snagged on it, lifted up out of the plain like the plain had set one thing down very carefully and walked away. You could not look at it and not orient to it. Bashir found himself doing it without deciding to — the mountain went *there*, and so everything else went *not-there*, and the geography arranged itself around the choice.

"Bolivia's highest," Naila said. "Six thousand five hundred and forty-two. Dormant." A pause. "It's been politely dormant for a very long time and I would like it to continue."

---

They reached Sajama village in the worst of the afternoon light, which here is the best — hard, clean, long, so the shadow of every adobe wall lay on the ground sharp enough to cut yourself on. Tin roofs. Woodsmoke going up straight in the still and then bending where the *surazo* found it. A herd of alpaca picking at the *paja brava* with the bored superiority of animals that have nothing to prove to anybody.

And on the flank of the mountain, where no tree had any business being, a forest. Low, gnarled, red-barked trees, the bark peeling in papery curls, the whole stand of them shorter than a man and older than the village. *Queñua.* The highest woodland on Earth, growing where the rules say woodland stops.

Niamh's hands moved beside her, fast, to the interpreter, Bashir caught the shape of *that should not be there* and *but it is.*

It was the LIE that found the other thing first.

"There," Frik said. "There-there-there." His finger went out toward the plain north of the village, then jerked, then steadied. "White trucks. Generator. That's — *kak* — that's a base camp. That's a funded base camp."

Bashir followed the finger. Past the last estancia, where the plain ran flat to a low rise, there was a cluster of vehicles too clean for the road that had brought them, a marquee the colour of a corporate logo, and the unmistakable geometry of people working in a grid. A drone went up off the rise as he watched — a black asterisk lifting into the violet-deep sky, hung there, then sliding north along a heading.

"They started without us," Mara said.

"They started without anybody," Naila said. "Look at the spacing on those survey poles. They've gridded a square kilometre already. That's days of work."

Bashir read the marquee through the long lens of the air. He couldn't make out the wordmark. He didn't need to. He'd met its cousins on four continents — the outfit that arrives clean-handed and well-funded with a press release already written, that calls what it does *opening* and *protecting* and *bringing to the world*, and means, underneath all three, *taking*. Not villains. That was the thing he'd had to learn the hard way. Just people who were very sure the thing would be better off known the way *they* knew it.

A figure had stopped at the edge of the village to watch the drone go up. An older woman in a *pollera* and a bowler hat, a bundle of something herbed and dried in the crook of her arm, her face turned to the black asterisk in the sky with an expression Bashir could not read at this distance and Niamh, beside him, could.

Niamh's hands went still. Then: *She isn't surprised,* the interpreter said for her. *She's seen it before. She's deciding something.*

"Deciding what?"

Niamh watched the woman a moment longer. *Whether we're more of them.*

The woman turned and went between two walls and was gone before Bashir had finished arranging the Aymara greeting he'd meant to offer her. He let it go unsaid. There would be a time. You did not walk up to a closed door and knock harder.

---

They set up in a room behind the village's one guesthouse — adobe walls a half-metre thick, a single bulb, a paraffin heater that lost its argument with the cold by ten p.m. every night for the rest of their stay. Naila put the big screen up against the wall and the satellite tiles came down off the link slow, the high-altitude bandwidth wheezing.

And then the thing came up onto the wall, and the room went quiet in the specific way it does when six people are each separately failing to take it in.

From above, the plain was scribbled on.

Not scribbled — that was the wrong word, the first word, the word the eye reached for before the eye understood. There was nothing frantic in it. The lines ran straight. Dozens of them in the first tile, then Naila zoomed out and it was hundreds, then she zoomed out again and the screen could not hold it and she had to scroll, and scroll, and the lines kept coming — pale threads laid across the ochre, radiating from points, crossing, running to the horizon of the tile and off it and presumably on, dead straight, for as far as anyone had ever bothered to look.

"Okay," said Frik, very quietly, which from Frik was the loudest thing in the room.

"Sixteen thousand kilometres," Naila said. "That's the working estimate. Of line." She let it sit. "The continental United States is about four and a half thousand across. So this is — " her hand moved, doing it — "roughly three times the width of America. Drawn. By foot."

"How many people drew Nazca's reputation?" Mara said. "And nobody's heard of this."

"Twenty-two thousand square kilometres of patterned ground," Naila went on. "Fifteen times the extent of Nazca. It's very likely the largest single piece of artwork any human has ever made. Anywhere. Ever." She turned from the screen. "And it is one of the least studied things on the planet."

Bashir watched the lines and felt the awe come up cold and clean off the screen, and felt, underneath it, something that wasn't awe at all and that he didn't have a word for yet in any of his languages.

The official story was not a mystery. That was what he kept having to hold onto, looking at the impossible scribble. The story was *known.* A British filmmaker named Tony Morrison had brought it to the outside world in the 1970s — pilgrimage lines, scored across the plain by the Aymara, linking the *wak'as* and the *chullpas* and the villages and, later, the white colonial chapels that the practice had simply absorbed into itself the way it absorbed everything, without surrendering the older centres. The University of Pennsylvania had surveyed it. *Tierra Sajama.* It was real, it was understood, it was alive, it was *theirs.* The lines stayed bright because the feet kept walking them and scraping the dark surface stones aside to the pale subsoil beneath. Stop walking and the plain would take them back inside a generation.

Three thousand years. The lines were ancient when the Inca arrived. Nazca fell silent and went to ruin and to legend. Sajama never fell silent. Sajama was being walked *this morning,* probably, by the woman with the bowler hat and the bundle of herbs.

Known. Reverently, thoroughly known.

And Bashir kept his eyes on the screen and could not, for the life of him, make the knowing fit the thing.

"Naila." He didn't look away from the wall. "Pick one. A long one."

She did. She found a single thread and traced it, and zoomed, and the tile resolved, and the line ran across the screen straight as a ruled edge — and ahead of it, square in its path, a ridge.

It hit the ridge and it did not bend.

"That's — " Frik started.

"It climbs it," Naila said. "Watch." She followed the line up the contour, the elevation shading darkening under it, the line going up and over the rise without so much as a degree of give. "It doesn't go around. There's flat ground forty metres to either side. It goes *over.*" She measured it with the tool, two clicks, the number coming up on the wall. "That segment's nineteen-point-something kilometres. About a metre wide. And I cannot find — " she scrolled the line end to end, hunting, the way Naila hunted, refusing to be wrong about what she wasn't finding — "I cannot find the bend. Anywhere. Over the hill, through the wash, out the other side. It holds the bearing."

"So they sighted it from up high," Mara said. "Stood on the mountain, picked the far point, walked to it."

"From where?" Naila pulled the view up, set the line against the terrain. "There's no vantage. There's no point on the ground where you can see both ends of a twenty-kilometre line over a ridge. The ridge is the whole problem — it *blocks* the line of sight. That's why surveyors use instruments. You can't eyeball past a hill that you yourself can't see over." She sat back. "And there are no instruments. No theodolite, no chain, no plan anyone has ever found. No central authority telling everyone where to put the next one. Decentralised. Built piecemeal. Over three thousand years."

The bulb buzzed. The heater ticked.

"The published survey says it plainly," Naila said, and she read it off her notes flat, no spin on it, the cold eye she was so good at. "*Neither the purpose nor how the makers achieved such precision is completely understood.*"

Nobody said anything for a while.

Then Frik, who could not help it, who never could, said in a small voice the thing the room had been circling: "No king made this. No engineer. No — *fok* — no boss. Nobody ever stood anywhere and saw the whole thing. And every one of them — every separate person, three thousand years of them — kept the line straight over the hill they couldn't see over." He blinked. "Why would you do that? Why would you all — *agree* — to do the hard thing? For thirty centuries? With nobody making you?"

Naila looked at the line on the wall. "That's not the question I can answer," she said. "I can tell you it's real. I can tell you it's not woo and it's not footpaths and it's not impossible. I can tell you it's *harder* than either of those, by hand and by foot." She turned the screen brightness down a notch, like the line was tiring her eyes, which maybe it was. "I just can't tell you how."

---

Late, when the heater had given up and the others had gone to find what warmth there was, Bashir stayed with the screen.

He pulled the view all the way back out. The whole network, what they had of it, every thread at once. From up here it was beautiful and it was total and it explained nothing — a vast and perfect god's-eye image of the largest thing his species had ever drawn, and the longer he looked at it the more certain he became that he was looking at the wrong thing entirely.

Because the makers had never seen this. Not one of them. Not the first walker three thousand years ago, not the woman with the herbs this afternoon, not anyone in between. There was no satellite then. There was no drone then. There was no *up.* Whatever the lines were, whatever held them straight over ground no eye could survey, it had never once depended on the view he was sitting here drinking in. The view was his. It had never been theirs.

Out on the rise to the north, the adversary's drone was a faint moving light against the violet sky, mapping by night now, climbing higher, hunting for exactly the angle Bashir already had on the wall in front of him and was beginning to understand was useless.

Solving it from above. The way Nazca got *solved.*

Somewhere out in the dark the woman in the bowler hat was — what. Asleep. Or not. Reading the room the way Niamh read it, the way they all read everything, because the world had never once let any of them stop watching it. Deciding whether they were more of *them.*

Bashir looked at the screen, at the sixteen thousand kilometres of bearing held by feet across three thousand years, and he reached for the word for what he was feeling, in every language he had, and came up — for the first time he could remember — short in all of them.

He turned the screen off. The line on the wall went dark and the line on the ground, out there, stayed exactly where it was.

You couldn't read it from here. You'd have to walk it.

And that was not yet his to do.
