# Chapter 19 — The Mapping Team Withdraws

The wind came up off the plain at first light and Mara felt it find the gap in her collar before she'd taken three steps from the truck. The *surazo*, Bashir had called it, the night the word came up over the stove — and like everything Bashir handed you, it arrived with its weight attached. A south wind. The kind that strips heat off skin and patience off people. By the time she crossed the frost-stiff grass toward the mapping team's compound, her hands were already in her pockets and the camera, for once, was zipped away.

She'd come to photograph the end of something. She didn't yet know whose.

The compound was three white expedition tents and a generator and a folding table that someone had bolted a satellite dish to, all of it pegged down hard against the wind. Tidy. That was the word that kept surfacing for her, the whole time they'd been here. The mapping team were *tidy* people. Their drone cases were foam-cut and labelled. Their survey stakes were flagged in the same orange, every one. Whatever else you wanted to say about the outfit that had flown in to "open" the Sajama Lines to the world, they had never once been sloppy, and they had never once been cruel. That was the part Mara had stopped expecting and couldn't stop noticing.

Reuben Castellanos was outside the largest tent with a mug in both hands and the dish behind him still ticking as it cooled. The project lead. Fifties, a geographer by training, a fundraiser by necessity. He'd given them the press release on day one without being asked — *Illuminating Bolivia's Forgotten Heritage* — and he'd meant every line of it. That was the thing about him you had to hold and not flinch from. He wasn't a thief in a movie. He was a man who genuinely believed that a thing unseen was a thing being wasted.

"You came to watch us pack," he said. Not unkind.

"I came to watch," Mara said. "The rest is up to the wind."

He almost smiled. Behind him, two of his people were striking the smallest tent, working the poles down in the cold without gloves because gloves slowed you. The orange stakes were already pulled, bundled in a drum, the plain behind them gone back to plain.

---

The official story is the easy one to tell, and it deserves telling straight, because it was true. There are thousands of lines out here. Dead-straight, scored across the Altiplano around Nevado Sajama, Bolivia's highest mountain — a snow cone standing alone over the high pale grass, the thing you orient by from anywhere, the way you'd orient by a tower. The Aymara have made these lines and re-walked them for more than three thousand years. Ritual paths, linking the *wak'as* — the shrines, the rock where a thing happened, the spring — and the *chullpas*, the old funerary towers, and the villages, and the white chapels the practice folded in after the Spanish came, the way a river folds in a new stream and keeps flowing.

Not a monument. A living sacred landscape, still walked.

Tony Morrison brought them to the outside world's notice in the 1970s. UPenn's people surveyed them under the Tierra Sajama name. The work was real, the scholarship serious, the respect plain on the page. And Reuben Castellanos had read all of it, more carefully than Mara had, and had concluded — reasonably, defensibly — that the largest single artwork humans ever drew should not be the least visited.

"You know the figures better than I do," he said, when she'd settled on the upturned crate beside him because standing in that wind was its own argument. "Sixteen thousand kilometres of line. Three times the width of the continental United States, scratched into the ground with no wheel, no instrument. Twenty-two thousand square kilometres of it. Fifteen Nazcas." He turned the mug. "Nazca gets a hundred thousand visitors. This gets us, and you, and a dozen herders who've walked it since they could walk."

"And you wanted to fix that."

"I wanted to *honour* it." He said it without heat, which was worse, somehow. "A boardwalk. Interpretive signage. A viewing platform on the ridge so people could see one line run twenty kilometres without bending. Funded. Real money. The kind that builds a school in the village while it's at it." He looked at her. "Tell me that's villainy."

She didn't, because it wasn't, and they both heard the silence agree with him.

---

Here is the part the platform could never solve, and Mara had watched Naila prove it with her own lungs, four days ago, on a ridge a kilometre south of where they now sat packing.

A line out here runs one to three metres wide and up to twenty kilometres long, and it is held arrow-straight over broken ground. Not flat ground. *Broken* ground — hills it could skirt, ravines it could detour, and it does not skirt, it does not detour. It climbs the hill rather than go around it. It holds the bearing.

And the people who made it had no central authority telling them where to put it. No surveying instrument to sight along. And — this is the one that gets into your chest and stays — no elevated vantage. Nowhere to stand and check the line from above. The makers never had the view. The published survey says it without flinching: neither the purpose nor how the makers achieved such precision is completely understood.

Castellanos's team had brought the view. They'd brought everything. GPS, a total station, a drone with a camera that could read a number plate from the platform he'd wanted to build. And on the second day — Mara had the frames, she'd never sell them — they had tried, for the fun of it and to settle a bet, to lay two hundred metres of survey-grade straight line over one low rise. Daylight. Vantage. A plan. Four trained people and a machine that did nothing but measure.

They'd come out the far side eleven centimetres off. Laughing about it, but off.

Eleven centimetres in two hundred metres, with the best kit on the continent and a god's-eye drone overhead to scold them.

The Aymara had held it over twenty kilometres of ridge and ravine. By hand. By foot. Piecemeal, across a hundred lifetimes, with nobody in command and nobody, ever, standing where they could see the whole of it.

That was the gap. Not *therefore aliens* — there were no aliens in this, the obscurity had spared it even that. Just the gap. The plain, measurable, eleven-centimetre gap between the planned daylight attempt and the flawless thing under their boots, and no closed door at the bottom of it.

---

A truck came up the track from the village. Not theirs — local plates, a man Mara recognised from the chapel and, beside him, smaller, wrapped against the cold, the woman the whole of this had finally turned on.

Mara wouldn't name her in a caption. She'd learned that much. The *yatiri* — Niamh had read her first, days ago, across a room, had read the difference in her face between *doesn't know* and *won't say* and had told the rest of them to stop pushing the second one. She got down from the truck slowly, the way you move at four thousand metres when you've nothing to prove, and she crossed to Castellanos and stood in front of him, and she did not ask him to leave.

She thanked him.

Bashir had drifted up from somewhere and was at Mara's shoulder, low, giving her the Aymara under the Spanish under the wind — not translating so much as *carrying*, the way he did, the words arriving with their hooks in. The *yatiri* thanked Castellanos for the offer of the school. She said the offer was good. She said the children would be glad of it and named two of them, which was its own kind of answer, because you don't refuse a man and then list his potential beneficiaries by name unless you mean for the door to stay on its hinges.

Then she said something Bashir didn't render for a moment, and when he did his voice had gone careful.

"She says — the line stays bright because feet keep it bright. She says if you build the platform, the feet stop, and then there is no line, only a place where a line was. She says you would be photographing the grave of the thing you came to honour."

Castellanos didn't answer right away. The wind moved the tent canvas. One of his people had stopped striking the second tent and was just standing, listening, the pole half-collapsed in her hands.

"I checked it," he said finally. To Bashir, but meaning her. "Your colleague. Naila. The straightness — I had my own people run it before I'd believe it from a stranger. We couldn't match it. I want you to know I couldn't pretend that away." He set the mug down on the crate, deliberate. "I came here certain that what was missing was *attention*. That the only reason the world's largest drawing was invisible was that nobody had pointed at it." He looked at the plain, the long lines running off it toward the mountain, pale on pale, legible only to a foot. "And I've spent two weeks learning that it's not invisible. It's just not for the eye. You can stand on every ridge in this district and you will not see it. The only people it was ever visible to are the ones moving along it, saying — " he stopped. "Saying whatever it is they say. Which is none of mine."

He turned to the *yatiri* and inclined his head, slightly, the gesture of a man who knew the local words for *forgive me* and had decided the smaller, surer Spanish would carry less risk of getting them wrong.

"We'll file it as paused," he said. "Officially. Pending community consent. Which is a phrase that means *no* and lets everyone keep their funding intact, and I'd rather you knew that than thought I'd dressed it up." A breath of something almost rueful. "The department will defer to the community. I'll see to it personally. It costs me a platform. It does not cost me a school." He glanced at Mara. "I'd like that on the record, if your photographer's willing."

Mara had her hands in her pockets and the camera zipped away and she kept it that way.

"I'll write it," she said. "I won't shoot it. Not this."

He looked at her for a second longer than the words needed, and she watched him understand that the refusal was the compliment — that the most sellable frame in two thousand kilometres, the white tent and the wrapped elder and the great blank mountain behind them, the *adversary concedes* frame that would have run on every wire — was being put down on purpose, the way you put a drone down on purpose, and that this was the only way she had of telling him she'd seen him clearly and decided not to take the easy thing either.

"Right," he said. "Yes. All right."

---

They struck the last tent inside the hour. The orange stakes were in the drum, the dish folded down, the generator silent. The compound went back to grass and the grass had already half-forgotten it. Mara stood with the crew at the edge of where it had been and watched the trucks load.

Naila was beside her, not quite looking at the lines, the way she did when a thing wouldn't resolve and she'd made her peace with that — not solved, *witnessed*, which she'd decided was a different and survivable thing. She'd run the geometry to the edge of what the instruments could do and she'd come to the same wall Castellanos had, the eleven-centimetre wall, and instead of leaning on it she'd taken her hand off it.

"He's not wrong, you know," Naila said. "About the attention. The biggest thing humans ever drew, and almost nobody's looked at it."

"No," Mara said. "He's not wrong about that part."

"He's just wrong about what looking is."

The *yatiri* and the man from the chapel got back in their truck. Before it pulled away she said something to Bashir through the window, and he stood there with it a moment after the truck had gone, the wind taking the dust off the track.

"What?" Mara said.

He didn't answer at first. Then: "She said we can come at dawn. The day after tomorrow. One line. Over the ridge." He turned the words over in his mouth like he was checking them for hooks. "She said — to walk it. Not to bring the machine. Not to bring the camera." A pause. "She didn't say not to bring you. She said not to bring the camera. They're not the same thing, the way she said it."

Mara looked at the line that ran from somewhere near her boots straight up the rise and over and down the far side toward the mountain, refusing the easy ground, holding the bearing nobody had stood high enough to set.

She thought about the eleven centimetres.

"No," she said. "They're not the same thing."

The mountain held its snow against the violet zenith and the lines ran out under it, pale, dead-straight, kept bright by feet, and waited — as they had for three thousand years — for the morning someone walked them again.
