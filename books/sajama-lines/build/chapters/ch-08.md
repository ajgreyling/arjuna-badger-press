# Chapter 8 — The Potato Revisited

The GPS unit said the line was straight to within ninety centimetres over four kilometres, and Naila did not believe it, because that was impossible, and so she walked the four kilometres again.

It was the third time. The sun had not yet cleared the shoulder of Sajama, and the cold was the kind that lived in the marrow rather than the skin — a dry, mineral cold that made her fingers slow on the screen. Each breath came short and expensive. At four thousand and something metres the body did arithmetic it never had to do at sea level: a step costs this much, a slope costs that much, do you have it. Mostly she did not have it. She walked anyway, because the numbers were lying, and Naila had a low tolerance for numbers that lied.

She had set the rover unit on the *wak'a* at the south end — a low cairn of dark stones with one pale offering-stone laid on top, a knot of dried grass tucked into a cleft — and she walked north along the scraped trail toward the *chullpa*, the squat funerary tower the line aimed at. One metre wide. The exposed subsoil ran pale as bone between the darker oxidised plain, the way a parting runs pale through dark hair. Underfoot the difference was nothing — a few centimetres of scrape, three thousand years deep. From the air it would be a stroke of chalk. From here it was just where your feet went.

She logged a fix every ten paces. Stop. Stand. Wait for the unit to settle, the little number agonising down — 2.1 metres of horizontal accuracy, 1.4, 0.9, *fix* — then mark, then walk. The ridge came up at the two-kilometre mark, a low brown swell of ground maybe forty metres of rise, nothing dramatic, the sort of hummock any sane trail would slide around to the east where the plain was flat and kind.

The line went over it.

Straight up the face, straight down the far side, and — she checked the unit, checked it again, breath sawing — it came off the back of the ridge on the *same bearing it went up*. Not a hair of correction. Whoever first walked this could not have seen the *chullpa* from the south *wak'a*; the ridge stood between them, a solid wall of ground. You cannot sight a target you cannot see. You cannot correct toward a thing you have never laid eyes on. And yet here was the line, climbing a hill it had no reason to climb, holding a heading it had no instrument to hold, arriving at a tower it could not have seen when it set out.

She sat down on the cold ground at the crest and pulled the data onto the tablet and tried to make it be wrong.

---

This was the part Naila was good at — better than good. The part where a pattern that everyone called *obvious* turned out, under measurement, to be a stack of impossible things wearing a coat. She liked the official story. She wanted to stress that, even just to herself, in the cold: she *liked* it. It was true and it was beautiful and the people who'd worked it out had earned it. Pilgrimage footpaths. Tony Morrison flying over in the seventies and seeing the chalk-strokes nobody had named. The Penn survey crews pegging out their transects across the largest drawing on Earth and finding it — what was the word in the paper, she could see it — *neither the purpose nor how the makers achieved such precision is completely understood.* That was an honest sentence. She respected an honest sentence in a survey paper the way other people respected a cathedral.

The trouble was that *footpath* did a great deal of quiet work.

A footpath was a thing made by laziness. By the cumulative wisdom of feet that wanted the easy way. You did not need a committee to make a footpath; you needed a desire line and time, and the path appeared where the walking was cheapest. Naila had mapped desire lines across university lawns for a methods course once. They bent. They always bent — around the wet patch, toward the gap in the hedge, away from the slope. Feet are economists. Feet hate hills.

These feet had walked up a hill for three thousand years and never once decided to go round.

She opened a clean sheet and started building the case against herself, because that was the only honest direction to build in. *Hypothesis: the straightness is illusory, an artefact of selection — we only notice the straight ones.* She'd looked. The Penn data and her own dozen transects agreed: the network was straight as a habit, straight as a rule, not straight as an accident. *Hypothesis: short straight segments chained together read as one long straight line, the way a polygon fakes a circle.* She measured the segment angles. If the line were a chain of guesses, the bearing would drift — a random walk, error compounding, the kind of slow wander any uncorrected process accrues. The arithmetic on a chain of independent guesses over twenty kilometres should put you hundreds of metres off your target. This line was off by a man's height. *Hypothesis: there was a vantage — a high point, a sighting post.* She'd stood on every rise within sight. From none of them could you see both ends of a single long line. The ground would not allow it.

She did the calculation she'd been avoiding, the one with the rover data fed into it raw: take the standard deviation of her fixes from the best-fit line, propagate it, ask what instrument you would need to do this on purpose, today, with a team and daylight and a plan.

A theodolite. A clear sightline or a chain of them. A datum. A surveyor who'd trained for it.

The makers had none of those, and had done it anyway, and had done it without anyone in charge telling them how, because there was no one in charge. It was not one genius with a secret. It was *everybody*, for thirty centuries, none of them able to see the whole, all of them somehow agreeing on a heading none of them could measure.

She put the tablet face-down on the stone. The wind came up off the plain and found the gap at her collar. Above her the cone of Sajama had taken the first light and gone the colour of a struck match, and around its lower flanks she could see the red, gnarled smudge of the *queñua* — trees where there had no business being trees, the highest forest on the planet, growing because the mountain said it could. Everything here was doing something it shouldn't be able to do. She was beginning to take it personally.

---

The walker found her on the crest.

Naila hadn't heard her come up — at altitude you heard your own pulse before you heard anyone's feet — but there she was, an Aymara woman somewhere north of sixty in a layered *pollera* skirt and a hat the wind couldn't touch, breathing as if the slope were nothing, which for her it was. Doña Rosa. Bashir had reached her, three days ago, in her own language, and a door that had stayed shut to the surveyors' clipboards had come not open but *ajar*. Naila had no Aymara. She had a tablet full of numbers and the few words Bashir had drilled into her, and she used one of them now — a greeting, badly — and Doña Rosa's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile but was related to one.

The woman looked at the rover unit on its tripod over the *wak'a*, then back along the line the way Naila had come, then at the tablet face-down on the stone. She said something. Naila caught nothing of it but the shape — a question, lifted at the end.

Naila turned the tablet over and showed her, because showing was a language. The best-fit line, dead straight, screaming red across the topographic shading. The hill rendered in contour. The line ignoring the hill. She pointed at the line, then at the actual ridge under their feet, then made a flat blade of her hand and held it level and rigid: *straight. This is straight. Over this. How.*

Doña Rosa looked at the screen for a long, courteous moment. Then she looked at Naila with an expression Naila had spent her whole life on the receiving end of and usually hated — the patient look people gave you when you had got the question wrong in a way that was somehow endearing. Coming from this woman, on this hill, it did not feel like being managed. It felt like being corrected by gravity.

She did not answer the *how*. She made a small gesture instead — two fingers, walking, along the line, north — and said a word Naila did know, because Bashir had given it to her: *sarnaqaña*. To walk. To go about. To live, the way you live a road by being on it.

Then she said something longer, and pointed not at the screen but at her own mouth, and walked the two fingers again, and Naila understood with her body before she understood with her head that the answer to *how is it straight* was being declined. Not hidden. *Declined.* The way you'd decline to answer how a song stays in tune — because the question contained a mistake about what kind of thing it was.

Naila tried once more. She crouched, smoothed a patch of pale subsoil with her palm, and drew with her finger: two dots, a line between, the line bowing wrong, then a line bowing right, then a perfect straight one. She tapped the straight one. She tapped the rover. She held up the tablet. *This. This is the achievement. This precision. Surely this is the thing.*

Doña Rosa crouched too, with a grace Naila's knees envied, and looked at the three drawn lines. She put her finger on the crooked one and shook her head, gently — not *wrong*, more *not the point*. She put her finger on the straight one and said *sarnaqaña* again, softly, almost amused, as if to say: *yes, and?* As if the straightness were not the cathedral but the floor of it. As if Naila had walked into the largest drawing on Earth and asked, with great seriousness, *how do you keep the chalk so white,* and the answer was: *by walking on it,* and the *why* of the walking was a separate and larger thing, and that thing was not on offer.

The straightness wasn't the trick. The straightness fell out of the practice the way a path falls out of feet — except it fell out *straight*, and the reason it fell out straight was the practice, and the practice was hers and not Naila's, and there the door stopped.

---

Doña Rosa stood, said one more thing — a kindness, by the tone of it — touched the offering-stone on the *wak'a* with the side of her hand in passing, and went on north along the line, the two fingers of her gait holding a bearing Naila's nine-hundred-thousand-rand instrument could only describe and never produce. Within a hundred metres she was a small dark figure on a pale thread, and the thread ran true to the *chullpa* she could not possibly have sighted from the south, and Naila sat on the cold crest with the most accurate map she had ever made of a thing she now understood less than when she'd started.

Hecklefish, who had appeared on the stone beside the tablet with the unexplained reliability of a thing that exists mainly in the margins, regarded the screen.

"So let me get this straight," he said. "You measured it."

"I measured it."

"And the measurement is perfect."

"The measurement is the problem."

"And the nice lady, who can actually *make* the perfect line, just told you the perfect line is the *boring* part." He let a beat go. "I love this place. They've got the largest drawing on Earth and to them it's *housekeeping.*"

Naila didn't answer. She was looking at the best-fit residuals — the tiny scatter of her fixes around the impossible straight, the breath-and-altitude wobble of her own hand — and underneath it, holding it, the thing the residuals were scattering *around*. The line her numbers could find and never explain. She had come up here to solve a footpath. She was going back down with a footpath that had been kept arrow-straight over a hill it had no reason to climb, for three thousand years, by people who would not tell her how, because *how* was the wrong word, and the right word — the word that walked north on two fingers and didn't look back — was theirs.

She put the cap on the rover. She did not feel solved. She felt, for the first time in a long methodical while, the specific and excellent vertigo of a problem that was not going to let her win — and, underneath that, smaller and warmer and harder to admit: she could not wait to walk the whole thing.

She just wasn't going to be the one who got to say what it was for.
