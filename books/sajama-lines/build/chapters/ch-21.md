# Chapter 21 — The Wink

The mapping team's diesel generator had stopped sometime in the night, and the silence it left was the loudest thing on the plain.

Bashir noticed it the way he noticed everything — sideways, through the ear, before the rest of him caught up. He was sitting on the cold step of the *estancia* with a tin mug going lukewarm in his hands, and the absence of that low industrial drone was a hole in the soundscape he kept reaching into, expecting to snag something. Nothing. Just the *surazo* moving the *paja brava* in long combed waves, and somewhere far off a single dog deciding whether it cared.

Down the slope, the orange tents were coming apart. Two of the surveyors were folding the tripod legs of the total station into their padded case with the tenderness men reserve for instruments that cost more than a house. The drone cases were already stacked. Vargas — the project lead, clean hands, good shoes, a man Bashir had decided early he could not quite hate — stood with a clipboard he was no longer writing on, watching his people pack.

It hadn't been a rout. That was the part nobody back home would understand. There'd been no shouting, no permit waved, no flashing light. There had been a conversation, in Aymara, that Bashir had carried between Vargas and the community council for the better part of an afternoon, and at the end of it the answer had simply been *no, not now, perhaps not.* And Vargas had nodded the way a reasonable man nods when he has run out of leverage and discovered, to his own surprise, that he is relieved.

"They'll be back," Naila said. She'd come out without a coat, which meant she'd been awake a while and had stopped registering temperature, which meant she'd been turning something over. She sat down on the step below him, close enough that he could feel the heat she radiated when her mind was working. "Different outfit. Better funded. Five years."

"Probably."

"And it greens over if they stop walking it." She said it flatly, a fact filed. "So the thing keeps itself safe by needing to be done. You can't archive a verb."

He let that sit. The light was just starting — that first violet bleed at the rim of the world that, at this altitude, didn't look like dawn so much as the sky being slowly switched on from somewhere behind it. Sajama was already lit at the crown, the cone going from grey to a pink so clean it embarrassed the word *pink.* Everything else on the plain was still the colour of ash and bone, waiting.

---

Here is the official story, and it is true, and the people who wrote it down did honest work.

Around four thousand years ago — give or take the way you have to give or take when nobody was writing it down — people on this plain began to walk between the places that mattered. A spring. A rock where something had happened. A tower of stacked stone where they laid their dead. They walked, and where they walked the dark oxidised crust of the desert scuffed away under their feet and the pale subsoil came up, a bright line drawn by the simple arithmetic of going there and going there again. They added the springs and the rocks. The Inca came and went — *came and went*, on a thing already old enough that the Inca treated it as ancestral. The Spanish came and built white chapels, and the walking absorbed the chapels too, the way a river absorbs a stone, without changing course.

Tony Morrison saw them from the air in the 1970s and told the world there was something here. Surveyors from Pennsylvania came and measured what they could. They wrote it up plainly, with the proper professional honesty that says *we know this and we do not know that*: a living sacred landscape, the lines maintained by use, sixteen thousand kilometres of them scored across twenty-two thousand square kilometres of ground. The largest single artwork human beings have ever made. Still being made. The instructions for its continuation are *keep going.*

That's the story. Bashir had spent three weeks inside its language and he believed every word of it. None of it was the hole.

---

Mara was up on the rise with the long lens, and she wasn't shooting.

He watched her not shoot for a while, which was its own kind of thing to watch. The light was doing what light does maybe twice a year on a plain like this — laying itself flat across the ground so that every scraped line stood up in low relief, bright trough and dark shoulder, and the whole web of it suddenly readable in a way it never was in the hard noon glare. A photographer who put that frame on a wall would not have to work again. She had the camera up. She had the exposure. Bashir saw her thumb find the shutter and rest on it, the way you rest a finger on a trigger you have decided not to pull, and then she lowered the body to her chest and just looked, with the eyes she was born with, at a thing she had decided was not hers to take down off the mountain.

He understood that. He had spent the whole book learning to understand that.

There was a sentence — he would not write it down, he would not even shape it fully in the part of his mind that translated — that the *yatiri* had said while they walked, three days ago, on the high segment where the line climbed the ridge it had no earthly reason to climb. She had said it not to him and not to teach him. She had said it *to the going*, the way you say the next word in a thing you are in the middle of saying. And his ear, which could not not parse a language's shape, had caught the grammar of it whole — the verb that held the bearing, the naming that came in an order that was not negotiable, the way the saying and the stepping were one motion and you could not have the line without both.

He had understood, walking it, what no map had told him and no drone could. The straightness was not engineering. Nobody had *solved* the hill. The line went over the ridge because the sentence went over the ridge, and the sentence did not bend because sentences do not bend — you say them in order or you have said something else. The line was the fossil of a saying. The largest drawing on Earth was legible only to the feet that made it, in the language that walked it.

And he was not going to tell anyone the sentence. That was the discipline. The man whose entire gift was reaching all the way in had found the one room where reaching all the way in was a theft, and had been answered — not by being let in, but by being trusted enough to be shown where the wall was.

---

But here is the thing, and it is a real thing, and you can check it.

A sentence does not need to be straight.

You can say the words to the spring and then the words to the tower in the right order and in the right breath while you walk, and you will get *there* — you'll arrive at the tower, the bearing held in your mouth the whole way. Nothing in the saying requires that the *trail* run true. A path that wandered would still be the sentence. A path that took the easy contour around the ridge, that did the sensible thing your lungs are screaming for at four thousand metres, would still link the spring to the tower in the right order and could carry exactly the same words.

But it doesn't wander. That's the hole, and it never closed.

Naila had measured it with a cold eye and no woo and she'd come to peace with it without explaining it away. Up to twenty kilometres at a stretch, a metre wide, dead straight over hills and ravines and the broken ground that has no opinion about your ritual and will happily put a rise between you and where you're going. Held straight by a society with no king to command it, no instrument to sight by, no hill high enough and central enough to read the whole thing from and say *that one's drifting, walk it back.* Built not by one crew in one season but by separate people, separate generations, three thousand years of them, each only ever seeing the few kilometres in front of their own feet.

No one ever saw it whole. There was no vantage. There still isn't, not from the ground — you need an aircraft, and the makers had no aircraft, which is precisely why the aircraft view explains nothing.

So who held the bearing?

Not a person — there was no person who could have. Not a plan — nobody's found a plan, and you don't plan a thing across a hundred and twenty generations. Not the saying — Bashir was sure of the saying now, surer than he'd ever been of anything he'd carried between two tongues, and the saying did not require the straightness. The saying could have curved. Something kept it from curving. Something told three thousand years of separate walkers, none of whom saw the whole and none of whom were ordered to, to refuse the easy contour and climb the hill and hold the line arrow-true over ground that gave them every reason and every cover to let it drift.

He sat on the cold step with his cold tea and felt the question sit down next to him, and he did not answer it.

---

"You've got your closed-mouth face," Naila said.

"I've got my no-comment face. They're different."

"To you."

The *yatiri* came up the track from the lower estancia then, unhurried, a small woman in a brown *pollera* and a hat the wind kept trying for and never quite got, with the rolling economical walk of someone who had spent a life not wasting a single step on this plain. She did not look at the orange tents coming down. She had already, days ago, looked at them exactly as much as she intended to.

She stopped where the line crossed the track — the bright pale scar of it running away north toward Sajama, away south toward a chapel Bashir couldn't see — and she stood on it for a moment with both feet, settling, the way you settle into a thing you're about to continue rather than begin.

Mara, on the rise, did not lift the camera.

The *yatiri* said something, low, to the going, and stepped off north along the line. Two others fell in behind her without a word, an old man and a girl maybe twelve, and the three of them walked the bright scar away from the crew, scuffing it that little bit brighter as they went, keeping it true over the first low swell of ground simply by putting their feet where the words said to put them.

Bashir's ear caught the front edge of what she'd said — enough to know it was the naming, the order, the bearing held in the mouth. Not enough, and not for him, to be the whole of it.

Behind them the line greened a little less. Ahead of them it stayed bright. The difference between those two facts was the entire reason any of it still existed, and it was being maintained, right now, by three people walking who would be dust before the question they were answering had even been properly asked.

"What's it for?" Naila said quietly — not to him. To the plain. To the walkers getting smaller. She was asking it the way Jakobus had asked it, three dawns back, and she already knew the only answer she was going to get, which was the answer Jakobus had been given and had gone quiet for: *it is for walking.*

A function, grounded and physical. A bearing held in a sentence, walked into the ground, kept bright by the keeping. That much was real and you could put your hand on it. The crew owned the telling of it. They never came within a continent of owning the thing.

But the function didn't close the hole. That was the strange beauty of it. You could know exactly what the line was *for* — for walking, for saying, for going to the spring and the tower in the right order with the right words — and still be left holding the one fact the function did not explain. The saying could have curved. It didn't. No king said don't. No surveyor said don't. No one stood high enough to even know it hadn't.

The walkers crested the swell and went down the far side, and for a moment only the *yatiri*'s hat was visible above the line, a dark dot riding the bright thread north toward the mountain, and then that too dropped from sight, and the line ran on without them up the ridge it had no reason to climb, holding its bearing for whoever came next, true as a drawn breath, over a hill no one ever stood on top of to check.

So. Sixteen thousand kilometres. Three thousand years. No command, no instrument, no vantage, no one who ever saw it whole.

Something kept it straight.

You decide what.
