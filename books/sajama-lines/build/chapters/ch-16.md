# Chapter 16 — The Night on the Plain

The cold came up out of the ground before the sun was properly down, the way it always did here — not a chill that arrived but one that had been waiting under the *paja brava* all day and rose the moment the light stopped feeding it. Mara felt it through the soles of her boots first, then her knees, then the small of her back where she'd crouched too long over the fire ring, feeding it dried llama dung and a few sticks of queñua that snapped like old bone.

She did not lift the camera.

That was the discipline now, the one she'd been practising for four days the way you practise not touching a bruise. The fire was beautiful. Of course it was. Firelight on dark Aymara faces under a sky going violet at the top and bruise-orange along the rim of the plain, Sajama itself a pale ghost over the herder's left shoulder, holding the last of the day on its snow like it was reluctant to let go. It was the most photographable thing she had stood in front of in two years. The frame composed itself without her. And she let it pass, and pass again, and the not-taking sat in her chest like a held breath.

Across the fire, Tomasa Quispe was telling a story Mara couldn't follow a word of.

She was the walker who had brought them here — older than she looked or younger, Mara couldn't tell, with the kind of face the wind had worked on until it stopped showing age and started showing weather. *Yatiri,* Bashir had said the first day, very quietly, and then had not used the word again, because using it felt like reaching for a handle that wasn't his. She read the lines. Not read them the way Naila did, with a tape and a clinometer and her jaw set against the impossible. Read them the way you read a sentence you already know by heart.

Now she was talking, and the four other walkers around the fire were laughing at the turns, and Bashir — sitting cross-legged with his hood down despite the cold, because the cold was nothing next to a language he could hear the shape of — was laughing half a beat behind. The half-beat was him translating. It was always him translating. Mara had watched him do it across a dozen countries and she still couldn't decide whether the half-beat was the loneliest thing she'd ever seen or the most generous.

He leaned toward her without taking his eyes off Tomasa. "She's saying her grandfather walked the Pumiri line so many times he could do it in the dark. With his eyes shut. The others are saying that's a lie because nobody can do that line drunk, and apparently the grandfather—" he listened, the corner of his mouth going up "—the grandfather was famously, *historically* drunk."

"And could he?"

"Do it drunk?" Bashir's head tilted. There was a longer pause, a longer listen. Tomasa said something else and the laughing changed key, went warmer and lower, and then folded itself away into the snap of the fire. "She says the line holds you up. If you say it right, it carries you."

Mara waited for more. There wasn't more. Bashir had stopped translating, and she understood — the way she understood a shutter that had jammed — that the stopping wasn't a failure to keep up. It was him declining to carry that one across. *Say it right.* Whatever Tomasa meant by saying it right, Bashir had heard the edge of it and set it down on the far side of the fire where it belonged.

---

Hecklefish, if Hecklefish had been allowed within a thousand kilometres of a sacred *wak'a,* would have had a great deal to say about a sixteen-thousand-kilometre artwork maintained, per the dossier, *by drunk grandfathers walking home.* Something like: *oh good, the largest drawing on Earth and the surveying instrument is a man who's had eight.* He would not have been wrong, exactly. That was the maddening thing about him. He'd point at the gap and the gap would be real.

Because here was the gap, and Mara had spent four days watching it refuse to close.

The line that ran north from this *estancia* — Tomasa's line, the one they would walk at dawn — went up over the Pumiri ridge instead of around it. Naila had walked the whole climb yesterday with the laser rangefinder, gone grey and breathless at four thousand metres, and come back and sat down hard and said the deviation across the visible two kilometres, over a *ridge,* was less than a body's width. No instrument. No vantage. You could not see the far end of it from anywhere on it; the ridge itself blocked the sightline at exactly the point you'd most need to correct. And the walkers had held it straight, by foot, for three thousand years, building it the way you build a path — by going. *Subtractive,* the survey called it. Scrape the dark stones, expose the pale ground beneath, keep walking so it never greens over.

Mara had photographed the line. The straightness didn't read in any frame she'd taken. It was the thing about this whole place that broke her tools clean in half: from above, from a drone, from the satellite tile Naila had spread across the truck's hood that first night, you could see *that* it was straight and you could not see *one single thing* about why or how, because the why and how were not in the geometry. They were in the going.

She set another knuckle of queñua on the coals and did not reach for the camera that was warm against her hip.

---

Niamh sat on Mara's left, close, the firelight giving her hands something to be legible in. The interpreter — Esteban, a quiet UPenn-trained Bolivian who'd come up with them from Sajama village — had gone to sleep an hour ago in the back of the truck, which meant the conversation had narrowed to whatever Bashir could relay and whatever Niamh could read off the faces. And Niamh, Mara had long since stopped being surprised, read more off the faces than Bashir got off the words.

She touched Mara's wrist. Pointed two fingers at her own eyes, then tipped them toward Tomasa, then made a small flat gesture, palm down, settling — the sign she used between them for *watch this, it's about to go quiet.*

Mara watched.

Frik, three places round the fire, had been doing what Frik did when he was nervous and trying very hard not to be, which was talk too much in the gaps and ride the edge of the thing that made the words jump. He'd been good all night — the cold and the altitude and the careful slow rhythm of the walkers had given his nervous system somewhere to put itself — but now he leaned in, all enthusiasm, and asked Bashir to ask Tomasa the question Naila had been dying to ask for four days.

"Ask her—" a small jerk of his shoulder, swallowed "—ask her how they *check* it. The straight. How does anyone keep it straight if you can't — *brick* — if you can't see the end?"

Bashir didn't ask it straight away. He held it, the way you hold a glass you're not sure is full. Then he asked it gently, in the long looping Aymara that Mara couldn't parse but could hear was *careful,* hedged round with what she now knew were courtesies — *forgive me, I am only asking, you do not have to.*

Tomasa looked at the fire.

This was the moment Niamh had flagged. Mara saw it land. Not a flinch — nothing so coarse. The walkers around the fire didn't go silent so much as *make a space,* the way a room makes space when someone says a name that shouldn't be said at dinner. One of the younger men picked up a stick and turned a coal. Tomasa's hands stayed in her lap.

She said something short. Bashir translated it short.

"She says: 'You don't check it. You say it.'"

Frik waited for the rest. Mara waited for the rest. There wasn't any rest. There was the fire, and the wind starting its night-work in the bunchgrass, and Sajama gone now to a darker grey against the stars that were coming out hard and close the way they only did this high, where the air was too thin to soften them.

Niamh's fingers moved against Mara's wrist again. Slow. *She knows.*

That was the whole of it, and Mara felt it go through her like the cold had gone through her boots. There was a difference — Niamh had spent her entire life on the wrong side of it, being talked over by people who assumed a silence was an absence — between *doesn't know* and *won't say.* Tomasa was not a woman who didn't know how the line stayed straight. She was a woman who had just been asked, kindly, by a guest she'd chosen to bring to her fire, to translate a sentence that did not survive translation, and who had given them exactly as much as the question deserved and not one syllable past it.

*You say it.* Said while walking. The dossier had a clean archaeological word for the radial logic of it — *ceque,* sightlines out from a centre, the way Cusco organised its shrines — and the word explained the map and explained nothing about the night. Because the saying was the surveying. The words you spoke as you went, in order, the naming of the *wak'as* as you passed them, the grammar of the going — *that* was the instrument. Walk it saying it right and the bearing held, and no one had ever needed a vantage because no one had ever been reading it from above. They'd been *inside the sentence.*

And Tomasa would tell you that, and let you feel the size of it, and she would not say the words themselves. Those were the ones that did the work. Those were not for the fire, not for Bashir's careful mouth, not for Esteban asleep in the truck and certainly not for the mapping team forty kilometres south with their drones and their press release about *opening the lines to the world.*

Hecklefish: *so the answer is a sentence and she won't tell you the sentence.* Yeah. *And the largest artwork on Earth is being kept secret by a woman who's giving you tea.* Yeah. That too.

---

Bashir didn't push it. Mara saw the cost of that on him as clearly as she'd seen the not-taking sit in her own chest. His whole gift was reaching all the way in — the *sawubona* of it, the door that half-opens because you knocked in the right language. He had spent his life being the one who got let through. And he set his hands on his knees and inclined his head a fraction toward Tomasa, an acknowledgement, the smallest possible *I heard where the door was, I won't lean on it,* and he changed the subject to the grandfather again, the drunk and famous grandfather, and the walkers laughed, and the space the question had opened closed back over like water.

Tomasa looked at Bashir for a moment when he did it. Mara caught the look because she catches looks for a living. It wasn't gratitude, which would have been too small. It was *recognition.* The look of a keeper clocking that the man across the fire knew what a kept thing was, and what it cost to keep it, and had just chosen — in front of all of them, in her own language — to help her keep it.

That, Mara thought, was the only thing they'd actually been given tonight. Not the words. Not how the line stayed straight. They'd been given the knowledge that there *was* a door, that it was real and old and held shut on purpose by someone entirely capable of opening it, and that they'd be allowed to walk up to it at dawn and stand beside it and feel the size of the room behind it.

She would not photograph the dawn walk. She'd already decided. The frame that would make her name — the line going arrow-straight up over the Pumiri ridge into the rising sun, pilgrims small and black against the pale scraped ground, the largest drawing on Earth caught in the one image that proved it existed — was the exact frame that would lift the meaning out of the feet and pin it to a wall in a gallery in a city where no one would ever say it right. The drone stayed in the case. The thieving frame stayed untaken. That was the whole of her discipline and it felt, sitting in the cold with the fire dying, less like a sacrifice than like finally putting down something heavy she'd been carrying since the last war.

Niamh nudged her. Pointed at Tomasa, then up the dark slope of the plain to where the line ran invisible toward the ridge, then made the small settling gesture again, palm down — *watch this* — but slower now, gentler, turned at the end into something almost like the sign for *trust.*

The walkers were banking the fire. Tomasa got up, joints and weather, and stood looking north at the ridge they couldn't see and would walk before the sun cleared it. She said one more thing, to no one, to the dark, to the line.

Bashir didn't translate it.

Mara didn't ask him to.
