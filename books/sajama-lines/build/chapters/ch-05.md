# Chapter 5 — The Trailhead

The cold came up through the soles of Niamh's boots before the sun cleared the ridge, and she read the morning the way she read everything — by where the bodies pointed.

Six of them stood at the edge of a thing she could not yet see. The plain ran pale and frost-burned to the foot of the mountain, and the mountain stood over it the way a parent stands over a sleeping room: not watching, exactly, but present in a way that organised the air. Sajama. White at the top against a sky already going that bruised violet it only went up here, where there was too little atmosphere left to soften it. Below the snow, the red-barked queñua trees clung to the slopes in their impossible groves, gnarled and low, the highest forest on the planet and looking like something dreamed rather than grown.

Niamh did not look at the mountain long. She looked at Doña Rufina.

The old woman stood three paces ahead of the crew with her back to them, and her stillness was not the stillness of waiting. Niamh had spent her whole life inside silence, and she knew its dialects the way Bashir knew the seam where one language gave way to another. There was the silence of someone who hadn't decided. There was the silence of someone gathering. And there was this — the silence of someone who had already decided everything and was simply letting the morning catch up.

Beside her, Yola the interpreter signed low and close, fingers tucked in against the wind: *She's reading the ground.*

Niamh nodded. She'd guessed. The yatiri's eyes moved along something at her feet, slow, the way you'd run a finger down a page in a script you'd known since childhood. There was a line there. Niamh could half-see it now as the light came round — a paler thread in the dark gravel, a hand's width, maybe two, running dead away from them toward the lower ridge and over it and gone. It did not waver. It did not consult the land. It went.

Forty metres off, the mapping team was setting up.

She felt them before she turned — a wrongness in the morning's weight, bodies clustered with the particular busy lean of people who are running a clock. Three of them, then four, around a tripod-mounted rig that caught the early sun on its lens housing. A generator coughed. A man in a clean technical jacket — Devereux, the one who shook hands like he was confirming a transaction — was walking the perimeter of where the line began with a tablet held out in front of him, framing it, framing the crew, framing Rufina's back.

Niamh watched his mouth. Distance and the angle made it hard, but she'd read worse. *...the radial origin point, you can see it converges right—* and then his face turned and she lost the rest in his cheek.

Frik had gone rigid the way he did when he was holding a sound down. His shoulder jerked once. He breathed through his nose, eyes on the team, and Niamh saw his jaw working at a word he wouldn't let out — not here, not at her back, *not at her back* he'd said in the truck last night, knuckles white, and Mara had said *you're fine, Frik, we're all white-knuckling it.*

Mara had her camera at her chest. Not up. Down.

Devereux's people had a drone on the ground beside the generator, rotors folded, a fat grey thing the size of a dinner plate. One of them — younger, sunburnt, eager — knelt over it. Niamh watched the way his hands moved and knew it was about to go up.

Rufina turned around.

She was not tall and she was not soft. Her face was the colour and texture of the plain itself, scored by sixty years of this sun and this wind, and her eyes when they came up off the ground and onto the crew were not unfriendly. They were *exact*. She looked at each of them the way she'd looked at the line — reading, placing — and Niamh felt herself read in turn and held still under it, the way you hold still when someone competent is taking your measure and you want them to get it right.

Rufina spoke. Two words, maybe three, the Aymara coming out of her low and flat and final.

Bashir answered.

Niamh couldn't hear it but she could see the whole shape of what happened to it — to the morning, to Rufina's shoulders. Bashir had stepped half a pace forward and said something back in the same tongue, and it was the *register* of it that landed, Niamh could tell, the way his voice must have dropped and curved into a courtesy that wasn't on any phrasebook page. Rufina's eyebrows moved. Just a fraction. The corner of her mouth did something that wasn't a smile and wasn't not one.

*He greeted her like a person,* Yola signed, and there was respect in the interpreter's hands. *Not — he didn't ask her anything. He just — said good morning the long way. The proper way.*

Niamh had watched Bashir do this for three books now and it still cost her something to witness, a small clean ache. He reached people through the door of their own mouths and the door half-opened and you could see the room behind it warm by a degree. And then — she'd watched this too — he'd stand in that doorway and be unable to walk away, translating the menu, translating the argument at the next table, carrying every conversation in the place and never once just being inside one. The loneliness of it had a shape and the shape was *standing in doorways.*

The drone whined up off the gravel.

Niamh felt it before she saw it — a stutter in the air, a frequency that lived in her chest more than her ears. The sunburnt kid had launched it and it climbed fast, tilting toward the line, toward the ridge, toward the place where the pale thread crested and vanished. Going up to get the picture. The god's-eye. The thing that had cracked Nazca on a thousand magazine covers — *see, it's a hummingbird, it's a monkey, mystery solved, the makers must have had* — Niamh stopped the thought. She watched Rufina.

Rufina did not look at the drone.

That was the thing. That was the whole thing, and Niamh felt it go down her spine like cold water. The old woman did not give the climbing machine so much as a glance. She watched the crew, and she let the drone go up, and her face said — clearer than any sign, clearer than any word Yola could hand her — *let it. Let it see everything. It will see the line and it will not see one single thing that matters, and you all know this, and the fact that you know it is why you are still standing here and they are over there.*

Niamh's breath caught.

Up the slope the drone hung over the crest and pivoted, and even from here she could see the operator's posture change — the lean toward the screen, the small frustrated reset. Whatever he was getting, it wasn't resolving into anything. Lines on dirt. A thread that ran over a hill for no reason a camera could give.

Devereux was coming over.

He had the walk of a man who'd already won the meeting in his head. He came up to the edge — to the *edge*, Niamh noted, to where the gravel changed, and stopped there as if a wall stood at the line's first metre, because somebody had already told him and he had already learned what *no* sounded like out here even if he hadn't accepted it. He held the tablet at his side now. He'd put away the frame.

His mouth, close enough now to read clean: *Doña Rufina. With respect. My team has permits from the department, we've coordinated with—* a name Niamh didn't catch *—and all we're asking is a few of my surveyors on the trail behind your group. We document, we don't disturb. We're trying to* protect *this. Get it recognised. Funded.* He turned the tablet face-up, a brochure-bright render of the plain crossed with neat green lines, a logo in the corner. *A managed heritage corridor. Boardwalks at the sensitive points. We keep the lines from—*

Bashir translated. Niamh watched him do it and watched the cost in it, because translating a man who was lying with his whole sincere heart is its own kind of labour — Bashir had to carry every word of it faithfully into Rufina's tongue without letting his own face editorialise, and his face did not, and that was the discipline of him.

Rufina listened. When Devereux said *protect* she did not move. When he said *boardwalks* something happened behind her eyes that Niamh would have called a wince in anyone else and called, in her, an old fatigue — the look of a person who has heard *we're here to help you* arrive over the top of the mountain her whole life and has buried most of the people who believed it.

She answered him in Aymara, four sentences, even and unhurried.

Yola translated for the crew, signing fast, her face apologetic at the bluntness of it: *She says the line does not need a boardwalk. She says a boardwalk is a thing you walk* on. *The line is a thing you walk.* A pause, the interpreter's hands hesitating over the next part. *She says — if the feet stop, the line goes back to the ground in a few years. The grass takes it. So a managed corridor with no feet on it is a — she used a word — it's a dead line. A picture of a line.* Yola's mouth tightened. *She says you can photograph a dead thing all you like.*

Devereux's face did the small math of a man recalculating. *Tell her,* he said, *we can arrange access for traditional users, of course, alongside the documentation phase, we'd never—*

He didn't finish, because Rufina had already turned away from him.

It was the cleanest thing Niamh had seen all morning. Not rude. Not a dismissal you could put in a complaint. She simply finished the conversation by ceasing to be in it, the way you close a book you've read the end of, and she turned to the crew and looked at them and the morning narrowed to the six of them and her.

She spoke. Yola's hands kept up.

*You.* Rufina's eyes went down the line of them and stopped, briefly, on Bashir, and then moved on, and Niamh understood that the stop had been the permission and the moving-on was that the permission was for all of them, given on his account. *You can come. To the next wak'a. Not far. You walk where I walk and you walk behind me and you do not walk on the line — you walk* beside *it, here—* and she gestured, a clear hand's-breadth to the right of the pale thread *—because the line is not for your feet.*

Niamh felt Naila beside her go very still and very alert, the particular attention Naila gave to a *rule*, a constraint, a thing with edges she could hold.

*You can listen.* Rufina went on. *Some of what I say I will say so you can hear it. Some of it is not for you and I will say it anyway and you will not ask what it was.* Yola's hands slowed here, careful, getting it exact. *The black box—* she meant Mara's camera, and Mara's hand had already tightened on it *—stays down at the places I tell you. You will know the places. If you do not put it down I will know, and that will be the last morning you walk.*

Mara nodded once. Niamh saw her thumb move to the lens cap and rest there, ready, and something in the older woman's shoulders eased a hair at the sight — she'd read Mara's hands the same way Niamh read everyone's, and she'd seen the answer before Mara gave it.

*And you—* Rufina looked at Bashir last, and what she said to him she said in Aymara, only to him, and she did not wait for Yola to render it. She watched his face take it.

Niamh watched it too.

Whatever it was, it was short, and it landed in him like a stone into still water — she saw the rings of it go out across his expression, surprise and then something more complicated, something that closed a door very gently from the inside. He did not translate it. Yola's hands stayed down; the interpreter looked at the ground, having understood and having understood, too, that it was not hers to pass on.

A sentence Bashir would not carry. Niamh filed it where she filed the most important things, the wordless things — the *won't say* she'd spent her whole life learning to tell apart from the *can't.* This was a *won't.* And it had been given to the one man in the world built to need it most, and he had — she watched him do it — *let it be his to keep.*

Rufina turned to the line.

Behind them the generator droned and the drone came down somewhere out of sight and Devereux's people gathered at the boundary they would not cross, and ahead the pale thread ran away over the frost-burned plain toward the lower ridge, dead straight, refusing the land, climbing the hill it had no reason to climb except that three thousand years of feet had said it would. The light was full now. The thread caught it.

Rufina stepped onto the morning beside her own line, and began to walk, and began to speak — low, steady, naming as she went — and the crew fell in behind her, one body's width to the right of the bright scraped sentence in the ground, and Niamh stepped off the gravel after them, into the rule and out of the camera's reach, with the mountain at her shoulder and the unread line running out ahead of her into a country that had never once been visible from above.
