# Chapter 22 — Real, Debated, Invented

The notebook is the one thing Bashir packs the way other people pack a passport: spine cracked open, rubber band gone slack, the cover gone the colour of the plain. He had meant, on the drive down off the Altiplano, to write nothing. To let the walk stay in his feet and not in ink. But the road from Sajama village runs four hours of washboard before it touches asphalt, and you cannot translate a road, and the silence of not-translating is the loudest thing he knows, so the pen comes out.

Hecklefish, riding the dash in his travel bowl, watches the pen the way a cat watches a laser.

"Oh good," he says. "The part where we *show our work.* Everybody loves a man's homework."

"It's not homework," Bashir says. "It's a gazetteer."

"It's homework with a fancy hat."

What goes in the gazetteer is this, and the crew agreed on it the night before, in the cold of the *estancia*, by the woodsmoke, with the interpreter's hands moving for Niamh and Mara's lens capped on the table where it had stayed for two days: *we write down only what we can stand behind.* Real. Debated. Invented. Three columns and a hard wall between them. The wall is the whole point.

---

**REAL.**

The lines are real. You can stand on one. Bashir did — a strip of pale ground a metre wide running off toward the white cone, the darker oxidised surface scraped away to bare the lighter subsoil beneath, and it ran *straight*, not the apologetic straight of a footpath that mostly wants to be where the walking is easy, but the other kind. The kind that climbs the hill rather than skirt it. He measured nothing. Naila measured everything.

The numbers are real and they do not get smaller the longer you look at them. Roughly **sixteen thousand kilometres** of line. Naila had said the comparison out loud on the plain, flat, the way she says true things: about three times the width of the continental United States, laid end to end, drawn by foot, with no wheel and no instrument and no plan anyone has ever turned up. The patterned ground covers something on the order of **twenty-two and a half thousand square kilometres** — call it fifteen Nazcas — which makes it, very probably, the **largest single archaeological artwork on Earth**, and — this is the part Naila kept turning over like a stone with a wrong weight — **one of the least studied.** A single line can run **up to twenty kilometres**, **one to three metres wide**, and hold its bearing over hills and ravines the entire way.

And here is the column-one fact that the others all lean on, the one that is not a flourish: the lines have been **made and re-walked for more than three thousand years.** They were ancient when the Inca came up onto the plain. They are walked in Aymara ritual *today.* Nazca fell silent. Sajama never did. The line stays bright because feet keep scraping it. Stop walking, and the dark surface creeps back, and the largest drawing on Earth begins, quietly, to close its own eyes.

The names that brought it to the outside world go in column one too, with respect and no decoration. **Tony Morrison** — the British explorer and filmmaker, not the novelist, and Bashir writes the disambiguation in because someone always asks — brought the lines to wider attention in the **1970s**. The University of Pennsylvania's **Tierra Sajama** project surveyed them. The published record, drawing on that work, says the thing plainly, and Bashir copies it out word for word because paraphrase is a way of taking: *neither the purpose nor how the makers achieved such precision is completely understood.*

"That's the whole book, isn't it," Hecklefish says, reading upside down, which he claims he can do. "They wrote your wink for you and put it in a survey. Lazy."

"They wrote a fact," Bashir says. "We're borrowing it."

---

There is a credit before any of it, and it goes at the *top*, not the back. Bashir is firm on this and Mara backs him and that settles it.

The lines belong to the Aymara communities of the Sajama district. The walking is theirs. The reading of the walking is theirs. Nothing in the gazetteer was gathered without leave, and the one thing that mattered most — the words said while walking, the saying that holds the bearing — is not in the gazetteer at all, because the *yatiri* did not give it, and a thing withheld is not a thing you go and fetch from elsewhere and slide in through the side door. Niamh had read that distinction off the woman's face before any of them, the way she reads every room: the difference between *doesn't know* and *won't say.* She'd put two fingers flat on the table. Won't say. Leave it.

So the gazetteer thanks the community, by the protocol they asked for, and stops where they asked it to stop. That stopping is the most honest line in the chapter. Bashir knows it. He writes it small and means it large.

---

**DEBATED.**

Then the column that earns its keep: the potato. What the crew *tried*, and what failed, written down without flinching, because the failures are the most useful thing they own.

*Attempt one: the eye in the sky.* They flew the drone the first afternoon, before they knew better. They pulled the satellite tiles. They had the god's-eye view that "solved" Nazca for the postcard racks — the whole web from above, radiating off the *wak'as* and the *chullpas* and the white chapels like cracks in old glaze. And it explained nothing. It was the most powerful tool any of them had ever carried up a mountain and it was a dead end that *looked* like progress, because — and Naila said this last, after a long quiet — **the makers never had it.** No one who scraped a metre of that line ever saw the line whole. The view that flatters the outsider is the one view three thousand years of walkers were denied. *Failed: the map is not the made thing. The made thing is the walking.*

*Attempt two: the straight line, by daylight, with everything.* This is the potato proper and Bashir gives it room. The adversary's mapping team — clean-handed, well-funded, not villains, just certain — had set up to lay a survey-grade line over a low ridge, to demonstrate, they said, how achievable the precision was. They had a total station. They had GPS. They had a vantage, a plan, daylight, and a man whose entire job was straightness. And they made a potato. The line wandered at the ridge crest where the slope stole the sightline; it had to be back-corrected; it took a morning and a crew and a battery they kept swapping, and at the end of it they had a few hundred metres of decent-but-not-arrow line and a lot of opinions about wind.

The makers had no instrument. No vantage. No one in command. They built it piecemeal across millennia, different hands, different lifetimes, no king to enforce a standard — and held it dead-straight for twenty kilometres for three thousand years.

"So the rich guys with the laser lost to dead people with no shoes," Hecklefish says, delighted, vicious, exactly right.

"The rich guys with the laser," Bashir says, "had a vantage and a plan and they wandered at the ridge. That's the column. Write it cold."

He writes it cold. *Failed: precision is not the hard part with instruments. It is the hard part without them. The gap between the planned daylight attempt and the flawless hand-and-foot result is the thing — not a wonder reached past, a wonder measured.*

*Attempt three: Naila's model.* She had wanted, for two days, to derive it — to find the trick, the back-sight cairn, the fire-by-night, the sighting-rod chain, the human method that produced the line, and to prove it was *neither* trivial footpath *nor* impossible woo but achievable, human, and stranger than both. She got partway. A radial system, sightlines node to node, the logic of a *ceque* scaled across a district — the Cusco shrines were organised on radial lines out from a centre, and Sajama reads like that idea grown to mountain size. It is a real and grounded frame. It accounts for the *radiating.* It does not, on its own, account for the *holding* — the refusal to bend for the ridge, kept by separate hands over three thousand years with no one to enforce it.

Naila had stood on the plain at the end and not finished the model. She'd said, in her flat way, that she was at peace not finishing it, and Bashir had watched her decide that was true and then make it true, which is harder than it sounds and is not the same as giving up. *Failed, and the failure is the finding: the pattern is real, measurable, humanly achieved — and not closed. Leave it open. Do not reach.*

---

**INVENTED.**

And now the small fenced paddock at the back, the one that exists so the wall stays visible: the fringe, labelled as fringe, never the narrator's anything.

There isn't much. That is itself the point and Bashir writes it down: unlike Nazca, with its spacecraft runway and its hummingbird for the believers, Sajama has been spared a developed woo tradition, because the world has barely looked at it. Obscurity was its armour. The honest open question here is internal to archaeology — *how, by hand, by foot* — not a claim about builders from elsewhere. That is exactly what makes the hole strong. It has nowhere fashionable to go.

What little reaching there was on this trip, Bashir attributes to the human who did the reaching, because a flaw belongs to a person, not to the page. One of the adversary's surveyors — frustrated at the ridge, watching his corrected line and the ancient uncorrected one run parallel and the old one straighter — had said, low, half to himself, that you could lay a grid like that off a sightline from *above*, that the geometry was too clean for ground-level work, that maybe someone, somehow, had surveyed it from the sky.

Bashir writes the man's over-reach down and labels it *his.* An Earth-energy grid, a ley-line lattice, a line sighted from space — these are things a tired, certain man reaches for at the foot of a ridge that just beat his laser. They are not findings. The narrator does not hold them. They go in the *Invented* column with a name attached and a note: *over-reach, dramatised, the flaw of a fallible man, never the book's verdict.*

"You're being awfully nice to the guy who tried to steal it," Hecklefish observes.

"He didn't steal it," Bashir says. "He got out-read and he stepped back. The locals said no, and we said no with them, and his project's paused. That's in column one." He looks up. "He's clean-handed. He's just wrong about the sky."

"That's the nicest thing you've ever said about anyone."

"It's the truest. They're not the same, but here they overlap."

---

The road touches asphalt. The washboard stops, and the silence changes register, and Bashir feels the specific relief he never says out loud — the one that comes when the noise of a hard problem resolves into something with clean grammar, the way a crowded room resolves into one language he can finally rest inside. The gazetteer has that grammar now. Real, fenced from debated, fenced from invented. The wall holds.

He gets to the endnote, the last thing, and he sits with it longer than the rest combined.

Because the temptation is to *finish*. To put the wink in his own mouth and answer it, to be the clever outsider who cracked it, and he can feel exactly how good that would feel and exactly why it would be a theft. The *yatiri* out-read every one of them on the thing that mattered. She walked the line with the words that held it and gave them the walking and kept the saying, and when Jakobus — last of all, off his home ground as always, deferring as always — had asked her the only question the crew were ever really built to ask, *what is it for,* she had given him the only answer that was hers to give, and given it to *him*, not to the page, and let it land in his weathered hands where he went quiet and handed the moment straight back to her.

*It is for walking.*

Bashir does not put that in column one as a solution, because it isn't one. He puts the walking itself in column one — ongoing, alive, theirs — and he leaves the rest where it belongs.

So the endnote, when he finally writes it, is short. The lines are still being walked. The practice did not pause when the cameras came and will not pause when they go; the cameras are not load-bearing and never were. The hole stays open because it is honest, not because anyone failed to close it. The map never told it. The instruments never held it. And the question the survey itself left lying in plain sight — the one no debunk shut and no believer earned —

he writes it down, exactly, and does not answer it:

*No king commissioned this, and no one alive ever saw it whole. So what told three thousand years of separate walkers — different hands, different lifetimes, nobody in command — to keep every line arrow-straight over the hills?*

Then he closes the notebook, snaps the slack rubber band back over the cracked spine, and watches the white cone shrink in the wing mirror, still presiding, still the thing the whole plain orients by, still being walked.

"You didn't answer it," Hecklefish says, almost gentle, for him.

"No," Bashir says. "It wasn't mine to answer."

The cone drops below the ridgeline. The line is still up there, bright, because somewhere behind them feet are keeping it that way.
