# Chapter 2 — The Mapping Team

The generator started before the sun cleared the ridge, and Mara heard it the way she heard everything now — as a thing that might be a thing, or might be nothing, her body deciding before her mind got a vote. Diesel. Steady. Not a vehicle. A camp.

She came out of the tent with the cold in her teeth and the cone of Sajama hanging over the plain like a held breath, pink at the snowline, and she stood there a moment letting the mountain do what it did to people, which was make them small and quiet, before she turned toward the noise.

Across the flat, maybe a kilometre off, white shapes had grown overnight. Not adobe. Not tin. Domed tents, the expensive kind, with a flysheet that snapped in the surazo and a logo on it she couldn't read at this distance but could already tell was *designed* — a word she'd used about a hundred press kits and never once as a compliment.

"They moved fast," Naila said behind her. She had her hands wrapped around a tin mug and her hood up and her eyes on the white shapes with the flat attention she gave a circuit diagram. "Yesterday that was empty ground."

"Yesterday they were a rumour." Mara lifted the long lens out of habit, framed the camp, and then — out of a newer habit, the one she was still learning — lowered it again without pressing the shutter. Just looked. Six domes. A larger marquee. Two pickups, dust to the windows. A drone case open on a folding table, foam cut to shape, the foam empty. So the drone was already up. She tilted her head back and found it: a fleck against the violet zenith, holding a slow box over the plain, methodical as a printer.

"They're surveying," Naila said.

"They're announcing." Mara had been to enough launches to know the difference. A survey is quiet. This had a marquee.

---

They walked over after breakfast because not-walking-over would have meant pretending, and Bashir said pretending was a thing you could smell. He led, which was correct; the language went in front. Frik came because Frik came, hands jammed in his pockets, jaw working on a syllable he was sitting on hard. Niamh came with the small notebook she read faces into, and her interpreter, Tomás, a quiet Bolivian-Welsh kid who'd learned BSL in Swansea and Aymara from his grandmother and had the rare gift of disappearing while he worked. Mara brought the camera and a promise to herself about it.

The man who met them at the edge of the camp was about forty, fleece and good boots, the unhurried friendliness of someone whose funding was secure. "You're the documentary lot," he said, in English, before Bashir had said a word in anything. "Word travels. There's about nine people on this plain and four of them are us." A grin. "Daniel. Daniel Roeg. I'm running the survey."

"Bashir." A small nod, and then — Mara watched him do it, watched him *choose* — he stayed in English, matched Daniel's register exactly, the easy mid-Atlantic of a man who'd done a lot of conferences. He could have gone anywhere. He went where Daniel already was. The door half-opened. Daniel relaxed another notch.

"Come see," Daniel said. "We've got coffee that didn't come out of a sachet."

The marquee was warm and bright and smelled of real coffee and hot electronics. A bank of monitors along one wall, and on the largest of them the plain laid out in false colour — the lines picked out in hot pink against a cool grey ground, a web of them, radiating, crossing, running off the edge of the frame and on, and on.

"LiDAR composite," Daniel said, with the helpless pride of a man showing you his child. "Drone-flown, ground-truthed. Six weeks of flying. We've mapped"— he touched the screen and the figure ticked up in a corner —"call it eleven thousand kilometres of confirmed line so far. We think the real number's north of sixteen."

"It is," Naila said.

He glanced at her, recalibrating. "You've read the Tierra Sajama work."

"I've read everyone's work." Naila wasn't being rude. She was being accurate, and accuracy in her came out flat as a ruler. She stepped close to the screen, close enough that the false colour lit her face pink, and traced one line with a fingertip — a long one, running from a node near the mountain's foot straight off toward the south, over what the topo shading showed clearly as a ridge. It did not bend at the ridge. It climbed it.

"That one," she said. "How long?"

"Bit under twenty K." Daniel was pleased to be asked. "Beautiful, isn't it. Dead straight over that whole rise. We've got bearing-deviation under half a degree across the run."

"With what instrument?"

"Total station, GPS base, the usual—"

"No." Naila turned to look at him, and Mara felt the temperature of the conversation change, the way you feel a cloud cross the sun. "Them. The makers. With what instrument."

Daniel opened his mouth, and Mara watched a small honest thing happen on his face, a thing she liked him more for. He didn't have an answer. He knew he didn't. "That," he said, "is the question, isn't it. Best guess is incremental. You sight short segments, you correct as you go, errors cancel over distance—"

"Errors don't cancel," Naila said. "Errors accumulate. That's what errors do." She looked back at the screen, at the line that climbed the ridge instead of skirting it, the line that had cost more to make than the easy line beside it which did not exist because no one had ever wanted the easy line. "If you walk a bearing by eye over a hill you can't see past, you drift. Every time. Half a degree over twenty kilometres is"— her lips moved —"under two hundred metres of lateral error. They held it to that. By foot. Over three thousand years, by *different* feet." She said it the way another person might say *and then the bridge held*, with a kind of cold awe that had no room in it for the word *miracle* and no patience for the word *easy*.

The marquee was quiet. The drone hummed somewhere overhead, mapping.

"Yeah," Daniel said, softer. "It does that to you, doesn't it."

---

While Naila and Daniel orbited the screen, Mara drifted, because drifting was how she saw. There was a folding table with the press kit fanned across it, the laminate catching the worklights, and she let her eyes graze it the way she'd graze a room before she lifted a camera in it.

*UNLOCKING THE GREATEST ARTWORK ON EARTH*, the cover said, over a drone shot of the lines so over-saturated the subsoil glowed like neon. Inside: phrases. *First complete survey. Definitive map. The mystery, solved.* A timeline. A rendering — and here Mara's drifting stopped, because the rendering was an artist's impression of a *visitor centre*, glass and rammed earth, tasteful, low, with a viewing platform and a little dotted path leading out onto a line, and tiny rendered people in tiny rendered anoraks walking it with their tiny rendered phones up.

A second board, behind it. *PROPOSED INTERPRETIVE TRAIL.* A map with one of the long lines highlighted in friendly orange, waymarked, with icons — a viewpoint, a rest stop, a QR code on a post.

She didn't photograph it. She wanted to. The frame was *right there*, the whole argument of the book in one laminate, the gloss render of the path next to the screen showing the real one. She felt the old pull in her shutter finger, the war-photographer's reflex that had never once asked permission, that had taken the worst day of a stranger's life and made it a prize-winning rectangle. She breathed. She let it go. Some images you don't take, not because you can't, but because taking them is the same gesture as the render: lifting the thing out of the feet that made it and hanging it on a wall.

But she read it. Reading it was hers.

"Roeg," she said, casual, camera down. "The trail. The waymarked one. Whose land does it cross?"

A small pause. The right kind of question landing in the wrong kind of silence.

"It's a partnership," Daniel said. "Community partnership. We've got a memorandum with the regional—" he caught himself. "We're talking to people. It's early."

Across the marquee, Niamh's hand moved against her thigh, low, the small private sign she made to Tomás that meant *watch*. Tomás watched. Mara watched Niamh watch.

---

The thing about Daniel Roeg, Mara decided, watching him pour her coffee he didn't need to pour, was that he was not a villain and would have been so much easier if he were. He believed it. The visitor centre, the trail, the QR posts — he believed they were *saving* the lines. He said so, unprompted, in the warm tent, with the genuine feeling of a man who'd flown six weeks of drone box-patterns over the most beautiful thing he'd ever mapped and could not bear the thought of it being forgotten.

"Nobody's heard of them," he said. "That's the scandal, isn't it? Fifteen times Nazca. The biggest thing people ever drew. And Nazca gets a quarter of a million visitors a year and a UNESCO listing and these"— he gestured at the screen, the hot-pink web —"get *me*. Get a Penn project from the nineties and a film from the seventies and then thirty years of nothing. If we don't put them on the map, properly, with infrastructure, with a *story* people can follow, they vanish. The young people leave for El Alto, the walking stops, and you know what happens then." He looked, for a second, genuinely stricken. "It greens over. The line you saw climb that ridge. Three thousand years and it greens over in *forty*, because the feet stopped."

It was, Mara thought, the most dangerous kind of true. He had the fact exactly right. The line lives by the walking. Stop and it dies. And from that true fact he had built a glass visitor centre and a QR post, the way you'd build a fence to save a wild thing, never noticing the fence was the thing that killed it.

Bashir hadn't spoken in a while. Mara found him at the marquee's open flap, looking not at the screens but out — out across the real plain, where, perhaps two hundred metres off, three figures had appeared. Aymara. An older woman in a bowler hat and layered pollera, two younger walkers behind her. They had stopped at the edge of the camp's churned ground, where the pickups had cut the surface, and they were not coming closer. They were looking at the white domes the way you look at something pitched on your floor.

The older woman's eyes went up — to the drone, the fleck in the violet, holding its slow box over the lines. She watched it for a long moment. Then she said something to the two behind her, short, and the three of them turned and walked back the way they'd come, out onto the pale ground, in a single file that was, Mara saw, dead straight.

"What did she say?" Mara asked. Quietly. Just to Bashir.

He didn't answer at once. His whole gift was reaching all the way in, and Mara watched the cost of it cross his face — that he *had* reached, that he'd caught it on the wind, and that he was deciding something about whether it came back out of his mouth in English in a tent full of monitors.

"She said," Bashir managed, and then stopped. Tried again. "It's — the verb. There's a verb. It means *to read aloud while walking*. One word." He looked at the drone, and then at the glowing screen, the plain rendered in colours no maker ever saw. "She said the machine is looking at the words and can't hear them."

Behind them, the figure on Daniel's monitor ticked up. *11,140 km confirmed.* The drone hummed. The marquee was warm and bright and smelled of good coffee and the thin electric ozone of everything that could be measured.

Frik, by the press kit, had been holding it a long time. Too long. The plain was quiet and the generator was steady and the word came out of him the way it had to, sudden and loud and entirely uninvited:

"*Wrong map!*"

Daniel turned, startled. Frik's jaw clenched, his hands flat against the table, his face doing the thing it did — the apology and the helplessness and the truth all at once. Tomás, gently, started to explain the Tourette's, and Daniel, to his credit, waved it off kindly, *no, no, all good*.

But Mara was watching the laminate under Frik's hands. The orange trail. The friendly waymarks. The QR post out on a line that the old woman, just now, had read aloud as she walked it, in a verb the survey didn't have.

*Wrong map.* It was, she thought, the only thing anyone had said all morning that was completely accurate, and it had come out of the one of them who could least help saying it, and Daniel had filed it under *symptom* and poured more coffee.

The drone changed pitch, banking for its next pass.

"Daniel," she said. "When does the press launch run?"

He brightened, back on solid ground. "Three weeks. We've got two networks and a streaming deal interested. *The mystery, solved.* Big reveal." He grinned the secure grin. "Why — you want in? Plenty of room for the documentary lot."

Out on the plain, the three figures had become one, a single dark stroke a long way off, holding their line over the ground toward the foot of the mountain, getting smaller, not bending.

Mara looked at them, and looked at the screen, and said nothing at all.
