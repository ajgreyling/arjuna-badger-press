# Project — *The Scramble* *(working title)*

> **Type:** homage / craft exercise · **Lineage:** Philip K. Dick, *A Scanner Darkly* (1977) — themes,

## Provenance & legal (binding)

- **Not public domain. Not licensed.** *A Scanner Darkly*, its characters (Bob Arctor / Fred, Donna,
  Barris, Luckman, Charles Freck), Substance D, the scramble suit, and New-Path are the **intellectual
  property of the Estate of Philip K. Dick.** This project is an **unauthorised homage and craft
  exercise** under the Arjuna Badger Press house tooling — *not* a licensed adaptation and **not for
  commercial release**. No claim of ownership; no commercial use implied. (Same standing as
  [`../no-fear-cycle/`](../no-fear-cycle/PROJECT.md), an unauthorised *Warhammer 40,000* fan homage.)
- **What we borrow:** the **craft and the engine**, not the text. Specifically — the split-self
  surveillance conceit (a man assigned to surveil his own undercover identity), the anonymity
  apparatus, dissociation as plot *and* prose, the slow tonal slide from stoner comedy into grief, the
  unreliable narrator whose two halves no longer recognise each other, and the documentary-cold coda
  that turns the whole book into an indictment.
- **What we do NOT do:** transcribe PKD's plot beat-for-beat with names swapped; reproduce his scenes,
  dialogue, or sentences; or "make minor changes and substitutions" to his text. That is a derivative
  work and a craft failure. **All prose, characters, scenes, place, and specific beats are original
  Arjuna Badger Press work** — the way the No-Fear novels are original prose over a borrowed world.
  Fidelity is to the *machine of the book*, not its letters. See
  [`canon/ADAPTATION_DOCTRINE.md`](canon/ADAPTATION_DOCTRINE.md).
- **The author's dedication page** (an in-memoriam to real people lost to addiction) is one of the most
  load-bearing things in the source. We honour the *gesture* with an original dedication, not a copy.

## Premise (renamed, modernized, original)

A stimulant called **Tilt** has eaten through a mid-size city the way the last drug did and the next one
will. **Vance Roan** is an undercover narcotics officer so deep in a dealing house that his handlers no
longer trust him with his own face: in the field office and on the record he is **Subject M**, a watched
target on a case he himself is running. The anonymizing kit the department issues — a **shroud rig** that
liquefies his identity on every camera and in every report — means his own superiors do not know that the
narc and the suspect are the same man. Then the order comes down to surveil Subject M around the clock.
Vance is assigned to watch himself, write himself up, and not realise it — because **Tilt** has begun
splitting the part of him that watches from the part of him that lives, and the drug's long quiet cost is
that the two halves stop being able to find each other.

The house he lives in — **Renko, Hutch, the girl, Dora** — is funny until it is not. The rehab that
catches him at the end, **Northway**, is the only institution that will take a burned-out user, and it
also, it turns out, has reasons of its own for harvesting men whose minds have already been scrambled
quiet.

## The structural engine (what makes it *this* book)

**The split-feed.** The book alternates two registers that are the same man and do not know it:
- **LIVED** — close third on Vance in the house: warm, funny, sensory, present-tense pull.
- **WATCHED** — the surveillance record on Subject M: flat, clausal, timestamped, the body-cam-and-report
  voice that *cannot* see in even as it sees everything.

The reader holds both halves the protagonist has lost. The horror is structural, not stated: we
understand the two columns are one life long before Vance does, and the prose **never says so**
(ANTI_PATTERNS §16, §28). The coda collapses the two registers into one plain documentary paragraph —
*smaller than the idea* (§20) — and lets the dedication do the rest.
