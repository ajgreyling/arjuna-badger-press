# Chapter One

The toaster had wronged Renko personally, and he wanted everyone to know.

"Look at the coil," he said. He was on his knees in front of the counter at six-forty in the morning, holding the toaster up to the window light like a jeweler with a flawed stone. "You see how the left side glows and the right side doesn't? That's not a manufacturing defect. That's a *signature*. Somebody crossed the nichrome wire on purpose so it heats uneven, so the bread comes out telling you something, and the day you learn to read it is the day they switch the pattern."

"It's a four-dollar toaster," Hutch said. He had three eggs going and was reaching for a fourth, which meant he believed in a good day. Hutch cooked the way some men prayed: too much, in case somebody came hungry. "You bought it at the gas station."

"That's exactly what you'd do. Buy it where nobody's looking."

Vance leaned in the doorway with his coffee and let the noise be a kind of warmth. This was the part he liked, the part that felt like being a person — Renko narrating a conspiracy theory of breakfast, Hutch breaking eggs one-handed and not listening, the kitchen smelling of butter and the cigarette nobody admitted to. He'd been up since five. He told himself it was the heat. The truth was the little white pill he'd taken at the bathroom sink, the way he took it most mornings now, so the day would have edges and he could keep both halves of his life from leaking into each other.

He was good at keeping them apart. That was the whole skill. You didn't think about it, you just held the door shut, the way you held your breath underwater — not heroically, just continuously, until you forgot you were doing it.

"The water heater's next," Renko said, setting the toaster down with the tenderness of a man releasing evidence into custody. "It cycles at four in the morning. I timed it. Why would it cycle at four? Nobody's drawing hot water at four."

"Thermostat," Vance said.

"That's what they want you to put in the report."

"There's no report, Renko."

"There's always a report." He said it lightly, grinning, and Vance grinned back, and somewhere under the grin a small cold thing turned over and went still, because Renko was wrong about the toaster and the heater and right about the world in a way that nobody at this table could afford to examine before coffee.

The eggs hit the plates. Hutch made a sound of satisfaction. "Dora might come by," he said. "She said maybe. I made enough."

"You always make enough," Vance said.

"That's so there's enough."

Vance ate standing up. He had a way of being useful in the mornings — he'd fix the screen door before he left, he'd top off the oil in Hutch's truck, small mechanical kindnesses he could do with his hands while the rest of him stood the door shut. He'd been a paramedic before this, years of bodies that needed something simple done fast, and the work had left him good with everyone's machinery but his own. He could find a pulse on a stranger in a dark stairwell. He could not have told you, that morning, what he'd done with the previous Tuesday.

That was the part he didn't look at. There were rooms in the week that came back swept clean. Not blank — *cleaned*, like someone had been through with a damp cloth and put the chairs back square. He'd notice the gap the way you notice a tooth gone, tongue going to the place, and then the pill would lift him up over it and he'd be useful again, and the day would have edges.

Dora came at seven-fifteen, which meant she'd been driving since six, which she would not say.

She didn't knock so much as appear, the way she always did — there, in the doorway, in a man's denim jacket two sizes wrong, holding herself the way you hold a cup you don't want to spill. "Somebody's optimistic," she said, looking at the eggs.

"Hutch is in love with the future," Renko said.

"I made you a plate," Hutch said.

"I'm not going to eat it." She said it gently. She never ate here. She'd take coffee and hold it and not drink that either, and the not-eating was so much a part of her that the house had stopped registering it as refusal and started taking it as weather. She set herself against the counter, near Vance, not touching, the careful eight inches she kept around her body like a moat.

She nodded at the toaster. "Renko solve crime this morning?"

"The toaster's a federal asset," Vance said.

"Course it is." She almost smiled. "Best-looking narc in the kitchen." She let it land on the toaster and then her eyes came up and went to Vance for exactly one beat too long, and there was something in them, a flatness with pressure behind it, the look of a person standing at a door deciding not to open it.

He read it as fondness. He wanted it to be fondness, and wanting a thing made a serviceable lens.

"You look like hell," she told him. Clipped, already turning away. "You sleeping?"

"Some."

"Don't *some.*" She put the coffee down untouched. "Sleep. When they're real tired, that's when they take the wrong thing by accident." A shrug, the truth pulled back the instant it was out. "Or don't. Your face."

And she was gone before he'd finished hearing it, the screen door he hadn't fixed yet banging once behind her, and the warning folded itself into the morning like everything else she said — one true thing per visit, dropped and immediately stepped on, so that you carried it out of the room without knowing your pockets were heavier.

He left at eight.

The field office had been a dental practice until two years ago and had never fully decided to stop. There was still a poster of a cross-sectioned molar in the hall, and the rooms had that particular acoustic deadness of places built to muffle a person making a small contained sound of pain. Vance went past the front desk, past the molar, into the back where the rig was.

You put it on in a closet that used to hold X-ray aprons. It was a vest and a collar and a thin band that sat above the ears, all of it heavier than it looked, and when it powered up the cheap mirror screwed to the inside of the door stopped showing you. That was the only word for it. The mirror went on reflecting the closet, the aprons, the dead bulb — and where your face should have been there was a smear, a worked-at smudge of light that the case files called, in their flat way, *a subject*. He'd worn it a hundred times and it still did something to the back of his neck, watching the place where he was supposed to be refuse to be anything.

He went into Stark's office a smear.

Stark didn't look up. Stark never looked up first; it was a thing he did, a small administration of who waited for whom. He was a flat gray man with a flat gray voice and a way of triaging the world into the saveable and the spent without ever raising it above the volume he'd use to order lunch.

"We're stretched," Stark said, which was how he said good morning. "I've got nine houses and resources for six and a supervisor upstairs who thinks the difference is attitude." He slid a folder across. "I need eyes on a residence. Round the clock as we can manage, which is not round the clock, which I'll deny saying."

Vance took the folder. Inside was a board — address, a grid of times, a code. **SUBJECT M.** The address was on Halford, the little stucco place with the screen door that banged, the toaster, the eggs.

"Activity's been flagged," Stark said. "Dealing-level. I want the pattern before I want the arrest. You log who comes, who goes, how often, hands in pockets. You don't engage. You're a camera." He was already onto the next folder. "Take it tonight. Tomorrow's worse."

Something moved at the very edge of Vance, far out past the door he held shut — a flicker, a tongue going to the gap, a swept-clean place where an alarm should have been and instead there was a tidy square of nothing, the chairs put back. *That's my—* The thought started and the rig hummed and the pill carried him up over it the way it carried him over everything, smooth, useful, edged.

"Halford," Vance said. His own voice from inside the smear sounded like someone else reading it off a card.

"Halford. You got a problem with Halford?"

"No," Vance said. "I know the neighborhood."

"Good. Then you'll be quick." Stark turned a page. "Watch the house."

Vance stood up, a subject, and took Subject M out into the heat to go watch it.
