# Foreword

This book owes a debt it cannot repay and will not hide.

In 1977 Philip K. Dick published a novel about a man assigned to spy on himself, and about a drug that
took the part of you that watches and the part of you that lives and quietly cut the wire between them.
He wrote it after years among people he loved who did not survive the thing he was writing about, and
he ended it not with a verdict but with a list of names, and a sentence that said: these were
punished, and the punishment was out of all proportion to the crime, which was that they wanted to play
a little while longer.

*The Scramble* is not that book. It does not retell it, borrow its people, or move its furniture into a
new room. What it borrows is the machine underneath — the idea, which belongs to the form now and not
to any one set of words, that you can build a tragedy out of a man watching the surveillance feed of
his own life and failing to recognize the face. The names here are ours. The city is ours. The drug is
called something else and does its damage by a different road. Where the older book had its scramble
suit, this one has a department-issue rig and a policy written by people protecting themselves from
knowing. The grief is meant to rhyme. The grief is the only thing meant to rhyme.

Arjuna Badger Press makes work this way on purpose, and says so on purpose. The house has written an
unlicensed homage before — a *Warhammer 40,000* novel that claimed nothing it had no right to claim —
and the rule is the same one twice: take the engine, never the text; disclose the debt; do not pretend
the idea was yours. You are holding a craft exercise that wants very badly to also be a real book. That
is for you to decide, which is the only honest way it could be.

The dedication on the next page is not his. We would not presume. But it is pointed, the way his was,
at the people the machine spends and does not count.

— *Arjuna Badger Press*
