# CANON — The Flower That Watches the Sky

- **Book id:** bird-of-paradise-flower
- **File slug:** strelitzia
- **Shelf:** Children's Library — read-aloud picture books, ages 4–8
- **Tradition:** original African pourquoi ("how it came to be") tale. The author's own composed
  story; the text and phrasing are original, the folk-motif (a rooted, sky-gazing, nectar-weeping
  bird = the bird-of-paradise / crane-flower / strelitzia) is the heritage being honoured.
- **Sister tale (CROSS-REFERENCE):** `how-king-lion` — *King Lion and the Birds Who Stole the Sky* —
  the companion pourquoi about the same flower, told from the **sky's** side (why the proud birds may
  never fly again). This book is the same story told from the **flower's** side: what it is like to be
  the one who is rooted, looking up. Shelve and read the two together.

## Spine (one sentence)
A child named {{CHILD}} finds a strange flower shaped like a bright bird looking up at the sky, and
Gogo tells how it was once a bird that flew, now rooted to the earth forever, lifting its orange-and-
blue face to the sky it cannot reach and weeping small drops of sweet nectar — and that even rooted,
it never once stops lifting its face to the light.

## The story under the story
Longing can be beautiful. There are things we lose that we will never get back — and the flower does
not pretend otherwise; its tears are real. But it does not turn its face down. It keeps lifting its
head to the light it remembers, and *that* — the lifting, not the reaching — is what makes it lovely.
The child learns two true things, gently: that you can carry a loss and still be beautiful; and that
the rooted things around us (the old, the still, the ones who cannot fly) are not less for being
rooted — they ask to be tended with tenderness, not pity. We hold this in the house's all-sides
spirit: the flower is not punished in *this* telling; it is honoured. Wistful, never frightening.

## Cast
- **{{CHILD}}** — the watching human child-hero, small and curious, who finds the strange flower in
  Gogo's garden at the edge of the veld and asks what it is. Named with the literal token everywhere.
- **Gogo** — the grandmother, keeper of the old stories; she tends the garden and tells {{CHILD}} the
  flower's tale with love, not fear. Warm, unhurried, sure of the world.
- **The Crane-Flower (the bird that was)** — once a bright bird that flew high and proud; now a bloom
  with an orange crest and a blue heart, rooted in the earth, forever lifting its face. Not a monster,
  not a fool here — a beautiful, longing thing. Its nectar-drops are its tears of remembering the sky.
- **The Sky** — almost a character: wide, blue, bottomless, the thing remembered and loved and
  unreachable. The light it pours down is what the flower lifts toward.
- **The small living world** — sunbirds, bees, a duiker at the fence, the morning wind — the company
  that keeps the rooted flower; the sunbird sips its nectar and carries a little of its longing away.

## The refrain (a child can say it back)
> *It cannot fly. It cannot rise.*
> *Still it lifts its face to the sky.*

Recurs across the spreads. Early on it is told of the flower with wonder; in the middle it turns
sorrowful (the bird that fell, the wings that stiffened); by the end {{CHILD}} says it back — now it
means *us*: even rooted, even when we cannot rise, we may still lift our faces to the light. Same
words, turned by the story.

## Spread plan (13 spreads)
1. Morning in Gogo's garden at the veld's edge; {{CHILD}} finds a strange flower shaped like a bird.
2. Close on the flower — the orange crest, the blue heart, the beak-shaped sheath; it *looks* like a
   small bird looking up. {{CHILD}} asks Gogo what it is.
3. Gogo sits down to tell it: "Long ago, little one, that flower was a bird, and the bird could fly."
4. The bird that was — high and bright over the golden veld, the whole blue sky its own, wing-glad.
5. The turn (told gently): there came a day the bird could not rise — the canon's companion sorrow,
   the sky lost. (Wings stiffening, feet finding the earth — sad, not frightening; no violence shown.)
6. The rooting — feet becoming roots, the bird sinking warm into the red earth, the veld holding it.
7. The first morning rooted — the bird-now-flower lifts its face to the sky it cannot reach. Refrain
   lands hard here: *still it lifts its face to the sky.*
8. The weeping — small bright drops of nectar gather at its lifted face: the tears of looking up and
   remembering flying. Beautiful, not grim.
9. But it is not alone — a sunbird comes, sips the sweet tears, and carries a little of the sky-longing
   away on its wings; bees, morning wind, the living world keeping it company.
10. Season turns — the flower stays, faithful, lifting its face every single dawn; the most beautiful
    thing in the whole garden *because* of its longing, not in spite of it.
11. {{CHILD}} understands — kneels, does not pity, simply *tends*: cups the lifted bloom, gives it
    water, sits with it in the light. Tending the rooted thing with tenderness.
12. Gogo's quiet truth — "We cannot all fly, my child. But every one of us can lift our face to the
    light." {{CHILD}} says the refrain back, and now it means people.
13. Close — evening; {{CHILD}} and Gogo leave the garden, the crane-flower still lifting its face to
    the last of the light; the turned refrain; "— THE END —". (Cross-reference note: read with
    *King Lion and the Birds Who Stole the Sky*.)

## Art direction
Warm painterly storybook tradition — soft gouache/watercolour light, golden-hour African veld, big
skies. Lamplight-and-morning palette: honey gold, warm ochre, soft cream, sky-blue, with the flower's
own orange-and-blue burning bright at the centre of every key spread. Dignified and specific: a
grandmother's garden at the edge of open grassland — red earth, aloes, a low stone wall, marula and
acacia beyond, round homes and a kraal fence in the far distance — never generic "jungle", never
caricature. The crane-flower rendered true to *Strelitzia reginae*: a fan of orange sepals like a
crest, a blue petal-tongue, the grey-green beak-shaped sheath (spathe) it lifts from, beaded with
nectar. The "bird that was" in the memory spreads is clearly the *same* orange-crested, blue-throated
bird as in the companion King Lion book — its crest and throat literally becoming the bloom. {{CHILD}}
small, brown-skinned, barefoot, real and tender; Gogo warm, grey-haired, unhurried. Landscape 3:2,
full-bleed, no text in image. Sorrow handled gently — the loss of the sky is wistful, never
frightening; no violence, no gore; the rooting is soft and warm, the earth holding rather than
trapping.

## Heritage / source note
Honours the southern-African pourquoi tradition that explains the natural world through the choices
and fates of animals — here the *Strelitzia reginae* (crane flower / bird-of-paradise), endemic to
the southern African coast, whose bloom truly does look like a bright crested bird lifting its head
to the sky, rising from a beak-shaped sheath and beaded with sweet nectar that sunbirds drink. The
flower's uncanny resemblance to a poised, grounded, upward-gazing bird is the real-world seed of this
invented tale. No sacred or initiation matter is used; the magic is the gentle pourquoi turn of
bird-into-flower. Companion to `how-king-lion`, which tells the same turn from the sky's side.
