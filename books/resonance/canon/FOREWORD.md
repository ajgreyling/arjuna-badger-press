# Foreword

### *by Reichl Macintoch* †

I built a career on a single, slightly disreputable conviction: that you can frighten and delight a reader far more thoroughly with a real equation than with any monster, provided you do the reader the courtesy of getting the equation right. The dinosaur is nothing. The *chaos mathematics* that lets the dinosaur loose — that is the thing that keeps a person up at night, because it is true, and the reader can feel that it is true, and there is no closing the book on something you suspect is actually out there.

This book is built on the same conviction, and built well, and I do not say that lightly, having spent thirty years watching people reach for my method and grab only the dinosaur.

Here is what the author has understood that almost everyone else misses. The terror and the wonder do not come from the impossible. They come from the *plausible* — from the thing assembled so carefully out of real parts that the reader cannot find the seam where fact becomes fiction, and so must entertain, against all comfort, the possibility that there is no seam. He has done his homework, which is the whole of the job and the part everyone skips. The engineering is real. The physics is handled with respect. When his characters explain a thing to you, the explanation is *correct*, and it is also, somehow, thrilling — which is the hardest trick in this entire trade, and he performs it as though it were easy.

And he has done the other thing I always cared about most, the thing the imitators never bother with: he has made the science a *moral* problem and not merely a spectacular one. It is not enough to build the marvel. Someone must decide what it is for, and who controls it, and what it costs the people standing nearest to it — and the book knows that those questions, not the marvel itself, are the actual story. The cleverest people in these pages are not made safe by their cleverness. They are made *responsible* by it, which is far more frightening, and far more interesting.

At the centre of it is an engineer I would have been glad to write myself and a little envious not to have — a mind that reads a built thing the way the rest of us read a sentence, and is therefore the first to understand exactly how much trouble everyone is in. That is the correct hero for this kind of story. Not the strongest person in the room, not the bravest. The one who *understands soonest*, and must then carry the unbearable interval between understanding and everyone else catching up.

I will not tell you what the marvel is. I will tell you that the author resists, throughout, the great temptation of the form — the temptation to let the wonder off the leash, to let it become magic, to stop doing the hard work of keeping it bolted to the real. He never lets go of the leash. Every astonishing thing in here is paid for, in advance, with a fact. That is discipline, and it is rarer than talent, and it is the whole reason you will believe a story you have every right not to.

Read it as I would have read it: with a pencil, ready to catch him cheating. You will not catch him. And somewhere around the middle, you will stop trying, and start worrying instead about whether the thing he is describing might actually be possible — which is precisely where he wants you, and precisely where the best of this strange trade has always tried to put you.

The science is real enough to hurt. That is the point. Begin.

*— Reichl Macintoch*
