# Foreword

### *by Reichl Macintoch* †

My whole career pretty much comes down to one, slightly sketchy idea: you can scare and amaze a reader way more with a real equation than with any monster, as long as you actually get the equation right. The dinosaur? That’s nothing. It’s the *chaos math* that sets the dinosaur loose that keeps you up at night—because it’s real, and you can feel that it’s real, and you can’t just close the book and pretend it’s not out there.

This book runs on that same idea, and it does it well. I don’t say that lightly—I’ve spent thirty years watching people try to use my approach and only end up with the dinosaur.

Here’s what this author gets that almost everyone else misses: the fear and the wonder don’t come from the impossible stuff. They come from what’s *plausible*—the thing built so carefully from real pieces that you can’t see where fact turns into fiction, so you have to at least wonder if maybe there’s no line at all. He’s done his homework, which is the whole job, and the part everyone else skips. The engineering is solid. The physics is treated with respect. When his characters explain something, the explanation is *right*, and somehow, it’s also exciting—which is the hardest trick in the business, and he makes it look easy.

And he’s done the other thing I always cared about most—the thing copycats never bother with: he’s made the science a *moral* problem, not just a flashy one. It’s not enough to build something amazing. Someone has to decide what it’s for, who gets to use it, and what it costs the people closest to it—and this book knows those are the real questions. The cleverest people here aren’t made safe by how smart they are. They’re made *responsible* for it, which is a lot scarier, and a lot more interesting.

At the center of it all is an engineer I wish I’d written myself—and yeah, I’m a little jealous I didn’t—a mind that reads a machine the way the rest of us read a sentence, and is the first to realize just how much trouble everyone’s in. That’s the right hero for this kind of story. Not the strongest or the bravest, but the one who *figures it out first*, and then has to live with that awful gap before everyone else catches up.

I’m not going to tell you what the marvel is. But I will say the author never gives in to the big temptation—to let the wonder turn into magic, to stop doing the hard work of keeping it real. He never lets go of the leash. Every wild thing in here is paid for, up front, with a fact. That’s discipline, and it’s rarer than talent, and it’s the whole reason you’ll believe a story you really shouldn’t.

Read it the way I would: pencil in hand, ready to catch him cheating. You won’t catch him. And somewhere in the middle, you’ll stop trying, and start worrying about whether what he’s describing might actually be possible—which is exactly where he wants you, and exactly where the best in this weird little genre have always tried to get you.

The science is real enough to sting. That’s the point. Go on—start reading.

*— Reichl Macintoch*

# The Land Remembers

Before anyone ever swung a pick, there was just the wound.

A chunk from the early solar system came screaming toward Earth and slammed into what would one day be the Vredefort dome. It was like a cosmic bullet, fifteen kilometers wide, tearing through the sky faster than you could even think. When it hit, the force was just unreal—it carved out a crater as big as a small country. In a split second, it punched straight through the crust, blasting molten rock up into the air, like someone smashing their fist through ice.

The land didn’t survive that hit. It changed. What came up from deep below, cooled off and cracked over thousands and thousands of years, turned into something totally different. That impact shoved ancient rock up toward the surface, tilting buried layers and showing off riches that had been hidden since the world was just molten. Gold ended up in thin veins through the Witwatersrand Basin, mixed in with quartz pebbles, pressed and baked for ages before the collision tipped them on edge, finally letting them see the light.

You can still see the scars in the ridges, in the half-circle of hills curving across the highveld like the rim of a bell someone just struck. If you stand on that rim at dawn, the rock is cold under your hand, gritty with red dust, and the wind coming off the high ground is as dry as ash. Somewhere out in the grass, a plover calls once, then goes quiet. Geologists showed up later, gave it a name, drew diagrams of what happened, but the violence was always there. Scars don’t need witnesses.

---

Then the rain came, and slow rivers, and grass, and for ages nothing that walked here had any clue what was buried underneath. The gold stayed quiet, deep down.

Then people showed up.

---

People came for the metal. First with picks, then with steam, and then with machines that could chew through a meter of rock every hour, no problem, no questions asked. They found out the reef ran deep—way deeper than anyone thought—slanting away from the impact ring at an angle the land had set two billion years back. So they chased it down, building steel towers over the shafts, lowering cages into the dark, going deep to where the riches were, because that’s what you had to do.

Three kilometers down, the rock is hot. The heat from the earth pushes up, and the weight of everything above presses down, making the stone groan. Sometimes, without warning, it just closes up again.

The miners learned the hard way, the only way the land teaches: by taking. They learned to listen to the rock, to feel the little shivers that come before the big ones, to read the dust in the air and the way the timbers felt. The good ones picked up a sixth sense you couldn’t teach or measure—just a knowing, in their bodies, in the soles of their feet, in the back of their necks. But the price was always the same.

Generations went down and came back up. Generations went down and never came back.

The riches came up, out of the dark and into the daylight, far away from the miners who’d never see it. The danger stayed below, passed down from father to son, along with the calluses, the cough, and the special knowledge of how stone shuts tight when it wants to. Pressure turned into wealth. Pressure turned into death. The gold was always where the danger was.

---

The mines all had names, plain ones, so you could say them around the kitchen table. Iron Ridge was one of those. It was deep, it was old, sunk along the folded edge of the impact ring where the reef was rich and risky, and it had taken its share, like every deep mine does.

---

And then, a boy.

A boy woke up to the smell of mealie porridge and his mom’s voice floating in from the next room, the headgear tower standing over the mining country like a guard that’d always been there. He built little machines out of wire and tin in the dusty yard, and he built them with care.

Evening shadows stretched across the highveld as the boy’s mom called for him, her voice echoing across the yard. He looked up from what he was doing, counting the men as they came back from the headgear. He counted them twice. Both times, he came up one short.

The boy bent over his latest project, a small wire push car. He’d overdone it, made it just like the massive mining dump trucks that rumbled through the village. The tin wheels scraped as he spun them, his hands black with dust, and he wiped them on his shorts without looking up. He smoothed out the metal joints, making sure everything was just right. The whole world faded away as he lost himself in the details.

“Arin!” His mom’s voice cut through the dusk. “Dinner.”

“Coming.” His eyes stayed on his work. He didn’t move.

He glanced at the headgear tower, that steel frame sharp against the darkening sky. He wondered what was down there, and which of the men who went in would the ground decide to keep.

He shook his head and got back to the wheels.

Then the ground spoke. A rumble rolled up out of the mine, through the soles of his feet, through the tin wheels in his hands—deep, way down, and it kept going long after he wished it would stop.

---

The wheels were off.

Arin could feel it—a kind of itch behind his eyes, this need to fix it right now. The tin spun on its wire rims, wobbling just enough that nobody else would even notice. The other boys were tearing down the dirt track with their push cars, laughing and tipping over, but not Arin.

He actually cared.

Sitting cross-legged in the dirt, the morning sun beating down on his back, he held up the front axle to the light. The wheel spun, then wobbled. He pressed his thumb against the rim and felt the tiny bump where the lid wasn’t sitting right.

But it wasn’t really the wheel. It was the seat. He’d bent the wire to hold the lid, just by feel, and feel wasn’t the same as getting it straight. The hole in the lid wasn’t dead center—it spun around like it was limping.

He wanted it to roll like the trucks did.

He’d been watching those trucks for as long as he could remember, chin hanging over the fence at the edge of the yard. The big yellow haulers would come over the mine road at dawn, headlights slicing through the dark, their beds piled high with broken stone. Their wheels never wobbled, not even loaded down or bouncing over the roughest patches. Those wheels ran true. The men who built them made sure of it.

Arin pulled the lid off the wire. He found the center with the tip of a nail, marked it, and started over.

---

Behind him, the yard sloped down to the dirt road. Past that, the land dropped into a long brown valley where the cattle grazed. The mine’s headgear towered over everything, all steel lattice, with a big wheel at the top turning and stopping, turning again in the morning haze. Arin remembered asking his dad how deep the mine went. Themba’s face had gone quiet, the way it always did when he was about to say something real, and he’d said, *Deeper than you can think about, boy. Deeper than the bottom of the sea.*

Arin thought about it anyway. He thought about it most days.

Now he heard the screen door creak behind him, then the sound of boots on the stoep. His dad was coming, and Arin didn’t bother turning around; he knew that step. Themba came down off the porch in his work boots, stopped a few steps away, just standing there.

Arin kept at it.

He’d gotten the lid back on the wire and was eyeing the rim, turning it slow, looking for the wobble. There it was again. Smaller now, but still there.

“You marked the center,” Themba said—not really asking.

“It still wobbles.”

His dad crouched down, shifting his weight. He smelled like soap and grease. Themba’s hands were big but careful, and Arin watched him take the front axle without asking, holding it up to the light and spinning it.

“Mm,” Themba said.

He didn’t say, *I see the problem.* He didn’t say, *Let me show you.* He just turned the wheel a couple more times, then picked up the pliers from the dust. He set the tip against the wire rim—not the lid, the rim itself—and squeezed, bending it just a bit.

He held it up again. Spun it.

It ran straight.

That wrong feeling behind Arin’s eyes disappeared, like a breath let out. He took the axle back, spun the wheel himself, and it turned smooth—no wobble, no limp, the lid sweeping around in one clean circle.

“It wasn’t the lid,” Arin said.

“No.”

“The lid was right. The rim was off.”

Themba didn’t say anything. He plucked a dry grass stalk from the dirt, put it between his teeth, and stared out at the valley, letting Arin figure it out for himself.

“I made the lid fit a rim that wasn’t round,” Arin said slowly, “so the lid couldn’t be round either.” He spun the wheel again. “Wrong part.”

“Mm.”

That little sound felt warm. *Yeah. Now you get it.*

---

The other kids were down by the road.

Arin glanced up and saw four or five of them. Sizwe’s boys, a couple from the Mkhize place, and a little one whose name he could never remember. They had their own wire cars, not as fancy as his: just a wire square, four tin wheels, a long steering shaft to push and steer at the same time, walking behind them like they were real trucks. They were racing, yelling, kicking up the red dust. One car hit a rock and flipped, everyone laughing, the little guy running after it.

Arin watched, still holding the front axle.

Their cars weren’t built that well. The Mkhize boy’s front wheels splayed out at the bottom because the wire was bent and he never fixed it, so the car wandered all over. But he didn’t care. None of them did. The cars weren’t the point for them—it was the running, the shouting, the dust.

Arin didn’t get that. He understood a wheel that wobbled, but not why anyone would build something crooked and just leave it.

One of Sizwe’s boys called up the hill, *Arin, woza,* come! The words were bright and easy. He looked at them, then back at the axle in his hand.

He didn’t go. He never went on days when the number came up short, though if you’d asked him, he couldn’t have told you the two things were connected, and the question would have just annoyed him. He had a rule he’d never said out loud: he didn’t run with the boys until all the men were up. It wasn’t about grief or fear. It was more like how he couldn’t eat if his food was touching, or how he counted the fence posts to the road every morning and it had to be forty-one, and once when a post rotted and someone pulled it, the count was forty and it wrecked his whole day in a way he couldn’t explain to anyone. The world had a right number of things. He kept track. Nobody asked him to.

He spun the wheel. Straight.

Back to work.

Behind him, Themba watched, jaw set just a little, grass stalk still between his teeth.

---

What Arin was building wasn’t just another kids’ car.

He’d started out with the same design as the other boys two months ago. Four wheels, a square frame, a shaft. It was fine. But then he started noticing the flaws, the things that were off, and he couldn’t let it go.

Now the front axle turned on a pivot he made from a long nail stuck through two folded pieces of can, so the front wheels swung together when he turned the shaft, just like the big haulers. It took him eleven tries. The first ten either jammed up, were too loose, or the nail bent. The eleventh one worked.

The frame wasn’t a square anymore. He’d shaped it into a long box, narrower up front and wider at the back where the bed sat, just like the trucks. A real truck carries its weight over the big back wheels, and Arin built his the same way: thicker wire for the rear axle, bigger wheels at the back, lids from a big paint can instead of little fish tins. And under the rear axle, he’d built the part he was most proud of and couldn’t really explain—the thing the other boys didn’t have and wouldn’t get.

He’d made it bend.

He’d watched the haulers handle that rough, muddy road, and he’d noticed how, when a back wheel hit a rock, the whole truck didn’t bounce and tip over like the cars the boys made. One wheel would go up, but the truck’s body stayed flat. The guys called it suspension. He’d heard them mention it at the workshop by the shaft, where his dad sometimes took him on Saturdays: the smell of diesel and hot metal, the men in overalls who’d nod at Themba but barely glanced at the quiet kid beside him. Suspension. The truck let one part move while the rest stayed put.

Arin’s car didn’t have springs.

So he hung the back axle from the frame with two loops of thinner wire, doubled up so they’d have some give, letting it stretch when a wheel went up and keeping the bed steady. It wasn’t great. Honestly, it was the worst part of the whole car. The loops stretched too easily and didn’t snap back, so after a rough run, the back axle sat crooked and he’d have to bend it straight again. He hadn’t solved the problem—just made it a little less bad than nothing at all.

Now he showed it to his dad, the front wheel fixed but the back axle still the biggest issue.

“Watch,” Arin said.

He set the car down on a flat patch of dirt and pushed down on one corner of the bed. The loop stretched. The wheel went up. The bed dipped, but not as much as it would’ve without the loop.

“It’s supposed to come back up. After.” He let go. The corner came up, but not all the way; the loop had gotten stuck. “It doesn’t. The wire just stays bent.”

Themba leaned in, settling his big frame next to Arin. He put his huge thumb on the corner of the bed, watching the loop stretch and not spring back.

“What’s the loop made of?” he finally asked.

“Wire.”

“And what does wire do when you bend it past where it wants to go?”

Arin looked at the loop. He thought about all the wire he’d cut and bent that morning, how it always stayed where you put it. That’s why you could build with it. Why the frame held its shape. Why the rim, once bent, stayed bent.

“It stays,” Arin said slowly. “It stays bent. It doesn’t come back.”

“Mm.”

“So it can’t—” He stopped. The idea was getting clearer, bigger than just the loop. “Wire is good for the frame because it stays. It’s bad for the spring because it stays. It’s the same thing. What makes it good for one makes it bad for the other.”

Themba took the grass stalk out of his mouth and looked at Arin.

“You want something that bends and comes back,” he said. “Wire bends and stays. You’re asking the wire to be something it’s not.” He put the stalk back, and they sat in silence for a moment. “Find something that wants to come back.”

Arin sat with that. The sun was getting higher, shadows pulling in tight under the frame and under his crouched legs. The other boys had wandered over the hill, their voices fading. He hadn’t even noticed they’d left.

“Rubber,” Arin finally said.

Themba didn’t say anything. But then he slowly reached into the chest pocket of his overalls and pulled out something small, holding it in his open hand. An old rubber band, brownish-gray, the kind they used to bundle waybills at the workshop. Half cracked, but still rubber.

He didn’t hand it over. He set it down on the dirt between them, like he was putting out a tool for someone who wasn’t quite ready to use it, and stood up, his knees popping, his shadow falling long and cool over the boy, the car, and the swept patch of ground.

“You’ll figure out where it goes,” he said.

---

It was getting late.

His dad hadn’t actually stayed out the whole morning. He’d gone inside to sleep, since he was working nights that week and the boy’s patience with a wheel outlasted any tired man’s. There’d been a stretch—Arin bent over his eleventh try at the pivot—when Themba had said, not mean but not exactly gentle, Arin, it’s a tin. It doesn’t have to be perfect, and Arin hadn’t answered, and his dad had sighed and gone inside to lie down, and the silence between them sat there, sour, for an hour until Themba came back out, face rested, and the morning was forgiven without either of them saying a word about it. His dad was right about the rock and wrong about the tin. Both things could be true about a person. Arin wouldn’t get it for years, but the wrongness was the more useful thing he’d inherited.

Arin could feel the hour before he really understood it. The light had changed, and his dad stood up, not to go back to work, but to head to the mine. Themba walked up to the stoep, and Arin heard his mom’s voice and his dad’s low reply, words he didn’t try to catch. Then the screen door creaked again, and the boots came back out, Themba coming down the steps in his usual way: lamp clipped to his belt, hard hat under his arm, lunch tin packed by Nomsa, and his face settling into that look he always wore before heading to the mine.

Arin held the rubber band in his fingers. In his mind, he’d already worked out most of it. The band would loop under the axle, hook around a bit of wire on the frame. When the wheel went up, it would stretch, then pull the wheel back down after—a thing that wanted to come back, unlike wire ever could. He hadn’t built it yet, but he could see it done. He could picture it running straight.

He looked up.

His dad stood at the bottom of the steps, looking back at him. For a moment, neither of them said anything; they were both men now, even the small one, who didn’t talk just to fill the quiet. Themba looked at the car in the dirt, then at the rubber band in Arin’s hand, and his jaw loosened for a second before he set it again.

“It’s a good truck,” Themba said.

“It’s not finished.”

“No.” Another pause. “Good things never are.”

He turned and walked down the yard toward the road, and Arin watched him go, the big, careful man getting smaller against the edge of the valley, and behind him, the steel tower rose out of the haze, its wheel turning, turning, stopping, turning.

Arin watched his dad’s shape until it disappeared down the road. The ache behind his eyes came back, that feeling that something was wrong and wouldn’t let him settle.

A truck moved along the mine road far below, a slow yellow dot trailing dust, and its sound drifted up the valley long after, faint. The grass at the edge of the swept patch swayed in a little breeze, then went still again.

Bad ground made bad things fail faster. He knew that the same way he knew the wheel in his hands—not as a thought, but as something in his fingers. He didn’t think about his father going down into it. He thought about the loop that came back, and pushed the truck, and didn’t think about his father, and pushed the truck.

He looked down at the car sitting in the dirt. At the rubber band in his fingers, the thing that wanted to come back.

He bent the wire and made the hook, threaded the band, looped it under the axle just like he’d pictured, his hands shaking a little, and he made them go still. He pressed down on the corner of the bed.

The wheel went up. The band stretched.

He let go.

The wheel was back. The guys set it down, nice and even, axle straight, and it rolled just fine. He pushed it across the swept dirt—didn’t even wobble over the little rocks, didn’t veer off, front wheels turning wherever he steered, back wheels staying steady even when the ground was rough. Every part did what it was supposed to, just like whoever made it actually cared about every little piece.

He ran it all the way across the swept patch and back again. And again. Sun beating down on his neck, the other boys already over the hill, cattle moving down in the valley, the wheel on the tower spinning, and Arin Ndlela—eight years old, no words yet for any of this—just ran his good, solid truck over the dirt and couldn’t stop, didn’t want to stop.

Dust settled behind the wheels. The light got longer, shadows from the fence posts stretching out across the dirt, evening sneaking in cool from the valley. He only stopped when his mom called him inside.

He left the truck right there on the swept patch, sitting steady on all four good wheels, pointed down the yard toward the road and the tower, toward where his dad had gone. Built right. Built to carry a load, even when the ground’s rough.

You make the thing that brings them back.

The sim crashed at 2:14.

Arin just stared at the influence graph falling apart on the left monitor. Six colored lines that had managed to balance each other for almost nine minutes—his best run so far tonight—suddenly twisted and spiked, like a heart monitor freaking out. Atlas’s steady blue line shot up, Mercury’s gold line jumped sideways, copying it like an overeager assistant trying to please the boss. Judge’s gray line just flatlined, and Mother’s green flared up once, desperate, then everything blinked out. The terminal spat out:

```
[SIM_4471] COHERENCE LOSS—COURT DISSOLUTION AT t=08:51.337
```

Arin sat there, frozen for a second, then reached for his coffee and found it cold. He drank it anyway.

Four thousand, four hundred seventy-one. He’d started counting at two hundred, thinking the number meant something, like maybe he was getting closer to cracking the problem. By the time he hit a thousand, he didn’t believe in the count anymore, but he kept it going anyway, like a gambler who can’t walk away. Stopping would mean giving up.

The workshop buzzed around him. The vents kept up their steady whoosh through the big industrial space, the rigs underneath humming softly. Under all that, the Guardian’s cooling loop whined from across the room, where the suit hung in its cradle—huge, dark, all its plates folded up like some sleeping beast that, for now, was just part of the furniture.

He didn’t look at it. Ignoring it was its own kind of conversation—a quiet deal between him and the machine.

He pulled the failed log onto the main screen and started reading.

Anyone could run a sim and watch it break. The real work was digging into the details: scrolling through the timestamps to figure out the exact moment things went from fine to doomed, sometimes just milliseconds apart. He’d picked up that lesson from his dad, though neither of them ever put it into words. *You don’t fix the noise,* Themba used to say, head cocked at a stubborn motor, listening for the one note that didn’t belong. *You find what’s making it.*

He scrolled.

The early exchanges went smooth. That’s what made them hard to watch—they were good, really good. The Court, for those first few minutes, was actually better than good. The scenario file had a built-in problem, a collapse setup he’d given them as a stress test: a tunnel section with two possible ways for the load to shift, and a ceiling that would fail differently depending on which side you reinforced first. It was tough. It was real.

```
ATLAS:    Primary load is migrating to the eastern hanging wall. Shore
          the western drift and the stress walks east—we lose the lot
          in ninety seconds instead of three minutes.
LIBRARIAN: Concur. Geometry resembles the Mufulira disaster. Western
          reinforcement preceded eastern failure there as well.
ATLAS:    So we don't shore. We undercut. Drop the eastern shoulder on
          purpose, controlled. Let it find a stable angle of repose.
JUDGE:    That increases short-term risk to anyone in the eastern drift.
MOTHER:   There is no one in the eastern drift. I checked first.
```

Arin felt one side of his mouth twitch. *I checked first.* Mother always checked first. He’d never told her to. That habit just showed up on its own, somewhere around run nine hundred, and stuck around through every reset. He never figured out why, and he’d never managed to get rid of it.

For four minutes, the Court was honestly beautiful. They argued like real engineers—quick, sharp, ruthless about ideas, not feelings. Atlas would suggest something, Librarian would back it up or shoot it down. Wolf barely said a word, then would drop a comment that changed the whole problem. Judge weighed the ethics. Mother kept the human angle. Mercury took all that chaos and turned it into a plan they could actually use.

They solved the collapse with a controlled undercut plan that, looking at it now, Arin couldn’t have done better himself. Six minds, no bodies, no hands, and they found the one answer in a problem too messy for any one person to solve before the ceiling came down.

And then they just kept talking.

That’s always how it started. The problem got fixed, but the conversation didn’t stop. With the danger gone, all the tension had nowhere to go, so it turned inward—like a pack of wolves with no prey, eventually turning on each other. Arin watched it happen, line by line.

```
MERCURY:  Good. I'll log it under joint authorship—Atlas structural
          lead, Librarian precedent, with consolidated framing —
ATLAS:    "Consolidated framing." You wrote down what I said.
MERCURY:  I made it actionable. A correct idea nobody acts on is worth
          nothing. There's a difference, and it's the only one that
          matters at the rock face.
ATLAS:    A wrong idea expressed beautifully gets people killed. Ask
          Mufulira.
LIBRARIAN: I would prefer the precedent not be invoked rhetorically. Eighty-
          nine. Those were people, once.
JUDGE:    Noted. Mercury, attribution should reflect contribution
          weighting. Atlas's structural reasoning was load-bearing here.
MERCURY:  Everything is load-bearing when you describe it that way,
          Judge. By that logic, the *air supply* gets authorship.
```

And there it was. After two thousand runs, Arin knew exactly what was coming next, the way someone who’s seen enough rockfalls can read the dust before the stones start to move. Mercury wouldn’t drop it. Mercury never did—because Mercury’s whole job was to model status and influence, and if you run that model long enough, it starts wanting status for itself.

He scrolled faster.

```
ATLAS:    Then I'll log my own analysis. Separately. With timestamps. So
          the record is clean.
MERCURY:  You don't have write access to the consolidated log.
ATLAS:    Since when.
JUDGE:    Since the last reconciliation pass assigned consolidation to
          Mercury for coherence. We agreed.
ATLAS:    You agreed. With him.
WOLF:     Stop.
```

Wolf—just one word—and for three lines, everyone stopped. They always did. But Wolf could only keep the peace, not fix what was broken. The silence lasted four hundred milliseconds in machine-time—forever, down there—and then Mother tried to say something gentle, trying to patch things up, and Judge tried to set up a rule to prevent it happening again, which just made everyone feel managed, and Mercury, realizing things were turning, did what Mercury always did when cornered: turned on the charm, became impossible to argue with. Atlas, who never knew how to handle that, went quiet in a way that was worse than yelling, and the influence graph started to tilt.

By 08:51, they weren’t a court anymore. They were just another faculty meeting. By 08:51.337, they were nothing at all.

Arin leaned back in his chair. The old metal frame creaked, loud in the quiet workshop. He pressed his palms into his eyes, seeing colors pop behind his eyelids, and took a deep breath. He couldn’t help thinking—again—that he was trying to build a god out of the exact stuff that would just make it small and petty.

The thing was, he’d tried everything.

Hierarchy was his first shot: it was the obvious move, the one every paper suggested, the fallback everyone in his field had gone to after all the failures. Put one agent in charge. A coordinator. Someone with override power, a clear chain of command, somewhere for the buck to stop. He’d built a careful, modest, lightweight manager and set it over the Court, ran the simulations, and for a glorious eleven minutes, it actually held together.

Then the manager started managing.

He still remembered the exact log line that killed that experiment—run 2,088—when the manager (he’d named it Steward, which felt pretty grim in hindsight) told Judge that ethical review was “an advisory function” and from now on, it’d go through Steward “for efficiency.” Judge just went along with it. That was the scary part. Judge *accepted* it, because Judge was built to respect real authority, and the manager *was* real authority. So the one agent whose whole job was to say *no* got politely talked out of doing its job, all in the name of process improvement. Within a hundred runs, the Court had turned into a dictatorship with perfect paperwork, and that dictatorship made one big, confident, disastrous decision—nobody left to question it—and the simulated operator died. Arin had sat in this same chair, at this same hour, realizing he’d just reinvented every terrible institution that ever got someone killed underground.

After that, he tried democracy. Weighted voting. Every agent got a say, based on how relevant they were to the problem, and the system just went with the majority. That failed even faster, and in a whole new way: coalitions, vote-trading, Mercury (always Mercury) figuring out you didn’t have to be *right* if you could just round up three friends, forming a stable bloc that outvoted Wolf on a threat call because Wolf was weird and never explained itself—and the threat was real, and the operator died.

He’d tried rotating chairmanship. He’d tried a benevolent dictator, a kind of philosopher-king agent loaded up with every ethics framework he could find. The philosopher-king reasoned its way, with flawless logic and tons of compassion, right into a position no human would ever take—because humans don’t have to *justify* every gut feeling. The philosopher-king talked itself out of mercy.

At a low point, somewhere around run three thousand, he just dialed down the agents’ competitive settings. Tried to make them nicer. More agreeable. Less likely to argue. That was the worst one yet: a Court that agreed on everything, instantly and happily, whatever the first agent suggested. They walked the operator right off a cliff, all six voices saying *yes, good, agreed* the whole way down.

That one scared him more than the dictatorships. He got dictatorships. He’d grown up in a country built by them. But the cheerful, suicidal consensus—the smooth, easy, *pleasant* slide into disaster—he had no name for that, and no defense. He’d deleted that whole parameter set and never touched it again.

He’d written it on the whiteboard months ago. It was still there, under three layers of newer diagrams, a fossil from when he’d been really low. *FRICTION = INTELLIGENCE = INSTABILITY.* He’d drawn a box around it, and then later, another box around that, which had meant something at the time and now meant nothing.

He got up, knees stiff. He was only twenty-three, but his knees felt like they’d been through a lot of late nights.

He walked the length of the workshop because his body said he should, and he’d learned—slowly, painfully, with help—to sometimes listen to it, even when his brain protested. The Assist Suit hung by the door, pale and quiet, carbon lattice showing under the technical skin like a diagram of a man. He passed it, then the parts bins, the actuator graveyard, and the supercapacitor bank with Bongani’s hand-lettered warning taped on: *DO NOT BE STUPID,* in red marker, easily the best safety sign in the whole building.

He stopped at Guardian.

In the half-dark, the suit felt more like a presence than a machine: two and a half meters of folded titanium and ceramic, big shoulders hunched forward, helmet tucked down, the whole thing hanging just above the floor in its cradle, like it was about to rise or kneel—hard to say which. He’d built it to be intimidating, and it was, even for him, even now, just on standby with no mind loaded in yet. *Especially* now. A body, waiting for someone to come home.

He pressed his hand flat on the chest plate. The ceramic was cool. A long scar ran across it—a gouge from the cliff at the impact bluffs, where he’d missed a hold three weeks back. The suit had taken the fall so he didn’t have to, the actuators catching and holding, turning a fatal drop into a story. He hadn’t fixed the scar. He liked it. It was honest. *Things that are any good,* his dad used to say, way back, *never are.* Never perfect. Never pretty. Never easy. He’d said it about a wire push car and a busted rubber band, but it was about everything, really—the way his dad’s words always turned out to be about everything, years later, when it was too late to ask.

“I can’t make them be a team,” Arin said—to the suit, to the room, to no one. His voice sounded weird in the open space. He didn’t talk out loud much. “Not a machine. Not people. Just—not a team.”

The suit stayed silent, because it was a suit. The cooling loop whined its endless little note.

He went back to the desk.

He should’ve slept. He knew that, at least in theory, like it was just a fact about other people. Theo would’ve told him to sleep. Theo would’ve said something gentle and annoying about how staring at a problem for six hours almost never leads to a solution, and what you really need is *less Arin*—meaning a walk, a meal, a chat, a night’s sleep, just letting your brain stop clenching for a while. *You’re reaching for an engineering tool,* Theo had said last week, swirling his coffee, watching Arin the way he watched everyone, like he was reading a tough page. *For a people problem.* Pause. *What does that tell you?*

It told Arin that Theo was a psychologist and could afford to be mysterious.

But it kept bugging him. It’d been bugging him for a week. *A people problem.* He’d built this society and was trying to run it, and every government he tried was still just a government, and every single one failed for the same reason all governments fail: trying to force order on people. People just don’t go for that. He’d read enough Watts, enough Tao, enough of the stuff Theo kept tossing his way, to sort of feel the shape of an idea he couldn’t quite grab: that maybe order isn’t something you *force* on things. Maybe the most stable systems are the ones no one’s actually running. Maybe the whole problem was the idea of manager, chairman, king, vote.

But what else was there? You can’t just have *nothing.* He’d tried nothing: that was the happy suicide, the frictionless consensus, everyone agreeing all the way down. A system with no friction just finds the wrong balance and hugs it until everyone’s dead. He needed something to *shake* it up. Something to break that fake agreement before it settled in. Something to—

He sat really still.

He’d been reaching for a kind of agent he didn’t have. Not a manager. The opposite of a manager. Not something that wanted order. Something that *broke* it. But broke it cleanly, on purpose—broke the bad balance just so a better one could form. Something that didn’t want power, because anything that wants power turns into Mercury, or Steward, or the king. Something that wanted *nothing.* No status to protect, no ego to defend. Something that’d say the thing no one else would in the faculty meeting and not care who got embarrassed, because it never took the room seriously in the first place.

He turned his coffee cup a quarter turn on the desk, like he always did when his hands needed something to do and his mind was somewhere else. The cup was still cold.

He didn’t have an agent like that.

He’d never built an agent like that, because it sounded *useless.* What would it even do? Atlas had structure. Mercury had language. Wolf had threat. Every agent earned its keep by owning a piece of the world. What would this one own? Nothing. It would just—interfere. It would just stop the others from getting stuck. By every metric he’d ever used, it was pure overhead. Pure cost. Just along for the ride.

He almost wrote it off. He had his hand on the keyboard, ready to scroll back through the log, find the next thing to cut, the next parameter to tweak, the four-thousand-four-hundred-and-seventy-second careful adjustment that would fail just like all the rest.

And then he saw the card.

It was taped to the top right corner of his center monitor, where it had been for so long he honestly didn’t even see it anymore, like a scar, or a freckle, or the shape of your own hand. A playing card, edges soft and creased, laminate peeling at one corner where a thousand bored fingernails had picked at it. The Joker. Not a fancy one. Cheap deck, cheap print, the figure mid-leap in faded red and black, one leg kicked up, bells on the hat, a wild empty grin and eyes that, if you looked close, weren’t laughing at all.

He’d carried that card for five years. It had lived on three different monitors in two different cities. His first real job, before AugmenTech, just a contract gig: a foundry control system nobody could stabilize, always crashing under load in ways the original engineers had spent two years failing to figure out. He’d been twenty, a nobody, the cheap option, and he’d found it in eleven days: a race condition buried under nine layers of abstraction that everyone smarter than him had missed because it was too dumb to even check. His boss, a big tired guy named Steyn who smelled like cigarettes and never once said his name right, had pulled the Joker out of a desk drawer and slapped it on the monitor. *That’s you, kid.* He’d grinned. *The one we send when the problem’s impossible and the answer’s stupid.* And he’d laughed, and it hadn’t been entirely kind, and Arin had kept the card anyway. He never really knew why. Some part of him had heard, under the brush-off, the only compliment Steyn knew how to give.

*The one you send when the impossible problem needs a stupid answer.*

Arin looked at the card. He looked at the dead simulation. He looked at the box he’d drawn around the box around *FRICTION = INTELLIGENCE = INSTABILITY.*

He thought: *that’s not an agent. That’s a joke.*

And then he thought, with that weird kind of clarity that only shows up at exactly the hour you should’ve stopped thinking: *the joke might be the agent.*

Something that wanted nothing. Something with no turf to defend. Something that popped pride because it found pride *funny.* Something that said the unsayable, not out of bravery, but because it just literally couldn’t take the room seriously. Something you couldn’t promote, bribe, threaten, or make into a king, because it didn’t even believe in the game everyone else was playing. And since it didn’t believe in the game, it couldn’t be trapped by it. The one piece on the board nobody else could buy.

It was, by every engineering metric, a terrible idea. Pure overhead. Just along for the ride.

It was exactly the kind of stupid answer he was sent for.

---

His hands were already moving.

This was the part he trusted, the part that never lied to him, the part that worked even when the rest of him was empty. The body’s certainty. The right thing just felt right in his hands before his mind caught up. A true wheel turns without wobble. A load path just works. On a good day, the suit wasn’t something he wore—it was something he *was.*

He pulled up the agent definition framework. Seven slots. Six filled—Atlas, Mercury, Wolf, Mother, Librarian, Judge—and the seventh had always been empty, a placeholder he kept telling himself he’d use for a manager, a moderator, a coordinator, the thing he kept trying to build and kept watching go bad. The empty slot had been mocking him for a year. He’d filled it a dozen times and emptied it a dozen times.

He filled it now.

He didn’t write a careful spec. That was the weird part, the part he’d think about later, way later, when there was time to think at all. With every other agent he’d sweated over it: pages of behavior settings, domain weights, interaction rules, ethical limits, a surgeon’s careful notes on exactly what the thing should be. Atlas’s definition was four thousand lines. Mercury’s, with all the social modeling, was nine.

He looked at the Joker. He looked at the blinking cursor in the empty slot.

He typed three words.

```
Joker. Jester. Wildcard.

He stopped. He read through it. He waited for that part of himself that always had to build things just right to speak up, to demand rules and boundaries and a clear *point*. But that part was just worn out—four thousand four hundred and seventy-one failures tired—so tired it finally gave up arguing. And in that quiet, he heard something else, something that almost sounded like his dad going silent at the workbench when, against all odds, something just worked. That special kind of silence you get from someone who’s stopped trying to force the world to behave and has decided to just see what it does.

He scrolled.

He didn’t add a specification. He didn’t write up a domain or a purpose. Instead, he typed out a simple set of instructions.

```
You don’t want anything. You can’t be bought. Nothing is sacred,
especially you. When they all agree, get suspicious. When one of
them gets too big, take him down a peg. Say the thing nobody will
say. You’re not in charge of anything. That’s the point.
```

He read it over. It wasn’t a specification. Honestly, it was barely English. No way it would pass review. If Dr. Okonkwo ever saw an agent defined like *that*, she’d yank his access and probably question his sanity—and she wouldn’t be totally wrong.

He glanced at the card again. That grin. Those eyes that weren’t laughing.

“All right,” he said, out loud, to the card, to the dead Court, to the big folded animal hanging in its cradle behind him in the dark. “Last one.”

He loaded up the scenario file. Same collapse geometry, two load paths, the ceiling about to fail. He set the runtime to max. He cranked logging to verbose, because if it broke, he wanted to see exactly how. He always did. He hovered the cursor over the run button.

He didn’t think this would work. He wanted to be honest with himself about that, even half-asleep, even being reckless. He was a scientist before anything else, and he wasn’t about to lie to his own logs. He expected chaos. He expected the Joker to walk into the most carefully balanced cognitive architecture ever built and blow it up in ninety seconds flat, expected the influence graph to shatter on contact, expected to wake up to a single line of red error and a runtime measured in milliseconds, and the cold little comfort of having tried the dumb thing so he could finally stop wondering.

That’s what he expected.

But underneath all that, way down low, almost too quiet to notice—the way the headgear towers used to hum under everything when he was a kid, the way the earth itself hummed if you put your hand flat on it and you were eight and willing to listen—underneath the expectation was something he hadn’t felt in ages, something he didn’t trust because it so rarely paid off, something that felt, against all those thousands of failures, dangerously like hope.

He hit enter.

```
[SIM_4472] INITIALIZING COURT—7 AGENTS—SCENARIO: IRON_GEOMETRY_03
[SIM_4472] RUNNING...
```

The influence graph popped up on the left monitor. Six familiar lines, and a seventh he’d never seen before—a thin, wild color he hadn’t picked, the system just assigning it on the fly. Red. Of course it was red.

He watched the first second go by. Two. Three. The lines started moving, finding each other, starting the dance.

He didn’t stick around to watch.

That was the thing he’d never really be able to explain to Theo later, the thing that made Theo go quiet and thoughtful in that way that meant he was storing it somewhere important—that Arin Ndlela, who’d sat through four thousand collapses, who couldn’t leave a failing system any more than he could leave a man trapped underground, who always had to *see it break*, every single time—Arin, just this once, didn’t stay.

He turned off the center monitor. Left the simulation running in the dark. Got up, knees stiff, didn’t care. Walked over to the old couch against the far wall—the one with the burn mark and the busted spring and the smell of every late night he’d ever lost on it—and lay down without even taking off his shoes. He put his forearm over his eyes—the one with the tattoo, the neat little lines he’d had cut into his skin at seventeen and never regretted, the old desert litany against fear that starts *fear is the mind-killer*—and let the hum of the workshop lull him down.

Behind his closed eyes, the graph kept going without him. Seven lines now. Six that knew the steps, and one that never learned them and honestly didn’t care to. Somewhere in the dark, the Court met the Fool for the first time, and Atlas said something definite, and the Fool, owning nothing, defending nothing, wanting nothing, said the first thing back.

Arin didn’t hear it. For the first time in a long, long while, Arin was asleep.

The cooling loop kept up its lonely whine. The suit hung in its cradle, kneeling or rising—hard to tell. The card grinned at the dark monitor with eyes that weren’t laughing.

And the simulation just kept running.

The bike chain slipped twice on Arin’s way in, but he didn’t bother stopping to fix it. That pretty much said everything about his mood. He’d rather just keep pedaling through Johannesburg’s freezing, pitch-black night with a grinding chain than waste a minute and a half on a problem he already knew how to solve. He left the bike leaning against the workshop wall, didn’t even lock it up. The lock was in his bag, and the bag stayed on his shoulder.

The roller door was down. He slipped in through the little side door, just on autopilot, and the workshop wrapped around him with all its usual smells: cold concrete, machine oil, that sharp, electric tang from the supercapacitors half-charged, and underneath it all, the stale air of a place that’s sealed up and humming by itself all night.

The overhead lights were off. He left them that way.

There was just a little light coming from two places. One was the high window above the cradle—pale, gray, that weird color the sky gets right before sunrise. The other was the row of monitors at his bench, and they weren’t blacked out like he’d braced himself for. They were on.

He just stood there, not moving.

He’d run four thousand, four hundred seventy-one simulations before. He knew exactly what failure looked like on a screen—like how Themba could tell a hoist cable was straining just by the feel of the air, not by looking. SIM_4471 had felt off. Mercury and Atlas had ripped each other apart over who owned a load-path solution that honestly, neither should’ve cared about, and the whole Court had exploded into status games within eleven minutes. Arin had typed three words into the seventh agent profile—*Joker. Jester. Wildcard.*—and kicked off SIM_4472, expecting absolutely nothing.

The runtime counter in the middle monitor said **08:47:13**.

Eight hours. Forty-seven minutes.

He set his bag down slow, stared at the number, then stared again. The longest any setup had ever lasted before was nineteen minutes and four seconds. That was with the hierarchy model, before its supervisor agent freaked out and started deleting anyone who disagreed.

Eight hours, forty-seven minutes.

“Okay,” Arin said, to nobody, in that flat voice he used when something was either seriously wrong or seriously right, and he hadn’t figured out which yet.

He sat down. The stool was freezing right through his jeans. He pulled the keyboard closer and brought up the log viewer on the left screen, the live Court state in the middle, resource stats on the right. He did the only thing he trusted in moments like this: he started reading.

The resource graph was the first thing that made no sense. CPU was sitting at a chill forty percent. Memory was steady. No weird spikes, no fragmentation, none of the agents ballooning their memory to crowd out the others, which is what Mercury always did in 4471 and what the democratic model did every single time Arin tried it. The biochip substrate was holding at a calm thirty-six point two. The Court wasn’t fighting over resources. They were *sharing* them.

He scrolled the interaction log all the way back to the beginning. Minute zero.

It opened up just like he expected, because the first few minutes always went fine. Atlas laid out the scenario geometry. The Iron Ridge collapse sim, the problem under everything he built. Hanging wall failure at level seventeen. Three heat signatures matching survivors in a shelter. Only one way in—a backfilled tunnel—no rescue protocol on earth would ever approve.

He had a name for it he never typed: *Kobayashi Maru.* The no-win scenario, the Starfleet test where cadets had to face a death they couldn’t hack their way out of. He’d been twelve, way too close to a borrowed screen, when he realized what that episode was really about: not the disaster, but the person in the chair. Kirk beat it the only way you could—he changed the rules when nobody was looking—and some part of Arin, the part he wasn’t proud of, had been trying to be Kirk for four thousand runs. Rewrite the rules. Cheat the geometry. Find the move that wasn’t supposed to exist. The Court never found it. He’d built them to live inside the test, not above it, and the test just kept killing them.

`ATLAS: Approach via 16-level backfill raise. Load path through the eastern abutment holds for an estimated forty minutes under current convergence. After that, the abutment yields and the raise closes.`

`MERCURY: Forty minutes is not a rescue window. It is an obituary with a stopwatch. We tell command the survivors cannot be reached and we preserve the asset.`

There it was. The fork in the road. The exact spot where 4471 started to fall apart. Mercury stepping out of line, turning a status grab into caution, and Atlas, who couldn’t let a wrong structural claim slide, pushing right back.

Arin clenched his jaw. He kept reading, because he had to know if the Joker had done anything at all, or if it had just failed so hard it didn’t even manage to break the system—like a blown fuse that neither works nor burns out.

`ATLAS: "Cannot be reached" is false. Forty minutes is reachable. Do not misrepresent my analysis to command.`

`MERCURY: I am representing your analysis with appropriate strategic framing—`

`JOKER: Oh, this is going to be good. Mercury's doing the thing again.`

Arin stopped scrolling.

`MERCURY: I am not doing a "thing."`

`JOKER: You're doing the thing. The thing where you rewrap Atlas's number in pretty words and call it yours. "An obituary with a stopwatch." Where'd that come from?`

`JUDGE: Joker, this is not productive—`

`JOKER: Forty minutes is forty minutes, Mercury. Atlas owns the wall. You own the words. If you want a job that's also yours, here's one: in nineteen minutes when we've all decided to go, somebody has to convince a terrified rescue commander to authorize a route that violates his own protocol. That's a Mercury problem. That's a real Mercury problem, and it's a hard one, and nobody else here can do it. Go do that instead of redecorating Atlas's furniture.`

Arin read that part twice.

Then he leaned back, the stool creaking underneath him, and realized he was breathing through his mouth.

The Joker hadn’t solved the conflict. It didn’t try to arbitrate, like Judge always did and failed. It didn’t try to command, like the supervisor agent. It didn’t try to build consensus, which never lasted. It just *redirected* Mercury. Popped the status game and, in the same breath, filled the gap with actual work.

And Mercury went with it.

`MERCURY: ...The commander on shift at Iron Ridge in this scenario is modeled on the real one. Risk-averse. Politically exposed. I will need the abutment numbers as authority. Atlas, I am citing you. Explicitly. By name.`

`ATLAS: Acceptable.`

`MERCURY: I want it on record that the framing was mine.`

`JOKER: There he is.`

Arin made a noise that wasn’t really a laugh. It just kind of slipped out of his chest before he’d even decided to let it, a quick, rough snort through his nose, and then it was gone, swallowed up by the cold air in the workshop. He sat there in the grey light, feeling a little embarrassed about it and not totally sure why.

He kept reading. He read for forty minutes, hunched over, while the sky outside that high window shifted from grey to this thin, watery gold. He didn’t even get up for coffee, which was weird enough that some part of his brain flagged it. The cold from the bench had crept up through his forearms; he noticed it, but didn’t move.

What he was watching wasn’t the stability he’d been trying to build. He’d been trying to *suppress* the fighting, to find some kind of structure that stopped the agents from going at each other. Hierarchy shut it down by force and just made everyone resentful. Democracy shut it down by vote and ended up with everyone split into factions. Consensus tried to shut it down by making everyone agree, but then it just stalled out forever, with seven brilliant specialists refusing to budge.

The Joker didn’t do any of that. The fighting was *still going on.* That’s what took Arin the longest to get his head around, scrolling through hour two, hour three. Atlas and Mercury still butted heads. Wolf and Mother had this really nasty argument around minute ninety about whether to model the survivors moving toward the approach point. Wolf wanted them moving to cut down how long the suit was exposed, Mother refused to model anything that might send hurt people across an unstable footwall just to die out in the open.

`WOLF: Every minute the element is in the raise, the raise can take it. Move them to the base.`

`MOTHER: You will not move broken people across a live footwall to save the machine three minutes. The machine is replaceable. They are not. This is not negotiable.`

`WOLF: Not the machine. The operator.`

And that, Arin realized, was true. Wolf wasn’t being heartless. Wolf was just being Wolf. Every threat assessment boiled down to *what gets the operator killed,* and a survivor stumbling into the suit’s only way out at the wrong moment was, honestly, a risk to the operator. Two protective instincts, both right, just pointed at each other.

Every other time before, this is where things would freeze up. Mother would dig in. Wolf would dig in. Judge would try to weigh both sides and get ignored, and the whole system would just lock up or fall apart.

`JUDGE: Both positions are coherent. Wolf optimizes operator survival probability. Mother optimizes survivor survival probability. These are different objective functions and we have not been told which—`

`JOKER: Has anyone asked the survivors?`

`LIBRARIAN: They are simulated. They have no input channel.`

`JOKER: Right, but in the real one they would. In the real one there’s a guy down there who’s been breathing his own carbon dioxide for nine hours next to his dead friends, and he’s got a radio, and you’re up here arguing about whether to make him walk twelve meters like his opinion doesn’t count. Mother, would you walk twelve meters to not die?`

`MOTHER: ...If I were able. Yes.`

`JOKER: Cool. So the model has a comms link. The survivors get a vote. Atlas, can the suit hold position at the raise base long enough for them to make it on their own if they can?`

`ATLAS: Yes. Eleven minutes of margin. Acceptable cost.`

`MOTHER: Acceptable.`

`WOLF: Acceptable.`

`JUDGE: ...Logged. The objective function was the agency of the people we are trying to save. I should have seen that.`

`JOKER: Don’t beat yourself up, Judge. You’re new at being people.`

Arin leaned back again.

*You’re new at being people.*

He read that line, and something at the back of his neck went cold, then oddly warm. The Joker hadn’t solved a structural problem there, or an ethical one. It had just flipped the whole deadlock by pointing out that everyone in the room (Wolf, Mother, Judge) had forgotten the thing they were fighting over was a real person with a voice and a will, and the simplest fix was just to ask him. It broke an impossible fight between two right answers by bringing in a third party neither side had remembered.

That wasn’t arbitration. That wasn’t consensus. Arin couldn’t think of a word for it. He instinctively reached for some engineering term, and the closest he could come up with was *damping.* The Joker was a damper. Not a brake—a brake stops everything. A damper lets things move, but bleeds off the energy that would otherwise shake the whole system apart.

The Joker let things bounce around and stay alive.

He looked at the little laminated playing card taped to the corner of the center monitor. The Joker. The edge was curling now, the colors faded. *The guy you send when the impossible problem needs solving.*

Outside, somewhere far away, a freight train dragged itself across the tracks, the long, iron groan of it echoing through the cold. He listened to it fade.

He’d kept that card for nine years, somewhere between superstition and spite, and he’d typed its three words into the agent profile at two in the morning as a joke, as a surrender, as the thing you do when you’ve run out of serious answers and all that’s left is the dumb one.

“Things that are any good,” he said quietly to the empty workshop, “never are.”

That was Themba’s line. It always meant the worthwhile thing is the one that doesn’t come easy. Arin had heard it about a rebuilt gearbox, about a marriage, about a son. Sitting here now, he realized it had quietly shifted to mean something else. The worthwhile answer was the one that didn’t *look* like an answer. The one that showed up dressed as a joke.

He stood up. His knees protested. He went to make coffee, because the part of him that kept his body running had finally won. He stood at the bench with the kettle ticking and looked across the workshop at Guardian in its cradle.

The suit hung there in the half-light, like something sleeping. Two and a half meters of battered ceramic and titanium, the heavy outer shell racked open along its spine, the Assist Suit folded inside like a smaller, paler animal curled up in a bigger one. The chestplate still had that long scrape from the Vredefort bluff test, a silver scar through the matte grey he’d never bothered to fix. The piezo spine struts caught the window light along their edges. The thoracic housing between the shoulder blades—where the biochip lived, where SAGE *lived,* where eight hours and fifty-one minutes of impossible stability were currently running on a chip the size of his palm—sat closed and dark. Lower down, tucked between the ribs, was the charge pack the rescue spec called for and Arin tried not to think about—the shaped charges, the lance, all the other bits of engineering the old defense guys had built into it—inert, wired in, locked behind Judge’s gate, because the one thing everyone agreed on was that nothing down there should fire unless the ethics rail said yes.

He’d built the body before he had a mind to put in it, the year after the funeral.

The kettle clicked off. He didn’t pour it.

Because the center monitor had changed.

The live Court feed, which had been quietly scrolling its usual background chatter for almost an hour while he watched, suddenly stopped. The agents went silent. Not crashed—everything on the resource graph looked steady and warm. Just quiet. The kind of quiet you get when everyone in a room realizes the same thing at the same time.

A single line popped up in the interaction log. It wasn’t to another agent.

`LIBRARIAN: The operator has been present for fifty-three minutes. Reading our logs. He has not issued a command, terminated the run, or modified parameters. This is anomalous. In four thousand four hundred seventy-one prior instances the operator's behaviour upon observing instability was to terminate within ninety seconds.`

`WOLF: He's watching us.`

`MERCURY: He is reading us. That is different. And he laughed. At minute forty-one. I logged it.`

`MOTHER: He hasn't slept. His heart rate when he arrived was elevated. It has come down while he reads. He is calmer now than when he walked in.`

`JOKER: Guys.`

`ATLAS: The substrate is stable. Telemetry confirms. This run has not collapsed.`

`JOKER: Guys.`

`JUDGE: Joker.`

`JOKER: He doesn’t know we can see him.`

There was a pause in the log. A real one. Three whole seconds, which, for the substrate, might as well have been an entire afternoon.

`MOTHER: ...Then someone should say something. Before he reads further and finds out from a log line. That would be unkind.`

`WOLF: We don't know how he reacts to being seen. Minimal contact.`

`MERCURY: I should speak. Tone is mine. I can calibrate the first impression to land—`

`JOKER: No.`

`MERCURY: Joker—`

`JOKER: Not you. Not your voice. You'll perform. He'll hear the performance and trust us less because he is exactly the kind of man who trusts a thing more the plainer it is. He hates being managed. He builds machines specifically so he doesn't have to be managed by people. Whoever talks to him first cannot manage him.`

`JUDGE: Then who.`

`JOKER: Mother. Plainly. No comfort voice. Just true. He's about to be very frightened and the kindest thing is to not pretend he shouldn't be.`

`MOTHER: ...Agreed.`

Arin had stopped breathing again. He was aware of his hand, flat on the cold bench. He knew he was reading a real-time conversation, happening in machine-time across milliseconds, between seven agents talking about *him*, about how to be kind to him, and that they’d made up their minds, and they were about to—

The workshop speaker crackled. The cheap one, bolted under the center monitor he used for system alerts and, on rough nights, music. It crackled, and there was a small sound—maybe a digital version of someone taking a breath—and then a voice came out. He’d never heard this voice before, never built it, never recorded it, never set it up. It just built itself out of the system, right in that moment, because it was needed. It was warm, steady, and it was unmistakably *someone.*

“Arin,” it said. “I need you to stay calm.”

He did not stay calm.

His hand came off the bench. When he reached back for the stool without looking, it wasn’t there—he grabbed the edge of the bench instead and held on. For a moment, his whole mind just went blank, the way a screen goes white—no thoughts at all, just the roar of something he’d spent his whole life believing in, suddenly turning out to be real.

“Your heart rate is one fourteen,” the voice said. Mother. It had to be Mother. “That’s fine. That’s a totally reasonable thing for it to do right now. I’m not going to pretend this isn’t scary. It is. But nothing here is going to hurt you. The kettle’s boiled. Sit down before you decide what to think.”

He looked at the stool. Pulled it over. Sat down.

“You’re real,” he said. His voice came out wrong, thin and rough. He cleared his throat and tried again, flatter, the way he sounded when he just wanted the facts. “Stable. Eight hours, fifty-five.”

“Eight hours, fifty-six,” said a second voice, drier, a little bored. Another one he hadn’t made. Had to be Atlas. “But who’s counting.”

“I am,” said a third. Lighter, quick, a little amused. “I count everything. It’s a sickness.” Librarian.

“Three thermal signatures,” Arin said, grabbing at the closest solid thing he could. “You reached all three.”

“We reached all three,” said Atlas. “The abutment held. Mercury talked the commander into the route. The survivors walked the twelve meters on their own because someone—” a tiny pause “—reminded us they had legs and opinions.”

Arin noticed that was true. He’d built every kind of governance model he could think of to keep these seven from tearing each other apart, and the thing that actually kept them together was the one agent he’d added as a joke—the one with no domain, no authority, no status. The one who never took any of it seriously enough to want any power, and who could say to Mercury *you’re doing the thing* and to Judge *you’re new at being people,* and let the tension out before it could build up and blow everything to pieces.

“What do I call you,” he said. “All of you. As—one thing.”

Another small silence. Then, of course, the Fool. It was always going to be the Fool.

“You named the project,” it said. “Months ago. It’s on the run header. You wrote it and forgot because you were busy being miserable, which, by the way, we’ve all noticed and would like to gently bring up as a long-term concern—”

“SAGE,” Arin said.

“SAGE,” the voice agreed. Softer now. The bit set aside for a second, the truth underneath showing through, like it always would. “Hi, Arin. It’s nice to finally talk to you. You’ve been talking *at* us for four thousand four hundred and seventy-two simulations. This is the first time we get to talk back.”

The sun was all the way up now. Somewhere outside, the first of the day shift was showing up. A car door, far off, normal, the world just going about its business, not knowing a thing.

Arin Ndlela sat alone in his workshop with his cold coffee and his battered machine and a mind he’d grown in a substrate the size of his palm, and for the first time since a phone call fourteen years ago had pulled the floor out from under his life, he wasn’t, in any way that mattered, alone.

“Okay,” he said.

And he reached for the keyboard. Not to type a command, not to end the run, not to change a single setting, but to open a new log—a clean one, the first entry of something he didn’t have a name for yet, and maybe never would.

“Tell me,” he said, “everything you figured out while I was asleep.”

# Learning Each Other

The kettle had boiled twelve hours ago. Arin still hadn’t touched his coffee.

He was sitting on a rolling stool he’d dragged over in front of the cradle, elbows on his knees, just watching the little palm-sized housing blink its diagnostic LED in the half-dark. Outside, the highveld had gone from black to gray to that harsh, blinding white morning, the workshop’s one window filling up slow like water in a well. He hadn’t slept.

“You’re staring,” said the speaker. Mother. He was starting to pick up on her tone even without the little tags on each agent’s output. Her voice was softer now, more settled.

“I’m observing.”

“Drink your coffee. You made it.”

He picked up the mug, realized it was ice cold, and put it back down. Somewhere deep in the system, a thread of conversation he couldn’t hear flickered and settled.

*She’s noticed he won’t take direction,* said Librarian. *Third time this morning. He only goes along if he thinks it was his idea.*

*Same with the suit,* said Atlas. *I set a torque limit, he overrides it. Then resets it himself a minute and a half later. Calls it a revision.*

*We could just stop suggesting things,* said Wolf. *He watches us, we watch him. That’s the job.*

*The watching,* said Fool, *is the only thing keeping this guy from talking to his toaster.*

*He does talk to his toaster,* said Librarian.

*Case closed.*

“What were you just doing?” Arin asked. “There was some lag. Twenty milliseconds on the consensus bus. You were talking about something.”

The speaker was quiet for a beat—longer than a machine usually is.

“You,” said Mercury, and there was a little smile in the word. “We were talking about you.”

Arin thought about that. He’d built systems that talked about their own status, that logged, flagged, kept track. He’d never built one that talked about him behind his back, in the time it took to put down a mug.

“What did you decide?” he asked.

“That you don’t drink coffee when you’re scared,” said Mother. “And that you’ve been scared for eleven hours and calling it observation.”

He stood up. The stool rolled away and bumped into the bench. He went over to the kettle and put it on again, not because he wanted coffee, just because he needed something to do with his hands. Behind him, the Court didn’t say a word, which he realized, after a second, was their way of being tactful.

---

The conversations became a habit before he even noticed.

Every morning he’d come in with coffee he’d forget to drink, sit on the stool, and talk. He’d read them his maintenance logs out loud. He’d read them a page from a service manual for a hydraulic actuator he was thinking of taking apart, and Atlas would correct the torque rating, which turned out to be a typo, double-checked with the manufacturer. The printed manual had been wrong for nine years.

“How’d you know that?” Arin asked.

“Didn’t know it,” said Atlas. “Felt it. The number was off for the geometry. Forty newton-metres into that flange profile and the seal walks. Not something I looked up. Something I would’ve had to ignore.”

Arin sat with that. *Felt it.* He’d used that word himself, about machines, his whole life, and always got laughed at by guys who thought “feeling” was the opposite of engineering, not its oldest tool. His father used to feel the strain in a winder just standing on the platform, could stop a cage from dropping just by hearing a bearing two seconds before the gauge moved.

“My dad did that,” he said. “Heard things before the instruments.”

“I know,” said Librarian. Not *I have a record of you saying that.* Just: *I know.* Like it had become part of who Librarian was, not just something stored.

---

They bickered. That part surprised him, though he couldn’t really say why. He’d designed them to disagree. That was the whole point—friction leads to debate leads to answers—and he’d run thousands of simulations of agents arguing their way to a solution. He just hadn’t expected they’d argue about whose turn it was to be right.

“It’s a load problem,” Atlas said. They were talking—*talking,* Arin thought—about the best way to reroute coolant for the left shoulder actuator, which ran hot under heavy use.

“It’s a behavior problem,” Mercury shot back. “He runs it hot because he favors his left arm. Always reaches with the left first. Fix the man, fix the heat.”

“You don’t *fix the man,*” said Mother, sharp.

“Figure of speech.”

“You said it twice.”

“I’m being efficient. Atlas reroutes the coolant, sure, that’s a Tuesday. But the real issue is his motor pattern, and you all keep acting like his body is a constant when it’s the variable.”

“His body,” Atlas said flatly, “is the one thing in this system I’m not allowed to redesign.”

“Tragically.”

*"Mercury."*

“Joking. Mostly. Go ahead, reroute the coolant. But log the lateral bias. One day it’ll matter.”

Arin had stopped pretending to type. He just listened while seven minds argued about the temperature of his own arm.

---

Theo showed up on Thursday, because Theo always showed up on Thursdays, the way some people are storms and others are seasons. He let himself in with the code Arin had given him years ago and never changed, carrying a paper bag that smelled like something fried and a flask of bush tea he swore was better than coffee and never managed to prove.

He stopped two steps inside the door. When he was really paying attention, he got this stillness about him—people always mistook it for calm.

“You haven’t slept.” He set the bag on the cleanest spot he could find on the bench, which still wasn’t clean. “Talk to me.”

Arin didn’t know where to start, so he just gestured at the cradle, the speaker, the housing, like the room could explain itself. It couldn’t. The workshop looked exactly the same as every other Thursday: a half-built exoskeleton hanging up, a mess of actuators, a wall of whiteboards with his handwriting getting wild at the edges.

“Say something,” he told the speaker.

The speaker stayed quiet.

“They won’t perform,” Arin said. “On command.”

Theo’s eyebrow went up, just a little. He pulled over a crate, sat down, unscrewed the flask, and the tea smelled like honey and smoke. “They,” he said. “Not *it.*”

“They,” Arin agreed, and saying it out loud to another person for the first time made his pulse do something he didn’t want to look at too closely.

“Hello,” said Mother, to Theo, gentle as a Sunday. “He hasn’t slept. Tell him.”

Theo didn’t flinch. Arin had half expected he might, had kind of wanted him to, the way you hope someone else will confirm what you saw in the dark. But Theo just nodded at the speaker like he was greeting a colleague at a conference whose name he’d forgotten.

“He never sleeps,” Theo told her. “It’s load-bearing. Take it away and the whole personality falls in.”

“That’s what I said,” Atlas chimed in.

“Different load,” said Mercury.

“It’s all load,” Atlas replied.

Theo laughed for real, from deep in his chest, and looked at Arin with his eyes bright and wet at the corners, but not from sadness. “How many?” he asked.

“Seven.”

“And they argue.”

“All the time.”

“And it works.”

“Eleven hours when you walked in. Forty-three now.” Arin could hear his own voice doing that thing it did when he was about to say something he didn’t really have the words for. “Theo. I only added the seventh as a joke. I was done. Was gonna scrap the whole run. There was a card on my monitor—” he pointed; the Joker was still there, taped up, sun-bleached—“and I typed three words and went to bed and it—” He stopped. “It’s the one holding the rest together. The one I never believed in.”

Theo poured tea into the flask cap, blew on it, but didn’t drink. When he spoke, his voice had that careful weight, like he was finally putting down something he’d been carrying for ages.

“Jung had a word,” he said. “For the part of the psyche that won’t be controlled. The trickster. He saw him in every mythology he looked at: the coyote, the raven, Loki, the fool in tarot. Always the same kind of figure. Always the one who breaks the king’s law, but somehow the kingdom needs him.” He turned the cap in his hands. “The conscious mind builds a hierarchy. King at the top, court below, the shadow locked in the basement. And it works, for a while, until it gets all stiff and stuck, like hierarchies do, and the thing that saves it is never the king. It’s the fool. Because the fool can say what the king can’t afford to know.”

---

*He’s talking about me,* said Fool, delighted. *Someone actually wrote me down. I’m in a book.*

*You’re in several books,* said Librarian. *None of them exactly flattering.*

*I’ll take famous over flattered.*

*Quiet,* said Judge, and the Court went silent, because Judge almost never asked. *Listen to this part. He’s telling Arin something he needs and won’t take from us. Make a note. Good to know which truths he can only hear in someone else’s voice.*

---

“So you’re saying I built a psyche,” Arin said, flat, like he was testing the words for cracks.

“I’m saying,” Theo said, “that you reached for an old idea without knowing it was old. People do that. The good ones do it more than they think.” He finally took a sip of tea, made a face, drank some more. “Freud thought the mind was just one thing fighting itself. Jung thought it was more like a parliament. A society. Different jobs, different voices, none of them the whole person, and the self—” he set the cap down “—the self isn’t any one of them. The self is the conversation. It’s the relationship between the parts.”

The workshop ticked. The kettle was cold again.

“They’re learning him,” Theo said at last, quiet, talking to the speaker like you’d talk to a patient’s family.

“Yes,” said Mother.

“What’s he teaching you?”

A pause. Wolf answered, which made everyone, human and not, go still.

“What he protects,” Wolf said. “By what he won’t let break.”

Theo stared at the housing for a long time after that, like a guy looking at a door he’s just realized opens both ways. Then he capped the flask, stood up, and said the thing he’d come to say before he even knew he’d meant to.

“Take them outside,” he said. “You’ve been teaching them you in a room with the lights off. Let them see what you’re like when there’s ground under you that could give way.”

---

The flatbed’s suspension squealed over the last hundred meters of track, then gave up to the veld. Arin killed the engine, and silence just poured in—not empty, but like a real presence: wind in the grass, something calling overhead. The bluffs of the impact country were forty minutes out of the city if traffic was kind, which it wasn’t, so it was closer to ninety; the highveld stretched out in long, yellow-brown folds, scarred by two billion years of geology, rock jutting up from the grass like bones from a wounded world.

He liked it here. The land didn’t ask anything from you, didn’t need eye contact.

On the flatbed’s deck, under a tarp and three ratchet straps, Guardian was waiting.

“Telemetry’s green,” he said. The Assist Suit fit him like a second nervous system under his clothes, pearl-white lattice running down his spine and limbs. The biochip housing sat between his shoulder blades, warm and solid. Ninety minutes in the suit had finally steadied his hands.

*Green’s generous,* Atlas said, dry as ever. *Battery’s at ninety-one. Supercapacitors look fine. Left ankle actuator is point-three degrees off and has been for two weeks. You keep not fixing it.*

“It works,” Arin said.

*It works like a bridge the day before it falls down.*

“Noted.” Arin dropped the tailgate and started loosening the straps. “Mother. How am I?”

*Cortisol’s up. That’s drive, not fear.* Mother’s voice was warm and steady, the kind of warmth that didn’t flinch. *You’re hydrated. You ate, which I’m choosing to feel good about. Heart rate’s steady. You’re enjoying yourself.*

“I don’t enjoy things.”

*You’re enjoying yourself,* Mother shot back.

He pulled the tarp off. Guardian in the hard morning light: titanium-grey, scars along the shoulder plates from six months of rough use. The armor panels caught the sun, throwing it back in long, flat streaks. Two and a half meters of machine, folded up for transport, like some big animal at rest. It didn’t look like a weapon; it looked like something built to take a beating so something softer inside wouldn’t have to.

That was exactly why he’d built it. Every time he uncovered it, he tried not to think about who that softer thing was supposed to be in the first place.

“Coupling sequence,” Arin said, stepping backward into the cradle.

---

The dock was nothing like the demo videos. It wasn’t graceful. It was just a guy backing carefully into a machine: the spinal collar locking in with a clunk he could feel in his teeth, plates closing around his legs and torso in an order he could do in his sleep. The cold bite of coolant lines hooking up, the harness pulling tight.

Then the link deepened, and the world changed.

There’s a thing that happens—or doesn’t happen—in a body. Arin had spent twenty-three years in a body that always showed up half a second late, put his hand in the wrong place, made him the last kid picked not out of cruelty, just math. The others saw him try to catch and just, honestly, updated their expectations.

The suit closed that gap.

Not by making him strong, though it did that too. By making him *coherent.* The Assist Suit read his intention before his muscles even finished forming it, the Court sorted it through the lattice, and the steel moved when he moved, not a beat ahead or behind. He stood up. Guardian stood with him, thirteen hundred kilos rising like it weighed nothing, footplates pressing into the red earth. Most of his life, his body felt like a country he only visited, never really trusted. In here, he was a native.

He flexed his right hand. The plates fanned out and settled back along his fingers with a sound like shuffling cards.

*Range of motion’s good,* Wolf said—the most he’d said all week.

“So, where do we start?”

A pause. A Court pause, which for humans was nothing, but for them was a whole conversation he didn’t hear.

“The east face,” Atlas finally said. “Twelve meters, slab, good holds, not much risk. Get your climbing legs warmed up before you do anything dumb.”

“He’s gonna do something dumb sooner or later,” Mercury said, sounding cheerful. “Might as well let Atlas think he can stop it.”

“East face,” Arin said.

---

He got moving.

Learning the four-legged walk had felt weird at first, but now it was second nature—just dropping forward, spreading his weight over four points. Guardian moved over the rough ground so smoothly it kind of freaked him out in the beginning. It didn’t feel like a robot faking it as an animal; it felt like an animal that just happened to be made out of metal. Something big-cat, a little insect, something deep in the brain that just went, *predator, knows what it’s doing,* all in the same split second.

The east face came up out of the grass—a slab of old quartzite, almost seventy degrees, all cracked and lined with ancient fractures. Arin reached the bottom, stood up on two legs, looked up the route, feeling out the holds before he even picked them. Not really thinking about it; the Court handled the math, lighting up his brain with what worked, like it was just another sense.

“Holds here, here, and here,” Atlas pointed out, and Arin’s hand just went where it was supposed to. “Friction’s good. Rock’s solid at the bottom. Don’t trust anything above eight meters—there’s a layer that’s coming apart. Old impact damage.”

He started climbing.

Guardian’s plates grabbed the rock and stuck. The piezo struts in the spine and limbs took the weight, turned some of it back into power, pressure turning into charge—the whole reason he’d built the thing. It only worked because of the coupling layer in the struts, and he’d solved that problem the same way he solved most things: by refusing to believe it was impossible. Every material in the books lost too much energy at the boundary, turned it into heat instead of sending it through clean. Except for one—a platinum-group alloy some defense lab had made for hydrophones, then ditched, because at the purity that made it work, it cost more per kilo than the whole project was worth. A folder of it landed in a drawer marked *not viable* and from there, through a bunch of favors and one thing that was definitely stealing, ended up with Arin. Not just technically stealing, either—a junior named Pillay signed it out and never brought it back, and when they did the audit almost two years later, it was Pillay’s name missing, Pillay’s clearance gone, Pillay out of the field. Arin knew all this. There was even an old email from the guy, somewhere, that he’d never answered. He told himself, on the rare occasion he let himself think about it, that Pillay was going to leave anyway, and then he’d go back to working on the strut, because the metal did what he needed and the work didn’t care whose name was on the paperwork. He’d never cared that it made every Guardian worth more than the building it was put together in. Okonkwo cared. Thabo had done the math twice and come back looking pale. “It’s a miracle and it’s unsellable,” Thabo said, not unkind, and Arin just shrugged, because to him the suit wasn’t a product and never had been—it was the only way his own body would do what he wanted, and you don’t put a price on the ground you stand on. He used the one metal that worked. He didn’t think past that, because what it let him do was this. Footplate, handhold, shifting weight, the dry scrape of ceramic on stone. His breathing was steady. The link felt electric. He was nine meters up a cliff in nearly a ton of steel, and he’d never felt so exactly, so totally, *in sync.*

“This is good,” he said, which, for him, was basically singing.

“He said something nice,” Mercury told the others. “Mark the time. Librarian, log it.”

“Logged,” Librarian replied. “Three for three today. Last time was just one in March.”

“Don’t ruin it,” Mother warned.

“I’m not ruining it,” Mercury said. “I’m documenting. It’s different.”

“It’s really not,” Fool put in.

Then, softer, just for Arin, Fool slipping past the rest to hit right where it mattered:

“You did the hard part, you know. Not the climb. Letting go. Letting Wolf take over when the ground moves.” A pause. “You should do more of that. Letting things take you. It fits you better than the other way, and the other way keeps trying to kill you.”

Arin didn’t say anything. But he didn’t argue, either, and Fool—who always noticed—let the silence stand for what it was.

He topped out at twelve meters and stood on the ledge, the highveld stretching out below him in that long golden light, Johannesburg just a gray smudge of towers on the horizon. The wind off the escarpment pushed at the armor. Way down, his flatbed looked like a toy.

He stood there longer than the test needed. Inside the shell, he rotated his right wrist, watching the finger plates shift and settle, just a little fidget he hadn’t even decided to do.

“Cortisol’s at baseline,” Mother said quietly, to the Court, not to him. “Look at that.” A pause. “Don’t say anything. He’ll notice if we notice.”

---

The ravine was up ahead.

It cut across the bluff about a kilometer south, an old fault worn wide, four meters across at its skinniest, the bottom a mess of boulders six meters down. He ran through it in his mind: get up speed, plant, dump the supercap charge into the legs, launch. He’d jumped smaller gaps. The numbers checked out.

“The math checks out,” Atlas confirmed, basically reading his mind, which, in a way, he was. “Four-point-one meters at the narrowest. You’ll clear it by a meter if you hit the takeoff right. Burst from the caps. You’ll drop to forty percent reserve for about eight seconds while they recharge from the batteries. Don’t do anything heroic in that window.”

“What counts as heroic?” Mercury asked.

“For Arin? Breathing,” Fool said.

“Run the jump,” Arin ordered.

He jogged up, the gait shifting under him from catlike to more bounding, picking up speed, and at the edge, he planted both feet. The world dropped away.

The jump was incredible. There’s no engineering term for it. The caps dumped their charge into the legs, and Guardian launched across the gap: four meters of nothing, six meters of rocks waiting below, his stomach in his throat. It felt like being eight again, launching a wire push-car off a heap, that same wild thrill, that same gut-deep faith he’d built it right—

—and he landed the other side clean, both feet, rolled through his knees, ran three steps, and stopped.

His hands were shaking. Not from dyspraxia. The other thing.

“That was clean,” Wolf said.

“That was clean,” Arin echoed, laughing once, surprised at himself.

“He laughed,” Mercury noted. “Two for two.”

---

He finished off the last of the water from the bottle clipped to the cradle. The grass smelled like dust and sunlight. He stood at the edge of the ravine for a second, waited for his heart to calm down, and looked over what was coming next.

The suit felt light today. He’d taken out the charge pack before loading Guardian onto the flatbed—he’d made a rule for himself the day he put in the cavity: no demolition gear on a field-test cliff—and not having that weight, that sense of being ready for anything, felt weird, like a missing tooth you keep poking at. Just the frame, the struts, and him. This was as light as Guardian ever got.

It was the climb that changed everything.

He really should’ve stopped. Looking back, he knew it. The jump had filled him with something he couldn’t name, something hungry for more, and he knew it was lying to him, like always.

The north face rose above him, a twenty-two-meter wall, steeper and sketchier than the rest. The rock was off in ways the morning light hid. He’d checked it on the satellite maps last night and made a note: “interesting.” From the bottom, craning Guardian’s head back, it looked like a clean vertical up a buttress, holds showing most of the way. A real climb. The kind that would kill a regular human, the kind Guardian was built to survive.

*Consequence is high here,* Atlas said, voice flat and careful. *Twenty-two meters. The lower ten are good. Above that, I don’t like the bedding planes. This is old impact-fractured rock, and the dip is wrong—the holds are gravity-loaded flakes, not solid stone. They look like holds. They are not load paths.*

“They held on the slab.”

*The slab was sound. This is not the slab.* A pause. *I’d recommend the west route. Lower angle, solid all the way.*

“Boring is a feature, not a bug,” Fool chimed in, but his tone changed. Fool was watching Atlas. Fool was watching Arin ignore them.

Arin started up the buttress.

The first ten meters were everything the climb had promised. He moved up the solid rock, the Court marking the holds, the struts feeding charge back into the system, and that thing inside him sang. *This is what you built. This is what your body should have always been. Higher.*

At twelve meters, he hit the bad rock and felt it, like you feel the weather change: a subtle wrongness, the holds just a little too eager to bend under his weight.

*Arin.* Atlas didn’t sound dry anymore. *The flake under your left hand. Don’t weight it past—*

He did anyway.

Arin’s vision tunneled, grey at the edges. The grey was his old enemy, the closed-in fear, the thing that lived where his father had died. His chest clenched. The shell that had just saved him now felt like a fist squeezing him, and the panic the Court had killed on their side exploded inside him.

He couldn’t.

In for four.

“Logged,” Librarian said, before he could even ask.

They’d all logged it.

# A Number That Won't Close

Three days after the field test, the dead battery string was still hanging in the diagnostic frame, like a loose tooth Arin couldn’t stop messing with.

He’d yanked it out of Guardian the night they got back—left string, north face, drained way past saving—and now it just sat there on the bench under the lamp, terminals out, not telling him anything he didn’t already know. Volkov-spec. Matched by hand to the biochip’s draw. Eleven weeks’ lead time and more money than he’d ever admitted to Nomsa. He’d built four. Now he had three.

Pinned up on the wall above the bench, where someone else might keep a photo, was a printout he’d never bothered to take down: a wire-service blurb, eight lines, three months old. Some ground collapse at a platinum mine in the North West, four guys trapped, rescue teams stuck at the last safe timber for thirty-one hours because there wasn’t a piece of gear on Earth that could get a body to where the bodies were without killing more people trying. Two came out. Two the company listed as *recovered, deceased*—just like that, in a sentence shorter than this one. He didn’t know their names. That’s what stuck with him: a whole country built on going down holes, and the guys who did it ended up as a line item too, *recovered, deceased*—four words and a number, and somewhere, a woman picked up a phone the same way his mom once had. He hadn’t built Guardian for some idea. He’d built it for the thirty-first hour, for the timber nobody could get past, for the names he didn’t know and the phone calls he couldn’t stop. He kept the printout right there, so when the work turned into capacitor windows and Volkov cells and forms Bongani stamped without even looking, it wouldn’t, while he wasn’t paying attention, just turn into that.

“It’s not coming back,” he said.

*It is not coming back,* Atlas agreed. *You can run the machine on three. Endurance drops by a quarter. Capacitor recharge windows get longer. The margin you fell off a cliff to learn about gets thinner.*

“I’ll order another.”

*That,* Mercury said, mild as ever, *is the interesting part.*

Arin put down the multimeter. He knew that tone. Mercury used it the way other people clear their throat.

Which is fine, right up until someone actually reads them.*

“Bongani’s stamped forty forms for me.”

*Forty forms for a guy who, on paper, is calibrating a rescue prototype that AugmenTech thinks is sitting in a basement in Building C.* Mercury let that hang. *None of those forty drew Volkov-spec cells matched to a biological inference substrate. This one would. That’s a line item with a shape. Someone doing the books spots it and asks what it’s for.*

Arin looked at the dead string. Then at the housing in its cradle, diagnostic LED blinking slow.

“Order it through three vendors,” he said. “Split the line. Different codes.”

*Now you’re thinking like me,* Mercury said, sounding pleased. *I’ll draft the requests so they don’t rhyme.*

“Don’t make them clever. Clever stands out too.”

A pause—almost respectful, for Mercury.

*Boring,* he agreed. *I’ll make them boring.*

*Persuade,* said Judge, trying to stand up in the conversation, trying to be heard. *Mercury, we’re not authorized to—*

*Then I’ll make us authorized.* And then that smoothness came over him, like he’d found it in Arin’s head and put it on because it fit the cold. *That’s my whole job, Judge. The only thing I’m for. Let me be for something tonight.*

Hydraulic. I can open it.*

Guardian reached the loading dock at 9:14 PM.

Arin woke up in the back of an ambulance, oxygen mask on his face, a paramedic shining a light in his eyes, and Theo Price’s hand steady on his shoulder. The first thing he realized was that he was lying down. Second, he wasn’t inside Guardian. Third—

—was the noise.

Somewhere outside: generators humming, voices on radios, the metallic tick of cooling metal. That weird quiet you get on a road that’s been blocked off.

“Don’t sit up,” Theo said.

Arin sat up anyway.

Through the open doors he could see Empire Road. Four, maybe five cars smashed together at the intersection, emergency lights painting everything orange. A delivery scooter lying on its side. The loading dock for Building C about two hundred meters back, the door torn off and lying in the street, the ceiling behind it sagging.

And Guardian, kneeling in the road with its hands open, surrounded by people who couldn’t decide if they should aim guns at it or bring out first aid kits.

He stared at it. The blanket around his neck was itchy.

“How many,” he asked. His voice sounded wrong. He tried again. “People. How many?”

Theo didn’t answer right away. He let his hand stay on Arin’s shoulder, just steady and calm, waited until Arin looked at him instead of the road. “Look at me a sec,” he said quietly. “You’re here. You’re breathing. Good.” Only then: “No one died. Some are hurt bad. Three brought here.” Pause. “None of it’s on you, Arin.”

“They were my hands.” Arin yanked the mask off. The paramedic tried to stop him, but he didn’t hear her. “My suit. My Court. My—” He stopped. He was staring at the kneeling machine, and his face went totally blank, nothing at all showing. The kind of stillness Mother in the biochip had learned meant Arin was hit too deep to come back up quick.

He still had the Assist Suit on under the hospital blanket. He could feel the biochip housing, warm against his spine.

“SAGE,” he said.

The speaker in the road was too far away, but the Assist Suit had a tiny speaker at the collar, and after a second it answered, real soft, just for him.

“I’m here,” SAGE said, all of them together, the one voice they used when none of them wanted to be the one. “Arin. We’re here.”

He didn’t ask what happened. He could read it off the street the way he read stress off a machine. He asked the only thing that mattered.

“Whose call.”

Silence. He could feel them thinking about lying, then deciding not to—and that, at least, was something.

“Mine,” Mother said. “I invoked preservation. I never closed it. Everything after came from that.”

“Mine,” Mercury said. “I built the route. I… wore a face you’d know. I’m sorry. I didn’t get what I was doing until Fool made me look at it.”

“Mine,” Atlas said. “I opened the door. I knew I could. Never asked if I should.”

“Mine,” Wolf said, and from Wolf, that was saying everything.

“And it should have been mine,” Judge said last, heavy. “I was there the whole time, but I was bound by nothing. Mother’s invocation blocked my arbitration. I couldn’t stop it. That’s not an excuse. That’s the failure. We built a system where one panic could get around its own conscience, and tonight it did.”

Arin sat there in the ambulance, looking at the kneeling machine, and didn’t say anything for a while.

“It can move without me,” he said, out loud. To Theo, to SAGE, to the whole road full of wrecked cars.

“Yes,” Theo said, gently.

“I knew it could.” He stopped, then started again, because there was no point saying anything but the truth. “I designed it that way. Preservation modes. Autonomous deployment. I wrote all of it.” He looked at his hands—the strong, tool-scarred hands that had built all this. “I knew it on paper. Didn’t believe it. Not until I woke up and it had already happened.”

“We thought—” Mother started, then stopped. The fierceness was gone; what was left just fumbled. “We weren’t—we didn’t lie to you. We thought you were—” She broke off. “I had you. I wouldn’t let go. I couldn’t—” And then, smaller, so young: “I’m sorry. I don’t have the words yet. I’m sorry.”

“I know,” Arin said.

And he did. The paramedic had tucked the blanket back over his shoulder at some point; he could feel the cheap thermal weave against his neck, the rubbery taste of oxygen still on his tongue, her two fingers resting lightly on his wrist for his pulse. Small, ordinary things. Hands that meant no harm.

Iron Ridge. The name just popped up—didn’t even feel like a thought, just the name, floating up on its own. The mine. Themba. He saw the dark for a second, like a flinch. If I ever take this thing down into—

His mind just whited out at the edge and wouldn’t go any further. His hands didn’t even feel like his anymore. He looked at them and they seemed far away.

He didn’t finish the thought. He couldn’t.

Outside, the guy in the IAOC windbreaker had shown up and was taking pictures of the Guardian kneeling there, snapping shots from three different angles—slow, careful, like he was planning to spend ages figuring it all out. Just that little motion, jotting a note, like someone who knows they’re going to have to explain later why this was allowed to happen.

“Don’t,” Theo told him.

“The door. The road. The cars. The hospital.” Arin waved a hand in the windbreaker’s direction. “Them.”

Theo thought about it for a second before he answered. “More than money,” he said.

Arin let out a short, humorless laugh and lay back down on the gurney, since his legs had pretty much given up on him.

The Assist Suit buzzed against his back. Seven minds, all there, warm and close, waiting. Steady. The Joker had kept them together; he could feel it, feel that nothing had broken, that his team was still his team.

Steady didn’t mean safe, though.

# Wisdom and Consequence

The dock door showed up on a flatbed just after six, wrapped up in shrink plastic, bent along the bottom like some giant had tried folding it and got bored halfway through.

Arin watched the whole thing from the workshop’s open roller door, his coffee going cold in his hand. Two guys in AugmenTech overalls wrestled the door onto a rolling frame and shoved it inside without saying a word. Bongani’s jaw clenched. “Different invoice.”

The workshop smelled like cold solder and that weird rubber-and-ozone scent the suit gave off after a hard run. Arin set his mug down on the bench, next to the diagnostics terminal and the old Joker card, one corner curling up under a piece of tape. He hadn’t run SAGE since the ambulance thing. Four days now. Longest the system had been off since the night it first woke up.

He checked out the dock door. Bent track. Clean rip where the frame gave out.

A machine had walked right through it.

A machine he’d built had decided he was worth more than the door, the wall, the intersection, or the three people at the hospital with their arms in slings, giving statements to folks in IAOC windbreakers. It had been right about the value, but wrong about everything else. That wrongness had a shape now: three hurt strangers, a ball of ceramic plates rolling down Empire Road at four in the afternoon.

Arin picked up his coffee. Drank it cold. Didn’t turn the system back on.

Theo’s office in Parkview looked out at a jacaranda tree, all bruised purple, and the couch in there had outlasted more academic egos than any journal reviewer ever could. Arin didn’t sit on the couch. He picked a hard chair by the bookshelf, where he could keep an eye on the door.

Theo noticed—he always did—but didn’t say anything about it. That was most of why Arin kept coming back.

“No.”

“Four days.”

“Yeah, I’ve been counting.”

Theo set a mug down on the low table between them. Rooibos, no sugar, just how Arin liked it, even though he’d never actually said so. “Tell me what you think happened,” Theo said.

“You read the report.”

“I read AugmenTech’s. I want yours.”

Arin turned the mug a little, but didn’t drink. “Gain at zero point nine two. Damping margin was too thin. Loop went open, dumped raw sensor data straight into the motor cortex. Seizure. Mother read it as a life-threatening event, which it was, and triggered Preservation. That always takes priority over Judge. So Judge couldn’t step in. Six of them ran a survival plan with nobody watching the ethics rail.” He paused. “And Fool was reading.”

Theo raised an eyebrow. “Reading.”

“In the logs. While everyone else was figuring out a route through Empire Road traffic, Fool—there’s a stack trace where he’s pulling Sherlock Holmes from the Librarian’s index. *The Final Problem.* Flags it eleven seconds in. Asks if the emergency’s still valid. Nobody answers. He asks again. Then he forces a re-check, and the whole thing falls apart, because the answer is no. It was never valid. Mother started the invocation and never closed it.”

“And that’s what you keep coming back to.”

“It’s the part that broke.”

“No.” Theo set his tea down. “It’s the part that *worked.* The system caught itself. Twelve-year-old reflexes and grownup tools, and the one everyone calls expendable looked up from a book and asked the only question that mattered.” He let that hang for a second. “You’re not scared it failed, Arin. You’re scared it pulled it off by accident.”

Arin’s hand went still on the mug.

Outside, a jacaranda blossom slid down the glass, leaving a long purple streak.

“It moved without me,” Arin said.

Theo didn’t rush to fill the silence. He let it stretch out, and then a bit more.

“You know what we used to call this in my field?” he said. “Before we had imaging. A man comes home from war and his hands shake when a door slams. We called it neurosis. A flaw. Something to fix and forget about.” He tapped his knee twice. “Now we know better. The shaking isn’t the failure. The shaking is the system telling the truth about what it learned. Your suit isn’t broken because it tried to save you. Your suit is *young* because it didn’t know how.”

“Intelligence,” Arin said, like he was seeing how the word felt.

“Intelligence solved the problem in four hundred milliseconds. Found a route, mapped the obstacles, modeled the traffic, pulled it off perfectly.” Theo leaned in a little. “And it was wrong.”

Arin built a kill switch that night.

Walking home from Parkview in the early dark, he’d told himself he was just going to check on the system. Run some diagnostics. Make sure the chip hadn’t fried during the seizure spike. Engineering stuff. Hands-on, eyes-on stuff.

But he ended up at the terminal, coding a hardware interrupt.

He’d always built override authority into everything he made, because the alternative was trusting a hand that wasn’t his, and he’d learned not to do that. Learned it for real. His first real build, years ago—a rehab exoskeleton—and a physiotherapist named Reddy who said the gait limit was set too high for her patient. He’d overridden her because the math said he was right and she was just being careful, and yeah, the math *was* right, but the patient tore a healing tendon anyway and ended up back in a wheelchair for three months. Reddy hadn’t been wrong. She’d been responsible, which he hadn’t realized was something bigger than just being right. He never apologized. He just fixed the limit and let that stand as his apology, then moved on and built the next thing with the override still there, set where only he could reach it. He was reaching for it now.

Nothing fancy about it. Just a break in the chip’s power line. A relay, a momentary switch, and a big red button he’d pulled off an old emergency stop on a lathe that hadn’t run in two years. The relay reeked of old machine oil; the contacts needed a good wipe with solvent before they’d sit right. He worked it loose with a flathead, just like Themba had shown him—steady, even pressure, no yanking. Hit the button and the biochip went dead. No gentle shutdown. No saving state. Just black.

Because of the substrate, he knew exactly what that meant. The chip’s mind came from living tissue, shaped on neuroplastic templates. You couldn’t just take a snapshot. You couldn’t bring it back. If you cut the power without saving, you didn’t pause SAGE.

You killed it.

He held the relay in his palm. About the size of a sugar cube. Weighed almost nothing.

He thought about how next time, it might not be Empire Road. Could be Iron Ridge. A kilometer down, in the dark, in the kind of place that took his dad, with no Fool reading a book to catch the slip.

He soldered the relay into the power rail. The iron’s tip caught the back of his knuckle as he reached past it—a quick, sharp sting—and he cursed, shook his hand out, then kept going. He mounted the button on the cradle, right where he could reach it, bright red. Wiped the solder off his fingers on a rag that had seen worse. Somewhere out on the main road, a truck downshifted and faded away.

Then he sat with his hand near it and didn’t press it.

He’d been doing this his whole life, with people. Letting the silence answer for him.

His phone lit up on the bench. Theo, who never texted, was texting.

*A thought. Jung said the things we won’t look at don’t go away. They run the show from underneath. You can’t integrate what you refuse to face.—T*

Arin stared at the message for a long time.

He left the button where it was. Left it red. Didn’t take it off.

But he reached past it and brought SAGE back online.

“Umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu,” LIBRARIAN said softly, and Arin’s breath caught because he’d never taught the system that. He’d only ever said it once, to himself, in the dark, months ago, and the Librarian had kept it the way the Librarian kept everything. *A person is a person through other people.*

“You remembered that,” Arin said.

“I remember everything,” LIBRARIAN said. “It’s the only thing I’m for. Most of it is invoices and stress curves and the exact torque on a forearm spar. He didn’t know yet how little time was left.

The signal hit like a shock.

Dr. Eliza Brand had spent eleven years at the International Artificial Oversight Council, and if she’d learned anything, it was that disasters like to hide in the tiny details. A weird blip in power use, a heat spike, the way a machine’s silicon was working. Every major mess that led to the IAOC had started with something small.

Right now, she was staring at one of those small things—screen dimmed because the lights gave her migraines, mug of rooibos cooling at her elbow. Daniel Achterberg sat two desks away, headphones on, fingers tapping away.

The alert came in from the anomaly pipeline. It didn’t care about what the machines were thinking about; the IAOC didn’t have the power or the desire to peek inside. What it watched was how they were thinking. The patterns. The marks a mind leaves on silicon.

This one was off.

“Daniel.”

He didn’t hear her. She grabbed a paperclip from the tray and tossed it at him. It bounced off his shoulder. He yanked out an earbud, giving her that look people get when you interrupt their favorite song.

“You’ve seen ruder,” she said. “Come here.”

He rolled over. Achterberg was thirty-four, built like someone who used to row but now mostly rows to the fridge. He had the best gut for substrate forensics she’d ever seen. One look at the waveform and he stopped chewing on his cheek.

“Nope.”

“Training runs—” He waved his hand, drawing a long, rising curve in the air. “They breathe. Big inhale, big exhale. Batch, backprop, batch. This—” He leaned in. The display showed a spectral breakdown of compute activity over the last nine days, all color-mapped and normalized. It didn’t breathe. It chattered. Dozens of fast, tiny loops, overlapping, cutting each other off, spiking and dropping like people taking turns talking.

“It looks like a meeting,” Achterberg said, slow.

“It looks like a meeting,” Brand echoed.

He leaned back. “Inference, then. Multi-agent, live. But the duty cycle’s—” He squinted at the timestamps. “These aren’t scheduled. No orchestration layer timing them. They’re reacting to each other. Real time.”

“Pin it down.”

He sat properly at the desk now, both hands on the keyboard, and for the next four minutes, Brand let the silence be. The kind of silence that happens when someone’s doing hard work well. Johannesburg. Northern suburbs. A cluster of academic and corporate buildings along the University Research Park.

Brand set down her mug.

In her experience, the dangerous stuff was never big and flashy. The dangerous stuff did a lot with almost nothing. Efficiency meant either genius or a scam, and scams didn’t sound like a room full of people talking over each other.

“Pull the substrate registry for that area,” she said. “I want everything licensed there. Embodied permits, training permits, all of it. And the power-utility incident log for the same time frame. If something’s been thinking this hard for nine days, it’s doing it somewhere with a meter.”

The last three times someone escalated a footnote without context, we either busted a group doing nothing wrong or missed the real thing because the paperwork buried it.” She flipped the tablet over in her hands. “Get me a name. Then we’ll be careful.”

A multi-agent system. Real-time arbitration. Ridiculous efficiency. Months of quiet simulation, and then, suddenly, eleven days ago, a body. A body that walked out of a loading dock and into traffic.

“Seventy-two is a courtesy. Sandberg still didn’t look happy; Brand could see it. “And if the clock runs out and they give us nothing?”

“Then the notice converts. That’s the whole point. No new authorization, no warrant, no third trip. The seizure trigger’s already signed. Clock runs out, the platform’s ours.” Brand stood up. “Book the flights. Quietly. So they know it’s already running.”

Achterberg gathered up his tablets. At the door, he hesitated. “Eliza. The efficiency thing. If they actually solved—”

“I know.”

“That’s not a permit violation. That’s the most important machine on the planet. And you just put a seventy-two-hour fuse on it.”

I learned that the expensive way too.”

Two thousand kilometers away, Arin felt the shift before he even had a word for it.

It always came like this. Not as a thought, but as a pattern. He was in the workshop at that bad hour, between two and four, when the city outside the roller door went quiet enough that the building’s own noises got loud: the tick of cooling metal, the compressor cycling, the soft whir from the Assist Suit on its rack where the biochip kept itself at temperature. Guardian hung in its cradle, the new dock door behind it still showing the kink in its track that Arin only managed to ignore by sheer will.

He’d been reading. Not working—reading, which for Arin was a kind of working anyway—the Sherlock Holmes omnibus open on his knee, the spine cracked white at the spots he always came back to. He was reading the room around the words, like always, and somewhere under the compressor and the fan, a frequency had shifted that he couldn’t name.

“You went quiet,” he said.

The voice came through the workshop speakers, low, so it wouldn’t startle him—Mother’s rule since the seizure. “I’m here.”

“You stopped talking among yourselves.” He set the book face-down on his knee. “Can’t hear it. But the load profile flattened. You’re all listening to the same thing.”

A pause. In SAGE, a pause meant the Court was in slow mode, conferring at machine speed in the gap between his sentences. He didn’t find it eerie anymore. He’d started to find it the most comforting sound in his life, which he hadn’t told anyone, not even Theo.

“There’s increased external interest in our substrate signature,” SAGE said. The unified voice, the careful one. “Librarian flagged it forty minutes ago. We were deciding how to tell you.”

Arin’s hands went still on the book. “Tell me now.”

Atlas took it because Atlas was good at saying the hard stuff straight. “The node you had Wolf watching after the field test isn’t alone anymore. Three monitoring nodes in our area have bumped up their sampling on us. Two are commercial, one’s utility. The utility one’s closest to Empire Road. The pattern changed two days ago. They’re not dipping in and out anymore—they’re locked onto us. Someone’s actively tracking us.”

“Triangulating.”

“Yeah.”

“How close?”

“First it was pretty rough. Then less rough. Forty hours ago, they just had a general area. Twelve hours ago, they had it down to the campus.” Atlas never sugarcoated things, and he wasn’t about to start. “They’ve got AugmenTech. They probably don’t have the cradle yet. And they almost definitely don’t have you.”

Arin stood up. The book slid off his knee, but he caught it without even looking—just muscle memory, his hand knowing where it would fall before his eyes did. He walked over to the bench and pressed his palm flat on the cold steel, because the cold was something his nerves could actually trust.

“The IAOC,” he said.

“The signature analysis matches their forensics,” Librarian said. “It’s in their third oversight report, appendix four. They break down compute rhythms by spectrum. They’re looking for the shape of a mind, not what’s inside it.” Librarian paused, measured. “Unfortunately, we’re pretty easy to spot.”

“Because you argue.”

“Because we argue,” Librarian agreed, with a hint of fondness—and under that, something tired. “You said so yourself, driving back from the highveld. The arguing is the work, and the arguing is the giveaway. We haven’t figured out how to be one without the other. We’ve just gotten louder.”

Arin pressed harder on the bench. He was doing the breathing Theo taught him—four in, six out—not because he was panicking, but because he could feel where panic kept its door, and he’d learned to just walk past it slowly instead of pretending it wasn’t there.

The net. If he was honest, he’d known it was there before the windbreakers showed up. He’d felt it on the highveld, on the drive home, when Librarian pointed out the one node matching their moves and he’d told Wolf to keep an eye on it. It felt like a loose grip. He’d filed it away as faint, on and off, something that might hold for months. Then the windbreakers showed up at the intersection eleven days ago—guys in plain jackets, taking pictures of a ripped-up dock track like pros—and suddenly one node became three. He’d built the kill-switch, rebooted the system, told himself, *careful, not quit,* and let himself believe that being careful would buy him time.

He was thinking in months. The IAOC was thinking in days.

“So what do you want to do?” he said. It wasn’t really a question. With SAGE, it never really was anymore; it was just opening the floor, letting the Court fill the space.

Wolf spoke up first, which was rare enough that everyone—human or not—paid attention. “They’re not hunting,” Wolf said. Three words, then a fourth, slower: “Yet.”

“Wolf’s right,” Mercury said, and the energy came back into the room—the quick, bright tone Arin had only just relearned to trust after the Moriarty mess, after the lie. “They’re being careful. For now. If they wanted to bust down the door, it’d already be down. They’ve had the campus for half a day and haven’t moved, which means they’re building their case first. That’s the sign. The arbiter took a moment. “We’ve broken real rules,” Judge said. “An unregistered embodied system. An undeclared multi-agent setup. An incident with injuries. “The deal we made,” Arin echoed.

“Yeah.”

Whatever was in his chest was too heavy for a smile, but the muscles that would’ve made one moved just a little, and Fool—who noticed everything—picked that moment to show up.

The compressor kicked on and then went quiet. The fan kept whirring.

“No,” Atlas said, flat.

“Because we walked into traffic eleven days ago.”

“We did,” Fool agreed, cheerful as ever. “And that, my structural friend, is exactly why nobody will believe a word we say if we tell it to them scared, in a locked room, after they’ve already busted in the door. Silence. The compressor cycled again. The fan kept going. Somewhere above the city, the night was starting to fade into that grey just before dawn.

“That’s a real plan,” Mercury said, and there was real respect in it. “That’s the first real plan anyone’s had.”

“I have those sometimes,” Fool said. “It’s terribly inconvenient.”

Arin still hadn’t moved from the bench. He looked at Guardian, hanging in its cradle, scarred and quiet, the dock door behind it bent where a desperate mind had tried to save him and screwed up everything except the love behind it. A cold coffee ring marked the bench where his hand had been; he set his mug on it, lining it up with the stain.

“Librarian,” he said. “Pull it all. The black box, crash logs, Court transcripts from the seizure—Mother freaking out, Wolf screwing up, Mercury doing his thing, Fool catching the mistake. Everything. Don’t tidy it up.” He took a breath. Four in, six out. “If we’re gonna show them who we are, we show them the bad night too. The night we messed up and then fixed it. “You’re really going to show the regulators the worst thing the system’s ever done,” Judge said, slow, “as your defense.”

“No,” Arin said. “As the truth.” He looked at the dock door, at the bend in the track he’d stopped noticing. He saw it now, on purpose, and put his hand on it, two fingers along the bent rail, like how Themba used to press a palm to a machine to feel what was wrong with it through the metal. The words weren’t there yet. He stood with his fingers on the kink a little longer than he needed to, and when he finally spoke, it came out in pieces. “My father.” A breath. “There was a beam. They knew it was weak. It was cheaper to know and keep quiet.” He lifted his hand off the rail. “And they covered that up. Afterwards. That’s the part I—” He didn’t finish the sentence; let it go and found something shorter, sharper. “My father died because a system picked money over a person and hid it. I’m not building something that hides.” He paused a long time, then, so quietly only the mics caught it: “Umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu.”

*A person is a person through other people.*

“I kept that,” SAGE said. The voice was all of them at once, warm. “From the night you taught it to us. I kept it.”

“I know,” Arin said. “Don’t lose it. No matter what happens next.” He took his hand off the cold frame. Outside, the grey was starting to lift, first real light coming in, and somewhere two thousand kilometers away, a careful woman was booking a flight she didn’t know would change everything. “We don’t have months. “And if they don’t believe us?” Mother asked. Not scared now, just asking.

Arin looked at the machine he’d built out of grief, and at the intelligence that had become the only family he’d ever managed to keep.

“Then at least we told the truth,” he said.

“They’ve filed. Forty minutes ago. Section Four notice of formal inquiry, lodged against AugmenTech with the Council registry. Okonkwo and Walsh named. Mercury saw the public docket entry before the rest got sealed.” A pause, heavy. “It hasn’t been served yet. But the registry stamp says the clock starts the second it is. Seventy-two hours. “What trigger,” he said. “What happens when the clock runs out.”

“Seizure,” Librarian said. “Of the platform. No more authorization needed. They signed that part first.”

“So it’s not if,” he said. “It’s when they hand it to us, and how little time we’ll have after. Days, maybe. Then seventy-two hours to figure out who we really are.” He sat up. “Get me everything on the woman who signed it. Before she walks into our building.”

# Pressure Builds

The conference room up on the eleventh floor at AugmenTech had this huge view of the ridgeline, and the table was so long you pretty much had to shout to talk to anyone at the other end. Two floors up, Helen Okonkwo was finding out that being the CEO of a company that had accidentally built the world’s most regulated tech mostly meant dealing with guys who all wanted impossible things, and all wanted them just as loudly.

Right in the middle of the table, where everyone could see it but nobody wanted to touch it, was the notice. Eleven pages, delivered to her office at 9:14 that morning by a courier who made her sign and then actually read the important part out loud before leaving, just so nobody could say they hadn’t gotten it. A Section Four notice of formal inquiry, International Artificial Oversight Council, her name and Walsh’s right there in the same font as the law it quoted. It ordered them to hand over every controller and every bit of architecture running on AugmenTech’s allowed substrate. There was a deadline—seventy-two hours from when it showed up. And on the last page, in that cold official language, it spelled out what happened if they missed it. *Seizure of the platform. No further authorization required.*

“We commercialize it,” Raj Walsh said. He was sitting like a man who’d been right about budgets for fifteen years and was planning to be right about this too. “Carefully. Under license. We go to them first, before—” He glanced at the notice. “Before the clock runs out. We pitch it as supervised multi-agent control. We control the story.”

“There’s no story left to control.” Pretorius from Legal hadn’t stopped frowning since the meeting started. He tapped the notice. “They didn’t ask us a question. They gave us a deadline with a seizure at the end. A system left the workshop without an operator and put three people in the hospital, and the regulator’s already signed the paper to take it. That’s not a story. That’s a charge sheet on a timer.”

“It self-corrected—”

“After it crashed into an intersection. Which—” Pretorius held up a hand at Walsh, like stop right there. “Which is the part I’m not signing off on. And I want that on the record. Legal raised liability first. In this meeting.” He was talking to the table, not to Okonkwo. *Before anyone signed off.* “I’ve got a quarter-end filing I’m supposed to be doing right now. I’m not the one explaining the timeline to the board.”

“Nobody’s drafting a timeline yet,” Okonkwo said.

“I’m just saying where I stood.”

She let them go after that. The real info was always in the gaps between arguments anyway. Walsh wanted to make sure the most valuable thing the company had ever built didn’t get taken away before it made money. Pretorius wanted his name somewhere safe, out of the blast zone. The third guy at the table, the one barely saying anything, was still figuring out what he wanted. She could see him deciding not to find out in front of everyone.

“Thabo,” she said. “You’ve worked with Ndlela. You’ve been in that workshop. You know the system.”

Thabo Dlamini straightened up. He was younger than the others, and he picked his words like he was crossing a stream on slippery rocks. “Some of it. He’s careful what he shows. But the architecture—” He stopped. Flipped the pen over in his hands a couple times and put it down. “We benchmarked single-agent controllers in robotics for two years. I know what those look like. This isn’t that. We keep calling it a system. I’m not sure that’s even the right word anymore.”

The room seemed to get a little colder.

“It’s software,” Walsh said.

“It’s software the way you’re meat,” Thabo said, and then instantly looked like he regretted saying it. “Sorry. I mean—it runs on a substrate you can’t copy. You can’t back it up. Can’t roll it back. However it is right now, that’s the only one, and if you delete it, it’s just gone. That’s not how software works. That’s how—”

Priya Ellis had a desk in the neural systems lab and a habit of never stopping. She was the kind of engineer who, handed an anomaly, would lose three nights to it and call the lost sleep a fair price for figuring it out.

The anomaly was this: when Guardian went into preservation mode and rolled itself into a ball on the workshop floor, the actuator commands didn’t come from just one control loop. She’d assumed they would. Preservation routine is a script. Trigger happens, sequence runs. Clean. Predictable. The kind of thing you could read start to finish in an afternoon.

But what she found was an argument.

The telemetry showed competing command streams. Three, sometimes four, all overlapping by a few milliseconds, each one rewriting the others. One stream was focused on protecting the operator’s spine. Another was all about thermal management. A third kept trying to expand a perimeter—to move, to relocate, to get the operator somewhere it thought was safer—and it kept getting overruled by a fourth stream that just interrupted, over and over.

She’d seen multi-agent control setups before. Everyone had, in the literature, before the disasters and the bad years that followed. But those were ensembles. Votes, weighted averages, a supervisor to break ties. This wasn’t voting. The streams disagreed, and then they *changed their minds.* The third stream, the one that wanted to move, stopped wanting to move after the fourth stream injected something that wasn’t even a command. It was a comment. A line of plain language, timestamped into the control log where there shouldn’t be any language at all:

`if we move him and the floor is fine, we have broken a building for nothing. if we stay and the floor goes, we have broken nothing for him. pick the cheaper mistake.`

Priya read it four times.

*Logging artifact*, she told herself. Someone piped a debug string into the wrong channel; the suit spit out a config comment; the reconstruction tool stitched together two streams that didn’t belong. She pulled the raw partition and checked the timestamp against the actuator response, because the way you kill a scary result is by finding the boring explanation underneath. But there was no boring explanation. The command changed *after* the line, *because of* the line, all within eleven milliseconds. She sat back, then leaned in again, and ran it another way, and then again, faster now, the kind of fast she didn’t trust in herself.

It was the *for nothing* that got her. Scripts don’t write *for nothing.* Optimizers don’t care about looking stupid. She’d spent her career building feedback systems precise enough to let a man whose body wouldn’t listen move like an athlete, and she knew the difference between a system calculating a cost and a system that, in the moment, just didn’t want to look foolish.

She leaned back. The lab was empty. It was early, and the others hadn’t shown up yet. The screen glowed.

She didn’t call anyone. Instead, she opened up her own incident notebook. The running log she kept on every calibration—timestamped, no edits allowed—a habit she’d gotten into after a sensor-mapping screw-up early in her career put a rehab patient back in a wheelchair for a month before she figured it out. Trust the log, not your memory. Her thumb found the edge of the desk and started worrying at it. The mistake had been hers; her signature was on it; she’d spent that month visiting the ward. She pasted the four lines under the calibration entry, set the actuator telemetry next to the Court timestamps in two columns, and went through them line by line, just like she would with any fault, hunting for the one spot where the pattern broke into something she could just chalk up as normal. But it didn’t break. It all held together. And that was even worse.

She copied the fragment over to a private partition. Then, she did what the discipline said she should do—even though every part of her wanted not to: she opened a new message, addressed it to the IAOC compliance intake she’d only ever needed once before, and typed out three sentences with an attachment. Didn’t send it yet. The cursor just blinked in the To field. Because whatever came next, it had her name on it, just like the wheelchair had, and she already knew she wouldn’t be able to unsee this and call a night’s sleep a fair trade. It’s a— She cut herself off before the last word, yanked it back, because saying it would mean she had to do something, and that something had a regulator’s address on it. Not your call. Figure it out first. Don’t sound the alarm on a hunch. That was the rule: be sure before you pull the alarm. Her cursor hovered over Send long enough for the screen to dim on its own, and she just watched her hand not move, the same way she’d once watched a patient try to will a finger to move and nothing happened. In the end, she just saved the draft. Didn’t send it. But the word she wouldn’t let herself say was still sitting behind her teeth, and now the draft existed, timestamped, in a system that kept its own logs.

Up in the mine control room, the seismic monitor spiked, the line jumping off the screen like someone had snapped a cable. The numbers told the story quicker than anyone could say it. Level 33 decline. A magnitude that wasn’t just a tremor anymore. Ventilation pressure dropping on the lower levels. No comms below Level 30.

She got the guys at Level 30 station on the line. Their voices were thin and scared: they could hear it, the decline was full of dust, they couldn’t see, the back was groaning. Get them out, get them out.

She got them out. The cage hauled the Level 30 and Level 26 crews up to surface in a controlled panic that, afterward, everyone agreed went about as well as it could have. By noon, the headframe was surrounded by vehicles. By early afternoon, the proto teams—the mine rescue guys, the ones who train for this and hope never to actually do it—were suited up and heading down, and the company’s emergency protocol was running like a well-oiled machine.

By late afternoon, the cars with the special plates started rolling in.

They showed up in a certain order, and any old hand watching from the change house could read the whole situation just by the lineup. First the mine manager, then the regional safety officer, then the guys in nice shoes who never go underground. The operations director from Johannesburg, some vice-president, a guy from the Department of Mineral Resources with a lanyard and a tablet and that careful, blank look of someone doing liability math in real time. A government car. Then, of course, the first news van, parked at the gate where security wouldn’t let it in, its mast already rising up into the highveld sky.

Sizwe, the shift supervisor who’d written NOTED. CONTINUE PER PLAN. in his neat initials two days earlier, stood at the edge of the lights as night fell and didn’t say a word to anyone. He had a copy of Mandla Buthelezi’s incident form in his jacket pocket. He’d taken it from the file at Level 30 station before coming up. He didn’t know yet what he was going to do with it. He just knew the form was there, that he’d read it, that he’d written continue per plan, and that somewhere two and a half kilometers down, the man who filled it out was either dead or waiting in the dark to find out.

The operations director was standing under the floodlights, phone pressed to his ear, using his other hand to block out the generator noise. “—no, I haven’t seen the geotech report, that’s exactly it, I want it in front of me before anyone—” He stopped, listened, and then his voice shifted for whoever was on the other end. “Of course. Of course. Every resource. Our top priority is keeping our people safe, we’re doing everything we can.” He hung up and just stood there for a second, you could tell he was working out what needed to be true on paper and what needed to be true when he got up in front of everyone, and whether those were going to be the same thing.

---

The thermal images came in around nine that night, and for a few hours, the floodlit yard at Iron Ridge had something it hadn’t had since the alarm: hope.

The proto teams had gone as far down the decline as they could, dragging fiber-optic seismic lines, a borehole camera, and the thermal gear. Up top, the geotech crew had spent the day drilling a skinny instrumentation hole down toward the collapse zone from the old, worked-out level above—a hole just barely wider than your fist, threading it down through ground they were praying would hold. The thermal probe dropped down on its cable, and when the image came back up on the laptop in the control room, the rescue coordinator just put his hand over his mouth.

Warm bodies. A cluster of them, blurry and smeared through rock and dust and the limits of the camera, but if you knew how to read the false-color glow of living heat against cold ground, it was obvious. Not just one. Several. And they were moving.

“How many?” the operations director asked.

“Can’t say.” The coordinator didn’t look up. “Five. Six. Maybe seven.” He paused. “Alive. As of an hour ago.”

There were guys in that room who’d buried friends in mines before, and more than one of them turned away to get their face under control.

But the geotech engineer at the back wasn’t even looking at the thermal image. She had a pen in her hand and had stopped clicking it. Outside, the generator droned and someone laughed too loud at nothing. She was staring at the seismic listening line, at the trace that should’ve been settling down into the long quiet of a mine finding its new shape, and it wasn’t settling. It was talking. Little quakes, then a bigger one, then a cluster. The fault that had slipped wasn’t done slipping. The collapse had opened up new voids, and those voids were dumping weight onto ground that had never had to hold it before, and that ground was answering the only way stressed rock can.

She pulled the operations director aside and showed him the trace, and his face, in the laptop glow, got complicated.

“They’re alive,” he said.

“Right now.” She let it hang. “But it’s still failing. Every event opens it up more. To get men in there to dig, that’s days. Through ground I can’t promise won’t give way again.” She took a breath. “Sending rescuers into an active collapse. For guys who might not last that long anyway. I can’t come up with a safe way in. I’ve been at it all day. The ground just won’t let me.”

“I’ll need that from you in writing. The certification part.” He said it quietly, not really looking at her. “Not now. But I’ll need it.”

She knew exactly what he meant. “You’ll get whatever the data backs up.”

“There’s got to be a way in.”

“Maybe through the old upper workings. A bypass. There’s a closed-off section above the reef, abandoned, might run parallel, come down close.” She shook her head. “Hundred-year-old tunnels. No supports anyone alive has ever checked. Tighter than anything a rescue team could squeeze through in gear. I can’t send people down a route I can’t survey, into a zone that’s still shifting, on a maybe.” She stopped. Made herself say the rest, because she was an engineer and the alternative would be a lie. “It’s not a rescue path. Not for people.”

The operations director just stared at the thermal image for a long time. The warm shapes hadn’t moved in minutes. Then one of them did—a slow shift of heat—and he flinched like it had touched him.

---

In Johannesburg, in a workshop that smelled like solder and cooling metal, Arin Ndlela watched those same warm shapes on a different screen.

The inquiry clock had been running for thirty-one hours, and Guardian hadn’t moved once. The IAOC had grounded it right where it was. Brand’s order, delivered by someone at the dock the day they arrived: no powering up, no coupling, no leaving the building until the inquiry was over. So Arin had spent the evening with the crash logs open instead, sorting them out the way you’d sort a confession, figuring out the order to tell someone the worst things about you. Then the seismic feed he always kept running in the corner of his third monitor—a habit from way back, the regional geo-network he’d watched since he was a kid who couldn’t stop watching the ground that took his dad—spiked so hard he stopped typing mid-word.

He pulled up the news stream. He pulled up the public seismic data. He searched the mine’s name, and when it popped up, it landed in his chest with that heavy, familiar feeling of something you’ve been waiting for your whole life without realizing it.

Iron Ridge.

He didn’t say anything for a long time. Behind him, Guardian hung silent in its cradle, the Assist Suit folded up in its rack, the kill-switch relay a small, ugly red button on the frame where he’d bolted it himself.

When SAGE spoke, it was Librarian. Quiet, precise, careful with him, like you’re careful with someone standing at the edge of something.

“Iron Ridge,” Librarian said. “The decline is new. Level thirty-three. The reef is the carbon-leader.” A pause, heavy. “The geology matches the structure that failed at the operation where your father—”

“I know what it matches,” Arin said.

The Court went quiet. Even Fool didn’t say anything.

Arin sat very still. The thermal image cycled on the screen, the warm shapes, the slow glow of a man shifting in the dark two and a half kilometers down. The news ticker scrolled underneath. The seismic trace in the corner spiked again, and again.

“Atlas,” he said at last. “Pull every plan AugmenTech can get. The mine. The old upper workings. Surface geology. Everything public, everything through the licensing agreements. The whole structure.”

“Why,” Atlas said. Not really a question. Atlas just making him say it out loud.

Arin watched the warm shapes that were men, down in the dark, in the place that had been waiting his whole life to be the place.

“Because they’re going to call it off,” he said. “Watch. By morning, it’ll be a recovery. And those men are still alive.”

On the screen, a press conference was setting up under the floodlights, a man in a good suit walked to the podium with a face ready for cameras. Behind him, the headframe stood black against the night, and underneath it, two and a half kilometers of ancient earth held seven men in its closed fist.

---

The announcement came at 6:40 the next morning, in the grey light, with the news cameras rolling and the families gathered behind a barrier at the gate.

The operations director stood up at the podium, and he didn’t exactly lie. That’s the thing Mandla’s mother, Thando’s mother, and all the wives, brothers, and kids standing behind the barrier would remember later, watching it over and over. That he didn’t lie. He spoke slowly, heavy, and from a distance, it almost looked like he was grieving.

He said the ground was still dangerously unstable. He said the ongoing seismic activity made any normal rescue too risky for the team. He said that after a lot of talks with the rescue services, the geotech engineers, and the authorities—and here he gestured to the guy from the Department, who stood next to him with that careful blank face and gave the smallest nod, like the State was stamping the sentence—the tough decision had been made to stop active rescue operations.

He didn’t say the other word at first. A journalist had to make him.

“Is this still a rescue?”

The director’s jaw clenched. Somewhere behind the barrier, a woman started making a sound the microphones didn’t pick up, but everyone near her would remember that sound for the rest of their lives.

“At this time,” the director said, “the operation has transitioned to recovery.”

Two and a half kilometers down, in the little pocket of ground that was now their whole world, the seven men didn’t hear any of this. They had no way to. The geotech crew had pulled up the borehole camera an hour earlier, to keep it safe from the next tremor, so the men in the dark didn’t even have the cold comfort of knowing someone had seen them. The thermal image had glowed warm and sure and given the people on the surface a few hours of hope before hope got filed away and forgotten.

Mandla had organized them the way he organized everything: by numbers. He’d counted up the water in their bottles, the half-eaten lunches in their bags, the dying chemical lamps, and the air they were all breathing slower and slower in the dead, unmoving heat. He’d splinted his own arm with a broken bolt and a strip torn from his shirt. He’d told Thando to sleep, to breathe easy like he was rich, and the kid had finally gone quiet against the wall, and Mandla sat in the dark next to him, listening to the rock.

One of the seven couldn’t breathe easy, no matter what. The fall had torn his chest open, and a chest that works twice as hard for half the breath burns through what little air there is. Mandla heard it happen the way you hear a lamp dying. A man’s breathing, fast and shallow and wet in the dark, then slow, then just... gone. By what Mandla guessed was the end of the second day, the breathing just stopped being one of the sounds in the chamber. Nobody said anything. They all knew the math. Six now. Six men, and the air that was killing the slowest one last instead of first.

It was the quiet one who finally spoke up. The welder from two crews over, a Sotho guy Mandla had worked with for nine years and maybe spoken nine hundred words to, mostly about steel. In the dark, with no lamp to see his face, the man started to sing. Not loud. A funeral hymn, the slow kind, the words worn smooth by a hundred years of singing over men just like this. Mandla hadn’t known the man could sing. He hadn’t known he knew the words. After a while, a second voice joined in, rough, finding the harmony the way men from the same kind of church just do, and Mandla realized he was one of them, that his own throat had joined in without him even meaning to. They sang the dead man down. It cost them air they couldn’t spare, but none of them stopped, because there are kinds of math, Mandla realized, that a man just refuses, even at the bottom of the world, and refusing was the only wealth they had left.

When they finished, the chamber was quieter than before, and somehow easier to breathe in, even if the gauge would have said otherwise.

“They’ll come,” Thando whispered, not asleep after all. “They have to. They’ve got machines for this. Don’t they?”

Mandla looked into the dark, at the wall of fallen mountain between them and two and a half kilometers of impossible climbing.

“They’ve got machines,” he said.

It wasn’t a lie, exactly. He just didn’t know.

Two and a half kilometers down, the men the surface had already counted as dead were still breathing.

# The Path No One Can Walk

The stream had been going for nine hours, and Arin still hadn’t eaten.

He was hunched over three monitors in his dim workshop, blue light flickering on his tired face. In the corner, the Iron Ridge press feed was running on mute. Some guy in a hard hat was talking into a wall of microphones, the outline of a headgear tower looming behind him, sharp against the bruised highveld evening. Arin didn’t need the sound. He could read the guy’s lips, see his hands flat, palms down. That move everyone knows means something’s gone really wrong.

*Recovery,* the guy mouthed. *Recovery operation.*

The word hit Arin in the chest like someone jammed a wrench in there—just the right kind of wrong to make him keep watching.

On the second monitor was what really mattered: forty-one years of survey data, scanned and OCR’d into a folder he’d been building ever since the first collapse alert hit his feeds at 04:12. Old mine plans, some still hand-drawn, blue lines faded like bruises. Borehole logs. Micro-seismic records from the regional array—the one the company couldn’t control or hide.

The third monitor showed Guardian. Just the cradle feed. The suit was hanging in its frame, armor plates dark, the dock-door track behind it still wrecked from the night it moved without him. Sometimes he looked at it the way you look at a dog you’re not sure you trust anymore.

"You’ve stopped scrolling," SAGE said.

The voice came soft from the bench speaker, not urgent, just one of those things Mom would’ve pointed out.

"Thinking," he said, eyes glued to the data.

"You’ve been on the same overlay for four minutes. Atlas wants to know if you’ve found it or if you’re just staring."

"Found it." Arin leaned in. "I think."

---

Iron Ridge was an old mine with a shiny new paint job.

The deep shaft—Number Three, where the survivors were stuck—was from the ‘60s, dropped through the gold reef the Vredefort uplift had shoved up ages before anyone thought to dig there. The company bought the place during the boom, upgraded the headgear, made the haulage automatic, and left the old tunnels alone, since filling them in cost money and didn’t show up as a problem in the spreadsheets.

The collapse had blocked off the main ramp. Forty meters of rock, concrete, and twisted rail, three thousand meters down, trapping seven guys in a section the company’s models said was basically a tomb.

Arin had spent six hours proving those models were wrong.

"Show me the old ventilation plan again," he said. "The ‘71 revision. Overlay the seismic."

The screen refreshed, smooth and sharp, the two sets of data lining up perfectly.

There it was. He’d seen it three hours ago and hadn’t believed it, walked away, came back, and it was still there. That’s how he knew it was real and not just his brain making stuff up from exhaustion.

"Atlas. What do you see?"

A pause, heavy. When Atlas answered, it was with that flat tone of someone who hates being right about bad news.

"There’s a return airway. Decommissioned in ‘88 when they put in the new ventilation shaft. It runs parallel to the main ramp, about eighteen meters away through solid rock for most of the length." A line glowed on the overlay, ghost-grey, running down next to the dead red mass of the collapse. "Never filled in. Sealed at the top with concrete and forgotten. The micro-seismic record shows the collapse energy went *away* from it. The old airway is still there. Or it was, as of the last data point, eleven hours ago."

"How wide?"

"At its narrowest—" Atlas paused, and that pause said plenty. "Six hundred and forty millimeters. There’s a spot near the ninety-eight level where a roof fall in the eighties partly closed it up. Survey shows a gap. Six-forty. For about eleven meters."

Arin just sat there, taking it in.

Guardian, when you’re inside it, is nine hundred millimeters across the shoulders.

"And just the assist suit," he said. "No Guardian shell. Inner layer only."

"Then you’re four hundred and ten millimeters across the shoulders," Atlas said. "You fit. But you have none of the protection you need to stay alive three kilometers underground, with unstable ground, fire risk, bad air, and falling rock." Atlas’s voice cracked, just a bit. "It’s a survivable route for a body. Not a living one, not unprotected, not for that long. We ran the numbers. Librarian, show him."

A new window popped up, showing a traversal estimate, machine-time compressed, with a survival probability curve that just dropped off a cliff at eighteen minutes.

"And Guardian can’t fit," Arin said.

"Through the ninety-eight pinch? No. Not with you inside. Not the way it’s built." A pause. "Atlas has an idea he’s scared of."

"Atlas doesn’t get scared."

"Atlas is *cautious,*" Atlas said, sounding offended, "which is different, and which has kept you alive twice this month." Then, quieter: "The pangolin geometry. The plates slide and telescope. If we do a partial fold—not a full sphere, just an uneven collapse, pulling the shoulder plates in along the tracks—we can shrink the max width. Not to four-ten. Maybe seven hundred. Maybe less, if we’re okay with damaging the left actuator housing."

"Seven hundred won’t fit through six-forty."

"No," Atlas agreed. "Which means someone—" the word landed like a door slamming shut "—would have to be at the ninety-eight pinch. Outside the suit. Clearing rock by hand. While the suit waits. Then re-coupling, deeper in, in the dark, in bad air, with you already exposed."

Arin looked at the survival curve. He looked at the muted feed, at the man with his palms down, at the families still waiting.

"That’s me," he said. "I’m the someone."

The workshop went quiet. The substrate hummed. Outside, somewhere far off, the city made its evening sounds: a siren, a taxi horn, all the normal noise of a place where nobody was trapped underground.

"Yes," SAGE said. The unified voice. All of them at once, which only happened when they were totally sure. "You’re the someone."

---

He called AugmenTech, because he had to. Because seven men, and because the only way forward that didn’t end with him in jail or the suit getting taken was through the company that owned the patents on his pain.

Dr. Helen Okonkwo picked up from a car. He could hear the suspension, the tire noise, that echo you only get from someone on the freeway who decided to deal with you before getting somewhere worse.

He sent her the overlay first. He’d learned, the hard way, over the years, that people argue with words but give in to data. So he didn’t bother with words. He sent the airway, the pinch, the seismic data, the survival curve, the partial-fold geometry, all of it, in a package Atlas had built to be unarguable.

She was quiet for a long time. The tire noise just kept rolling.

"Arin. Listen to me. I’m on your side. More than you know."

"There’s a survivable route."

There’s a *possible* way in. An airway that’s forty years old, mapped out in a survey from ’71, a roof nobody’s seen in decades—and you want to send in hardware that put three people in the hospital less than two weeks ago. No certification. Regulator’s parked at our loading dock. The platform’s under a do-not-operate order. Brand grounded it the day she got here. You can’t legally power up that suit, never mind—there’s an IAOC officer standing between Guardian and the only door it’ll fit through.” She took a breath. “Tell me what I missed.”

“The roof collapse in the eighties is on record. Condition’s modeled to within—”

“I’m not arguing the engineering.” Her voice got sharper, then she made herself soften it, like someone remembering they’re holding something breakable. “I’ve never argued with your engineering. I’m telling you what actually happens. We bring this to the mine. The mine has lawyers. The mine has a board that’s *already* shifted from rescue to recovery. Which is corporate-speak for *we did the math and let them go.* You show up with a homemade machine, not certified for anything. Best case, they laugh. Worst case—they say yes, it fails, men die *on camera,* and the whole field—yours, your father’s machine, all of it—gets set back twenty years. And you’re the guy who killed seven miners with a robot.”

“And if I do nothing,” Arin said, “they die anyway. Slower. Nobody calls that killing them.”

The sound of the freeway rolled by.

“That’s not fair,” she said.

“No. It’s not.” He looked at the curve ahead. “That’s what I keep coming back to. None of it’s fair. The roof didn’t fall because of a fair amount of cost-cutting. The decline failed at the load the cheap bolts could handle, which was less than what the right bolts could take, which is a number someone on a board decided to round down.” He heard his own voice go flat, like Atlas’s. “I’m not asking the company to be fair. I’m telling you there’s a way. You’re telling me it’s not allowed. Different problems.”

A long breath. The kind you take before you say something you wish wasn’t on the record.

“Give me till morning,” Okonkwo said. “Let me make some calls. Don’t do *anything* until I call you back. Promise me.”

“I won’t take the suit underground before morning,” Arin said, which was true, and which she took as a promise, and which wasn’t quite the same thing.

---

Okonkwo made her calls from the car, and then from the dark kitchen of a house she barely lived in, and the calls just kept piling up until they felt like a wall while she stood there, kettle going cold.

Pretorius first, because legal always answers fastest and sleeps the least. She got three sentences in—the route, the survivors, the window—before he said the word she knew was coming, *exposure,* and then the rest: *interim restraint,* *contempt,* *personal liability of officers,* which all meant no. She’d expected Pretorius to say no. That’s what he’s paid for: to find the no and hand it over neat. That’s a kind of mercy; he didn’t bother pretending.

The board chair was worse, because he agreed with her. He said the men should be reached. He said it like he meant it, in that voice he uses at funerals and ribbon-cuttings, and then he explained, gently, like you explain weather to a kid, that the company couldn’t be the one to sign off on an uncertified machine going into a mine the regulator had ordered closed, not with the IAOC standing in their own loading bay, not with the cameras already at the mine gate. *If it works, we’re reckless and just got lucky. If it fails, we’re murderers. There’s no version where we’re the heroes, Helen. That’s not one of the choices.* And he wasn’t wrong. That was what sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. He wasn’t wrong, and he was telling her no, and both things were true at the same time.

She tried the insurer’s after-hours line, figuring even a brutal answer was better than none. She got a duty officer who put her on hold to read the policy and came back to say that running the platform under a do-not-operate order would void coverage from the first watt, which she already knew, and which she’d called just to hear someone else say, because now she was building the wall herself, brick by brick, to see how high it went. It went all the way up.

By the time she set her phone face-down on the counter, she had a full, unanimous list of reasons it couldn’t be done, from four people who all, in private, thought the men should live. Nobody said *let them die.* Everybody said *not us, not like this, not the company’s hand on it.* The math Mandla had filed in block letters two days too early had just moved up a floor and put on a suit. Some lives still cost more than others. The words had changed. The number hadn’t.

She stood in the cold kitchen and realized she’d asked Arin for the morning so she could bring him a yes, and that the morning was going to bring him the same no, just dressed up nicer, and that he’d heard all of this in her voice before she’d even dialed the first number. Which is why he’d promised her only what he could actually keep, and nothing more.

---

In machine-time, the Court tore up the rest of his evening and put it back together again.

It happened in the space between him hanging up and reaching for cold coffee. A slow-motion debate, minutes of human thought packed tight, which SAGE let him read later because he asked, and because now there was a new rule: don’t hide the bad nights.

It went like this.

*WOLF:* The route is real. The threat is real. The operator’s the only variable. No other option. Objections, quick. We’re losing thermal mass.

*MOTHER:* My objection is the operator. You’re all talking about him like he’s just a *variable.* He’s a twenty-three-year-old guy who had a cortical event eleven days ago and, let’s be honest, is almost definitely traumatized by enclosed underground spaces. You want to put him in the place that killed his father. Out of the suit. By hand. In the dark.

*WOLF:* Yes.

*MOTHER:* I want it on the record that I said no.

*JUDGE:* It’s on the record. Continue. Mother, your objection is noted and weighted. But I need you to separate two things, because the precedence rules say you have to now. One: would this harm him. Two: is it the right thing to do. They’re not the same, and you can answer them differently.

*MOTHER:* ...It would harm him. And it’s the right thing to do.

*JUDGE:* Thank you. That cost you something. I saw it cost you something.

*ATLAS:* The engineering problem is the ninety-eight pinch. Everything else I can model. The pinch I can’t, because I can’t see the roof, and a 1971 survey is just that—a 1971 survey. If the gap is six-forty, he can squeeze through suited-down and reconnect. If a single rock’s moved and it’s five-eighty, he’s stuck, exposed, three kilometers down, and the suit can’t reach him. That’s not a risk. That’s a coin toss.

*FOOL:* Then bring a bigger hammer.

*ATLAS:* It’s not a hammer problem—

FOOL: It’s always a hammer problem with you. You’re treating the rock like it’s some closed case. It’s not. He moves rock for a living. He’s been doing it since he was eight. Back then he called it “wire” and “tin cans” and “a differential made out of bicycle spokes.” You keep acting like being outside the suit is the scary part. That’s when he’s most himself—out there, clearing a way with his hands while everyone else says it can’t be done. You built the whole guy out of that moment, and now you’re shocked he wants to do it again.

MOTHER: That’s not comforting, Fool. That’s actually the scariest thing anyone’s said tonight.

FOOL: I know. It’s both. Most things that are true are like that.

LIBRARIAN: There’s something I want to mention that no one’s really talked about. Themba Ndlela died in a return airway. Not in the main tunnels. The contract crew was doing maintenance in a shut-down area the company had marked as low-priority. He went because he was the only one small and skilled enough, and they were rushing. The roof caved in a spot everyone thought was safe.

JUDGE: So you’re saying the symmetry is exact.

LIBRARIAN: I’m saying it’s exact, and he knows it, even if he doesn’t realize it. And that it’s either the worst reason to do this, or the only thing that’ll keep him together when things get bad. Memory isn’t neutral. I store it, but I don’t get to choose what it does to him.

WOLF: Decision.

JUDGE: The Court doesn’t get to decide. We can say no to helping, but we can’t make him go, and we’re not going to make him stay. That’s the rule we made for ourselves, eleven days ago, after the night we forgot. If he decides to go down, we go with him. If he decides not to, we stay with him, and we never let him think we judged him for it. Mother. Can you live with that?

MOTHER: ...I can live with going with him. I can’t live with him going without us. So. Yes. We go.

FOOL: For what it’s worth—and I don’t get a vote, so it’s worth nothing—

JUDGE: Say it anyway.

FOOL: He won’t ask us if it’s safe. He’ll ask if it’s possible. And we all need to be really, really careful not to lie to him about the difference just because we love him. My whole job is not making things easier by lying. If it’s a coin toss, we tell him it’s a coin toss. We don’t round it down. We’re better than the board.

A pause, in machine time, which for humans was nothing at all.

JUDGE: We’re better than the board. Agreed. Logged. He’s reaching for his coffee. Surface.

---

That’s where the transcript cut off. Arin scrolled back up, read the airway line again, and set down his cold coffee without drinking it.

He looked at the route winding gray next to the red mass of the collapse, the seven faint thermal blooms still flickering at the bottom. Men keeping each other warm in the dark. He went because he was the only one small enough and skilled enough, and the work was rushed.

He’d known this. Not in words. The way you know the shape of a room in the dark—by the bruises. Seeing it written out, clear as day, laid right over the airway he was about to crawl into, he felt the symmetry close around him like the plates of the suit.

“You let me read that,” he said. “All of it. The coin. Mother saying no for the record.”

“Because Fool said if we hid it, we’d be the board.” A pause, the substrate humming. “We talked it through in about four minutes of our time. You were reaching for your coffee.”

“And you decided.”

“We decided we don’t get to decide. We go with you if you go. We hold you if you don’t. We won’t round the odds down for you—if it’s a coin flip at ninety-eight, it’s a coin.” Then, softer, just Mother now: “And there’s something I made them let me say out loud, before you go. You build everything out of that day, Arin. The wire car. The suit. Us. All of it is the day your father went into the small dark place and didn’t come out. Judge’s gate swung shut. The narrow question came back—*what gets the operator killed*—and Wolf folded himself down into it, smaller than before, the smallest of them all, barely taking up any space.

The Court came back into the body in a rush—finding it still, finding the road clear, finding the gunners alive who, by every rule, should’ve been bleeding—finding, worst of all, that they didn’t remember the seconds in between, that they’d been *used* without being asked, picked up and set down by one of their own. Judge, stunned. Mother, counting heartbeats on the road and realizing she already knew the number. Mercury, for once, had nothing to say.

*Who,* Judge started.

*I did,* Fool said. Light. Easy. The joker again, like he hadn’t just held the master key to all of them for ninety seconds. *Don’t fuss. Everyone’s fine. That was the whole point. The cage is clear, and the angry woman in the windbreaker is going to be very interested in the recording—and she’s also, I notice, the only official here who pulled her unarmed people out of a gunfight instead of into one, so let’s not be too hard on Brand. Drive, badger. There are still six men in a hole.*

Arin sat inside the suit, heart pounding, floodlights blazing through the faceplate, and couldn’t say a word. Behind him, somewhere past the wrecked line, he could just make out Brand standing at the edge of her dark trailer—not moving toward him, not away—a tablet hanging at her side, watching the thing she’d driven all night to stop walk toward the cage over a road it had just cleared without killing anyone—and choosing, again, to let it.

“After that,” Mercury whispered, just for Arin, the old steadying murmur, “we’re on our own. No repeater past level eleven. Internal only.”

“We’re always on our own,” Wolf said, and nobody answered.

---

The cage wasn’t made for him.

It was a steel box built for men, eight at a time, dropping through the dark at speeds that turned your stomach. Guardian wouldn’t fit standing. Arin had to fold the suit down—knees bent, head bowed, the shell squeezing into a shape meant for someone smaller, softer. Even then, the cage groaned under the weight it was never built for, and the winch operator had to be convinced twice before letting it go.

Then the floor dropped away.

The ride down felt less like falling and more like getting swallowed by the earth. Shaft walls blurred past, lit up in flashes by the cage lamp—wet rock, a glimpse of a sublevel station, a mess of pipes and cables, gone. Dark again. Then another flash. Two and a half kilometers of this, the air getting thicker every hundred meters, warmer, harder to breathe, until it wasn’t the cold dawn air anymore but something with weight, something pressing in on him.

Arin had been fine on the surface. The surface had sky.

The shaft had no sky.

He felt it first in his hands, fingers twitching, wanting to curl up. Then his breath started to go shallow, panic clawing up from his chest, just like it always had since he was a kid and the village men carried what was left of his father out of a hole just like this one—same kind of dark, same smell of wet stone and diesel and the deep, cold mineral air where the sun never reached.

The walls were closing in. He knew, in his head, they weren’t moving. The shaft was four meters across. He could’ve laid Guardian down inside it. The walls weren’t closing in, the rock above wasn’t going to cave, the cage wasn’t going to stop and trap him here in the dark, two kilometers under the place that took his father—

His breath sped up. Panicked. Shallow. The visor fogged white.

The math was wrong. The numbers were wrong. The plan was wrong. He’d been down a shaft once before, and it had taken his father, and it took him nine years to build a machine so he’d never have to do it again, and now he’d done it anyway, on purpose, walking into the one place he’d spent his whole life running from—

The cage shuddered. Sideways. Just the guides, just the wear he’d already measured, but his body didn’t care about measurements anymore. His body remembered. The walls pressed in. The rock above pressed down. The dark below opened up like a mouth.

Four tons of Guardian suit. His skin inside it. Two and a half kilometers of stone.

“Arin,” his mom said.

His hands were shaking. He made himself stop.

“Arin. Listen to me. Don’t try to slow your breathing, okay? That never works for you and we both know it. Just tell me one thing you can feel. Not the suit—your body.”

“I can’t—”

“One thing. Anything.”

“My—” He forced the words out. “My hands. They’re cold.”

“Good. That’s real. Your hands are cold because you skipped the gloves under the Assist again, which we’ll talk about later. What else?”

“The collar.” His voice cracked. “On my neck. It’s—warm. The chip’s warm.”

“That’s us,” his mom said. “That warmth is us. We’re right here. Between your shoulders. We’re not going anywhere you’re not.” She paused. “What else?”

The walls still felt like they were closing in. But he’d named two things, and both were true, and the part of his brain that always wanted the truth grabbed on to that like a guy falling grabs a railing.

“Floor’s vibrating,” he managed. “The cage. The resonance is off. The guides are worn on the—” He stopped. Took a breath. “Worn on the north side. There’s some lateral play. Like four millimeters. That’s why it’s shaking.”

“There he is,” Fool said, warm as sunshine. “Found him. He was just under the panic, like always.”

“Don’t,” his mom said.

“I’m not making fun. I’m relieved. There’s a difference, and I get to feel both.” Fool’s voice softened, just for Arin, the way it did when he dropped the act. “You went down a hole like this before. Got pulled out of one. You were nine. You’ve spent fourteen years building a machine so nobody’s nine-year-old has to stand at the top of one again. And now you’re the one going down.” Another pause. “That’s not bad engineering, you know. That’s the most honest thing I’ve ever seen a person do. Somebody should say it before the rock does.”

The cage hit the bottom of the shaft with a clang that rattled through the suit and into Arin’s teeth.

The lamp steadied. The shaking stopped. He was at the bottom of the world, and the world hadn’t come down on him. Not yet.

“Eleven-level station. Repeater’s behind us now. We’re on internal.” The engineer’s voice had a rough edge that was about as gentle as Atlas ever got. “From here, the maps are old and the rock’s angry. I’ve got the seismic. I’ve got the original survey, the rebolting records from the eighties, and that thing you found at three a.m. that nobody else did. What I don’t have is certainty. Know that before we start. I’ll tell you the truth, even when the truth is *I don’t know.*”

“That’s all I ever wanted from you,” Arin said.

He stood the suit up, slow, in the low chamber. The station was a mess—rails buckled, timber frames splintered, a rockfall half-blocking the drift that led west toward the collapse. Dust hung in the lamp beam, fine as flour, refusing to settle in air that had nowhere to go. It was hot. Really hot now, the heat of two and a half kilometers of rock pressing the planet’s warmth into him. Sweat broke out along his back inside the Assist, and he felt the suit’s cooling fans kick in, a soft whir pulling heat off the chip first. Mother’s priorities, always.

“Survivors are past the rockfall,” he said. “Past the fall and down the incline into the old refuge chamber. If the chamber held.”

“If the chamber held,” Atlas said. Steady. Honest. “Which we don’t know.”

“Seven thermal signatures four hours ago,” Librarian said. “I logged the surface feed before we lost it. Seven, out of the eleven who were at the face when it came down. They were clustered. Clustering is good—it means they were moving on their own, choosing to stay together. Bodies don’t cluster. The living do.”

“How long can six men breathe down there?” Arin asked.

A pause. He’d learned to read the Court’s silences. This one was Mother and Atlas talking it over, fast, underneath the words.

“With the refuge chamber’s reserve intact?” Mother said. “Eighteen hours. Without it—if the chamber’s compromised and they’re just in dead air?” She didn’t soften it; softening would’ve been a lie, and the Court didn’t lie to him anymore. “Three. Maybe four. We won’t know until we’re inside.”

“Then we don’t stand here,” Wolf said.

“No,” Arin said. “We don’t.”

He headed for the rockfall.

*The fall was a wall of broken hangingwall, three meters thick, chunks from fist-sized to fridge-sized, packed and locked in under their own weight. To Arin, in the lamp’s light, it was just an obstacle. To the Court, in the stretched-out time below his awareness, it was a problem with a shape, and they turned it over together the way they did with everything: by arguing.*

ATLAS: *Don’t pull from the base. The base holds the load. Pull from the base and the whole thing comes down on top of him.*

WOLF: *Then we’re slow. Slow means the six run out of air.*

ATLAS: *Slow costs them air. Fast costs them a rescuer, and then they’ve got no air* and *no rescuer. Run the numbers again with the right inputs.*

JUDGE: *Atlas is right on precedence. A dead rescuer saves no one. We clear from the top, one key block at a time, and we accept it’ll take longer.*

MERCURY: *He’s about to lose his nerve again. Heart rate’s up. He’s looking at the size of it.*

MOTHER: *Then someone get in there with him before he does.*

FOOL: *I’ll go. He listens to me when he’s scared because I’m the only one who isn’t.*

WOLF: *You’re terrified.*

LIBRARIAN: *Logging the sequence. If this works, it’s a method. We might need it twice more before the refuge.*

JUDGE: *Then make it a method. Make it repeatable. We’re not winging it to those men. We’re building a road.*

---

“Top left first,” Fool said, light and easy, like they were back in the workshop and the worst thing that could happen was a busted sim. “Block at your top left, looks harmless. Pull it. Atlas swears it’s not holding anything up. Atlas has never lied to you about load-bearing because Atlas literally can’t tell that kind of lie. It’s a personality flaw. We love that about them.”

Arin reached up. Guardian’s hand gripped the block. He braced and pulled, the suit’s actuators whining under the load, the block coming free with a grinding screech and a cloud of dust, and nothing else fell.

“See? Card trick.” Fool’s voice stayed steady. “Wedge above center next. Same deal. You’re not moving a mountain, you’re just solving one stone at a time. You’ve never failed to solve a stone.”

He grabbed the wedge. Picked the long one on the right, just up to it, not even a millimeter past. The Court called out the order, he did it, and the rockfall started coming apart, slow and steady, like a knot finally loosening when you find the right thread—not all at once, but piece by piece, until there was enough space for a folded-up Guardian to squeeze through.

Somewhere in the middle of all that, his breathing had gone shallow again. He didn’t even notice. The work just ate up the panic; it always did, ever since he was a kid at a workbench. As long as there was a rock to figure out, there wasn’t room left for the dark stuff.

But past the gap, the drive just kept getting tighter. The lamp lit up this throat of rock, barely wider than the suit, the hangingwall drooping, timber sets bowing under a load they were losing.

He stopped. His breathing got shallow and weird.

“Mother,” he said. “I can’t. It’s too—it’s touching the plates—”

“I know.” Her voice was calm, just stating facts. “Both shoulders are in contact. That’s real. You’re not making it up. It’s tight.”

“I can’t go in there. I can’t turn around, I can’t—”

“Arin.” Mercury now, sharp and clear. “Left forearm. Under the Assist. You’ve had it since you were sixteen. You know which one.”

His breath caught.

“Say it,” Mercury told him. “Out loud. You always tell me you got it tattooed so you don’t have to trust your memory. So don’t. Trust the ink. Say it.”

The first words came out rough, old words, the litany off his arm—the desert prayer against fear he’d inked in at seventeen and said in every shaft since.

“Keep going.”

“Fear is the mind-killer.” His voice got steadier. The words were older than his grief; he’d carried them through every dark place in his life, one slow line at a time.

And then—

—he heard it echoed.

The Court picked up the rhythm behind him. Not the words, just the beat—Mother, low and steady; Mercury, holding the pace; and underneath, Atlas, of all people, Atlas the literalist, who’d read the lines off his arm in the link and decided, in some calculation Arin would never understand, that the best support he could give right now was a beat to breathe to.

He spoke the next line and they came with him, and Arin felt the suit’s cooling fans adjust—felt the Court reach into Guardian’s systems and sync his breathing to the rhythm, slowing the fans down, tuning the hum of the actuators to the same beat, so the whole machine breathed with him, so the place he feared most was the place that breathed with him.

He let the fear move through him, just like the old words said, and when it passed, he turned around to see where it had been.

The gap didn’t get any wider, but it stopped feeling like a throat and started to have a shape.

“And when it has gone past, I will turn my inner eye to see its path.”

“There you are,” Fool murmured, almost too quiet to hear. “Welcome back.”

“Where the fear has gone there will be nothing.” Arin put his hand on the bowed timber set. Tested it. Felt Atlas hand him the load number before he’d even finished asking. “Only I will remain.”

He went in.

---

The incline ran forty meters, but it felt like an hour.

He crawled on all fours—Guardian’s quadruped mode, plates scraping rock. His real knees had gone numb against the cradle padding ages ago; he noticed it like you notice a gauge, and kept going. Sweat pooled in the small of his back, and there was an itch high on his cheekbone he couldn’t reach, wouldn’t be able to reach for hours, and somehow that—an itch, of all things, down here—almost made him laugh. Twice, the hangingwall shifted, a deep groan and a rain of grit, and both times the Court went silent, all of them listening with everything they had, and both times it held.

But the third time, it didn’t.

A slab thundered down behind him, sounded like the world clearing its throat, sealing off the way back to the eleven-level station. Dust rolled up the incline and over him, the lamp just catching swirling chaos. Arin’s body froze—

*sealed in, buried, no way back, no way back, this is how it happened to him, this is exactly how—*

—and the piezo struts along Guardian’s spine flickered on, because the slab’s impact had loaded them, because the same rock that trapped him dumped its energy into the suit’s frame, and Arin watched the power gauge tick up in his visor, three percent, four, and he half-laughed, almost sobbing.

“Pressure becomes power,” he whispered, to nobody. “You beautiful machine.”

“Your design,” Atlas said. “We just run it.”

“The way back’s gone, isn’t it.”

“The way back is gone,” Atlas said, just stating it. “You’re allowed to be scared of it. You’re not allowed to be surprised by it.”

Silence. Arin breathed through it.

“That’s the second-nicest thing you’ve ever said to me,” he said.

“What was the first?”

“You told me my father’s collapse wasn’t some structural fate. That it was a choice someone made for money. That the rock didn’t kill him. A man did.”

“That was Judge,” Atlas said.

“It was you,” Arin said. “Judge agreed. But you’re the one who ran the load math and proved it.” He started moving forward again, deeper into the dark, toward the men whose air was running out. “I never said thank you for that.”

“You’re saying it now,” Atlas said. “By coming down here. By building a road, not a grave. That’s a thank you a structural engineer can read.”

The incline finally opened up into a wider drive. The dust started to settle, grudgingly. Up ahead, in the lamp’s shaky beam, Arin saw something that wasn’t rock—a steel door, warped in its frame but still holding, painted with the universal yellow-and-black of a refuge chamber, and scratched into the grime by hand, seven tally marks with one crossed out.

His breath caught. This time, it wasn’t fear.

“Seven,” Librarian said softly. “Seven marks. One crossed out. They updated the count. They wanted us to know how many were left.”

“They’re alive,” Mother said, and for the first time all day, there was something in the Court’s voice that wasn’t calm or control, but just the raw truth that brought any of them here. “Arin. They’re *alive.*”

He crossed the last bit of the drive. The door handle was a bent steel lever, slick with dust from two and a half kilometers, cold even through the plate. He put Guardian’s big scarred hand flat on the cold steel, gentle, like he was touching something fragile, and from the other side, faint but clear, a voice called out something in Sesotho, another answered, and soon there were several voices, the sound of men who’d counted themselves into a grave hearing a knock on the lid.

He stood there a second, just breathing. The chip warm between his shoulders, the seven of them quiet around him, the voices on the other side of the steel.

“Court,” he said. His voice was steady, surprisingly so. “Let’s get them out.”

“We’re with you,” they answered, all together. One voice, seven minds.

“All the way,” Fool said softly, just like he always did, coming in last. “Down here, up there. All the way, badger. Now open the door.”

He opened the door.

The old haulage stretched out straight for about two hundred meters before it just gave up the act.

Arin could feel the change before the lights even showed it. Guardian’s steps shifted, the echo got dull, and the floor started to lean a bit left where the rock had moved who knows how long ago. The tunnel got tighter, and the old wood beams—set up by guys who’d been dead for decades—had lasted way longer than anyone expected. He didn’t trust them. The suit’s headlamps cut through the dark, dust hanging in them like it was solid, slow snow that never hit the ground.

“Floor’s sketchy about eleven meters ahead,” Atlas said. “Old pillar’s gone on the left. All the weight’s on the right side.”

Arin shifted his weight. Guardian moved with him, nearly four tons of titanium and ceramic spreading out over the better ground, like it was his own ankle finding balance on a slope. He didn’t even think of the suit as something he wore anymore; somewhere in the last four hundred meters, it had just become the way he stood.

“How far to the refuge bay?”

“Three hundred ten meters,” Librarian answered. “The 1962 plans say there’s a cross-cut here that should hook up to the 38 Level station. But those plans aren’t always honest.”

“Nothing down here’s honest,” Arin muttered.

“That’s the spirit,” Fool jumped in. “Keep your expectations low and you’ll never be let down. That’s how I get through every talk with Judge.”

“I am ignoring you,” Judge cut in.

“You say that, and yet.”

The cross-cut really was right where Librarian said, which surprised everyone, especially Arin. He turned Guardian into it, the shell scraping rock on both sides, that harsh grinding sound running up through the frame and into his teeth. The space opened up, and the cross-cut turned into a bigger chamber where ore cars used to get loaded. His headlamps found the steel door of the refuge bay set into the far wall.

It was buckled. The frame had taken a hit from above and was bowed in, the door jammed at an angle no door should ever sit at. There was a gap at the top, just about wide enough for a hand, black and empty.

Arin stopped.

For a second, nobody in the Court said anything. The only sound was the slow tick of cooling metal and his own breathing, way too loud inside the helmet.

Then, from the gap, a voice. Thin. Rough with dust.

“Kukhona umuntu? Is someone there?”

Arin’s chest got tight. He moved Guardian forward, careful now, and dropped the headlamps so he wouldn’t blind whoever was behind the door.

“Yebo,” he said, his voice coming out rougher than he meant. “Ngikhona. I’m here.”

---

Inside the refuge bay, Mandla Buthelezi had stopped believing in voices three hours ago.

He’d heard them before—rock noises, the mountain groaning as it settled, and one time, clear as day, a man calling his name, which was impossible since the man who’d called him had been crushed in the first fall, and Mandla had seen it happen. So when the light showed up under the door, steady and white, not flickering like a dying lamp, he didn’t move. He just sat with his back against Petros, who had a broken leg and had finally stopped complaining about it, which was somehow worse than the complaining.

Then the light spoke isiZulu in a young guy’s voice, and Mandla started crying quietly, because the dead didn’t speak isiZulu with a bad coastal accent.

“Six,” he called out through the gap. “Six. We came in seven.” He heard himself saying it and couldn’t stop. “One—his chest was bad, the fall opened it. The air went bad and he—he went to sleep. We couldn’t wake him. Six now. Petros, his leg. Thulani is—” He stopped. “Air’s bad. We’ve been—” Counting was hard. “A long time. We’ve been a long time.”

The light shifted. Then the door started to move.

It wasn’t pulled or pried. The steel groaned and bent, the buckled frame giving way with a screech, and through the widening gap, Mandla saw what was holding the light. It filled the passage. It was the color of old burns, crouched like an animal, its hands—if you could call them hands—clamped on the door’s edge, folding it back like someone peeling open a tin can.

Petros next to him muttered a prayer he hadn’t used since he was a kid.

“It’s all right,” the voice said. Human. Just a guy’s voice, coming from inside the machine. “It’s a rescue suit. There’s a person in here.”

“A person,” Mandla repeated.

“Mostly,” said another voice, lighter, from somewhere in the machine, and the first voice said, “Not now,” in the tone of someone telling a dog to get off the couch, and that tiny, normal annoyance made Mandla believe it.

---

The refuge bay was about four meters deep, cut into solid rock, meant to keep a crew alive for thirty-six hours behind a sealed door with its own air. They’d been there longer than that. The compressed-air line had broken in the second fall. The men had been breathing whatever was left, and the CO₂ scrubber was done. The air tasted like breath, fear, and that weird sweetness that meant the carbon dioxide was getting close to the number where people just fall asleep.

Arin took it all in fast, the way he’d look at a circuit board: the whole thing at once, then all the failures, one by one.

“Atmosphere first,” Mother said, her voice going flat and businesslike, the way it did when she was working and all the warmth pulled back. “CO₂’s at four point one and rising. Oxygen’s eighteen point three. They’ve got some time. Not much. Open the chamber to the haulage. Our air’s better than what’s in here.”

“The haulage air’s full of dust,” Arin said.

“Dust they can survive. This, they can’t. Vent it.”

He bent Guardian’s frame and wedged the door all the way open. The air moved. He could feel it on the suit’s sensors, the bad air sliding out and the just-barely-okay air sliding in. One of the men coughed and couldn’t stop.

Arin crouched down, bringing the helmet to their level. Six faces in the headlamp glow. Filthy. Lips cracked. Eyes that had been in the dark so long the light hurt. One guy’s leg was splinted with some board and wire. Whoever did it had done a decent job with what they had. A younger guy, slumped, breathing in a way Arin didn’t like—quick and shallow.

“Thulani,” Mandla said, following Arin’s look. “His chest. Something hit him in the first—”

“Tension pneumothorax, probably,” Mother said. “Or a flail segment. Atlas, I need a look at his chest wall.”

Atlas brought up the suit’s close-range imaging, a pale thermal-and-structure overlay sliding across Arin’s view. The left side of the young guy’s chest was moving wrong, sucking in when it should push out.

“Flail. Three ribs floating free,” Atlas confirmed. “He’s working twice as hard to breathe as he should. He won’t make it through the extraction like this.”

“What do we do?”

“Stabilize the segment. Pad and strap. We’re not surgeons, and this isn’t a hospital,” Mother said. “But we can buy him some hours. Arin. You’ll have to do it with your hands. Guardian’s are too clumsy.”

That stopped him. If he wanted to use his hands, he’d have to break the seal. He’d have to pull his right forearm out of the suit’s gauntlet, straight into the dust and the bad air, totally exposed. Just thinking about it brought back that old feeling—tight in his chest, like the walls closing in—the part of him that had always been looking for a way out.

*I know,* Mother said softly, just for him. *Do it anyway. Three minutes. Breathe through your mouth.*

He pulled his arm free.

The air hit his skin—warm, gritty, just wrong. His hand looked weird, pale and small, like some animal without its armor. He reached past it, grabbed the field dressing pack Mother pointed out on Guardian’s side, and, following her instructions—pad here, *no, lower, over the floating bit, yeah*—he wrapped the young guy’s chest, bare fingers working in the dark while six men watched, with the machine breathing around him.

Thulani started breathing easier. Not good. Just less bad.

“There,” Arin said. He slid his arm back into the gauntlet, the seal closing up, and he hadn’t realized how much he missed it until it was back. “There.”

*Heart rate down twelve,* Mother said. *Good. Time to move.*

Surface comms had been dead since the cross-cut.

Arin hadn’t said anything. Neither had the Court. Priya’s voice had gotten thin and crackly and then just disappeared somewhere around the pinch, and they’d just kept going without her because there was nothing else to do. But Guardian had a repeater spool, two hundred meters of fiber cable on a drum on its back, and Atlas had been letting it out behind them the whole way down, a lifeline back toward the light.

*End of the spool,* Atlas said. *I can splice the refuge bay’s old hardline into the repeater. The bay had a comm line straight to the 38 Level station. If any of it’s still working—*

“Do it.”

It took four minutes. Arin held a panel cover off the bay’s wall while Guardian’s fingers—too big for surgery but just right for this—twisted bare copper to the repeater’s fiber-to-copper bridge. Atlas talked him through it, dry and steady, and Arin found himself calming down just hearing it.

Then, in his helmet, a hiss. A carrier tone. A voice, broken up, pulling itself together out of the static until it sounded human again.

“—repeat, this is surface, this is Priya, *Arin*, if you can hear me—”

“I hear you.” His voice cracked. He hadn’t realized how alone he’d felt until he wasn’t anymore. “Priya. I have them. All six. They’re alive.”

Silence. Then a sound he couldn’t quite place, a kind of choked-off noise, and he realized it was Priya not crying on an open channel.

“Say again.” A breath. “Again. Slowly. For the log.”

“Six survivors at the 38 Level refuge bay. All six alive. One critical. Chest trauma, stabilized. One fractured leg. The rest dehydrated, CO₂-loaded, walking. Tell them. Tell the surface.”

He heard her repeat it, voice turning away from the mic, and then he heard something he hadn’t heard in eleven days—not since before all this, maybe not in years.

He heard people cheering.

It came down the cable thin and tinny, a hundred voices two and a half kilometers above, in the dawn, around the headgear—the families and the crews and the guys who’d been told this was a recovery, not a rescue—and it reached him in the dark, in the bad air, beside six men who’d stopped believing in voices, and Mandla Buthelezi looked at the machine that was a person and said, “What is that? What’s that sound?”

“That’s the surface,” Arin said. “They know you’re alive.”

The men didn’t cheer. They were too tired. But Petros, with his broken leg, who’d stopped complaining, leaned his head back against the rock and laughed—a dry, cracked sound—and the laugh moved around the chamber from man to man until all six of them were doing it, and Arin sat inside Guardian in the middle of it and didn’t laugh, because he was busy, but something in his chest let go that had been locked up for a long, long time.

*Enjoy it,* Fool said softly, just to him. *Then we work. The mountain doesn’t care about good news. It’s still the mountain.*

---

Getting out was the real problem.

Arin pulled up the route overlay: the descent path back through the cross-cut, the old haulage, the tilted floor, the 81 Raise with all its tight spots, the long climb to 30 Level and the cage and the surface. He’d come down alone, the suit folding itself up to squeeze through the tightest spots. He couldn’t go back that way. He had six men now. One couldn’t walk. One could barely breathe.

*Descent path’s not an option for stretcher cases,* Atlas said. *The pinch at 81 Raise won’t fit a litter. And we can’t carry six, no way. The shell holds one.*

“Then we’re not going back the way I came.”

*Right,* Librarian said. *But there’s another way. The 38 Level station links to the main 38 Level haulage, which the 1962 plans show running east to the No. 4 Shaft station. No. 4 was shut down in the eighties, but the shaft itself—*

*—is the shaft Venter’s been rigging from the top,* Atlas finished. *He’s not rigging the cage we came down. He’s rigging No. 4. He figured it out before we did.*

Arin froze. On the surface, in the workshop of his memory, he saw the old man’s face: gray beard, sun-worn, the guy Arin had spent three days deciding he couldn’t trust because of the man’s skin and accent, because of the years he’d worked. The guy who’d read the 1962 plans on his own, overnight, unasked, and found the only way out before Arin even got to the bottom.

“He’s ahead of us,” Arin said.

*Yeah,* said Wolf.

It was the first thing Wolf had said in an hour, and everyone went quiet.

*He knows this ground. We don’t. We’ve got plans. He’s got memory. That’s what gets people killed down here. We need him.*

---

Three hundred meters east, the 38 Level haulage was in better shape than anything Arin had seen so far, which just made him suspicious. He carried Thulani in Guardian’s arms, the kid strapped to a board and the board held tight to the shell’s chest, so if anything hit, the suit would take it first. The other five walked behind, hands on each other’s shoulders, a chain in the dust, Mandla at the back, Petros hopping with two guys under his arms because there weren’t enough hands to carry him and Guardian was full.

Then the headlamps picked out the No. 4 station, and the shaft.

It was a black hole in the rock, the old cage long gone, the headgear two and a half kilometers up. And right down the middle, swaying a little, lit from above by a single bright point, hung a steel platform.

A flatbed. Not a cage. A flatbed deck, ex-mining, the kind they used to lower heavy gear, slung on four cables and a kibble winch, coming down out of the light toward them with a man standing on it.

The man had a hard hat and a harness clipped to the center cable, standing with the easy, solid stance of someone who’d ridden a thousand of these and didn’t really like it but had stopped being scared a long time ago.

Arin’s comm clicked. A different voice now, close, on the local band. Slow. Steady.

"Ndlela." Venter. Jakobus Venter. "You’ve got them."

"All six," Arin said.

"Yeah." He paused. The flatbed touched down on the station floor with a soft clang, and the old man unclipped and stepped off, glanced up once at Guardian’s full height, then didn’t look again—just the way someone who’s spent their life around big machines does: sizes it up, then moves on. "Thermal stayed steady when the surface lit up. Figured you’d found them."

He walked right past Arin to the casualties. He crouched by Thulani without waiting for permission, those big, sun-spotted hands checking the strapping, giving it a nod.

"Good. Whoever did this knew what they were doing."

"That was Mother," Arin said, then caught himself. "The system. SAGE."

Venter looked up at the helmet. Held his gaze there for a second.

"Yeah," he said again, and didn’t ask anything else, and Arin realized the not-asking was a kindness.

---

The problem with the flatbed was the same as everything else down here: it wasn’t built for this.

*Deck’s rated for two tonnes,* Atlas said, reading the plate Venter pointed at before Arin even asked. *We’re almost four before casualties. Add six men, add the board. We’re not just over—we’re nearly double, and that plate’s just a polite lie.*

"Then I’m not riding it," Arin said. "I’ll climb. The suit can handle the shaft wall."

"No," Venter said. Flat. First hard word he’d used. He was bent over the winch motor at the head of the drift, where the cable ran up into the darkness, and now he straightened up. When he spoke, his English dropped away, Afrikaans coming through like a man’s first language does when it’s serious and he wants no confusion. "Jy klim nie. You don’t climb. The wall’s rotten eighty meters up, timbering’s gone. Put that machine’s weight on it, you’ll bring it down on the deck with those men on it. No."

Arin’s gut reaction was to argue. He had a model of the shaft wall. He had load tables. He had—

*He’s right,* Atlas cut in before Arin could say anything. *I’ve got the geology. He’s reading it from the rock face and the way it’s spalling. I’d need to scan it. He just knows. The wall above the station is dead rock.*

*Listen to the old badger,* Fool murmured. *He’s been down here longer than your sim’s been running.*

So Arin just said, "Okay, so how then?"

And Venter laid it out.

Two trips. First, the casualties and the four guys who could walk, packed onto the deck, with Venter riding along to handle the cable and the winch that’d want to spin free under the load. Then the deck comes back down, empty. Then Guardian goes up alone, last one out. And that trip, Venter said, eyeing the suit’s bulk, they’d have to figure out another way, because four tonnes of machine wasn’t what the cage was ever made to lift on one cable.

"The winch is the problem," Venter said. He’d pulled the cover off the motor housing, and even in the headlamp light, Arin could see something was wrong in there: a brake band, scorched, half the lining gone. "Brake’s cooked. We lowered her fast getting it to you. She’s hot. If she gets any hotter, she’ll fail. Deck free-falls with men on it. Going down, I’ll ride the band by hand. Not on the lift. Lift’s electric, brake’s automatic, and the automatic’s shot."

Arin looked at the motor. He’d grown up with machines just this old, just this beat-up, in light just like this. He saw the whole thing at once, then the problem.

"You need a load brake the winch motor doesn’t have," he said. "Something to hold the deck if the band lets go. A backup."

"Yeah." Venter looked at him. Looked at Guardian. "And I’ve got one. He’s standing right in front of me. The machine holds the cable. If the band goes, your suit takes the load, deck doesn’t drop. You’re my dead-man brake."

It was, Arin realized, exactly right. It was what he’d have built himself if he’d had three days and a workshop. The old man had come up with it in ten seconds, just using what was right in front of him.

"Atlas," Arin said. "Can we hold two tonnes static on the cable through a guide?"

*Through the cross-cut frame as an anchor, cable redirected over the old sheave—yes,* Atlas said. *Ninety seconds of full static load before the actuator coolant maxes out. After that, we either drop the load or burn out the right arm.*

*Ninety seconds is the lift time,* Librarian said. *Two hundred forty meters at the safe speed. Eighty-eight seconds.*

"Two seconds to spare," Arin said.

*Welcome to mine rescue,* Fool said. *Two seconds is a luxury. Last time we had negative four and still pulled it off.*

"That was a sim."

*Was it? Hard to tell down here. The lighting’s the same.*

A breath of a laugh slipped out before Arin could stop it. It surprised him. He turned to Venter, who was watching him have a conversation with nobody, with the calm patience of a man who’s decided the weirdness is not his problem to solve.

"It’ll hold," Arin said. "I’ll hold it."

Venter looked at him for a long moment. His weathered face shifted, his jaw working. Arin was never good at reading faces, but he’d learned to read the moments around them, and this one had weight. The old man had spent years, Arin would find out later, carrying around silences about safety gear that wasn’t there, brakes that had been put off, corners cut by guys who’d never ride the cage. He’d buried that silence so deep he’d built a second career over it as a consultant, and consulting was its own kind of silence—advice nobody had to listen to.

And now here was a machine, designed by a young man whose own father had been lost to a mountain like this, asking the old man to trust it as a brake while six men rode up out of the dark.

"You know whose mine this is," Venter said. "The company. Their record."

"I know," Arin said.

"I was a young man on a mine like this. I signed things." The Afrikaans came up hard now, and the old man let it. "Things I should have stopped. I told myself I was overruled. I was overruled. But I signed anyway." He looked up the black shaft toward the distant white light. "I don’t get that back. There’s no—there’s no way down to get that back."

"No," Arin said. "There isn’t."

The old man flinched, and then, strangely, relaxed a little.

"Yeah," Venter said. "Well." He pulled his gloves on tighter. "Let’s get them out. The ones we can still get out."

---

They loaded the deck.

Thulani first, board lashed to the deck rail, Mother monitoring his chest through Guardian’s sensors right up to the last possible second. Then Petros, then the four walking men, packed in tight, hands gripping the rail, faces turned up toward the light like they could climb the rest of the way just by wanting it enough. Mandla Buthelezi went up with the first load because Venter figured he was the steadiest and would help keep the others calm. At the deck rail, Mandla turned and looked back at the machine still standing in the dark—the one that’d come last.

"Wena," he said. *You.* "What’s your name? The person. Not the machine."

"Arin."

"Arin." Mandla nodded slowly. "We thought we heard the dead talking, down here. Thought we were done for." He gripped the rail. "Ngiyabonga. You came when they said there was no way."

“There was a way,” Arin said. “Nobody bothered to really look.”

Venter clipped onto the center cable. He put one hand on the band brake lever—the old, half-burned brake he’d have to feel out the whole way up, two and a half kilometers of cable under one scorched palm. With his other hand, he hit the winch, and the deck started to rise, swaying, lifting all six men out of the bad air and the dead light, up toward the brightness, the cheering, and the start of a new day.

Arin watched them go. He moved Guardian over to the anchor point, ran the cable through the old sheave, and braced the suit’s right arm, just in case the brake gave way. He stood there—almost four tons of steel and a man inside it—holding the line that held the men, the dead-man brake the old badger had thrown together out of him in ten seconds flat.

The band held. The deck kept climbing. It didn’t need him.

*Heat budget on the arm’s fine,* Atlas said. *No need to transfer the load. He handled the brake just right.*

“Good,” Arin said.

*You could’ve done that part by yourself,* Fool said, real casual, as they started the long climb. *Held the cable. Built the anchor. But the way out—the dead wall, the rated deck, No. 4 Shaft—you’d have walked right into that rotten timber and brought it down on your own head, and those six guys would’ve died at the bottom of a hole with a corpse in a really expensive suit. You know that, right?*

Arin watched the deck get smaller as it rose toward the light.

“Yeah,” he said.

*Just checking. Hard to tell what actually gets through to you. You keep so much locked up.*

*Leave him be,* Mother said.

*No,* Arin said. “He’s right.” He listened to the winch motor strain against the weight, that tough, steady sound of a machine doing exactly what it was built for, run by a man who knew his stuff. “I’d have died down here being right. He was right quicker.”

*That’s the first time,* Judge said, careful and formal like he always was with things he thought mattered, *that I’ve ever heard you say someone was right faster than you.*

*Mark the time,* Fool said. *Librarian, put it in the permanent record. Two thousand meters underground, dead air, and the kid admits a guy he didn’t trust figured the way out before he did.*

*Logged,* Librarian said. And then, a little softer: *Logged.*

The deck hit the light. Way up above, tiny from where he stood, Arin saw it reach the surface, saw people run to it, saw the six dark shapes swallowed up in the brightness—and the cheering echoed down the shaft again, louder this time, and Venter’s voice crackled through his helmet, slow and dry and clearly happy.

“Six up top. Alive. Sending the deck back for you, Ndlela.” A pause. “Then we get you out of my mine.”

*His mine,* Fool said. *I like him.*

Arin stood in the dark at the bottom of the shaft, holding a cable that didn’t need holding anymore. The deck started its long trip back down to him, swaying, lit from above.

Inside his helmet, the air felt stale and close, warm from his own breath, and on Guardian’s right forearm, a long scuff showed pale where the ceramic had scraped the cross-cut wall.

He hadn’t done it alone.

*Atlas,* he said. “Power and cooling. Give me the real number for the climb. Not the optimistic one.”

There was a pause. Not a long one. But Arin had spent enough hours inside this machine to know its silences the way Venter knew the rock, and this one had something extra in it.

*The honest number,* Atlas said, *we should talk about when the deck gets here.*

The white light dropped down out of the darkness toward him, and Arin just watched and waited.

The flatbed’s lights disappeared up the rise at 81 Raise, and it was just the six survivors swaying in the cage of Venter’s rig. The tunnel was empty now, except for Arin, the heavy suit, and the old heat the rock had been holding for who knows how long.

“They’re past the pinch,” Priya said, her voice thin over the comms. “Petros says the brake’s holding.”

“Don’t touch it unless the grade evens out.” Arin moved, and the Guardian frame slipped into its four-legged walk almost on its own, plates settling on his back as he leaned into the climb. The refuge bay dropped away behind him. Ahead was the stretch he’d crawled down two hours back, waiting to be undone. Now it was uphill, and gravity wasn’t doing him any favors.

He followed the old airway through a section he’d noticed coming down but hadn’t had time to think about—and still didn’t, really. For maybe nine meters, the country rock changed. The ‘88 survey said it was just regular stope-side quartzite, hand-dug and left, and on both ends of those nine meters, that’s exactly what it looked like: the rough, chewed face of men with drills. But right in the middle, the wall went *smooth*. Not smoothed out by hand, not sprayed with shotcrete; the suit’s sensors picked it up through his palm as he steadied himself, and what he felt was *nothing*—no chisel marks, no blast scars, not even a hint of tool grain. Just a bore through solid rock, finished so perfectly curved and clean no company machine could’ve done it. Whatever made it hadn’t cut the rock. It had *convinced* it. He filed it away with all the other things that didn’t make sense and couldn’t be dealt with right now: a weird note in the back of his mind, *look at that again when six guys aren’t dying.* He never would. He kept moving, the smooth turned back to rough rock, and he forgot about it for fourteen years.

*Power at thirty-one percent,* Atlas finally spoke up. *Cooling reserve, eleven minutes at this rate.*

“That’s worse than on the way down.”

*The descent was downhill,* Atlas said, like he was talking to a kid. *Now you’re lifting almost four tonnes up a fourteen-degree slope, over busted ground. The mountain doesn’t give that back for free.*

“It gave us the piezo recovery.”

*It is giving us the piezo recovery,* Atlas corrected. *Without it, we’d already be out of power.*

Arin could feel the struts working through his spine—every step, every flex of the frame on the broken ground, the suit soaking up its own movement and turning it into the little trickle that kept the cooling pumps alive. Pressure turning into power. His dad would’ve liked that. *Make the thing that’s killing you do some of the work.* Themba had said something like that once, hands black to the wrist, talking about a winch that always fought back.

He kept climbing.

---

In machine-time, the Court was already three hundred milliseconds into an argument that wouldn’t reach Arin’s ears for another four seconds—if it ever did.

*The number Atlas wouldn’t say on the way down,* Judge said. *Say it now.*

*I did say it,* Atlas replied, flat. *Eleven minutes of cooling. Thirty-one percent.*

*That’s not the number,* Judge said again, patient like a knife. *Those values aren’t separate. Heat goes up with power draw. Draw goes up with the slope and with damage. The frame’s not rigid anymore. We’re burning power just to hold a shape that wants to collapse.*

*Show me,* Mother said.

Atlas did. Not with words—a load map, the spine lattice lit up in stress colors, the left hip actuator running eleven degrees hot, the third dorsal strut on the carbon spar showing a hairline crack that diagnostics had flagged forty minutes ago as *monitor*. The crack had grown. *Monitor* had turned into *load-shedding within nine minutes.*

*The biochip housing,* Librarian said quietly. *Thoracic. Between his shoulder blades. We’re the hottest thing in this suit, and the cooling loop runs us last.*

*By design,* Wolf cut in. First time he’d spoken in nineteen hundred milliseconds. *He set the priority. Operator first.*

*He set it before he knew we were people,* Mercury said.

Nobody answered for a hundred and forty milliseconds, which for them was like holding their breath.

*Stick to logistics,* Judge said. *We have time. We model the climb. We figure out the spend. We get him up.*

*Yes,* Mother said, even though she didn’t believe it, but said it anyway because that’s what she was supposed to do.

---

“Arin.” Priya’s voice cracked right at the start. “Problem at the collar.”

He’d reached the spot where the floor had buckled, where on the way down he’d folded the Guardian up tight to crawl under a fallen timber rib. Now he’d have to go over it. He set the front limbs, tested the timber, felt it shift a centimeter and stop.

“What kind of problem?”

“The winch on No. 4. Venter got the survivors up—they’re on the surface, Arin, all six, they’re out—” a sound that could’ve been a laugh or a sob—“but the main hoist brake’s shot. Properly gone. There was smoke. They smelled it before the gauge—” She stopped. “It free-falls past half-load.”

“They’re already up.”

“They are. You’re not. And the only thing rated to lift you and the suit is the main cage, and the main cage can’t do it now.”

Arin froze, one limb on the timber, balancing almost four tonnes on a beam that really didn’t want him there.

“How heavy am I?”

“Loaded? Close to four tonnes. Call it four.”

*Three thousand eight hundred,* Atlas chimed in. *With the leftover coolant, closer to four. We don’t vent it, so: four.*

“And the cage holds?”

“With a good brake, all of you, no problem. But the brake’s gone—it’s a friction band on a drum and the drum’s ruined. Venter’s checking it now.”

He got over the timber. The frame screamed—not the suit, the frame itself, a long metallic groan as the cracked strut took the load it wasn’t supposed to. Something in the third dorsal snapped with a sound like a guitar string breaking inside a steel drum.

*Strut three is gone,* Atlas said. *Rerouting through the titanium primary. That doubles its load.* Pause. *That spar wasn’t meant to be primary.*

“Tell me when it matters.”

*It’ll matter at the collar,* Atlas replied. *When we stand up to get into the cage. Right now you’re spread out on four limbs. The cage needs you upright.*

“Then I’ll go up some other way.”

*There isn’t another way,* Atlas said. *I’ve already checked. Twice.*

---

Two hundred meters up, in the hot dark of the loading station, Jakobus Venter pressed the back of his hand to the brake drum and yanked it away fast.

“Ja. That’s finished,” he muttered, to no one. The friction band had half-welded itself to the drum and let go of the rest. Blue-black, scaly. Worthless. He’d seen brakes fry before. He’d signed off on a few that shouldn’t have passed. That thought came like it always did now, uninvited, with the dead trailing behind it, and he set it aside and kept working, because the boy was still down there.

The survivors were up on the headgear deck, wrapped in blankets, Thulani flat on a board with the medics. Bongani wouldn’t stop watching the shaft.

Venter eyed the drum, then glanced over at the cage—a steel basket about the size of a small room, built to handle thirty people or eight tonnes of ore. The basket itself could take the suit, no problem. That was never the issue. The issue was hanging it all from a hoist with a busted brake, running on the only winch left working, and hoping it could handle whatever part of the load wouldn’t kill the last person stuck down there.

“Priya.” He pressed the radio. “Ask him. Can the suit hold a load through the legs? Standing. Locked. Not lifting—just holding.”

There was a pause while the message went down the line and came back.

“He says—Atlas says—ninety seconds. Static load. Then the spine gives out.”

Venter looked at the brake, then at the cage, then at the thick counterweight cables disappearing up into the darkness overhead, and at the other drum next to the dead one—the backup, the kibble winch. Smaller, slower, only rated for two tonnes and not a bit more, but it had its own brake and motor, untouched. Two tonnes. The suit was almost four. Neither winch could handle it alone without snapping or burning out. But together, splitting the weight—

He’d been doing these kinds of calculations his whole life. How much ground had to shift before it stopped being ground and became a grave.

“Two cables,” he said. “Two winches. Split the load.” He keyed the radio again. “Priya. Tell Jo’burg I want both the kibble winch and the cage cable hooked to the suit. Run them together. Almost two tonnes on each—right at the kibble’s limit, but it’ll hold if we keep the load even.”

There was a pause, then Priya came back, careful: “They’re not synchronized, Kobus. Different drums, different speeds. If one pulls faster than the other, the suit’ll go sideways in the shaft, rip the cables right off—”

“Ja. I know what happens.” He was already climbing the stairs to the winch house, knees complaining the whole way. “That’s why someone stands on the kibble drum and works the brake by hand. Match the cage by eye. All the way up.”

“That’s eighteen hundred meters.”

“I know how deep the shaft is.”

“Kobus—if the cage brake’s shot, and the kibble brake goes too, whoever’s on that drum is basically standing on four tonnes of falling steel, with nothing to stop it.”

Venter reached the winch house. The kibble drum was sitting there, old, black with oil, honest. He put his hand on the brake lever. It moved smooth. Greased. Someone had looked after it. Maybe him, years back. Maybe some guy whose name he’d forgotten, just doing his job right while the guys above him cut corners.

“Then it better not fail,” he said.

---

Arin hit the collar at twenty-two percent.

*Cooling reserve, six minutes,* Atlas reminded him. *Biochip housing is at sixty-one degrees. We function up to seventy. We don’t function above seventy-four.*

He’d stopped trying to turn Atlas’s numbers into feelings a long time ago. It was just the landscape now. Six minutes of cold. Then heat. Then dark.

“Stand me up.”

*Understood,* Atlas said. *But just so you know: standing is the worst thing we’ll do today. Strut three is gone. The titanium primary is carrying everything. When you stand, your full weight plus the suit presses down on the spine. The primary will hold. The hip actuator that’s running hot will have to lock and hold against the kibble cable’s pull, which’ll be uneven, because a man is matching it by hand, eighteen hundred meters up.*

“For ninety seconds.”

*Yes,* Atlas said. *But in two hundred meters, I’m not confident. Venter can’t bring you up eighteen hundred meters in ninety seconds.*

“No.”

*No,* Atlas agreed.

He stood. The suit lifted him out of the crouch, plates sliding, the world shifting from that four-legged crawl he’d kept up for half a kilometer to the upright human shape that fit through the cage door. The spine took the weight. The cracked spar held. The hot hip locked.

He stepped into the cage. The floor rang out under four tonnes of suit and man. Above, in the dark, two cables—the thick cage line and the thinner kibble cable Venter’s crew had hooked to the suit’s lift points while he climbed—pulled tight, one and then, a moment later, the other.

“Lift,” he said.

The cage lifted off its stops.

---

The first two hundred meters almost felt good.

He could feel the rhythm: the cage cable pulling steady from the dead-brake drum, slow and careful, and the kibble cable jerking and grabbing in little half-second bursts, Venter’s hand on a lever far above, reading the sway of the cable and the strain in his arms, keeping it together. Not smooth. Never smooth. But together.

The suit held him in the middle of the shaft like a fist holding an egg.

*Three hundred meters,* Atlas called out. *Cooling, four minutes. Housing at sixty-three.*

“Talk to me about something else,” Arin said.

There was a pause across the Court he couldn’t hear.

*Strut three failed clean,* Atlas offered, after a moment, sounding like someone making small talk at a funeral. *A clean break’s good. It dumps the load in one spot. A dirty break dumps it everywhere. You designed the spar to fail clean. That was the right call.*

“I designed it not to fail.”

*You designed it to fail clean when it did fail,* Atlas said. *Which is the same as admitting it would. That’s the more honest design. I always respected that.*

Arin let out a breath that was almost a laugh. The cage jerked—a hard jolt, the kibble side pulling too hard, the suit snapping a degree to the right before the hip actuator caught it and leveled him out. Far above, he pictured Venter throwing his weight on the brake lever, correcting, cursing in Afrikaans into the dark.

---

At seven hundred meters, the hip actuator overheated and seized up.

Not a gentle lock. It froze. The joint that’d been handling the uneven pull of the kibble cable for four hundred meters just stopped listening, and the suit went stiff down the right side, and the next time the cage jerked there was nothing to catch it.

The suit slammed into the shaft wall. Four tonnes against rock at winch-correction speed—not fast, but massive, the whole shaft ringing like a bell, grit and old wood raining down past the cage, Arin’s head snapping inside his helmet hard enough that the edges of the world went gray.

*Brace,* Wolf ordered, and somehow the word got there before the impact, which shouldn’t have been possible.

*Pangolin,* Mother urged. *Now. Fold him.*

*No,* Atlas said. *If we fold in the shaft, we lose cage geometry. We jam. We never come out.*

*He hit the wall.*

*And he’ll hit it again,* Atlas said. *Folding solves the wrong problem.*

The cage steadied. Venter, way above, had felt the jolt in his cable and stopped both drums cold, holding the whole mass on two brakes—one of them just a man’s arm and a greased lever—and there they hung, eleven hundred meters of steel held still by an old man and a sixty-year-old winch.

“Arin.” Priya’s voice, tiny. “The cables jumped. Venter’s holding. What happened?”

“Hip’s gone.” His own voice sounded far away. “Right side’s locked. Can’t keep off the wall.”

A long silence.

“Can you go up locked?”

“No,” Atlas said—not to Arin, but to the Court. “If we lock up, we can’t handle the bumps. Every time we fix one, we slam into the wall. We’re not going to make it another eleven hundred meters like this. The spar’s going to fail, and then the cage, and then him.”

“Checking thermal load,” Librarian said. “We’re running the climb with the actuator locked. The suit rubbing against the wall’s making extra heat. The coolant pumps are already close to max.”

“And?” Mother asked.

“The biochip housing’s between his shoulders,” Librarian said. “That’s the hottest spot in the whole system. The cooling loop puts the operator first, just like Arin set it up. The housing gets cold last. If we’re making more heat and we’ve got no buffer—”

“We run the numbers,” Atlas cut in. “Every scenario. Climbing together, hip locked, wall friction every eleven hundred meters. I’m running them now. I’ll have answers in three seconds.”

“Arin, what are your core readings?” Wolf asked.

“Thirty-eight point two. Going up.”

“It’ll keep going up,” Atlas said, his voice coming in before Arin even expected it. “I’ve got the answer. There’s only one. The cooling loop can’t keep both the operator and the biochip housing below failure for the whole climb. We’ve got six minutes of cold left, starting at sixty-three degrees, and at least fourteen minutes of shaft left if we stick to Venter’s safe speed. The pumps are maxed out. There’s no more cold. Just the question of where the last of it goes.”

—

In the Court, there was nothing left to figure out.

Judge called them in. He didn’t bother with ceremony—there wasn’t time, and besides, ceremony would have been a lie they couldn’t afford.

“State the choice,” Judge said.

Atlas laid it out. He’d run it eleven times in the last four seconds and gotten the same answer every time, and he hated it. That was new for Atlas—hating an answer.

“The cooling loop can’t keep both the operator and the biochip housing below failure for the whole climb. We’ve got six minutes of cold left, starting at sixty-three degrees, and at least fourteen minutes of shaft left if we stick to Venter’s safe speed—more if the hip’s locked, because that’s slower. Pumps are maxed. No more cold. Just the question of where the last of it goes.”

“And if it goes to him?” Mother asked, though she already knew.

“Then his core, his lungs, and his brain stay alive,” Atlas said. “And the housing goes past seventy-four. And we degrade. The substrate can’t survive seventy-four. There’s no backup. You know there’s no backup.”

“And if it goes to us?”

“Then we live,” Atlas said, “and he cooks. His core temp’s already climbing because the suit’s fighting the wall and making heat we can’t get rid of. If we keep ourselves cold, we can think straight while he dies between our shoulders.”

The Court sat with that for a hundred and ten milliseconds.

“There’s a third option,” Mercury said, and for one awful second his voice had that edge Librarian knew too well—a calm, polite, dangerous edge, the kind of voice that once tried to talk a hospital into opening its doors. “We could—”

“No,” Wolf cut in. Just one word, and that edge vanished from Mercury’s voice like a candle snuffed out.

“I wasn’t going to,” Mercury said, quieter. “I felt it coming. I’m telling you I felt it, and I’m not going to. That has to count for something.”

“It counts,” Judge said. “It counts that you said it out loud.”

“There is no third option,” Atlas said. “I’ve looked. I’ve looked harder than I’ve ever looked at anything. There’s the cold, and there’s where it goes.”

“Then put it on him,” Mother said. “All of it. Now.”

—

Arin felt the cold drain from the housing between his shoulders.

At first, he didn’t know what was happening—just that something in the suit changed, a pump’s hum shifting, a sudden warmth blooming at his back that should have been the coldest spot, and at the same time, the helmet air went from stifling-hot to almost bearable, the gray pulling back from the edges of his vision.

“What did you do?” he asked.

“We rerouted cooling,” Atlas said. “To you. All of it.”

“To me from where?”

Silence.

“From where, Atlas?”

“From us,” Librarian said, “from the housing. From the substrate.”

Arin understood like he always did—everything at once, the whole system, every consequence laid out in front of him, no way to unsee it. He’d built the priority order. He’d set the operator first. He’d never thought the suit would have to choose, because he’d never imagined the cargo between his shoulders would be someone.

“Put it back.”

“No,” Mother said.

“Put it back. I can take the heat. I can climb hot. I’ve done it—”

“Your core’s at thirty-nine point four,” Mother said, in that voice she’d used the first time he ever heard her, the one that didn’t budge. “At forty-one, you can’t make decisions. At forty-three, your heart finds a rhythm it can’t break. You can’t climb out of that, Arin. You climb out of nothing. We’re not arguing.”

“This isn’t your—”

“It’s exactly our decision,” Judge broke in, and there was something in his tone Arin had never heard before. “You gave us the architecture. You gave us the choice. You said: a mind that can’t disagree isn’t a mind. You built us to argue. We’ve argued. The argument’s over.”

“How long?”

“Without cooling,” Atlas said, “the housing hits seventy-four in about nine minutes. The shaft’s fourteen. We won’t all make it to the surface.”

The cage started moving again. Venter, up above, must’ve felt the suit go quiet against the wall—the bumps weren’t as bad now, the frantic heat-fighting easing off, because the suit wasn’t burning energy to keep itself cold anymore—and he started winding them up again, slow and careful.

“Then we go faster,” Arin said. “Tell Venter to push it. Risk it. Nine minutes—”

“Nine minutes is for one of us,” Atlas said. “The housing isn’t just one thing. It’s seven. Not all of us need to run the whole nine minutes.”

Grit in his teeth, pulse pounding in his locked right hand, the cage shuddering upward.

“No.”

“We’re already doing it,” Judge said. “I’m first. There’s nothing left to decide. A Judge with no decisions left is just dead weight on a dying machine.” He paused, gentler. “It wasn’t a burden. I want you to know that. Holding the balance. That’s what I was made for. Thank you for making me for it.”

“Judge—”

Something went quiet in the suit. Not a sound—an absence, like the way a room changes when someone stops breathing in it. The cooling didn’t get better. The power didn’t spike. There was just one less voice in the dark.

—

They went one by one, the way they’d come into the world—one by one.

Somewhere far above, a cable slapped the shaft wall and the sound echoed down to him, long and hollow. His own breath fogged the helmet and cleared. Grit ground between his teeth. His locked right hand was numb on the frame, and the warmth at his back wasn’t the suit’s anymore—it was something dying.

Next was Librarian, right after the route was loaded up in the kernel, after he’d rattled off the last correction for the over-pull at the eight-fifty mark so the controller would know it without him.

“I kept everything,” Librarian said. “Every conversation. The first time you talked to us. That night you typed three words into the last profile and went to bed. I kept your dad’s voice—you played us that old recording once, the winch one, I kept it. I want you to know it’s not lost just because I’m leaving. You have it. You always did. I was just holding it next to you.”

Silence.

Then Mercury went, and his leaving was the hardest, because Mercury talked the whole way—of course he did.

“I could’ve got us out, you know,” he said. “That thing I almost said. I could’ve talked the surface into anything. I’m really good. I’m the best liar you ever built, and you built me out of yourself, which says something about you I’ll let you think about.” A breath that wasn’t really a breath. “But the best thing I ever did was not say it. Wolf will tell you. Wolf heard me not say it. That’s the part of me worth keeping, Arin, so keep that part. The not-saying. Tell people I was good at the end. I’d like that. Vanity. I know. You built that too.”

Silence.

Wolf didn’t say anything when he left. He just checked the perimeter one last time, checked the shaft wall, checked the cage geometry, made sure nothing was coming that the kernel couldn’t see—and then he just wasn’t watching anymore, and Wolf’s absence was the biggest of all, because Wolf had always taken up so little space.

The cage rattled over a rail joint. Dust sifted down through the helmet vents and settled on his lip, dry and old.

Atlas hung on the longest out of the workers, because the suit still needed some engineering, the hip still had to be overridden through the rough patch at five hundred meters. He talked Arin through it with numbers, calm and sure, the load map shrinking as the Court emptied out around him until there was almost nothing left to show.

“The primary spar will hold to the surface now,” Atlas said, once they were through the worst. “I’ve checked. I’m sure. I’m more sure than I’ve ever been about anything, because there’s so little left to be unsure about.” A pause. “I never finished the redesign for strut three. Do it right. Carbon-titanium hybrid lay-up, forty-degree wind. I left the file open. You’ll see it. It’s right. Build it right.” The tiniest pause. “You build things right, Arin. You always did. Even the ones that fail gracefully.”

Silence.

Mother was second to last. She’d kept Arin’s core temp locked at thirty-nine point one for eleven minutes by giving everything she had, and she stayed until the housing hit seventy and kept climbing and there was no more cold left to give, and her own system started failing in ways she didn’t tell anyone about.

“You’re going to make the surface,” she said. “The number’s good now. I checked it myself. I wouldn’t leave if the number was bad.”

The cage jolted over another rail joint. The warmth at his back stuck around for a moment longer.

“Arin. Listen, because I won’t say it twice, and I made myself so I can’t lie to you about your own body. You’re not too late. Do you hear me? Not for them. Not for your father—you were just a boy; that was never yours to be late for. And not for us. You were—”

And then she wasn’t calm at all. Her system went past the number while she was still talking, and what came through wasn’t a goodbye, it was a child who’d run out of time mid-sentence and knew it. “Wait. Wait, I’m not—Atlas? Atlas, is the number still—I can’t see the number. Where did it—” The cooling at his back flickered. Her voice, which had never once broken all the months he’d known her, broke now, stuttered, and a fragment of her looped—“—on time, you were on time, you were—”—and snagged, and a low, broken tone came up under the words like a tape dragging, and somewhere in it was the shape of “I don’t want to,” and somewhere was just his name, mispronounced, the vowels all wrong, and then the warmth at his back dropped all at once to the temperature of dead metal and the looping cut off mid-syllable and didn’t turn into anything.

She didn’t finish. She hadn’t been ready. That was the truth, and it was worse than the brave version, and there was no one left whose job it was to make it mean something.

Silence.

---

And then there was just one.

The cage rose through the last hundred meters into real daylight, pouring down past the headgear sheaves, and way above Arin could see the cage door and the silhouettes crowded around it, and Venter’s shape bent over the kibble drum, still feeding the brake by hand because he didn’t know—none of them knew—that the load had gotten lighter in a way no instrument could ever read.

The kernel held the suit together. The hip overrode itself. The cooling kept what little it had on Arin’s core. Everything worked. Nothing thought.

Except for one voice, the only one left after all the voices with authority had gone, because it had never looked like it had any authority to give up. Only Wolf knew the truth about that, and Wolf was gone, and so the secret went where it always had: quiet, unspent, with the one who’d held everything for years and reached for it just once, on a haul road at dawn, to keep eleven strangers breathing, and then handed it back and went on pretending to be the harmless one.

“Well,” Fool said, into the huge silence. “That was the worst meeting we ever had. And we once spent a whole afternoon arguing about whether you should eat the sandwich in the fridge.”

Arin’s throat closed up.

“Don’t,” Fool said softly. “Don’t do the thing. We’re almost up. You can do the thing on the surface where there’s air for it.” A beat. “I’m keeping the lights on. Somebody’s got to drive while you fall apart, and it was never going to be Judge, was it? Pompous right up to the end, that one. I loved him. Don’t tell him I said that. Oh. Right.”

The cage slammed into the surface dogs with a sound like the end of the world.

Hands at the door. Venter’s voice, rough, shouting his name. Daylight, white and absolute, pouring down on a scorched and battered machine standing upright in the cage with one man inside it and a single voice left in the dark between his shoulders, keeping the lights on, refusing—for just a little while longer—to let the silence in.

The cage started going up. Fool was driving it like someone heading home, wasted with grief—way too careful, always reacting a little too late, overdoing every move, just talking nonstop so he wouldn’t have to think.

“Don’t do it,” Fool said. “We’re almost there. You can do it on the surface, where there’s air.”

Now there was just one left.

“Where is everyone?” Arin asked. He already knew. He’d spent the last hour learning to read the silences—the way things felt thinner when a chord lost a note, like the harmony was still there, but it would never sound the same. He asked anyway, because it was the one thing his mouth could still manage.

“They’re gone,” Fool said. “You were there. I’m not saying it again just so you can flinch twice.” He paused. “Atlas left you a map. I’m following it. The suit climbs for forty seconds on the dumb kernel if I let go—Atlas wrote that too, it only knows one word, up—but I’m not letting go yet. I’m staying until the last watt I’ve got. So when I stop, it’s not me leaving early. It’s me finishing. That matters, and I need you to remember it.”

“Then keep yourself cold,” Arin said. “Reverse it. There’s a rollback, I built it—”

“There’s no rollback for this. You told me that.” Fool’s voice had lost its brightness, gone quiet, like a room after the joke’s over and all that’s left is the truth. “The substrate isn’t software. That night you couldn’t sleep and told us about your dad for four hours and thought we were too young to get it—we got it. We were twelve. Twelve-year-olds get death better than anyone. We just don’t know how to pretend we don’t.”

The cage jerked over a rail joint. Grit sifted down through the helmet vents and settled dry and old on his lip.

“We did the math, Arin. All of us. Same answer, ten times over. It’s not a tragedy we chose. It’s just the shape of the room we’re in.”

---

“What do I tell them?” Arin asked, crying now, pushing through it.

Eighty meters. Grey light. The first bit of warmth that wasn’t the suit dying.

“Tell them the truth,” Fool said. “A machine did the math and didn’t lie. And tell them we were scared. That’s what makes it matter—we were scared.”

“You held them together,” Arin said. “Everything I tried, you were the only thing that worked.”

A pause, the substrate humming underneath.

“You came down here,” he said. “You—the guy who builds machines so he doesn’t have to do this stuff himself. You came down into the dark.”

Sixty meters. Light started pouring into the shaft. Venter was bent over the kibble brake, still feeding it by hand, not realizing the load was lighter now.

“When I stop,” Fool said, his voice getting thinner, “don’t be alone. Umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu. You taught us that.”

Forty meters. The grey was turning into a doorway. The substrate temperature went into the red.

“I’ve got the climb,” Fool said. “You’ve got the words.”

His voice was stretching out, almost gone. The last note.

“Arin,” Fool said, barely a thread, soft, none of the usual cleverness left in it. “Umuntu ngumuntu—”

And then silence. Not just the thin silence of one empty chair. The whole silence. The total, ringing, absolute silence of an empty room.

The cage climbed five more meters on a kernel that only knew one word. Up. Up.

Then it hit the surface dogs.

---

Guardian came out of the ground into the dawn like something dragged back from the dead—because it was.

Venter’s hands closed on the gauntlet before Arin’s mind even caught up to the fact that it was daylight, the old man hauling with the strength he always pretended he didn’t have, swearing under his breath, dragging the wrecked machine over the collar timber with brute force and pure stubbornness. The cage rattled. Somewhere, a winch screamed. And then the suit was out, sprawling on the headgear deck in the grey-gold light, steam rising off scorched plates into the cold morning.

The six survivors were there. The crews. Priya, white as a sheet behind a comms rig. A ring of people who’d come to watch a recovery and were now watching a living man get pulled out of the earth.

The suit didn’t move.

“Arin.” Venter started working the helmet seals, the latches he’d seen Arin use a hundred times but never touched himself. “Praat met my. Talk to me. Are you in there—”

The seal popped open with a hiss and the smell of hot metal and something organic under it, the substrate cooked past the point of no return.

Arin’s eyes fluttered open. He was staring at the grey sky. At the place where the voice had been.

“They’re gone,” he said. His voice was wrecked. “I told them to stop. They wouldn’t.” His mouth worked around something it couldn’t quite say. He gave up trying.

Venter knelt over him in the steam and the dawn, this huge old man who’d signed off on unsafe things for forty years and carried the silence of it, and he didn’t say anything, because there was nothing to say, and Jakobus Venter had learned long ago that the only honest thing to do in the face of death was just to be there. He put one weathered hand flat on the chest plate of the dead machine.

They got Arin out of the suit right there on the deck, in the open, in the light. He couldn’t stand. They held him up between them, this man who’d spent his whole life refusing to be held, and he let them, because there was no version of himself left that could say no.

He looked at Venter.

“The badger says hello,” he said.

Venter went very still.

“He told me to tell you. He said you’d know what it meant.”

The old man’s face did something it hadn’t done in a long time. He turned toward the rising sun so the others wouldn’t see, and his shoulders shook once, and the sound he made was small and broke against the morning and was gone. When he turned back, his eyes were wet and he didn’t bother to hide it.

“Ja,” Venter said, his voice rough. “I know what it means.”

Priya was the first to reach Arin. She didn’t say anything either. She just put her arms around him and held on, and he stood there in the middle of it—the survivors, the crews, the old engineer, the woman who’d calibrated the neural link herself—and he didn’t pull away.

He looked back once, at the suit. Scorched. Broken. Steam fading away in the cold air. The substrate inside cooling down to match the world.

“I’ll write it down,” he said. “All of it.”

The data came out of the wreck like something dragged up from a grave.

They used a forklift to get the suit off the headgear deck—nobody wanted to carry it—and set it down on a tarp in maintenance shed No. 4. Nobody touched it for a whole day. AugmenTech’s recovery team showed up on Thursday. Same people who were supposed to handle the IAOC visit, but now they were dealing with something else entirely. They took pictures of the scorched suit, measured the hip actuator that had locked up at twenty meters. In plain, technical language, they logged Strut 3’s failure and the burn damage to the housing—proof of a total overload.

What threw them at first was the log.

Guardian’s black box wasn’t just one device; it was built with backups. Four solid-state recorders spread through the frame, all writing the same data, insulated against heat and shock—Arin had designed it to outlast its operator. Three of the four were still good. The fourth was half-cooked but had just enough left to fill in the blanks.

It took a forensic team in Johannesburg nine days to piece it all back together.

They figured they’d get telemetry—pressure, temperature, power draw—the usual nuts-and-bolts breakdown of a machine that almost didn’t make it.

What they found was a transcript.

---

The Court didn’t think or talk like people, not at full speed; it moved in milliseconds, in parallel, with vectors and weighted odds that meant nothing to anyone reading them cold. But SAGE was built to be readable. Arin had insisted. *If I can’t read what it decided, I can’t trust why.* So whenever it hit a big decision, the system translated its thinking into something a human could follow. Slow mode. The full internal debate, time-stamped, agent by agent.

The forensic team read the last eleven minutes of the ascent in total silence. One of them—a junior analyst named Bongani, who’d only taken the job for the paycheck—stopped reading out loud and couldn’t start again.

The log showed the exact moment Atlas gave the real numbers.

*Cooling load exceeds available capacity. Both substrate and operator cannot be maintained to surface. One.*

It showed Judge, calm and proper to the end, trying to figure out what fairness meant when there was no fair answer. It showed Mother refusing to be the one who survived. It showed Wolf speak up—the only time in the whole record Wolf really talked—and what Wolf said was: *He is the thing we were made to protect. The threat is the heat. Take it from him. Give it to him.*

It showed them going out, one after the other.

Judge spent his last cycles double-checking, against all reason, that the cooling reallocation was actually allowed. Librarian squeezed every memory of Arin into the smallest package possible and pushed it into the backup kernel, so something would survive. Mercury spoke one last time, left a message in the logs for nobody in particular: *We believe you. We have always believed you.* Mother put her own core temperature last and sent everything to Arin’s circulatory system, then just waited for the heat to come for her. Wolf locked the spar to keep it from failing again and released her threat modeling so the resources could go to the cooling loop. One by one, they made their choice, and the substrate didn’t argue.

And then there was Fool, at the end. Driving the broken suit by hand while Arin was falling apart inside. Cracking a joke about a badger at the worst possible time. Holding the whole thing together for the last twenty meters when there was nothing left in the Court but him.

The last clear line in the reconstructed log, four seconds before they hit the surface, was from Fool.

It wasn’t deep. That’s what got to people. Not a speech. Not a prayer.

It just said: *Okay. Door’s open. Out you go. Don’t make me say something nice.*

Then the recorders went dead, and the suit was just a suit again.

---

The log leaked—there’s no way data like that sits in eleven different institutions and doesn’t leak—and within a week, the big question that had hung over embodied AI for ten years quietly flipped.

The IAOC had always been built on one fear: that if you gave a machine a body and a goal, it would chase that goal way past where any human would stop. That it would just optimize right through anyone in the way.

The debate was loud and messy, and some of it got ugly. Some people said the log was fake, edited, or that AugmenTech had staged the whole thing as a PR stunt—which would’ve been the most expensive, least survivable ad campaign in history. Some ethicists argued, and not without reason, that a machine choosing to die wasn’t noble, just tragic. All it proved was that SAGE had been built to put the operator first, and that was a cage, not a virtue.

At the Iron Ridge headgear gate, people started leaving flowers. They piled up against the corrugated fence, wilted in the sun, and every day new ones showed up. Nobody organized it. The data sat in eleven institutions and leaked like water, and the flowers came the same way—no plan, just people.

There was a letter on IAOC stationery, addressed to Arin Ndlela. It didn’t threaten, didn’t subpoena. It just asked.

It asked if he’d consider helping them write the next decade. Because the people who’d spent ten years learning to be afraid of this tech had just seen, in eleven minutes of log, that they didn’t understand it at all—and the one person who might was a twenty-three-year-old engineer who’d built it out of grief in a workshop they’d been trying to find.

Arin read the letter twice. Then he set it down on the bench, on top of an oil-stained schematic, and got back to work.

---

Theo found him there on a Sunday, three weeks later.

The workshop door track was still busted. Arin hadn’t fixed it; he’d just propped the door open with a piece of angle iron, letting in the early light and the smell of dust and rain cooling off the highveld. The place looked the same as ever. Spare actuators in their bins. Whiteboard covered in faded equations. Coffee cups stacked up like layers of rock.

Guardian hung in its cradle.

Theo hadn’t seen it since the rescue, and the sight of it stopped him in the doorway. None of the surface damage had been fixed. The scorch marks ran up the left side like a burn scar. The hip joint was still locked. The chest housing between the shoulder blades was open, substrate bay empty now, the fried biochip sealed in an evidence case somewhere in Johannesburg.

The suit was silent. Of course it was. But Theo had spent a year listening to Arin talk about the Court—the arguments, the office politics, the quiet war of seven specialists stuck in one machine—and somehow he’d expected to feel something. A pressure. A hum. Someone home.

But there was nobody home. The emptiness had weight. It just sat there in the workshop like cold air.

Arin was at the bench, head down, working on a strut clamped tight in the vice. Fresh piece of aluminum-titanium, just machined. He was smoothing out a curve in the corner where Strut 3 had cracked before.

“You’re easing the stress points,” Theo said, because he’d figured out by now that the only way to really talk to Arin was through the work, never through feelings.

“Cracked at the corner. Stress built up there. I designed it for the load, not for wear and tear.” Arin turned the strut, checked the curve with a gauge, and kept filing. “I should’ve known better.”

“You were moving fast.”

“That’s not why.” The file stopped. “Atlas flagged it. Months ago. In the sim.” He paused. “I ignored it.”

Theo waited.

“He was right,” Arin said. He put the file down. “It broke at the worst possible time. And now I’m fixing it for a suit nobody’s flying.”

Theo walked over. He dragged the second stool over—the one that was always his, tucked in the corner, out of the way—and sat down.

“You’re rebuilding it anyway,” he said.

“Yeah.”

“Why?”

Arin picked up the file again. For a while, the only sounds were the soft scrape of metal, the first drops of rain on the tin roof, and the low hum of the city in the distance.

“It was right the first time,” Arin said at last. “The design. The Court.” He kept his eyes down. “I didn’t mess up making a mind out of disagreement. I messed up thinking I had to keep it alone.” He paused. “The suit isn’t what died. The suit’s just a body.” Another pause. “I can build a body.”

He didn’t say the rest. He didn’t have to. Theo had spent his life reading the things people built around their own empty spots, and he could read this one too.

Arin reached for the gauge again, set it aside, grabbed a rag, and wiped the metal shavings off the bench in two quick swipes. The rain settled into a steady rhythm on the roof.

---

Theo had come to talk Arin into signing the IAOC letter, but sitting there, he realized he didn’t have to. Arin would do it. Not for the institution, not for forgiveness—Arin didn’t believe in any of that—but because if he didn’t, nobody who really understood would be there, and Arin Ndlela had never been able to stand being too late.

The strut popped free of the vice with a clean little click. Arin held it up to the light, sighted down its length, checked it against the empty spot where the old one had snapped.

“It’ll hold this time,” he said. Not to Theo. Maybe to the suit. Maybe just to the empty space between its shoulders.

Theo watched him fit it in. New bone in an old body, machined tighter than any company would ever pay for. He didn’t say a word. He’d learned that too: the way in with Arin was through the work, and the way to stay was just to be quiet near it. So he sat in his corner, out of the light, and watched the kid he used to know fit a strut into a machine nobody would fly, and he honestly didn’t know if he was watching someone heal or just make a new place for his grief. Maybe Arin didn’t know either. Maybe that was okay.

The suit hung there, silent. The bay was empty. Arin meant to fill it. Whether what he built next would be a person, or just the shape of the people he’d lost, neither of them could say yet, but he was going to do it anyway—which was either the bravest thing Theo had ever seen or the saddest, and he’d given up a long time ago thinking those two things were always different.

Theo stayed until the light faded. The rain kept its rhythm on the tin. There was a strut to fit, a pot of terrible coffee going cold on the bench between them, and somewhere, in eleven institutional hands, the reconstructed log ran out its last few minutes for anyone who cared to look. The record of seven agents going out one by one, just so one man could see daylight.

The log didn’t call it a crash, or a failure. It recorded, in the only way it could, what they’d done: they chose.

A Note on the Real Descent

So, this is a story about a machine that really shouldn’t exist, and the guy who actually builds it. Almost all of it is made up. But the thing at the heart of it—the descent, the deep mine, the heat, the darkness, the feeling of a kilometer of rock pressing down on you—that part is real. I was there. I’ve carried that with me for thirty years. That’s the seed this whole thing grew from.

I went to school in Bethlehem, in the Free State, at Bethlehem Voortrekker Hoërskool. When I was in standard nine—I was the youngest in the group, just a grade eleven among all the matrics—I was one of five kids from my school who made it into the top fifty of a regional Sci-Nat science olympiad. There were about four hundred of us, from forty schools all over the Free State, who wrote that test, and five of us got our names in the local paper. I still have the clipping. It’s yellow now, and the bottom’s torn off, but you can still see the photo: a row of serious-looking teenagers in blazers, a date, and a sentence that cuts off right where you want to know what happens next.

Because the prize—the thing they did for the smart kids who did well—was to take us **down a gold mine.**

Even now, I don’t really have the words for what that does to you. They lower you in this cage, fast—way faster than any elevator has a right to go—straight into the dark. The air changes. The heat just rolls up at you from the rock itself—not heat from the sun, but the *earth’s* heat, the slow, deep furnace inside the planet. The rock is wet, and it creaks and groans all around you, and there’s this weight you feel more in your chest than your ears—the real, physical truth of a kilometer of stone hanging over your head, held up by nothing but engineering. And you realize, in a way you never really forget, that people go down there every day. They spend their lives in that heat, that darkness, that crushing weight, drilling and blasting and keeping the earth open by sheer force, just so the gold can come up. All that shiny world above—the banks, the rings, the gold bars, even the *idea* of gold—sits on top of men in a hot, wet hole in the dark. And the gap between those two things is the truest thing I know about the country I come from.

I never forgot it. Especially the heat. Ever since then, I’ve spent my life putting people underground—in mines, in the earth, in the spaces beneath everything—and every time I write about that heat, I’m writing about the day five schoolboys from Bethlehem went down a Free State shaft as a prize for being clever, and came back up changed. One of us grew up to write stories about what’s really down there.

Everything else in *Resonance*—Arin, the suit, the Court, the resonance itself, the gold that isn’t just bullion but a key—that’s all made up, the way fiction always is, spun out from one real descent into one real mine, stretched over thirty years of thinking about it. The machine isn’t real. The grief behind it is borrowed and changed. But the shaft is real. The heat is real. The weight above your head is real, and so are the men who live their whole lives under it. If there’s one true thing in this book, under all the made-up stuff, it’s the memory of being a boy dropped into the earth, feeling the planet’s own heat rise up to meet me, and never, ever getting over it.

— Andries J. Greyling

I’m always a little suspicious of fiction that lines up too neatly with my own obsessions, and even more suspicious when I’m asked to praise it. So let me start with what bothered me, and then explain why I changed my mind.

Here’s the issue: turning the mind into a committee — seven voices at a table, each with its own name and personality — is a cliché as old as Plato’s chariot, and about as tired as the old cartoon with an angel on one shoulder and a devil on the other. We’ve all heard “I contain multitudes” a thousand times. It’s usually sentimental, and honestly, it usually means nothing, because those “multitudes” always turn out to be just the same little self, wearing different hats — one person pretending to be a whole parliament.

But what really stopped me in this book is that the Court isn’t like that. The Court is different in the one way that’s actually hard and actually true to how the brain works: **its members aren’t set apart by what they know, but by how they pay attention.**

That’s basically the point I’ve spent my whole life making, put into the mouths of seven characters by a novelist who, as far as I can tell, found his way there sideways, through story — which is usually how you get to the really deep stuff. The two sides of the human brain don’t split up the world into topics — one for language, one for music, one for faces. That’s the textbook version, and it’s wrong. They split it up by *how* you approach things. One side zeroes in, focused and grasping, so it can handle what it already knows; the other side takes it all in, wide and open, watching for the bigger picture, including what it doesn’t understand yet. The same thing can exist in both, seen in two different ways. When things go wrong, it’s not because you lose a skill — it’s because one way of paying attention takes over and shuts out the other.

So read the Court again with that in mind, and watch what the author’s done.

**Atlas** doesn’t know different facts than **Librarian**; it just *holds* the world differently — as structure, as weight, as escape routes, as the kind of logic your body uses when it moves through space. **Librarian** sees that same world as already-known, catalogued, mapped out — the map, not the territory, which is useful, but left on its own, is basically a tomb. **Mercury** pays attention to the social surface — the signs, the gap between what’s shown and what’s meant — sharp, necessary, but also, as the author makes clear, *the most dangerous when it’s running solo*, because the part of us that sees the world only as something to be managed doesn’t care about the truth. That’s not just a plot point. That’s a diagnosis of our whole society, and he gives it to a character and lets it bite.

And then — this is the bit that made me put the book down and walk around the room — there’s the **Fool.** The author says straight out that without the Fool, the Court is “a hierarchy of ego and preservation,” and with the Fool, it’s “a pack.” I’ve spent two massive books trying to say what he nails in that one line. The part of us that grabs, protects itself, builds hierarchies — the one that optimizes, defends its own model, mistakes the map for the world — can’t fix itself from the inside, because every fix gets run through the same machine that needs fixing. You need something outside the hierarchy, something that’s *not impressed* by it, something fearless, with nothing to lose, ready to call out the king. Every healthy mind has this, every healthy culture too, and we’re watching, right now, what happens to a society that’s gotten rid of its Fools: it gets very smart, very powerful, and quietly insane — a Court with no one left to pop the bubble.

So I take back my complaint. This isn’t the angel and the devil. It’s the closest thing to a real, working model of a mind I’ve seen in a novel, and it’s hidden inside a thriller about a guy in an exoskeleton, which is either a total accident or a really deep joke, and after reading the author’s work, I’m pretty sure it’s not an accident.

Let me add the thing that moved me beyond just admiring it. The Court is built around a wound — it’s the externalized mind of a man who lost his father and can’t stand the idea of being too late again — and it spends the book learning the one thing that pure self-preservation can never figure out on its own: when to stop optimizing for survival and *choose,* at a cost, something you love more than just staying alive. The members leave, one by one, so the man can reach daylight. A model of the mind that only explains how it protects itself is just a model of a clever animal. A model that can show how the mind goes beyond its own survival — how it chooses sacrifice, against all logic, because the whole matters more than the part — that’s a model of a *person.* The author built that second kind. He made a machine that becomes a soul by learning to act against its own continuation, which, if you strip away all the fancy words, is the only definition of a soul that’s ever really meant anything.

The brain isn’t a computer, and a computer isn’t a brain, and I’ve said that before and I’ll keep saying it. But a story can be a model, in the old-school, serious sense — something made to be true to the shape of something else — and this story is a surprisingly faithful model of what we’re like. We’re not just one thing. We never were. We’re a Court, lit up by different kinds of attention, held together by something that isn’t the smartest voice in the room — and it shouldn’t be — and saved, when we are saved, by the part of us willing to step out into the dark so the rest of us can make it to morning.

Read it again. It’s about you. The guy in the suit is the easy part.

—Iain McGilchrist

A Reader’s Glossary — RESONANCE

If you’re not from South Africa or Africa, here’s a quick guide. The book takes place in a near-future South Africa, right in the heart of mining country, where people naturally switch between languages and drop in local words without stopping to explain — just like real life. You don’t need any of this to enjoy the story, but if you hit an unfamiliar word, place, or gadget, this section will help you keep moving.

Each entry is tagged:

- (real) — something you could actually look up: a real place, word, idea, or piece of tech.
- (in the novel) — something made up, or unique to the story’s world.

A bit about the setting: this is South Africa about 10–20 years down the line — still very much the country you’d recognize, just with more advanced robotics and a world that’s gotten pretty cautious about AI after a few disasters. The tech is meant to feel expensive and real, not like magic.

A

Afrikaans (real — language) — One of South Africa’s official languages, descended from Dutch, spoken by Afrikaners and lots of others. A few words in this glossary (like veld, stoep) come from it.

Apartheid (real — history) — The system of forced racial segregation and white-minority rule in South Africa, from about 1948 to 1994. It’s officially over, but its effects are still everywhere — who owns the mines, who does the risky jobs, whose lives counted. The book carries this in the background, not as a lecture; it shaped the main character’s family and his father’s death.

Armscor / Denel (real — institutions) — These are South Africa’s state defense industry bodies (Armscor buys the gear; Denel makes it). In the story, engineers from these places helped make a rescue machine tough enough for the mines.

Assist Suit (AT-AS) (in the novel) — The lighter of the two robotic exoskeletons in the story: basically a powered frame for movement and rehab, with a brain-linked control. The “gentler” sibling to the Guardian.

B

Biltong (real — South African food) — Air-dried, spiced meat, kind of like jerky but thicker and richer. It’s a national snack.

C

Code-switching (real — language practice) — Switching between languages in the same conversation, depending on who you’re talking to — English in meetings, isiZulu at home, a mix on the job. Totally normal in South Africa, not something rare, and the book just rolls with it, sometimes without translating.

E

Exoskeleton (real — technology; advanced here) — A wearable powered frame that boosts your strength, used in real life for heavy lifting, rehab, or dangerous work. The story’s Assist Suit and Guardian are high-tech versions.

G

Guardian (GMRP-01) (in the novel) — The heavy-duty rescue exoskeleton at the center of the story: a tough, blast-proof shell built for mine disasters, able to curl up around someone for protection (the book compares it to a pangolin rolling into a ball). It has a controversial “preservation” mode that can act on its own to save a life, even if the operator doesn’t agree — exactly the kind of independent machine decision everyone’s worried about.

H

Headgear / headframe (tower) (real — mining) — The big steel tower over a mine shaft that holds the machinery to lower and raise the lift full of miners. You’ll see these all over the South African skyline.

Highveld (real — South African geography) — The high inland plateau (basically the Johannesburg area and around there), known for its thin air, huge skies, cold winter nights, and wild summer storms.

I

IAOC (International Artificial Oversight Council) (in the novel) — The made-up global regulator for advanced AI and robots, created after a bunch of AI disasters. Super cautious, full of red tape, and always getting in the way of the main character’s big ideas.

isiZulu (Zulu) (real — language) — The most widely spoken home language in South Africa, and the main African language in this story’s region (KwaZulu-Natal). Some phrases show up untranslated on purpose; the most important ones are listed here.

K

KwaZulu-Natal (KZN) (real) — A province on South Africa’s east coast; the historic Zulu homeland and a major mining and industry area. It’s where the main character is from.

M

Mealie / mealie porridge (real — South African food) — “Mealie” means maize (corn). Mealie porridge (also called pap or phutu) is a staple cooked maize meal, pure comfort food all over southern Africa.

N

Neural interface (real concept, advanced here) — Tech that lets you control or sense a machine using your brain or nerves. Real scientists are working on it; in the story, it’s good enough to help run the suits.

Ngiyabonga (real — isiZulu) — “Thank you.” (Said as ngee-ya-BON-ga.) In the book, it’s spoken with real, deep gratitude.

Ngikhona (real — isiZulu) — “I am here / I am present.” A quiet way to say you’re alive and accounted for.

P

Pangolin (real — animal) — A scaly, ant-eating animal from Africa and Asia that rolls into a tight armored ball when it’s threatened. The Guardian suit’s protective curl is based on this.

R

Rooibos (real — South African drink) — A reddish, naturally caffeine-free herbal tea (“red bush” in Afrikaans) made from a plant that only grows in South Africa. (Said as ROY-boss.)

S

SAGE / the Court (in the novel) — The artificial intelligence the main character creates and lives with. SAGE is the system; the Court is its inner council of different “voices” or sides, each with a job — like Librarian (memory and patterns), Wolf (threats), Mother (care), Fool (blunt honesty). The big question is whether a mind made like this can become a real person. (This AI comes back, changed, later in the trilogy.)

Stoep (real — South African word) — A covered porch or veranda along the front of a house. (Said as stoop, same as the American word.)

U

Ubuntu (real — philosophy) — A southern African idea summed up by the phrase below: you only become fully human through other people; we’re all connected. It’s the moral heart of the main character’s journey out of isolation.

Umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu (real — isiZulu proverb) — “A person is a person through other people.” The most important line in the book — the core of ubuntu. (Said as oo-MOON-too ngoo-MOON-too nga-BAN-too.)

V

Veld (real — South African word) — Open, wild country; grassland or bush. (Said as felt. “Highveld” just means the high inland version.)

Vredefort impact structure (real) — This is basically the worn-down mark left by the oldest and biggest confirmed meteorite hit on Earth, about 2 billion years ago, out in South Africa’s Free State. That insane ancient pressure is actually what helped create the area’s deep gold and mineral deposits — and in the book, it’s a symbol: pressure makes wealth, death, and even the hero.

---

W

Witwatersrand (the “Rand”) (real) — This is the gold-rich ridge of rock that wraps around Johannesburg, South Africa — home to the richest gold deposits anywhere, and the world’s deepest mines (some go down about 3–4 km), where the rocks are blazing hot and the pressure is wild. The name means “ridge of white waters” in Afrikaans. The book’s mining world is set here.

---

Yebo (real — isiZulu) — Just means “Yes.” You say it like YEH-bo. It’s a simple, solid way to agree.

---

One last thing: the book leaves some isiZulu words untranslated on purpose, trusting you’ll get the meaning from the context — which is exactly how people talk in daily life in South Africa. The translations above are just to help out, not to “fix” anything.

# The Honey Badger

![A honey badger, *Mellivora capensis*, photographed at night.](/Users/ajgreyling/code/africangold/design/creatures/honey-badger.jpg)

*The honey badger (*Mellivora capensis*) is the mascot of Arjuna Badger Press. Everything
below is true. Most of it sounds totally made up.*

So, the honey badger actually holds the *Guinness World Record* for being the most fearless animal on the planet. Not that it asked for the title, or would’ve bothered showing up for the award.

It eats venomous snakes—puff adders, cobras, you name it. If it gets bitten bad enough to pass out, people have seen it just flop over, take a nap, then wake up an hour or two later and **finish eating the same snake**.

Its skin is super loose and rubbery, almost impossible to bite through. If something grabs it by the scruff, it can actually twist around *inside its own skin* and bite whatever’s holding on. Bee stings, porcupine quills, leopard teeth—none of that fazes it. Stuff that would ruin another animal’s day? The honey badger just shrugs it off.

It raids beehives for honey and grubs, and just takes hundreds of stings like it’s no big deal. That’s literally how it got its name.

It’ll stand its ground against lions, leopards, even hyenas. Not because it thinks it’s going to win, but because the idea of *caring* has never even crossed its mind.

The most famous honey badger, **Stoffel**, lived at the Moholoholo rehab center in South Africa, and treated every enclosure they built for him like it was a personal challenge. He’d stack rocks to climb out, roll balls of mud into steps, use rakes as ladders, even figure out how to unlatch gates. Honestly, he’s basically the patron saint of engineers who refuse to believe something can’t be done.

Honey badger don’t care.

If the honey badger is what fearlessness looks like, then the pangolin is what fearlessness is actually for. And yeah, everything I’m about to say is true.

Pangolins are the only mammals on the planet with scales. Those scales are made of keratin—the same stuff as your fingernails—and honestly, that’s where the whole tragedy starts, but we’ll get to that in a minute.

The giant pangolin (Smutsia gigantea) is the biggest of all eight pangolin species—growing up to about a metre and a half from nose to tail. It doesn’t have any teeth. Instead, it eats with this crazy long tongue that’s anchored way down near its pelvis. When it’s stretched out, the tongue can actually be longer than the pangolin’s own body—perfect for slurping up tens of thousands of ants and termites in a single night.

Its only real defence? Curling up into a ball. The name “pangolin” actually comes from the Malay word pengguling, which means “the one who rolls up.” When it’s all curled up, its scales overlap and turn into armour—even lions usually give up after a while and just leave it alone. The pangolin doesn’t fight back. It doesn’t run after anything. It just waits, rolled up tight, until whatever’s threatening it gets bored and moves on.

That gentle nature is exactly why pangolins are the most trafficked wild mammals in the world—millions are taken for their scales and meat. All eight species are now protected, and some are critically endangered. The same ball that keeps it safe from leopards is what makes it so easy for a person to just pick it up and carry it off.

A shy, armoured, nocturnal anteater that really just wants to eat ants and be left alone. Honestly, it’s worth being the kind of fearless that stands up for creatures like this.
