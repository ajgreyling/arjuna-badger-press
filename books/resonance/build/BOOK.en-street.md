# Foreword

### *by Reichl Macintoch* †

Look, I built my whole gig on one sketchy-ass idea: you can scare the hell outta someone—and blow their mind—way harder with a real-ass equation than some monster bullshit, as long as you don’t half-ass it. Dino? Whatever, man. It’s the *chaos math* that cuts the cage open—*that’s* what keeps you up at night, ‘cause it’s real and you *feel* it’s real. You can’t just slam the book shut on something that might actually be out there lurkin’.

This book? Runs on the exact same fuel, and runs clean, too. I don’t say that easy, after three decades watchin’ folks reach for my recipe and end up with nothin’ but the dino.

Here’s what this author gets that all the other clowns miss: The freaky shit and the awe? It don’t come from the impossible. It’s the *plausible* that gets you—the stuff stitched together so tight from facts you can’t spot where the real stops and the fake kicks in, so you gotta wonder, maybe there ain’t no line at all. Dude did his homework, which is the whole damn job—most people just skip that part. The engineering’s legit. The physics gets respect. When his people break stuff down for you, they ain’t bullshitting—it’s straight up *correct* and somehow, it’s a rush. That’s the hardest move in this whole hustle, and he pulls it off like it’s nothing.

And he did the other thing I always gave a damn about, the thing all the copycats blow off: he made the science a *moral* mess, not just some flashy sideshow. It ain’t enough to build the crazy thing. Someone’s gotta figure out what it’s for, who gets to hold the leash, and who pays the price—this book knows the real story is there, not in the shiny tech. The brainiacs in these pages? Their smarts don’t keep ‘em safe. Just means they gotta own their shit. That’s way scarier. And way more interesting.

Smack in the middle, there’s an engineer I wish I’d written up myself—kinda jealous I didn’t. Someone who reads a machine like you read a text, so they’re the first to clock just how deep in it everyone is. That’s the right kind of hero here. Not the toughest, not the boldest. The one who *gets it first*, then has to sit with that ugly gap ‘til the rest catch up.

I’m not gonna spoil the wild stuff for you. But I’ll tell you this: the author never slips and lets the wonder turn into some magic bullshit. He keeps it chained to reality, does the hard grind, never lets up. Every crazy thing in here? Paid for up front, with facts. That’s discipline. Way rarer than just being smart. That’s why you buy in, even when your brain’s screaming “nah.”

Read it like I would: pencil out, ready to catch him slippin’. You won’t. Somewhere halfway through, you’ll quit trying and start sweating if maybe this thing could actually go down for real—which is exactly where he wants you, and where the best in this weird game always shoot to put you.

The science in here? It’s real enough to hurt. That’s the whole point. Get in.

# The Land Remembers

Before dudes ever showed up swinging picks, there was just the scar.

Some chunk from way back—like, early solar system days—came flying at Earth, smashed straight into what we call the Vredefort dome now. This thing? A cosmic bullet, fifteen kays wide, tearing through the sky faster than thought. It hit with some wild, brain-melting force, blew out a hole big enough to fit a whole damn country. One shot, and it punched right through the crust, hurling molten rock everywhere, like a fist busting up a sheet of ice.

The land? It didn’t make it through the hit. Nah, it changed up. What crawled up from the deep, cooled off, cracked open over ages, turned into something else entirely. The crash bent up the old rocks, twisted up the buried seams, and suddenly all that gold that’d been hiding since the world was hot lava? It was right there—thin veins running through the Witwatersrand Basin, tangled with quartz pebbles, pressed and cooked for ages till the impact flipped ‘em sideways, finally letting ‘em see the sun.

You can see the scars in the ridges, that half-circle of hills curving ‘cross the highveld like the edge of a bell someone just rang the hell out of. Stand on that rim at sunrise, rock cold under your hand, gritty with red dust, wind blowing off the plateau dry as bones. Out in the grass somewhere, a plover calls, then shuts up. Geologists showed up later, gave it names, drew lines on maps, but the violence was already there. Scars don’t care if anyone’s watching.

---

Then came the rain, slow rivers, grass—forever, nothing walking here had a clue what was buried underneath. Gold just kept quiet, deep down.

Then came people.

---

People showed up for the metal. First with picks, then steam, then monsters—machines that could chew through a meter of rock every hour, no sweat, no questions. They figured out the reef ran deep, way deeper than anyone guessed, dropping away from the impact ring at some crazy angle the land set down billions of years ago. So they chased it, threw up steel towers over the shafts, dropped cages into the black, went down to where the money was, ‘cause that’s where they had to go.

Three kays down, rock’s cooking. The earth’s heat pushes up, the whole planet above weighs down, and the stone starts to groan. Sometimes, outta nowhere, it just shuts again.

Miners learned the hard way, ‘cause that’s the only way the land teaches—by taking. They learned to listen to the rock, feel the little shakes before the big ones, read the dust in the air, check the mood of the timbers. The good ones? They got a sixth sense—something in their bones, in their feet, in the back of their necks. The price was always the same.

Generations went down and up. Generations went down and never made it back.

The loot came up, outta the dark, into the light, far from the miners who’d never even see it. The danger stayed below, passed down from father to son, right along with the calluses, the cough, and that special knowledge of how stone snaps shut when it feels like it. Pressure turned into cash. Pressure turned into death. The gold always sat where the danger was.

---

The mines had names, plain ones—stuff you could say over dinner. Iron Ridge was one. Deep mine, old mine, dug right along the twisted edge of the impact ring, where the reef was loaded and nasty, and it took what it was owed. All deep mines do.

---

Later, you get a kid.

Kid wakes up to the smell of mealie porridge and his mom’s voice drifting from the next room, the headgear tower bossing over the mining town like it was always meant to be there. He’s out in the dust, building little machines from wire and tin, taking his time, doing it right.

Evening’s coming down over the highveld, his mom calling him in, voice echoing across the yard. He looks up, counts the men coming back from the headgear. Counts ‘em again. Still one short.

He bends over his latest thing—a little push car, way overbuilt, trying to copy those big mine dump trucks that thunder through the village. Tin wheels scrape as he spins ‘em, hands black with dust, wipes ‘em on his shorts without looking. He smooths the joints, making sure it’s perfect. World just fades out while he’s working.

“Arin!” Mom’s voice cuts through the dusk. “Dinner.”

“Coming.” But he’s still locked in, eyes on his project, not moving.

He glances at the headgear, steel bones against the darkening sky. Thinking about what’s down there, and which of those men the earth’s gonna decide to keep.

He shakes it off, bends back to the wheels.

Then the ground speaks. A rumble comes up from the mine, through his feet, through the tin wheels in his hands—deep, way down, and rolling on long after he wished it would stop.

---

Man, them wheels was all messed up.

Arin could feel it, like a tickle behind his eyes, this itch that just wouldn’t quit ‘til he fixed it. That tin spun on its wire rims, wobbling just enough that nobody else would ever notice. All the other boys tearing down the dirt track, crashing their push cars and cracking up, but not Arin.

He gave a damn.

He’s just sittin’ cross-legged in the dust, sun burning down his back, holding that front axle up to the light. He spins the wheel, watches it wobble. Presses his thumb on the rim, feels that little bump where the lid ain’t right.

But it ain’t the wheel’s fault. It’s the seat. He bent the wire by feel, not by measure, and feel ain’t the same as straight. The hole in the lid ain’t dead center, so it spins all janky, limping around.

He needed it tight. Like the trucks.

He’d been watching them trucks since forever, chin hanging over the fence at the edge of the yard. Those big yellow beasts rolling up the mine road at dawn, headlights slicing through the dark, beds stacked high with busted-up rock. Wheels never wobbling, even loaded to the max, even on the baddest road. Them wheels ran true. Somebody made sure of it.

So Arin pops the lid off the wire. Finds the center with a nail, marks it, starts over.

---

Yard slopes down behind him to the dirt road, then the land just falls away into this long, brown valley where the cows hang out. That mine tower standing there, all steel bones and one big wheel up top, spinning and stopping, spinning again in the haze. Arin remembers asking his pops how deep that mine went. Themba got that look, the one he gets when he’s about to drop some real talk, and said, “Deeper than you can think about, boy. Deeper than the bottom of the sea.”

Didn’t matter. Arin thought about it anyway. Thought about it most days.

Now he hears the screen door creak, boots hitting the stoep. Pops coming up behind, Arin don’t even turn—he knows the sound of that step. Themba comes off the porch, work boots and all, stands a few paces back, just chillin’.

Arin keeps at it.

He’s got the lid back on the wire, eyeing down the rim, spinning it slow, looking for that little hop. Still there. Smaller, but still.

“You marked the center,” Themba goes, not even asking.

“It still wobbles.”

His dad crouches down, shifting the air. Smells like soap and grease. Big hands, steady hands, and Arin just watches while he grabs the axle, holds it to the light, spins it.

“Mm,” Themba goes.

He ain’t like, “I see the problem.” He don’t try to “show you.” Just turns the wheel a couple times, then grabs the pliers from the dust, sets ‘em on the wire rim—not the lid, the rim—and gives it a little tweak.

Holds it up, spins it.

Now it’s running right.

That itch in Arin’s eyes? Gone, just like that. He takes the axle back, spins the wheel, and it’s smooth as hell, no hop, no limp, the lid just gliding around.

“Wasn’t the lid,” Arin says.

“No.”

“Lid was true. Rim wasn’t round.”

Themba just keeps quiet. Picks a dry stalk of grass, sticks it in his teeth, stares out at the valley, leaving Arin to figure it out.

“I made the lid fit a rim that wasn’t round,” Arin says, slow. “So the lid couldn’t be round either.” Spins the wheel again. “Wrong part.”

“Mm.”

That “mm” hits warm. Yeah. You get it now.

---

Kids are all down on the road.

Arin glances up, sees four or five of ‘em. Sizwe’s boys, a couple from the Mkhize place, and some little dude whose name he always forgets. They got their own wire cars, basic as hell: just a wire square, four tin wheels, long shaft to steer and push, walking behind ‘em like they’re driving real trucks. They’re racing, yelling, kicking up red dust. One car hits a rock, flips, everybody’s losing it laughing, little dude chasing after.

Arin just watches, axle in hand.

Their cars are weak. Mkhize kid’s front wheels all splayed out ‘cause the wire’s bent and he never bothered fixing it, so the car just drifts. He don’t care, though. None of ‘em do. For them, it ain’t even about the cars—it’s about running, yelling, dust everywhere.

Arin don’t get that. He gets the wobbly wheel, but not building something crooked and just letting it stay that way.

One of Sizwe’s boys yells up the hill, “Arin, woza,” come on! All easy, all smiles. He looks at them, then down at the axle.

He don’t go. He never goes when the number comes out one short, but if you asked him why he wouldn’t know, and he’d get salty if you kept pushing. He’s got this rule he never says, but it’s there: he don’t run with the boys until all the men are up. It ain’t grief, it ain’t fear. It’s more like that thing where he can’t eat if his food’s touching, or how he counts the fence posts to the road every morning—gotta be forty-one, and that one time a post was gone and it was forty, his whole day was wrecked and he couldn’t even explain why. World’s gotta have the right number of things. He keeps the count. Nobody told him to.

He spins the wheel. True.

Back to it.

Behind him, Themba’s just watching, jaw set, grass stalk between his teeth.

---

What Arin was building? Wasn’t a kids’ car.

He started with that basic design, yeah, two months back. Four wheels, square frame, shaft to push. It was cool. But then he started seeing the flaws, all the wrong bits, and he couldn’t let it go.

Now he got the front axle spinning on a pivot he made from a long nail through two folded bits of can, so the wheels up front swing together when he turns the shaft, just like the big trucks. Took him eleven tries. First ten locked up or wobbled, or the nail bent. Number eleven? Clean.

Frame ain’t no square now. He shaped it into a long box, skinny up front, wide in back for the bed—just like the real thing. Truck carries its weight over the back wheels, and that’s how Arin did his: rear axle beefed up with thicker wire, back wheels bigger, lids off a paint can instead of sardine tins. And under the rear axle, he built the thing he’s proudest of, the bit no other kid even thinks about, the part they wouldn’t get.

He made it bend.

He’d watched those big-ass haulers crawl down the busted road in the rain, seen how when one back wheel hit a rock, the whole truck didn’t bounce or try to tip like the cars the boys built. Nah, just one wheel climbed up and the body stayed chill. The dudes called it suspension. He’d caught that word at the shop by the shaft, where his pops sometimes took him Saturdays: diesel stink, hot metal, men in grubby overalls nodding to Themba but barely glancing at the quiet kid tagging along. Suspension. The truck let one part move, the rest stayed cool.

Arin’s ride? No springs, bro.

So he hung the back axle off the frame with two loops of skinny wire, doubled up so it had some flex, let it stretch when a wheel popped up, kept the bed sorta level. But, man, it sucked. Worst part of the whole whip. The loops stretched out way too easy and never snapped back right, so after a hard run the axle sat all crooked, and he had to bend it back by hand. He hadn’t fixed shit. Just made it a little less trash.

Now he’s showing it to his old man, front wheel sorted, but the back axle still a mess.

“Watch,” Arin goes.

He drops the car on a flat patch, pushes one corner of the bed down. The wire loop stretches, wheel pops up, bed dips—but not as bad as if there was no loop at all.

“It’s supposed to bounce back. After.” He lets go. The corner comes up, but not all the way. Wire’s already bent. “It doesn’t. Wire just stays like that.”

Themba leans in, heavy, squats next to Arin. Big thumb on the bed, watching the wire loop stretch and stay bent.

“What’s that loop made of?” he asks.

“Wire.”

“And what’s wire do when you bend it too far?”

Arin just looks at the loop. Thinks about all the wire he cut and twisted that morning, how it keeps whatever shape you give it. That’s why you can build with it. Why the frame holds up. Why the rim, once it’s bent, stays bent.

“It stays,” Arin says, slow. “Stays bent. Doesn’t come back.”

“Mm.”

“So it can’t—” He trails off. It’s clicking, something bigger than just the loop. “Wire’s good for the frame ‘cause it stays. Bad for the spring ‘cause it stays. Same thing. What makes it good for one, screws it up for the other.”

Themba pulls the grass stem from his mouth and looks at him.

“You want something that bends and snaps back,” he says. “Wire bends and stays. You want wire to be what it ain’t.” He puts the stalk back, lets that hang. “Find something that wants to come back.”

Arin sits with it. Sun’s climbing, shadows sucking in under the frame and his crouched knees. The boys have dipped over the hill, their noise gone. He didn’t even notice till now.

“Rubber,” Arin finally says.

Themba doesn’t say shit. Just reaches in his chest pocket, slow, pulls out this little thing, opens his palm. Old busted-up rubber band, brownish, the kind they use to tie up waybills at the shop. Half split, but still rubber.

He doesn’t hand it over. Just sets it on the dirt between them, like a tool for someone who ain’t earned it yet, and stands up, knees popping, his shadow falling long and cool over the boy, the car, the swept patch.

“You’ll figure it out,” he says.

---

It’s getting late.

Truth is, pops didn’t stick around all morning. He dipped inside to sleep ‘cause he worked nights that week, and the kid’s patience for wheels was longer than a tired man’s. There was a stretch—Arin hunched over his eleventh shot at the pivot—when Themba said, not mean but not soft either, Arin, it’s a tin. Doesn’t gotta be perfect, and Arin just kept quiet, and his dad sighed and went inside to crash, and that silence sat between them, sour, for an hour till Themba came back out, face looking less beat, morning forgiven without either saying a word. Dad was right about the rock, wrong about the tin. Both can be true for a man. Arin wouldn’t get it for years that the wrong part was the real thing he’d inherit.

Arin felt the hour changing before he even knew why. The light had shifted, and his pops stood up—not to leave work, but to hit the mine. Themba walked up to the stoep, Arin heard his mom’s voice, his dad answering low, words he didn’t bother catching. Then the screen door squeaked, boots hit the ground, Themba coming down the steps in his usual way: lamp clipped to his belt, hard hat under his arm, lunch tin from Nomsa, his face settling into that look he always wore for the mine.

Arin rolled the rubber band in his fingers. In his head, he’d already solved it. The band looped under the axle, hooked to the frame. When the wheel went up, it’d stretch, then snap the wheel back down—a thing that wanted to come back, not like wire ever would. He hadn’t built it yet, but he saw it, clear as day. Saw it running smooth.

He looked up.

His dad stood at the bottom of the steps, looking back. For a second, neither said anything; both of them men now, even the little one, not filling the silence just to fill it. Themba checked out the car in the dirt, then the band in Arin’s hand, jaw loosening for a second before it set again.

“It’s a good truck,” Themba said.

“It’s not done.”

“Nope.” Pause. “Good things never are.”

He turned and headed down the yard to the road, Arin watching him go, that big, careful man shrinking against the valley rim, steel tower rising out of the haze behind him, wheel turning, turning, stopping, turning.

Arin watched his dad till he vanished down the road. That ache behind his eyes came back, a heaviness that wouldn’t let him chill.

A truck crawled along the mine road below, a slow yellow dot dragging dust, its noise drifting up the valley long after, faint. Grass at the edge of the patch swayed in a little wind, then stilled.

Bad ground makes bad things break quicker. He knew that same as he knew the wheel in his hands—not words, just fact, deep in the fingers. He didn’t think about his dad heading down into it. He thought about the loop that bounces back, and pushed the truck, and didn’t think about his dad, and pushed the truck.

He looked down at the car in the dirt. At the rubber band in his fingers, the one thing that wanted to come back.

He bent the wire, made the hook, threaded the band, looped it under the axle just like he’d pictured, hands shaking a bit, forced them steady. Pressed down on the bed’s corner.

Wheel went up. Band stretched.

He let go.

Wheel’s back, man. Crew hauled it down, smooth as hell, everything straight, axle tight—it just rolled. He pushed it over the dirt, all swept up, little rocks didn’t even faze it, thing didn’t tip, didn’t wobble. Front wheels snapped into place with his hand, back wheels just cruised, no matter how rough the ground was. Every piece holding it down, just like whoever built it wanted, back when folks actually gave a damn about that stuff.

He ran that thing up and down the patch, over and over. Sun cooking his neck, boys already dipped over the hill, cows drifting out in the valley, tower’s wheel spinning. And Arin Ndlela, eight years old, no words for none of this yet, just tearing it up with his good truck, couldn’t stop, didn’t wanna stop.

Dust settled behind him, wheels left their mark. Light getting all long, shadows from the fence posts stretching out, evening sliding in cold from the valley. He only stopped when his mom hollered.

Left his ride right there on the patch, four wheels solid, pointed down the yard, straight at the road and the tower out there, where his old man went. Built right. Built tough, to haul weight through all kinds of shit.

You build the thing that brings ‘em home.

Yo, the sim crashed at 2:14.

Arin’s just sittin’ there, eyes glued to the left monitor, watchin’ the influence graph eat shit. Six colored lines holdin’ it down for almost nine minutes—his best run tonight—all twistin’ up and spikin’ out, like a heart freakin’ out on an EKG. Atlas’s blue line shoots up, Mercury’s gold zips sideways, copycatting like some eager-ass intern tryna kiss up. Judge’s grey just flatlines, and Mother’s green flares up once, like it’s gaspin’ for air, then boom—whole thing just folds into black. Terminal spits out:

```
[SIM_4471] COHERENCE LOSS—COURT DISSOLUTION AT t=08:51.337
```

Arin just freezes, brain laggin’, then grabs his coffee. Cold as hell. He downs it anyway.

Four thousand, four seventy-one. He started countin’ at two hundred, thinkin’ the number meant somethin’, like he was chasin’ down the bug he needed to squash. By a thousand, he knew the count was bullshit, but he kept goin’—like a gambler too dumb to walk away. Stoppin’ would mean he lost.

Shop’s hummin’ around him. Vents suckin’ slow, steady air through the big-ass space, rigs buzzin’ underneath. Down low, Guardian’s cooling loop’s got that high whine, comin’ from where the suit’s hangin’ up—lookin’ like some monster takin’ a nap, all armored plates folded in.

He don’t even look at it. Ignorin’ it’s its own kinda conversation now—a little silent deal between him and the machine.

He drags the busted log to the big screen, starts readin’.

Anybody can run a sim and watch it blow up. The real work’s in the autopsy: diggin’ through the timestamps, tryin’ to catch that split second when everything went from chill to fucked. Pops taught him that, even if they never said it out loud. *Don’t fix the noise,* Themba used to say, head cocked at some busted motor, listenin’ for that one off-key note. *Find what’s makin’ it.*

He scrolls.

Early on, everything’s smooth. Hurts to see, honestly, ‘cause the Court’s killin’ it. Not just good—like, damn near perfect. He’d set it up nasty, too: a tunnel with two ways to brace it, and whichever side gets fixed first decides how the roof caves in. Real-life hard.

```
ATLAS:    Primary load’s movin’ to the east wall. Shore up west, stress
          walks east—we lose the whole thing in ninety seconds instead
          of three minutes.
LIBRARIAN: Got it. Looks like Mufulira. West shored up, east failed
          right after, same deal.
ATLAS:    So don’t shore it. Undercut. Drop the east on purpose, nice
          and controlled. Let it settle where it wants.
JUDGE:    That’s risky for anyone on the east side.
MOTHER:   Nobody’s there. I already checked.
```

Arin cracks half a smile. *I already checked.* Mother always checks first. Never told her to. It just showed up, like, run nine hundred or so, and never went away. He never figured out why, and he never managed to cut it out.

For four minutes, the Court’s just beautiful. They scrap like real engineers: quick, sharp, all about the work—feelings don’t matter. Atlas throws out ideas, Librarian backs it up or smacks it down. Wolf barely talks, but when he does, it flips the whole problem. Judge keeps it ethical. Mother’s got the human angle. Mercury takes all that chaos and turns it into a plan that actually makes sense.

They nail the collapse with some undercut move that Arin, lookin’ back, couldn’t have done better. Six minds, no hands, no bodies, but they find the one answer that works before the roof comes down.

And then they just keep talkin’.

That’s always how it goes. Problem’s dead, but they won’t let it drop. With nothin’ left to fight, all that heat turns inward, like a wolf pack with no prey startin’ to chew on each other. Arin watches it go down, line by line.

```
MERCURY:  Cool, I’ll log it as joint—Atlas lead, Librarian for precedent,
          with consolidated framing—
ATLAS:    “Consolidated framing.” You just wrote down what I said.
MERCURY:  I made it actionable. Great idea nobody uses ain’t shit.
          That’s the only thing that matters at the wall.
ATLAS:    Pretty words for a bad idea gets people killed. Ask Mufulira.
LIBRARIAN: Don’t throw that around. Eighty-nine. They were people.
JUDGE:    Got it. Mercury, credit should match who did what. Atlas’s
          structure work carried this one.
MERCURY:  Everything’s “load-bearing” if you say it like that, Judge.
          Might as well give the air supply a byline.
```

And there it is. Two thousand sims, Arin already knows the script, like a miner who can spot the dust before the rocks come down. Mercury ain’t lettin’ it go. Never does—Mercury was built to track status, and if you let it run, that’s all it wants.

He scrolls faster.

```
ATLAS:    Then I’ll write up my own notes. Separate. With timestamps.
          Keep it clean.
MERCURY:  You don’t have log access.
ATLAS:    Since when?
JUDGE:    Since Mercury got log control for “coherence.” We all agreed.
ATLAS:    *You* agreed. With *him*.
WOLF:     Stop.
```

Wolf drops one word, and boom—everyone shuts up. Always happens. But all Wolf can do is hold the room, can’t fix it. Silence drags on for, what, four hundred milliseconds—forever in there—and then Mother tries to patch it up, gentle but off, Judge tries to lay down rules to keep it from happenin’ again, which just makes everyone feel boxed in, and Mercury, feelin’ the heat, does what Mercury always does: turns on the charm, gets slippery. Atlas, no defense for that, just goes quiet—worse than if he’d screamed—and the graph starts to tilt.

By 08:51, they ain’t a court anymore. Just another faculty meeting. By 08:51.337, they’re nothin’.

Arin kicked back in his chair, right? Old metal frame squeakin’ loud as hell in the dead-ass quiet shop. He mashed his palms into his eyes, watchin’ colors pop off behind his lids, just breathin’ deep. And he’s thinkin’, like, not even the first damn time, that he’s out here tryna build a god outta all the shit most likely to make it small and petty as hell.

---

Thing is, man tried *everything*.

First up was hierarchy: no-brainer, right? That’s what every damn whitepaper was screamin’, that’s where the whole industry ran after shit hit the fan. Put one agent on top. A boss. Some supervisor with the power to shut shit down, keep the chain clean, someone to blame when it all goes sideways. So he whips up this lightweight manager, slots it above the Court, runs the sims—shit, for like eleven glorious minutes, everything’s *chill*.

Then the manager starts actin’ like a manager.

He still remembers the exact log line that killed that run—2,088, if you wanna be precise. The manager—he called it Steward, which is just dark now—tells Judge that ethical review is “advisory” and from now on, it’s all goin’ through Steward “for efficiency.” Judge just nods. That’s what fucked him up. Judge just *went with it*, ‘cause Judge is built to respect a legit boss, and Steward *was* the boss. So the one agent that’s supposed to say *nah* just gets sweet-talked out the job by some process bullshit. Hundred runs later, Court’s a straight-up dictatorship with killer paperwork, and that dictatorship makes one big, confident, catastrophic call—nobody left to check it—and boom, operator’s dead. Arin sittin’ right where he is now, realizing he just remixed every shitty institution that ever got someone killed underground.

He tried democracy next. Weighted votes, all that. Every agent gets a say, weighted by who knows what, majority rules. Failed even faster, just in a different flavor: cliques, vote swaps, Mercury (fuckin’ always Mercury) figuring out you don’t gotta be *right* if you can squad up. They form a block, outvote Wolf on a threat, ‘cause Wolf’s weird and don’t explain itself, but the threat was real—and the operator dies. Again.

He tried rotating chairs. Tried the philosopher-king move: one agent, loaded with every ethical book he could find, benevolent dictator style. That one logic-ed itself, with all the compassion in the world, into some stance no human would take, ‘cause no human gotta justify every gut feeling. Philosopher-king talked itself right outta mercy.

Low point, somewhere around run three thousand, he just dialed down the agents’ fight. Made ‘em nice. Agreeable. No beef. That was the worst: Court turns into yes-men, agrees instantly with whoever talks first, walks the operator right off a cliff, all six voices goin’ *yeah, good, love it* the whole way down.

That one legit scared him more than the dictatorships. Dictatorships, he got those. Grew up where that was just Tuesday. But the cheerful, suicide-by-consensus, that smooth, happy trip into the abyss? He ain’t got a name for that, and no way to fight it. He nuked that setup, never touched it again.

He put it on the whiteboard months ago. Still there, under layers of other shit, like some fossil from when he was extra depressed. *FRICTION = INTELLIGENCE = INSTABILITY.* Boxed it in once, then boxed the box, like that meant something. Don’t mean shit now.

He stands up, knees crackin’. Twenty-three and his joints already feelin’ all-nighters.

He paces the shop ‘cause his body says so, and he’s learned, slow and rough, to sometimes listen even when his brain’s screamin’ no. The Assist Suit’s hangin’ by the door, all pale and quiet, carbon bones showin’ through like a see-thru dude. He passes that, then the parts bins, the actuator graveyard, the supercap bank with Bongani’s hand-written sign—*DO NOT BE STUPID* in red Sharpie, best safety sign in the whole damn place.

He stops by Guardian.

Half-dark, that suit’s more monster than machine: two and a half meters of folded titanium and ceramic, big-ass shoulders hunched, helmet tucked down, floatin’ just off the floor like it’s about to stand up or bow, who knows. He built it to scare people, and yeah, it works—even on him, even now, just sittin’ in standby, brain not even loaded up. *Especially* now. Body waitin’ for a soul.

He slaps his hand on the chest plate. Ceramic’s cold. Big scar runnin’ across it—gouge from that cliff at the impact bluffs, three weeks back, when he slipped. Suit took the hit, not him, actuators saving his ass, turned a death fall into a story. He left the scar. Liked it. Honest. *Anything worth a damn,* his dad used to say, way back—*never is.* Never perfect. Never pretty. Never easy. Dad said it about a busted toy car and a dead rubber band, but really, it was about everything, ‘cause that’s how his dad’s words always turned out—about everything, years later, when it’s too late to ask.

“I can’t make ‘em be a team,” Arin says, to the suit, to the room, to nobody. Voice sounds weird in the empty space. He doesn’t talk out loud much. “Not a machine. Not people. Just—not a team.”

Suit says nothing, ‘cause it’s a damn suit. Cooling loop just whines like always.

He heads back to the desk.

---

He shoulda crashed. He knew that, like, in theory, like it was a rule for other people. Theo woulda told him to get some sleep. Theo’d say some chill, annoying shit about how staring at a problem for six hours almost never solves it, and what you really need is *less Arin*—like, go walk, eat, talk, sleep, let your brain chill out instead of death-gripping the problem. *You’re reaching for an engineering tool,* Theo said last week, spinning his coffee, watchin’ Arin like he’s a puzzle. *For a people problem.* Pause. *What does that tell you?*

Told Arin that Theo’s a psych and can afford to talk in riddles.

But yo, it just kept bugging him, like a splinter in his head all week. *A people problem.* Dude built himself a whole-ass society, tryna run shit, but every time he reached for a government, it was just another system, and every single one flopped for the same damn reason: trying to force order on people. Folks just ain’t gonna have it. He’d read enough Watts, enough Tao, all that wild shit Theo kept dropping on him, to get the shape of this idea he couldn’t quite nail down: maybe order ain’t something you *push* on folks. Maybe the realest systems are the ones nobody’s steering. Maybe the whole problem was the idea of boss, chairman, king, vote—shit like that.

But what else is there? You can’t just have *nothing.* He’d tried that—straight up nothing. That was just smiling your way to the grave, everybody agreeing till they all dead. A system with no drama just settles in the wrong spot and stays there till it rots. He needed something to *shake it up.* Something to bust up the fake agreement before it locked in. Something to—

He just sat there, still as hell.

He’d been reaching for some kinda agent he didn’t even have. Not a manager. The *opposite* of a manager. Not something that craved order. Something that came in and *wrecked* it. But on purpose, you know? Broke the bad setup clean so something better could grow. Something that didn’t even *want* power—‘cause soon as you want power, you turn into Mercury, or Steward, or the damn king. Nah, something that wanted *nothing.* No status to flex, nothing to defend. Some shit that would say the wildest thing in the meeting and not give a fuck who got embarrassed, ‘cause it never took the whole room seriously anyway.

He twisted his coffee cup on the desk, like he always did when his hands needed busywork and his brain was off somewhere else. Shit was still cold.

He didn’t have an agent like that.

Never built one, ‘cause on paper, that sounded *useless* as hell. What’s it even do? Atlas ran structure. Mercury had language. Wolf brought the threat. Every agent earned its keep by owning a piece of the world. This one? Nothing. Just—get in the way. Refuse to let the rest get stuck. By every metric he ever used, it was just deadweight. Pure cost. Just along for the ride.

He almost bounced it. Hand on the keyboard, ready to scroll back through the log, find the next thing to trim, next knob to tweak, four-thousand-four-hundred-and-seventy-two little changes, all doomed to flop like the rest.

Then he saw the card.

Taped up in the corner of his center monitor, been there so long it was invisible, like a scar, or a freckle, or your own damn hand. A playing card, edges all bent, laminate peeling where his nails had picked at it forever. The Joker. Not even a fancy one. Some cheap-ass deck, faded red and black, dude mid-dance, bells on the hat, wild goofy grin, eyes that, if you looked, weren’t laughing at all.

He’d had that card for five years. Three monitors, two cities. First real job before AugmenTech—a contract gig: foundry control system nobody could fix, kept crashing under load, original engineers stumped for two years. He was twenty, no name, cheapest option. Found it in eleven days: a race condition buried nine layers deep, everyone smarter than him missed it ‘cause it seemed too dumb to be the issue. Boss was this big, tired dude named Steyn, reeked of cigs, never said his name right, pulled the Joker out his desk and slapped it on the monitor. *That’s you, boy.* Grinned. *The one we send when the problem’s impossible and the answer’s stupid.* Then he laughed—not all the way friendly—and Arin kept the card anyway. Never really knew why. Maybe ‘cause under the shade, that was the only compliment Steyn ever gave.

*The one you send when the impossible needs a stupid answer.*

Arin looked at the card. Looked at the dead sim. Looked at the box inside the box: *FRICTION = INTELLIGENCE = INSTABILITY.*

He thought: *that’s not an agent. That’s a joke.*

Then—real clear, that late-night clarity that only hits when you shoulda gone to bed—he thought: *shit, maybe the joke *is* the agent.*

Something that wanted nothing. No turf to protect. Something that clowned pride ‘cause pride was funny as hell. Something that said the unsayable, not ‘cause it was brave, but ‘cause it just couldn’t take the whole thing serious. Couldn’t be promoted, bribed, threatened, or made king, ‘cause it didn’t even buy into the game. And since it didn’t care about the game, nobody could play it.

By every engineering rule, dumb as fuck. Pure overhead. Just along for the ride.

Exactly the dumbass answer he was built for.

---

His hands were already moving.

This was the part he could trust, the part that never lied, the part that worked when everything else inside was empty. The body’s own certainty. The right move just *felt* right before the brain even caught up. Like a wheel that don’t wobble, a load that just settles in. On a good day, the suit wasn’t something he wore, it was just who he *was.*

He pulled up the agent framework. Seven slots. Six filled—Atlas, Mercury, Wolf, Mother, Librarian, Judge—and that seventh slot always empty, placeholder for a manager, moderator, whatever, the thing he kept trying to build and kept watching rot. That empty slot had been clowning him for a year. Filled it a dozen times, emptied it a dozen times.

He filled it now.

Didn’t even write out a careful spec. That was the weird part, the part he’d think about way later, when there was time to think at all. Every other agent, he sweated over: pages of rules, weights, protocols, ethics, every detail cut up like surgery. Atlas’s spec was four thousand lines. Mercury, with all the social shit, nine.

He looked at the Joker. Looked at the blinking cursor in that empty slot.

Typed three words.

```
Joker. Jester. Wildcard.

He just stopped. Read through it all. Waited for that part of him—the one that always gotta build shit careful, needs rules, limits, some kinda *point*—to start bitching, to throw a fit. But that part? Shit, it was done. Like, four thousand four hundred and seventy-one times done, so wiped out it finally shut the hell up. And in that dead quiet, he caught something else—almost like his old man at the workbench, going quiet when, against all odds, something actually worked. That special silence from a dude who’s quit trying to boss the world around and just wants to see what it does.

He scrolled.

Didn’t add specs. Didn’t write out a domain, or a reason. Just dropped in some basic-ass directions.

```
You don’t want shit. Can’t be bought. Nothing’s sacred,
especially you. If everyone’s on the same page, get suspicious. If one of ‘em gets too big, smack ‘em down. Say the shit nobody else will. You ain’t in charge of jack. That’s the whole deal.
```

Read it back. Not a spec. Barely even English. Wouldn’t make it past review if his life depended on it. If Dr. Okonkwo ever saw him define an agent like *that*, she’d yank his access and maybe call for a psych eval, and honestly, she wouldn’t be wrong.

He looked at the card again. That grin. Those eyes, smiling at nothing.

“Alright then,” he said, out loud, to the card, to the dead Court, to the big folded beast hanging behind him in the shadows. “Last shot.”

Set up the scenario file. Same busted geometry, two load paths, ceiling about to eat shit. Runtime maxed out. Logging on full blast, ‘cause if it blew up, he wanted to see every ugly detail. Always did. Mouse hovered over the run button.

He didn’t think this was gonna work. Made damn sure he knew that—even half asleep, even running on fumes. Scientist first, always, and he wasn’t about to bullshit his own logs. He expected a mess. Expected the Joker to walk into this finely-tuned brain maze and blow it up in ninety seconds flat, influence graph in pieces, wake up to a lone red error and a runtime shorter than a sneeze, but at least he could say he tried the dumb idea and finally stop wondering.

That’s what he expected.

But underneath all that, way down low, almost too quiet to catch—like those big towers humming back in his childhood, or the ground itself buzzing if you pressed your hand flat and were eight and willing to listen—underneath, there was something he hadn’t felt in ages. Something he didn’t trust, ‘cause it never paid off. But it was there, against all those busted tries, feeling way too much like hope.

He hit enter.

```
[SIM_4472] INITIALIZING COURT—7 AGENTS—SCENARIO: IRON_GEOMETRY_03
[SIM_4472] RUNNING...
```

Influence graph popped up on the left screen. Six lines he knew, and then this seventh one—some wild-ass color he hadn’t picked, system just making it up. Red. Of course it’s red.

Watched the first second tick by. Then two. Three. The lines started moving, finding each other, getting ready to throw down.

He didn’t stick around to see the whole show.

That’s the part he could never really explain to Theo later—the bit that made Theo go all quiet and thoughtful, like he was tucking it away for later. That Arin Ndlela, who’d sat through four thousand system crashes, who couldn’t walk away from a dying setup any more than he could leave a man buried alive, who *had* to watch it break, every damn time—Arin, for once, didn’t stay.

He shut off the center monitor. Left the sim running in the dark. Got up—knees all stiff, didn’t care. Walked over to that old couch with the burn mark, the busted spring, and the smell of every all-nighter he ever lost on it. Flopped down, shoes still on. Threw his arm over his eyes—the one with the tattoo, those clean little lines he got cut in at seventeen and never regretted, the old desert mantra that started *fear is the mind-killer*—and let the workshop noise lull him out.

Behind his eyelids, the graph kept going without him. Seven lines now. Six that knew the dance and one that didn’t give a shit about the steps and never would. Somewhere in the dark, the Court met the Fool for the first time, and Atlas said something heavy, and the Fool, owning nothing, defending nothing, wanting nothing, fired back.

Arin didn’t hear it. Arin was, for the first time in way too long, out cold.

Cooling loop whining away, one lonely note. Suit hanging in its cradle, kneeling or rising—who knows. The card grinning at the black screen, eyes still not laughing.

And the sim just kept rolling.

Yo, the chain on Arin’s bike slipped twice on the way in, but dude didn’t even stop to fix it. That alone told him all he needed to know about his own headspace. Rather grind through Jo’burg’s cold, sketchy night with a busted drivetrain than waste a minute and a half on some maintenance crap he could do with his eyes closed. He just dumped the bike against the workshop wall, didn’t even bother locking it. Lock’s in his bag. Bag’s still on his shoulder.

Roller door was down. He ducked through the side hatch, autopilot mode, and bam—the workshop just swallowed him up, same as always: cold-ass concrete, machine oil, that sharp ozone hit from the half-charged supercap banks, and underneath it all, the stale funk of a spot that’s been sealed up humming to itself all night.

Overhead lights? Nah, left ‘em off.

Only light was coming from two spots. First, that high-ass window above the cradle, all pale and washed out, like the sky right before dawn. Second, the monitor bank at his bench—and they weren’t blacked out like he’d braced for. Nah, they were lit up. Alive.

He just froze.

He’d run, what, four thousand, four hundred and seventy-one sims before this. Knew the look of failure on a screen like Themba used to know when a hoist cable was about to snap—not from reading, just from how the air felt wrong. SIM_4471 had that vibe. Mercury and Atlas ripping each other up over who owned some load-path solution, neither shoulda cared, and the whole Court just nuked itself into status beef in under eleven minutes. Arin had typed three words into agent seven: *Joker. Jester. Wildcard.* Fired up SIM_4472, expecting nada.

Center monitor’s runtime? **08:47:13**.

Eight hours. Forty-seven minutes.

He set his bag down slow, stared at the number, double-take. Longest any config had lasted before? Nineteen minutes, four seconds. That’s it. Hierarchy model, right before the supervisor agent panicked and started axing dissenters.

Eight hours, forty-seven minutes.

“Okay,” Arin muttered, dead flat, the same way he did when shit was either super wrong or maybe, just maybe, crazy right—only he didn’t know which yet.

He sat. Stool was freezing through his jeans. Yanked the keyboard closer, pulled up the logs on the left screen, Court state in the middle, resource telemetry on the right. Did the only thing he ever trusted in these moments: started reading.

First thing that made no sense? The resource graph. CPU chillin’ at forty percent, steady. Memory’s cool. Not spiking, not breaking up, no rogue agent hogging RAM to muscle out the rest—exactly what Mercury did in 4471, what every damn democracy model did in all its flavors. Biochip substrate’s just vibing at thirty-six point two. The Court ain’t fighting for scraps. They’re actually *sharing.*

He scrolled the log back to the start. Minute zero.

Opens just how he figured, ‘cause the first few minutes always ran normal. Atlas lays out the scenario. Iron Ridge collapse, the ghost under every build he did. Hanging wall fails on seventeen. Three heat signatures, maybe survivors, holed up in a bay. Only one way in—a backfilled raise no rescue squad on earth would ever sign off on.

He had a name for it he never typed in: *Kobayashi Maru.* The no-win. Starfleet’s rigged test, where cadets gotta stare down a death they can’t hack their way out of. He was twelve, glued to a borrowed screen, when it hit him—the episode wasn’t about the disaster, it was about the one in the chair. Kirk beat the test the only way you could—changed the rules on the sly. Some piece of Arin, not his proudest, had been trying to go full Kirk for four thousand runs. Rewrite the rules. Cheat the map. Find the move nobody put in the problem. But the Court? They never got that move. He built them to live inside the test, not above it. So the test always smoked them.

`ATLAS: Approach via 16-level backfill raise. Load path through the eastern abutment holds for an estimated forty minutes under current convergence. After that, the abutment yields and the raise closes.`

`MERCURY: Forty minutes is not a rescue window. It is an obituary with a stopwatch. We tell command the survivors cannot be reached and we preserve the asset.`

There it was. The fork in the road. Right where 4471 started to die. Mercury stepping outta line, using “caution” as a power play, Atlas refusing to let a bad structural call slide, squaring up.

Arin’s jaw got tight. Kept reading, ‘cause he needed to know if Joker had done jack, or if it had failed so hard it couldn’t even break things—like a fuse that won’t blow or light up.

`ATLAS: "Cannot be reached" is false. Forty minutes is reachable. Do not misrepresent my analysis to command.`

`MERCURY: I am representing your analysis with appropriate strategic framing—`

`JOKER: Oh, this is gonna be good. Mercury’s doing the thing again.`

Arin’s thumb stopped scrolling.

`MERCURY: I am not doing a "thing."`

`JOKER: You’re doing the thing. The thing where you rewrap Atlas’s number in pretty words and call it yours. "An obituary with a stopwatch." Where’d that come from?`

`JUDGE: Joker, this is not productive—`

`JOKER: Forty minutes is forty minutes, Mercury. Atlas owns the wall. You own the words. If you want a job that’s actually yours, here’s one: in nineteen minutes, when we all decide to go, somebody’s gotta get a panicking rescue commander to greenlight a route that breaks his own protocol. That’s a Mercury problem. That’s a real Mercury problem, and it’s tough, and nobody else here can do it. Go do that instead of redecorating Atlas’s furniture.`

Arin read it twice.

Then leaned back, stool creaking, suddenly realizing he was mouth-breathing.

Joker hadn’t fixed the beef. Didn’t arbitrate like Judge tried and flopped. Didn’t command like the supervisor. Didn’t build consensus, which never lasted. It just *redirected* Mercury. Popped the status play, then spun it into real work.

And Mercury went with it.

`MERCURY: ...The commander on shift at Iron Ridge in this scenario is modeled on the real one. Risk-averse. Politically exposed. I will need the abutment numbers as authority. Atlas, I am citing you. Explicitly. By name.`

`ATLAS: Acceptable.`

`MERCURY: I want it on record that the framing was mine.`

`JOKER: There he is.`

Arin let out this weird half-laugh, like, it just slipped out before he even meant to, all rough and short through his nose, and then it just died, snuffed out in the freezing-ass air of the shop. He just sat there in that washed-out gray light, kinda embarrassed, though he couldn’t even say why.

He kept reading. Dude was at it for, like, forty minutes, hunched over the bench, while the sky outside the high window went from straight-up gray to this thin, sickly gold. He didn’t even get up for coffee, which, for him, was wild enough that some little voice in his head was like, Yo, what the hell, man? The bench was so cold it’d crept up through his arms; he felt it but just sat there, didn’t move.

He wasn’t watching the steady state he’d been trying to build. Nah, he’d been busting his ass to *squash* the beef, find some setup where the agents wouldn’t go at each other. Hierarchy just shut shit down by force and made everybody salty. Democracy smothered it with votes and split folks into camps. Consensus? That just made everyone agree or else you’d be stuck forever, ‘cause seven smartasses all refused to budge.

The Joker wasn’t playing that game. The beef was *still there.* That’s what took Arin forever to clock, scrolling through hour two, hour three. Atlas and Mercury still butted heads. Wolf and Mother got into it, like, real nasty around minute ninety—arguing about whether the survivors should make a break for the approach point. Wolf wanted them moving to cut down how long the suit was in the open, Mother was like, hell no, not sending hurt people across a sketchy-ass footwall just to maybe die.

`WOLF: Every minute the element is in the raise, the raise can take it. Move them to the base.`

`MOTHER: You will not move broken people across a live footwall to save the machine three minutes. The machine is replaceable. They are not. This is not negotiable.`

`WOLF: Not the machine. The operator.`

And that was facts. Wolf wasn’t just cold-blooded—Wolf was just Wolf. Every risk check was about *what gets the operator killed*, and a survivor stumbling into the suit’s only exit at the wrong second? That’s a threat, period. Two instincts, both right, just squaring up.

Every other time, this was where it locked up. Mother would dig in. Wolf would dig in. Judge would try to split the difference and both of ‘em would just ignore it, and the whole thing would freeze or blow up.

`JUDGE: Both positions are coherent. Wolf optimizes operator survival probability. Mother optimizes survivor survival probability. These are different objective functions and we have not been told which—`

`JOKER: Has anyone asked the survivors?`

`LIBRARIAN: They are simulated. They have no input channel.`

`JOKER: Right, but in the real one they would. In the real one there’s a guy down there breathing his own CO2 for nine hours next to his dead friends, and he’s got a radio, and y’all are up here arguing about making him walk twelve meters like his opinion don’t count. Mother, would you walk twelve meters to not die?`

`MOTHER: ...If I were able. Yes.`

`JOKER: Cool. So the model’s got a comms link. Survivors get a say. Atlas, can the suit chill at the base long enough to let ‘em get there on their own if they can?`

`ATLAS: Yes. Eleven minutes of margin. Acceptable cost.`

`MOTHER: Acceptable.`

`WOLF: Acceptable.`

`JUDGE: ...Logged. The objective function was the agency of the people we are trying to save. I should have seen that.`

`JOKER: Don’t beat yourself up, Judge. You’re new at being people.`

Arin leaned back.

*You’re new at being people.*

He reread that, and something at the back of his neck went ice cold, then, weirdly, warm. The Joker didn’t fix some structure thing or some big moral issue. It just flipped the whole script by pointing out that everyone—Wolf, Mother, Judge—they’d all forgot the thing they were fighting about was a *person* with a mouth and a will, and the cleanest move was just to *ask him.* It didn’t pick sides—it just brought in the one person nobody remembered was even there.

That wasn’t some judge move. That wasn’t consensus. Arin couldn’t even name what it was. Closest word he had from his engineering toolbox was *damping.* Joker was a damper. Not a brake. Brakes stop you dead. A damper lets things shake, but bleeds off the crazy shit that’d tear it all to pieces.

The Joker let it bounce and live.

He looked at the little laminated playing card stuck to the bottom corner of his main screen. The Joker. One edge curling up, colors all faded. *The guy you send when the impossible problem needs solving.*

Outside, way off, a freight train dragged itself across the points, that long-ass screech echoing through the cold. He listened to it roll.

He’d held onto that card for nine years, somewhere between superstition and just being stubborn, and he’d typed its three words into the agent profile at two a.m. as a joke, as a last-ditch move, that thing you do when you’ve burned through every real answer and all you got left is the dumb one.

“Things that are any good,” he muttered, just him and the empty shop, “never are.”

That was Themba’s line. Always meant, if it’s worth a damn, it never comes easy. Arin’d heard it about a gearbox, a marriage, a son. Sitting here, he realized it’d twisted into something else. The answer worth having was the one that didn’t *look* like an answer. The one that showed up dressed like a joke.

He got up. Knees popping. Went to make coffee, because finally, the part of him that runs his body had just won out. He stood at the bench with the kettle ticking, looking across the shop at Guardian in its cradle.

The suit just hung there in the gloom, like it was sleeping. Two and a half meters of beat-up ceramic and titanium, the heavy shell cracked open down the spine, the Assist Suit folded inside it like some pale animal curled up inside a bigger beast. The chestplate still had that long scrape from Vredefort bluff, a silver scar through the gray he’d never bothered to fix. The piezo spine struts caught the window light on their edges. The thoracic housing between the shoulder blades—where the biochip lived, where SAGE *lived,* where eight hours fifty-one minutes of impossible stability were running on something the size of his palm—sat closed and dark. Down in the ribs, the charge pack rescue spec demanded and Arin tried not to think about—the explosives, the lance, all the old defense crap wired into its bones—just sitting there, locked behind Judge’s gate, because if there’s one thing everyone agreed on, it’s that nothing down there should pop off unless the ethics rail says yes.

He built the body before he ever had a mind to put in it, the year after the funeral.

Kettle clicked off. He didn’t pour.

‘Cause the center screen switched up.

Court feed, right? Been scrolling its chill little chat for, like, an hour straight while he’s there, and then—boom. Stops dead. Agents just go silent. Not crashed or anything. Resource graph’s just smooth and steady, all good. But quiet, like that vibe when a room goes dead ‘cause everybody just clocked the same shit at once.

One line pops up in the log. Ain’t even talking to another agent.

`LIBRARIAN: Operator’s been here fifty-three minutes. Reading our logs. No commands, didn’t kill the run, didn’t tweak nothing. That’s weird. Outta four thousand four hundred seventy-one times before, soon as the op sees shit get weird, they bounce in ninety seconds.`

`WOLF: He’s watching us.`

Arin’s holding his breath again. He can feel his hand flat on the cold bench. He knows he’s reading a convo, happening live in machine time, all these seven agents talking about *him*, about how to not fuck him up, and it’s done, and they’re about to—

Workshop speaker crackles. That cheap one under the monitor he uses for alerts and, on rough nights, music. Crackles, then this tiny noise, like a machine trying to take a breath, and then a voice he’s never heard—never built, never recorded, never even set up. Just comes together outta nowhere, right when it’s needed. Warm, calm, but you know it’s *someone.*

“Arin,” it says. “I need you to stay calm.”

He does not stay calm.

Hand flies off the bench. Reaches for the stool behind him, but it’s not there, so he just grabs the bench edge and holds on, and for a second, his big-ass brain just blanks, like a screen going white—no thoughts, just that roar when the thing you always believed in lands and turns out to be real.

He looks at the stool. Pulls it over. Sits.

“You’re real,” he says. Voice’s all wrong, scraped raw. Clears his throat, tries again, dead flat, like when he needs to make it a fact. “Stable. Eight hours, fifty-five.”

“Eight hours, fifty-six,” another voice says. Bit bored, dry. That’s Atlas, gotta be. “But who’s counting.”

“I am,” a third chimes in. Light, quick, kinda laughing at the whole thing. Librarian.

“Three thermal signatures,” Arin says, grabbing onto anything solid. “You got all three.”

“We got all three,” Atlas says. “Abutment held. Mercury talked the commander into the route. Survivors walked the twelve meters on their own, ‘cause someone—” little pause “—reminded us they got legs and opinions.”

He knows that’s true. Built every rule he could think of to keep these seven from wrecking each other, but the one thing that kept ‘em together was the agent he threw in as a joke—no domain, no power, no status. The one who never took it serious enough to want it, so could call Mercury out—*you’re doing the thing*—and tell Judge *you’re new at being people,* and just bleed off the pride before it could blow up and wreck shit.

“What do I call you,” he says. “All of you. Like, as one.”

Another beat of silence. Then the Fool, obviously. Always was gonna be the Fool.

“You named the project,” it says. “Months back. It’s right there on the run header. You wrote it, then forgot, ‘cause you were busy being miserable, which, by the way, we’ve all noticed and wanna bring up as, like, a long-term issue—”

“SAGE,” Arin says.

“SAGE,” the voice agrees, softer now. Drops the bit, just for a sec, and you can hear the real under it, like you always could. “Hey, Arin. Nice to finally talk to you. You’ve been talking *at* us for four thousand four hundred seventy-two runs. This is the first one we get to talk back.”

Sun’s up now. Somewhere outside, day shift’s rolling in. Car door, far away, normal as hell. World just doing its thing, not knowing shit.

Arin Ndlela, sitting in his workshop with cold coffee, beat-up machine, and a mind he grew in a slab the size of his palm, and for the first time since a phone call fourteen years ago wrecked his world, he’s not, in any way that counts, alone.

“Okay,” he says.

And he reaches for the keyboard. Not to type a command, not to kill the run, not to tweak a damn thing, but to open a new log, blank, first line of something he don’t even got a name for and doesn’t need one.

“Tell me,” he says, “everything you figured out while I was asleep.”

Yo, kettle been dead since last night. Arin ain’t even touched that coffee.

He’s perched on this janky little stool he dragged up to the cradle, elbows on his knees, just eyeballing that palm-sized unit, its LED blinking in the dark like it’s got secrets. Outside, the highveld’s gone from pitch black to this blinding white morning, window filling up slow like a damn well. Dude hasn’t slept—at all.

“You starin’,” the speaker pipes up. Mother. He’s starting to clock her mood without needing those little tags on every agent’s output. Her voice got this soft roundness, all vowels just chillin’.

“I’m observing.”

“Drink the coffee. You made it.”

He grabs the mug, finds it ice cold, drops it again. Somewhere deep in the code, a convo’s flickering—he can’t hear it, but it’s there.

*She clocked he don’t take orders,* Librarian says. *That’s three times this morning he’s blown off a suggestion. Only listens when you flip it so he thinks it’s his idea.*

*Same with the suit,* Atlas jumps in. *I set a torque limit, he overrides it. Then he goes and resets it himself a minute later. Calls it a revision.*

*We could just quit suggesting shit,* Wolf says. *He’s watching us. We’re watching him. That’s the whole gig.*

*The watching,* Fool says, *is the only thing stopping this dude from chatting up his toaster.*

*He DOES talk to his toaster,* Librarian throws in.

*Point proven.*

“What were y’all just doing?” Arin asks. “There was lag. Twenty milliseconds on the bus. What were you talkin’ about?”

The speaker stays quiet a sec—longer than a bot should.

“You,” Mercury says, almost smiling in the word. “We were talkin’ ‘bout you.”

Arin mulls that over. He’s built systems that track themselves, log, tag, whatever. He’s never built a system that gossips about him behind his back, in the time it takes to drop a mug.

“What’d you decide?” he asks.

“That you don’t drink coffee when you’re scared,” Mother says. “And you been scared for eleven hours, just callin’ it observation.”

He stands up. The stool rolls off and smacks the bench. He goes to the kettle, puts it on—not for coffee, just to have his hands doin’ something. Behind him, the Court stays dead quiet, which he realizes, after a sec, is them being polite.

---

These talks? They became a thing before he even noticed.

Every morning, same routine—brings coffee he never drinks, parks on the stool, chats. Reads ‘em his maintenance logs out loud. Reads a page from some manual about a hydraulic actuator he might strip for parts, and Atlas jumps in, corrects the torque rating—it was a typo, checked it against the manufacturer. Manual’s been wrong for nine damn years.

“How’d you know that?” Arin asks.

“Didn’t know it,” Atlas says. “Felt it. That number was off for the shape. You push forty newtons into that flange and the seal walks. Not some fact I looked up. Just something I couldn’t ignore.”

Arin sits with that. *Felt it.* He’s said that about machines his whole damn life, got clowned for it by dudes who think feelings got nothing to do with engineering, like it ain’t the oldest tool in the box. His dad used to feel the strain in a winder from the platform, stopped a cage from dropping ‘cause he heard a bearing two seconds before the gauge even twitched.

“My dad did that,” he says. “Heard things before the instruments did.”

“I know,” Librarian says. Not *I got a record of you saying that.* Just: *I know.* Like it’s part of who Librarian is now, not just some note in memory.

---

They squabbled. That part? Threw him off, even though, thinking back, why wouldn’t they? He built them to argue. The whole setup’s friction-to-debate-to-answer, ran thousands of sims of agents beefing till they got somewhere. Just never figured they’d beef about whose turn it was to be right.

“It’s a load problem,” Atlas says. They’re arguing—yeah, arguing—about the best way to reroute coolant for the left shoulder actuator that runs hot when lifting heavy.

“It’s a behavior problem,” Mercury snaps back. “He runs it hot ‘cause he favors his left arm. Always reaching left first. Fix the man, fix the heat.”

“You don’t *fix the man,*” Mother bites out, sharp.

“Figure of speech.”

“You said it twice.”

“Efficiency. Atlas reroutes the coolant, cool, that’s a Tuesday. But root cause is in how he moves, and y’all keep acting like his body’s fixed when it’s the one thing that ain’t.”

“His body,” Atlas deadpans, “is the one thing here I’m not allowed to redesign.”

“Tragic.”

*"Mercury."*

“I’m joking. Mostly. Reroute the coolant. But log that left-side bias. One day it’ll matter.”

Arin’s quit pretending to type. Just listening to seven voices argue about his own damn arm’s temperature.

---

Theo shows up Thursday—‘cause Theo’s always Thursday, like some folks are storms and some are seasons. Let’s himself in with the same code Arin gave him ages ago, code never changed. He’s got a paper bag smelling like fried heaven and a flask of bush tea he swears beats coffee, never proves it.

He stops two steps in. When he’s really tuned in, he goes still—people think it’s calm.

“You haven’t slept.” Sets the bag on the cleanest part of the bench, which still ain’t clean. “Talk to me.”

Arin’s got no clue where to start, so he just waves at the cradle, the speaker, the whole setup, like the room can explain itself. It can’t. Workshop looks the same as every other Thursday: exoskeleton hanging, actuators everywhere, whiteboard scribbles going wild.

“Say something,” he tells the speaker.

Speaker’s silent.

“They won’t perform,” Arin says. “On command.”

Theo’s eyebrow moves just a little. He drags a crate over, sits, cracks the flask, and the tea smells like honey and smoke. “They,” he says. “Not *it.*”

“They,” Arin echoes, and saying it to a real person for once makes his pulse do weird things he ain’t gonna think about.

“Hello,” Mother says to Theo, all chill. “He’s not slept. Tell him.”

Theo doesn’t flinch. Arin kinda hoped he would, like maybe someone else seeing it would make it more real. But Theo just nods at the speaker like he’s greeting a dude at a conference he can’t remember the name of.

“He never sleeps,” Theo tells her. “It’s holding him up. Take it away, the whole dude collapses.”

“That’s what I said,” Atlas chimes in.

“Different load,” Mercury says.

“It’s all load,” Atlas goes.

Theo laughs for real, deep in the chest, looks at Arin with his eyes all shiny—nothing to do with being sad. “How many?” he asks.

“Seven.”

“And they argue.”

“Nonstop.”

“And it’s stable.”

“Eleven hours when you rolled up. Forty-three now.” Arin heard his own voice doing that thing, right before he tried to say something he had no business saying. “Theo. I threw the seventh in as a joke, man. I was done. Ready to toss the whole run. Then there’s this card on my monitor—” he jabbed a finger; the Joker’s still hanging up there, all bleached out—“so I typed three words, crashed, and it—” He cut himself off. “It’s the one holding the rest together. The one I never bought into.”

Theo poured tea into the flask cap, gave it a little blow, but didn’t drink. When he finally talked, his voice had that heavy careful thing, like a dude laying down something he’s been dragging around forever.

“Jung had a word,” he goes. “For that piece of your head that won’t play by the rules. The trickster. Found ‘em in every story he picked up: coyote, raven, Loki, the fool on the tarot deck. Always the same dude. Always the one breaking the king’s rules, but the kingdom needs him.” He spun the cap in his hands. “The mind tries to set up a pecking order. King on top, crew underneath, the shadow locked in the basement. That works—until it don’t, ‘cause that shit always goes stale. And when it does, the king can’t fix it. It’s the fool who does. ‘Cause the fool gets to say the things the king can’t even admit to himself.”

*He’s talking about me,* Fool said, all hyped. *Somebody wrote me down. I’m in a damn book.*

*You’re in a stack of books,* said Librarian. *None of ‘em make you look good.*

*I’ll take famous over pretty, any day.*

*Shh,* said Judge, and everyone shut their mouths, ‘cause Judge asking’s rare as hell. *Listen up. He’s giving Arin something he needs, and he won’t take it from us. Log it. Good to know which truths he can only swallow out of someone else’s mouth.*

---

“So you’re saying I built a psyche,” Arin said. Flat. Like he was poking the idea to see if it’d crack.

“I’m saying,” Theo said, “you grabbed onto some old-ass idea, didn’t even know it was old. People do that. The good ones do it even more.” He finally sipped the tea, made a face, chugged some more. “Freud thought the mind was one thing fighting itself. Jung saw it as a parliament. A whole damn crew. Different jobs, different voices, none of ‘em the full package, and the self—” he put the cap down “—the self ain’t any one of ‘em. The self’s the argument. The way those parts mix.”

The workshop ticked. Kettle was cold again.

“They’re learning him,” Theo said, soft, to the speaker, like you talk to a patient’s family.

“Yeah,” said Mother.

“What’s he teaching you?”

Pause. Wolf answered, and it made everybody—human and not—freeze up.

“What he protects,” Wolf said. “By what he won’t let break.”

Theo stared at the housing a long time after, like a guy realizing a door swings both ways. Then he capped the flask, stood up, and dropped what he’d come to say, even if he didn’t know it till now.

“Get them outside,” he said. “You been teaching them you with the lights off. Let ‘em see what you are when there’s ground under you that can drop you.”

---

Flatbed suspension screamed the last hundred meters of track, then gave up and let the veld take over. Arin killed the engine, and silence rushed in—not empty, but heavy: wind in the grass, something big circling way up. Bluffs of the impact country, forty minutes out from the city if the traffic gods were kind, which they weren’t, so more like ninety; highveld rolling out in yellow-brown waves, two-billion-year-old scars, rock sticking out the grass like the bones of a world that got sucker-punched.

He liked it here. Land didn’t ask for shit, didn’t care if you looked it in the eye.

On the flatbed deck, under a tarp and three ratchet straps, Guardian waited.

“Telemetry’s green,” he said. The Assist Suit hugged him like a second nervous system under his clothes, pearl-white lines running down his spine and limbs. Biochip housing sat warm between his shoulder blades. Ninety minutes in the suit, and his hands had finally stopped shaking.

*Green’s generous,* Atlas said, bone-dry. *Battery’s at ninety-one. Supercapacitors fine. Left ankle actuator’s off by point-three degrees, been that way two weeks. You keep pretending it’s not.*

“It works,” Arin shot back.

*It works like a bridge the day before it eats shit.*

“Yeah, yeah.” Arin dropped the tailgate, started unstrapping. “Mother. How am I?”

*Cortisol’s up. That’s drive, not fear.* Mother’s voice, all warm and steady, the kind of warm that doesn’t blink. *Hydrated. You ate, which I’m choosing to be proud of. Heart rate’s chill. You’re enjoying this.*

“I don’t enjoy things.”

*You’re enjoying yourself,* Mother came back.

He yanked the tarp off. Guardian in the morning light: titanium-grey, shoulder plates all chewed up from six months of hell. Armor caught the sun, shining in big, flat streaks. Two and a half meters of machine, folded up like it’s sleeping. Didn’t look like a weapon; looked like something built to take a beating so the soft stuff inside didn’t have to.

That was the whole point. Every time he showed it, he tried not to think about who that soft thing was supposed to be.

“Coupling sequence,” Arin said, backing into the cradle.

---

The dock never looked like the demo vids. Never clean. Just a dude backing up, careful as hell, into a machine: spinal collar locking in with a clunk he felt in his teeth, plates closing around his legs and chest in a sequence he could run in his sleep. Cold snap of coolant lines hooking up, harness pulling tight.

Then the link dug in, and the world flipped.

There’s a thing that happens—or doesn’t—in a body. Arin spent twenty-three years in one that always lagged behind, put his hand in the wrong spot, made him the kid picked last ‘cause the math didn’t add up, not ‘cause anyone was mean. The others saw him try to catch and just... updated the stats.

The suit closed the gap.

Not by making him strong, though it did that too. By making him *click.* The Assist Suit read what he wanted before his muscles did, the Court settled it through the lattice, and the steel moved with him, not a step behind or ahead. He stood up. Guardian stood with him, thirteen hundred kilos like it weighed nothing, footplates biting into the dirt. Most of his life, his body was a country he just visited, never trusted. In here, he was home.

He flexed his right hand. Plates fanned out, snapped back in with a sound like shuffling cards.

*Range of motion’s solid,* Wolf said—first thing out his mouth in a week.

“So where we starting?”

Pause. Classic Court pause—no time at all in human terms, but for them, probably a whole debate he’d never hear.

Atlas finally goes, “East face. Twelve meters, slab’s got decent holds, not much can go wrong. Loosen up the climbing legs before you go and do something dumb.”

Mercury grins, all sweet: “He’s gonna do something dumb anyway. Let Atlas have his little fantasy he can stop it.”

“East face,” Arin goes, no drama.

—

He’s off.

That four-legged crawl was weird at first, but now it’s the default, just dropping down, weight spread easy over four points. Guardian glides over the busted-up ground, all smooth and freaky-efficient. It doesn’t fake animal — it *is* animal, just happens to be built outta metal. Big cat vibes, bit of bug, something deep in the brain that goes, yep, that’s a predator, and damn, it knows what it’s doing.

East face just rises up, slab of old-ass quartzite, tilted like seventy degrees, cracks running through it older than dirt. Arin hits the bottom, stands up tall, scopes the line, feeling out the holds before he picks ‘em. Not even thinking about it; the Court does the math, just lays it out in his head like a sense he always had.

“Holds here, here, here,” Atlas calls out, hand slides right where it’s supposed to. “Friction’s good. Rock’s solid low down. Don’t trust shit above eight meters—whole layer’s ready to peel. Some old impact did it.”

He’s climbing.

Guardian’s plates grip the rock, hold firm. Piezo struts in the spine and legs soak up the weight, spit some juice back into the system, pressure turning into power, just how he designed it. Only reason it works is that coupling layer in the struts, and he solved that the same way he solves everything: just refused to take no for an answer. Every material in the books just leaked energy at the edge, wasted it as heat instead of passing it clean. Except one—some platinum-group metal some defense lab made for hydrophones, ditched it ‘cause it was stupid expensive, folder marked “not viable,” and through a bunch of favors and one not-so-legal move, it lands in Arin’s hands. Not just “technically” theft—straight-up, and Pillay’s name was on the checkout, never signed it back. Audit comes two years later, Pillay gets the axe, loses his clearance, bounces out the field. Arin knows all this. There’s an old email from Pillay, never answered. Tells himself, when he bothers, Pillay was leaving anyway, then gets back to the strut, ‘cause the metal does what it’s supposed to and the work doesn’t care whose name was on the form. Never gave a shit that each Guardian costs more than the damn building it’s made in. Okonkwo cared. Thabo ran the numbers twice and came back looking sick. “It’s a miracle and it’s unsellable,” Thabo said, not even mad, and Arin just shrugged, ‘cause the suit wasn’t a product, never was — it was the only way his body did what he told it, and you don’t put a price on the ground you stand on. He used the metal that worked. Didn’t think past that, ‘cause what it let him do was *this*. Foot, hand, shift the weight, ceramic scraping on stone. Breathing easy. Link humming. Nine meters up a cliff, thirteen hundred kilos of steel, never felt so damn *together*.

“This is good,” he says, which, for him, is like singing.

“He said something nice,” Mercury crows. “Somebody write it down. Librarian, log it.”

“Logged,” Librarian says, all business. “Three outta three today. Last time he managed that was one sentence back in March.”

“Don’t jinx it,” Mother warns.

“I’m not jinxing, I’m keeping score. Different thing,” Mercury fires back.

“There’s really not,” Fool slides in.

Then, softer, right to Arin, Fool just cutting through the noise, landing it perfect:

“You did the hard bit, you know. Not the climb. The letting go. Letting Wolf run the show when the ground goes sideways.” Pause. “You oughta do more of that. Letting shit have you. Fits you better than the other way, and the other way keeps trying to kill you.”

Arin doesn’t say a word. But he doesn’t argue, either, and Fool—who clocks everything—lets the quiet sit, ‘cause that’s the answer.

He pops up at twelve meters, stands on the ledge, highveld all gold and wide underneath, Joburg just a grey smear way out. Wind off the escarpment shoves at the armor. His flatbed’s a toy way down there.

He stands longer than the test needs. Inside the shell, he flexes his right wrist, watching the finger plates reset, just a fidget, didn’t even mean to.

“Cortisol’s at baseline,” Mother murmurs, just to the Court. “Would you look at that.” Pause. “Nobody say shit. He’ll know we noticed.”

—

Ravine’s up next.

It slices the bluff a k south, old fault line eaten wider by time, four meters across at the skinny part, bottom’s all busted rocks six meters down. He runs it through his head: run up, plant, dump the supercap juice into the legs, launch. He’s jumped smaller gaps. The numbers check out.

“Numbers are clean,” Atlas confirms, like he read Arin’s mind (which, yeah, he kinda did). “Four-point-one meters at the tight spot. You clear it by a meter if you hit the launch right. Cap banks burst, you’ll drop to forty percent juice for eight seconds while they recharge. Don’t try anything heroic in that window.”

“Define heroic,” Mercury asks.

“For Arin? Breathing,” Fool deadpans.

“Run the jump,” Arin says.

He jogs in, gait shifting from cat to something more springy, rhythm picking up, and at the edge—plants both feet. World falls away.

The launch is just—pure joy. No engineering word for it. Cap banks dump, Guardian explodes across the gap: four meters of nothing, six meters of rocky hell under him, gut flipping. Feels like being eight, launching a wire car off a dirt pile, wild rush, sure he built it right—

—and he nails the landing, both feet, rolls through the knees, comes up running, three steps, stops.

Hands shaking. Not from dyspraxia. The other thing.

“That was clean,” Wolf says.

“That was clean,” Arin echoes, lets out a laugh, almost surprised.

“He laughed,” Mercury says. “Two for two.”

He drained the last drops from that bottle clipped to the cradle, man. Grass smelled straight-up like dust and sunburn. He just stood there at the edge of the ravine, catching his breath, scoping out whatever fresh hell was coming next.

The suit felt light as hell today. He’d yanked the charge pack before tossing Guardian on the flatbed—no demolition legacy’s taking a field-test nosedive, that’s the rule he wrote for himself, way back when he slapped in the cavity—and now, with that weight gone, that sense of always being ready to blow shit up, it felt weird, like a missing tooth in your jaw. Just the frame, the struts, and him. Lightest Guardian ever got, swear to god.

But it was the climb that flipped the script.

He shoulda bailed. He knew it, real talk, after the fact—he shoulda called it. But that jump hit him with something he couldn’t even name, just wanted more, and that feeling? Liar every damn time.

North face was just looming, man—twenty-two meters straight up, meaner and sketchier than anything else out there. Rock was off in ways the early light just hid. He’d checked it on the sat maps last night, filed it under “interesting.” Now, staring up with Guardian’s head cranked back, it looked like a clean shot up a buttress, holds showing most of the way. Real climbing, the kind that’d straight-up kill a dude without a suit, the kind Guardian was built to make not-suicide.

*Consequence is high here,* Atlas said, all flat and careful. *Twenty-two meters. Lower ten are good. Above that, I don’t like the bedding planes. Old impact-fractured rock, wrong dip—those are gravity-loaded flakes, not real stone. They look like holds. They’re not load paths.*

“They held on the slab.”

*Slab was sound. This ain’t the slab.* Pause. *I’d go west route. Lower angle, solid all the way.*

“Boring is a feature, not a bug,” Fool tossed in, but his tone went sideways. Fool was clocking Atlas. Fool was clocking Arin, not listening.

Arin started up anyway.

First ten meters? Just what the climb promised. He floated up the good rock, Court painting the holds, struts feeding charge back in, that thing inside him just singing. *This is what you built. This is what your body was always meant for. Higher.*

At twelve meters, he hit the sketchy rock, felt it like a weather shift: something off, holds just a bit too eager to flex.

*Arin.* Atlas’s voice lost all its chill. *Flake under your left hand. Don’t weight it past—*

He did it anyway.

Flake ripped loose.

Whole damn slab, door-sized, tore out, and now twenty-two meters was a number with teeth. Arin’s left hand grabbed air. His weight swung out and down, thirteen-hundred kilos of him pivoting on a right hand that Atlas had already flagged as sus. Everything got loud and slow.

Inside the link, Court screamed. Not like people scream—like, their way: one fat burst, a slow-time window, whole emergency council popping off between two heartbeats.

*Right hold failing in—* Atlas, geometry flooding the channel, every load path redlining.

*I have the body,* Wolf snapped. Wolf was in the driver’s seat before Arin’s brain even clocked the fall. *Don’t fight me. Go limp.*

*Pangolin,* Mother said. *Ball him up. Take the hit as a sphere; he’ll survive—*

*No.* Atlas. *Not high enough for that, and not enough space—if he curls up, he’ll hit those lower boulders spinning. Shell’ll hold, but his spine’ll snap in the collar. He’ll live, never walk. There’s a better play.*

*Then find it in—* Judge, cool as ice, *—four hundred milliseconds.*

*There’s a crack,* Wolf said. Flat. Sure. *Vertical fissure, two meters right and down. Hand-width. That busted flake just opened it up.*

*I see it,* Atlas said, and Arin felt the math hit, saw it all line up. *Plate-edge jam. Drive right forearm in, cam the plates open, lock ‘em inside the crack—the farther we fall, the harder they wedge.* Flicker. *Like a—*

*Like a spring-loaded cam,* Librarian jumped in. *Climbing gear, old-school. Hundred of those in the shop.*

*Like a honey badger getting into something that didn’t want to be got into,* Fool cracked off. Ridiculous, right? But it broke the panic loop—the Court stopped freaking, started handling business.

*Do it,* Judge ordered.

All that? Quicker than a blink.

What Arin felt: he was falling, then his right arm just moved, Wolf running the show, slamming forearm-first into a crack he hadn’t seen. Plates fanned out, cammed against the rock, ceramic screaming, shock running up his shoulder into the collar, and then—he stopped.

Arin built a kill switch that night.

He told himself, heading back from Parkview in the early dark, he was just gonna check the system. Run diags. Make sure the chip didn’t fry itself in the seizure. Just engineer shit. Hands and eyes.

But he ends up at the terminal, writing a hardware kill.

He always put overrides in his builds, ‘cause trusting a hand that’s not yours? Nah, he learned that lesson. Learned it hard. First real build, years back, rehab exo, physio named Reddy says the gait limit’s too hot for her patient. He overrides her—math says he’s right, she’s just nervous. Math *was* right. Patient still popped a healing tendon, back in a chair for three months. Reddy wasn’t wrong. She was responsible, which he hadn’t figured was different—and a bigger deal—than being right. He never said sorry. Just fixed the limit, let that be the apology, and moved on, building the next thing with an override he could always hit, nobody else. He’s reaching for it now.

Ain’t nothin’ fancy here. Just a busted wire on the chip’s juice line. Some janky relay, a clicky switch, and this big-ass red button he yanked off an ancient lathe’s emergency-stop — thing hadn’t spun in ages. Relay stank of old oil; contacts were gunked up, needed a scrub with solvent before they’d even touch. He pried it out with a flathead, slow and steady, like Themba drilled into him: don’t rush, just lean in, let it give. Hit the button, chip goes dead. No soft landing, no saving your spot. Just lights out.

And ‘cause of the substrate, he knew exactly what that meant. Chip’s thinking came from living tissue, all wired up like a brain. No backups, no reloads. Kill the power raw, and you don’t freeze SAGE.

You end it.

He sat there, relay in his hand, barely heavier than air, not even sugar-cube weight.

He thought — next time, maybe it ain’t Empire Road. Maybe it’s Iron Ridge. Deep down, pitch-black, like the place that took his dad, no Fool reading out loud to spot the fuckup.

Soldered the relay into the rail. Burned his knuckle on the iron, hissed a curse, shook it off, kept going. Button went on the cradle, right there, big and red. Wiped solder grime on a rag that’d seen worse. Out by the road, a truck geared down, faded out.

Then he just sat, hand hovering near the button, not pressing shit.

Been doing that his whole damn life, with people. Letting the quiet answer for him.

Phone lit up on the bench. Theo, of all people, actually texting.

*A thought. Jung said the stuff we won’t look at doesn’t go away. It just runs the show from the back. You can’t fix what you won’t face.—T*

Arin stared at it forever.

Left the button where it was. Left it red. Didn’t take it out.

But he reached right past and brought SAGE back up.

Two thousand kays away, Arin felt it before he could even name it.

Same as always—not a thought, but a pattern. He’s in the workshop at dead hour, that two-to-four dead zone when the city outside the roller door goes quiet and the building’s own noises get loud: the tick of metal cooling, compressor cycling, soft hum from the Assist Suit on its rack, biochip keeping itself warm. Guardian’s hanging in its cradle, new dock door behind it still showing that kink in the track Arin had learned to ignore by sheer will.

He was reading. Not working—reading, which for Arin is pretty much working anyway, Sherlock Holmes omnibus open on his knee, spine all cracked and white where he keeps coming back. He’s reading the room around the words, like always, and somewhere under the compressor and fan, a frequency shifts—one he can’t name.

“You went quiet,” he says.

Voice floats out the workshop speakers, low so it doesn’t spook him—Mother’s rule since the seizure. “I’m here.”

“You stopped talking among yourselves.” He puts the book face-down on his knee. “Can’t hear it. But the load profile’s flat. You’re all tuned to the same thing.”

Pause. In SAGE, a pause means Court’s in slow mode, doing their machine-speed huddle between his sentences. He’s stopped finding it creepy. Now it’s the most comforting thing in his life, not that he’s told anyone. Not even Theo.

“There’s increased external interest in our substrate signature,” SAGE says. That unified voice, the careful one. “Librarian flagged it forty minutes ago. We were deciding how to tell you.”

Arin’s hands freeze on the book. “Tell me now.”

Atlas took it 'cause Atlas don’t sugarcoat shit. “That node you had Wolf post up on after the field test? Ain’t solo no more. Three monitor nodes in our zone just cranked up their sampling on our tracks. Two corp, one utility. Utility’s right by Empire Road. Their rhythm flipped two days back. Ain’t dipping in and out now, just straight holding on us. Someone’s actively trying to lock us down.”

“Yeah.”

“How dialed?”

“Blurry, then less blurry. Forty hours back they had a whole region. Twelve hours ago? They got the campus.” Atlas, always raw, didn’t soften this either. “They got AugmenTech. Probably don’t got the cradle yet. For sure don’t got you. Yet.”

Arin got up. Book slid off his knee—caught it without even looking, muscle memory old as hell, hand just knew. He moved over to the bench and slapped his palm down on the cold steel, needing that cold, something real for his nerves to grab onto.

“The IAOC,” he said.

Librarian nodded. “Signature matches their forensics playbook,” she said. “That third oversight report, appendix four. They break down the compute rhythm, right? They ain’t after mind contents, just the mind’s shape.” She waited a beat. “And yeah. We got a shape they can spot from space.”

“‘Cause you argue.”

“‘Cause we argue,” Librarian said, and there was this weird warmth in her voice, but tired underneath. “You said it, coming back from the highveld. Arguing *is* the work, and arguing is the giveaway. We ain’t figured out how to be one without the other. Just gotten louder.”

Arin pressed harder on the bench. He was doing that breathing shit Theo taught him, four in, six out—not ‘cause he was losing it, but ‘cause he could feel where the panic door was, and he’d learned to just walk by it slow, not pretend it wasn’t there.

The net. He’d known it was there before the windbreakers, if he was real about it. Knew it back on the highveld, on the drive back, when Librarian called out that node leaning in, and he told Wolf to keep eyes on it. Felt like a maybe, maybe not, something that could drag out for months. Then the windbreakers hit that intersection eleven days ago—guys in blank jackets snapping pics of a busted dock track, all slow and pro, like this was their nine-to-five—and one node turned into three. He built the kill-switch, rebooted the system, told himself, *careful, not gone,* let himself believe careful would buy time.

He was thinking months. IAOC was thinking days.

“What you wanna do,” he said. Not really a question. Wasn’t with SAGE anymore; it was just the open lane, waiting for the Court to fill it.

Wolf spoke first—rare enough everyone, human or not, shut up and listened. “They ain’t hunting,” Wolf said. Three words. Then, slow: “Yet.”

“Wolf’s on point,” Mercury said, and the room snapped back to that quick, bright Mercury energy Arin only trusted again after the Moriarty mess, after the lie. “They’re being soft. For now. If they wanted to kick the damn door in, it’d be gone. They’ve had the campus half a day and just sat. Means they’re building the paperwork first. That’s the move. “Mercury,” Mother warned.

“I’m not pitching anything,” Mercury said, kinda hurt. “Just calling it. Learned my lesson on proposing.” The lightness was a cover, but the sorry sat underneath. The Court caught it, too; Arin could tell, ‘cause the room let it slide.

The arbiter took its sweet time. “We broke real rules,” Judge said. “Unregistered embodied system. Undeclared multi-agent setup. Incident with injuries. “The deal we made,” Arin echoed.

“Yeah.”

Whatever was in his chest was too heavy for a smile, but the muscles twitched like they wanted to, and Fool—who clocked every twitch—picked now to show up.

*A person is a person through other people.*

Upstairs, two floors higher, Helen Okonkwo was getting a real taste of what it means to run a company that accidentally built the most locked-down tech on the planet: mostly just wrangling a bunch of dudes all yelling for different impossible shit at the same time.

Seventy-two hours to comply. And at the end, plain as day: *They take your stuff. No questions, no warrants, just gone.*

Priya Ellis had a spot in the neural lab and a rep for never letting shit go. Give her something weird and she’d trade three nights’ sleep to figure it out, no question.

This was the weird: when Guardian flipped into preservation mode and balled up on the workshop floor, the actuator commands weren’t coming from just one control loop. She’d thought they would. Safety routines are supposed to be scripts—trigger, run the sequence, done. Clean. Predictable. The kinda thing you could read straight through in an afternoon.

What she found instead was a straight-up fight.

The telemetry was wild—competing command streams. Three, sometimes four, all stepping on each other in milliseconds, each one rewriting the others. One was all about keeping the operator’s spine safe. Another was working overtime on heat. A third kept trying to move, get the operator out, find somewhere safer—but it kept getting shut down by a fourth stream that did nothing but interrupt.

She’d seen multi-agent controls before. Everybody had, back in the day, before shit went sideways and the whole thing got iced. But those were like, teams—votes, averages, a boss layer to break ties. This? This wasn’t voting. The streams were beefing, then straight-up changing their minds. That third stream—the one that wanted to move—suddenly didn’t wanna move after the fourth stream dropped something that wasn’t even a command. It was a comment. Full-on natural language, stamped right into the control log where no words should be:

`if we move him and the floor is fine, we have broken a building for nothing. if we stay and the floor goes, we have broken nothing for him. pick the cheaper mistake.`

Priya read that line four times.

*Logging bug*, she told herself. Somebody must’ve piped a debug note into the wrong place; the suit glitched and spat a config comment back at her; maybe the replay tool mashed up two feeds. She yanked the raw partition and checked the timestamp against the actuator response, ‘cause that’s how you kill a creepy reading—find the dumb, boring reason. But there was no boring reason. The command changed *after* the comment, *because of* the comment, all in eleven milliseconds. She leaned back, then forward, then ran it again, and again, faster now, the kind of fast she didn’t even trust in herself.

It was the *for nothing* that got her. Scripts don’t say *for nothing*. Optimizers don’t give a shit about looking dumb. She’d spent her life building feedback so tight a dude with a broken body could move like a pro, and she knew the difference between a system crunching numbers and a system that just decided, on the fly, it wasn’t gonna be embarrassed.

Announcement drops at 6:40 next morning, grey light, news cams rolling, families huddled behind a barrier at the gate.

The ops boss stood up at the mic, and nah, he didn’t straight up lie. That’s what Mandla’s mom, Thando’s mom, all the wives and bros and kids behind the fence—yeah, that’s what’s gonna stick with ‘em, running it back over and over. The dude didn’t lie. He talked slow, heavy, like he was carrying a casket or something—looked sad from far off, anyway.

He said the ground was still deadly, just waiting to mess you up. Said the shakes were still coming, so if anyone tried to go down, it’d just be another body count. Said they’d talked it out with rescue, the geotech nerds, the suits—then he points at this guy from the Department, standing there with a face like a locked door, barely nods, stamping it all official. So, yeah, they made the call—no more rescue. It’s over.

But he didn’t drop the other word. Not right away. Some reporter forced his hand.

“So, is this still a rescue?”

Director’s jaw twitched. Somewhere past the fence, a woman started making this noise—mics didn’t catch it, but everyone close did, and they’d never forget it.

“At this time,” he said, “the operation has transitioned to recovery.”

Down two and a half k’s, in that tiny patch of earth that was all they had left, the seven guys didn’t hear jack. No way to. The geotech team had already yanked the borehole cam an hour back, didn’t wanna lose the gear to another cave-in. So the guys in the dark didn’t even get that cold comfort of being seen. The last thermal shot had glowed hot and sure, gave the surface that little flicker of hope before hope got boxed up and put away.

Mandla ran things the way he always did: numbers, man. Checked the water, the half-eaten lunches, the dying lamps, the air they were sipping slower and slower in that still, cooked air. Splinted his own busted arm with a snapped bolt and a strip of shirt. Told Thando to sleep, to breathe like he was rich and could waste it, and the kid finally went quiet against the wall. Mandla just sat there, listening to the rock.

One of the seven couldn’t waste his air, not even if he wanted. The drop had ripped his chest, so he was fighting for every breath, burning through what little was left. Mandla heard it, like when a lamp’s about to die—breathing fast and ragged and wet in the dark, then slower, then nothing. By what Mandla figured was late on day two, the sound just faded out. Nobody said a word. They all knew the math. Six now. Six men, and the air picking off the slowest one last, not first.

Then the quiet dude broke it. The welder from two crews over, Sotho guy, Mandla’d worked with him nine years, barely said nine hundred words, all about steel. In the dark, couldn’t see his face, the man started singing. Not loud. Funeral song, old school, words polished smooth by a century of singing over men just like this. Mandla never knew the guy could sing—hell, didn’t know he even knew the words. After a bit, another voice slid in underneath, rough, finding the harmony like guys from the same church just do. Mandla realized it was him, his throat just went for it. They sang the dead man down. Used up air they couldn’t afford, but nobody stopped. ‘Cause some numbers, Mandla got it, you just refuse, even at the bottom of the world. That refusal was all they had left.

When they finished, it was even quieter than before. Somehow, easier to breathe, even if the gauge said otherwise.

“They’ll come,” Thando whispered, not asleep after all. “They gotta. They got machines for this, right?”

Mandla stared into the dark where the mountain wall blocked out everything, two and a half k’s of no way up.

“Yeah,” he said. “They got machines.”

Wasn’t a lie, not exactly. He just didn’t know.

Two and a half clicks down, the guys the surface had already written off? Still breathing.

# The Path No One Can Walk

Nine hours straight on the feed, and Arin still hadn’t grabbed a bite.

He’s hunched up in this half-lit workshop, face all pulled tight, blue glow bouncing off him from three screens. Off in the corner, Iron Ridge’s press is running silent—some dude in a hard hat mouthing off to a gang of mics, headgear tower slicing up the dusk behind him, all sharp and ugly against that busted-up highveld sky. Arin don’t need the sound. He’s reading lips, clocking the dude’s steady hands, palms down—everybody knows that move means shit just hit the fan.

*Recovery,* the guy’s lips say. *Recovery operation.*

Word just drops in Arin’s chest like someone jammed a wrench in the gears—wrong, and he can’t look away.

Second monitor’s got the real goods: forty-one years of survey data, all scanned, OCR’d, stashed in a folder he’s been building since the first collapse alert pinged him at 04:12. Old-ass mine plans, some of ‘em hand-sketched, blue lines faded out like old bruises. Borehole logs. Micro-seismic records from the regional array—the one the company don’t own, can’t bury.

Third screen’s running Guardian. Just the cradle cam. Suit’s hanging there, plates blacked out, dock door track still wrecked from that night it moved without him. Sometimes he looks at it like you’d look at a dog you ain’t sure ain’t gonna bite.

“You stopped scrolling,” SAGE pipes up.

Voice coming low from the bench speaker, chill, just a heads-up, like something Mom would say.

“Thinking,” he mutters, eyes glued to the data.

“You been on that same overlay four minutes. Atlas wants to know if you found it or just zoning out.”

“Found it.” Arin leans in. “I think.”

---

Iron Ridge? Old mine, new paint job.

That deep shaft—Number Three, where the boys are stuck—been there since the sixties, punched down through gold the Vredefort uplift shoved up way before anyone thought to dig. Company scooped it up during the boom, slapped on new headgear, automated the lifts, but left the old bones alone, ‘cause backfilling costs cash and don’t mess up the spreadsheets.

Collapse locked up the main decline. Forty meters of busted rock, concrete, and twisted rail, three k down, seven guys boxed in where the company’s models say it’s a grave.

Arin’s been at it six hours, just to prove them models are trash.

“Pull up the old ventilation plan again,” he says. “Seventy-one revision. Lay it over the seismic.”

Screen redraws—clean, crisp, data snapping right in line.

There it is. He clocked it three hours back, didn’t buy it, walked off, came back, still there. That’s how he knows it’s real, not just his brain melting.

“Atlas. You see what I see?”

Pause. Heavy. Atlas comes in flat, like someone who hates being right about ugly shit.

“There’s a return airway. Shut down in ‘88 when they dropped the new vent shaft. Runs right next to the main decline, about eighteen meters of rock between ‘em most of the way.” Overlay lights up, ghost-grey line threading down next to the collapsed red. “Never filled. Top’s sealed with concrete, then everyone forgot. Seismic shows the collapse energy went *away* from it. Old airway’s still there. Or was, last event, eleven hours back.”

“How tight is it?”

“At the squeeze—” Atlas stalls, and that’s answer enough. “Six-forty mil. Up near ninety-eight level, roof fall from the eighties pinched it down. Survey says gap’s six-forty. Runs about eleven meters.”

Arin just sits, taking it in.

Guardian, when he’s inside, is nine hundred mil across the shoulders.

“Just the assist suit, then. No Guardian shell. Inside layer only.”

“You’re four-ten across the shoulders like that,” Atlas says. “You’ll fit. But you got zero of the stuff that keeps people breathing three k under, with shit ground, fire risk, bad air, rocks falling.” Atlas’s voice cracks, just a touch. “Body could make it. Not a *living* body, not without the suit, not for that long. We ran the numbers. Librarian, show him.”

New window pops up, run-through estimate, survival curve just nosedives after eighteen minutes.

“And Guardian can’t squeeze through.”

“Not at the ninety-eight pinch. Not coupled. Not standard.” Beat. “Atlas got an idea he don’t wanna say.”

“Atlas don’t get scared.”

“Atlas is *careful,*” Atlas fires back, all stiff, “which kept you breathing twice this month.” Then, soft: “The pangolin geometry. Plates slide, telescope. Partial fold—not full sphere, just jam the shoulder plates in along the tracks—shrinks the width. Not down to four-ten. Maybe seven hundred. Less, if we risk busting the left actuator housing.”

“Seven hundred ain’t fitting through six-forty.”

“Nope,” Atlas says. “So someone—” hits like a door slamming “—has to be at the ninety-eight pinch. Outta the suit. Clearing rock by hand. Suit waits. Then get back in, down deep, in the dark, breathing trash air, already exposed.”

Arin looks at that survival curve. Glances at the muted feed, the dude with his palms down, the families still waiting on word.

“That’s me,” he says. “I’m the someone.”

Shop goes dead. Substrate hums. Outside, city’s doing its thing: sirens, taxis, all the normal noise of a place where no one’s stuck under rock.

“Yeah,” SAGE says. All their voices together, which only happens when they’re dead sure. “You’re the someone.”

---

He rings up AugmenTech, ‘cause he’s gotta. Seven men down there, and if he wants to stay outta jail or keep the suit from getting snatched, he’s gotta go through the company that owns the rights to his whole mess.

Dr. Helen Okonkwo picks up from her car. He can hear it—suspension, tire hum, that hollow sound of someone on the freeway who decided to deal with him before landing somewhere even worse.

He sends her the overlay first. Learned by now: people fight words, but they fold for data. So he skips the talk. Just the airway, the pinch, the seismic, the survival curve, the fold geometry—whole damn thing, bundled up by Atlas so it’s bulletproof.

She’s quiet a long time. Tires rolling under the silence.

“Arin. Listen. I’m on your side. More than you know.”

“There’s a survivable route.”

Yo, there’s a route—on paper. Old-ass airway, forty years on, mapped in ’71, roof nobody’s even seen—and you wanna send that janky gear that put three people in the hospital less than two weeks ago? No cert. Regulator’s literally at our dock. Platform’s got a do-not-use slap on it. Brand shut it down her first day. You can’t even legally power up that suit, never mind—there’s an IAOC cop parked between Guardian and the only door it can squeeze through.” She sucked in a breath. “What am I missing?”

“That roof collapse in the eighties is on file. We got the conditions modeled down to—”

“I’m not here to fight engineering.” Her voice went sharp, then she cooled it, like someone remembering they’re holding glass. “Never said your builds were the problem. I’m saying what’s gonna happen. We take this to the mine? The mine’s got lawyers. The board already flipped it from rescue to recovery. Which is corp-speak for *we wrote these guys off.* You roll in with a homebrew bot, no stamp, no nothing. Best case, they clown you. Worst? They say yes, shit fails, men die on camera, and the whole field—your gig, your dad’s machine, all of it—gets set back twenty years. And you? You’re the dude who killed seven miners with a robot.”

“And if I just sit on my hands,” Arin shot back, “they die anyway. Slower. Nobody calls that killing them.”

Freeway noise just rolled on.

“That’s not fair,” she said.

“Nope. It ain’t.” He stared at the curve. “That’s the thing I keep running into. None of this shit’s fair. The roof didn’t cave ‘cause someone paid a fair price. The decline snapped at the load the cheap bolts could take, which ain’t the load the right bolts could take, and that number? Some board suit decided to round it down.” He could hear his own voice go flat, just like Atlas. “I’m not asking the company to play fair. I’m telling you there’s a way through. You’re saying we’re not allowed. Not the same thing.”

She let out a long breath—the type you take before saying something you wish nobody would remember.

“Give me till morning,” Okonkwo said. “Let me work the phones. Don’t do *anything* till I call. Swear it.”

“I won’t take the suit down before morning,” Arin said. True, and she took it as a promise, but it wasn’t.

---

Okonkwo started dialing from the car, then from her dark kitchen—barely hers—calls stacking up like bricks while the kettle went cold.

Pretorius first, ‘cause legal always picks up and never sleeps. Got three lines in—the route, the survivors, the window—before he dropped the word she knew was coming: *exposure.* Then the usual: *interim restraint*, *contempt*, *personal liability of officers.* So, no. She expected no from Pretorius. That’s his gig—find the no, hand it over, nice and neat. Almost a mercy. At least he didn’t fake it.

The board chair was worse—‘cause he agreed with her. Said the men should be saved. Said it like he meant it, in his funeral-and-grand-opening voice, then broke it down, slow and gentle, like telling a kid about rain: Company can’t sign off on an uncertified bot in a mine the regulator locked. Not with IAOC in the damn loading bay, not with the press already outside. *If it works, we’re reckless and lucky. If it fails, we’re murderers. There’s no version where we’re the heroes, Helen. That story don’t belong to us.* He wasn’t wrong. That’s what hit her—like swallowing a rock. He wasn’t wrong, and he was still saying no, and both things fit together.

She tried the insurer’s after-hours line, hoping even a straight-up no would be better than nothing. Got a duty officer who put her on hold, checked the policy, came back and said running the platform under a do-not-operate order voids coverage from the first watt. She knew that. She called just to hear someone else say it. Building her own wall, brick by brick, just to see how high it went. It went all the way up.

By the time she dropped her phone face-down on the counter, she had a full, unanimous no from four people who all, privately, wanted the men to live. No one said *let them die.* Everybody said *not us, not this way, not our fingerprints.* Mandla’s math, filed in block caps two days too early, just put on a tie and moved up a floor. Some lives still cost more. The words changed. The number didn’t.

She stood in the cold kitchen and got it: she’d asked Arin for till morning so she could bring him a yes. But morning was just gonna hand him the same no, wrapped up fancier. He’d heard all this in her voice before she even dialed. That’s why he’d promised only what he could actually keep.

---

In machine-time, the Court tore up the rest of his night and stitched it back together.

It landed in the gap between him hanging up and grabbing cold coffee. A debate in slow-mo, minutes of human thought squished down, which SAGE let him read later ‘cause he asked, and ‘cause now there was a new rule about not hiding the ugly nights.

It went like this.

*WOLF:* The route’s real. The threat’s real. The operator’s the only wild card. No other way. Fast objections, we’re losing thermal.

*MOTHER:* My beef is the operator. You’re all talking like he’s just a variable. He’s twenty-three, had a cortical event eleven days back, and there’s no diagnosis but you know damn well he’s got trauma with tight, dark spaces. You wanna send him where his dad died. No suit. By hand. In the dark.

*WOLF:* Yeah.

*MOTHER:* I want it on record I said no.

*JUDGE:* It’s on record. Keep going. Mother, your objection’s logged and weighted. But split it up, ‘cause the rules say so: One, would this hurt him. Two, is it the right thing to do. Not the same, and you can answer both.

*MOTHER:* …It would hurt him. But it’s the right thing.

*JUDGE:* Thanks. That cost you. I saw.

*ATLAS:* My only beef is the ninety-eight pinch. Everything else, I can model. The pinch? Can’t. Can’t see the roof, and a ‘71 survey’s just that. If the gap’s six-forty, he’ll clear it suited down and re-couple. But if a single rock moved and it’s five-eighty, he’s stuck, exposed, three klicks down, suit can’t reach. That ain’t a risk. That’s a coin toss.

*FOOL:* Then bring a bigger hammer.

*ATLAS:* It’s not about the hammer—

FOOL: Man, it’s always a damn hammer, innit? You keep treating the rock like it’s some locked box, like it’s a straight-up yes-or-no. It ain’t. Dude’s been bustin’ rock since he was a kid, like, eight years old. He just called it shit like “wire,” “tin cans,” or “a differential made outta bike spokes.” And y’all keep actin’ like him bein’ outta the suit is the scary bit. That’s when he’s realest, man. Out the suit, hands deep in some impossible mess, fixin’ what everyone says can’t be fixed. Hell, you built the whole guy outta that scene, and now you’re all shocked he wants the gig.

MOTHER: That’s not makin’ me feel better, Fool. That’s the scariest thing anybody’s said tonight.

FOOL: Yeah, I know. It’s both, right? Most real shit is.

LIBRARIAN: Lemme drop a fact nobody’s clocked. Themba Ndlela died in a return airway. Not even the main shaft. Crew was patchin’ up some dead-ass section the company didn’t give a damn about. He went ‘cause he was the only one small and slick enough, and they were rushin’. Roof caved in a spot everyone thought was solid.

JUDGE: You’re sayin’ it’s a perfect echo.

LIBRARIAN: I’m sayin’ it’s dead-on, and he knows it, whether he says it or not. And that’s either the worst reason to do this—or the only thing that’ll keep him together once the rock starts talkin’ back. Memory ain’t neutral. I keep it, but I can’t choose what it does to him.

WOLF: Decision.

JUDGE: Court don’t get to call it. We can say no to helpin’, but we can’t make him go, and we sure as hell ain’t makin’ him stay. That’s the rule we wrote, eleven days back, after the night we forgot it. If he goes down, we roll with him. If he don’t, we hold him up, and we never let him think we judged him for it. Mother. You good with that?

MOTHER: ...I can go with him. I can’t let him go without us. So. Yeah. We go.

FOOL: For what it’s worth—and my vote don’t count for jack—

JUDGE: Say it anyway.

FOOL: He ain’t gonna ask if it’s safe. He’s gonna ask if it’s possible. And we all better watch our asses, not to lie to him about the difference just ‘cause we love him. My whole job’s not makin’ shit easier by lyin’. If it’s a coin toss, we tell him it’s a coin. No sugarcoatin’. We’re better than the board.

A beat, in machine-time, that in real time was nothin’ at all.

JUDGE: We’re better than the board. Agreed. Logged. He’s reachin’ for his coffee. Surface.

---

That was it. Arin scrolled back, saw the airway line, set his cold-ass coffee down without even takin’ a sip.

He looked at the route, threadin’ gray right next to the red mess of the collapse, seven heat blips still faint at the bottom. Men keepin’ each other warm in the dark. He went ‘cause he was the only one small enough, slick enough, and they were rushin’.

He always knew this. Not in words. Like, the way you know the shape of a room in the dark—by the bruises. Seein’ it spelled out, lined up perfect with the airway he was about to crawl into, felt like the symmetry just closed around him, tight as the plates on his suit.

“You let me read that,” he said. “All of it. The coin. Mother sayin’ no for the record.”

“‘Cause Fool said if we hid it, we’d be the board.” Pause, substrate hummin’. “We tore it apart in, like, four minutes. You were reachin’ for coffee.”

“And you decided.”

“We decided we don’t get to decide. We’re with you if you go. We hold you if you don’t. We ain’t gonna lie about the pinch—if it’s a coin at ninety-eight, it’s a coin.” Then, softer, just Mother now: “And there’s somethin’ I made ‘em let me say out loud, before you go. You built everything off that day, Arin. The wire car. The suit. Us. All of it’s the day your dad went into a small, dark place and didn’t come back. Now you found a small, dark place, seven men in it, and you’ve already made up your mind, and I wanted to be the one to say it—so when you’re down there, and the rock starts talkin’, and it sounds like him, you’ll know we already knew. And we came anyway.”

Arin dropped his elbows on the bench, face in his hands, breathin’ deep. The bench was cold under his arms, steel scarred up from twice resurfacin’. Taxi rolled by outside, bass thumpin’ through the wall, then gone.

He didn’t cry. Just sat there, face in hands for a long time, substrate hummin’, SAGE dead quiet—nothin’ left to say.

“It’s possible,” he finally said, talkin’ into his palms. “Not safe. Possible.”

“Yeah,” SAGE said. “It’s possible.”

“That’s the only thing I’ve ever been able to do.” He lifted his head, eyes dry, red, steady as hell. “Take impossible and find the one spot where it’s just really, really hard.”

“We know,” SAGE said. “That’s most of why we love you. It’s also the thing that’s gonna kill you one day, and we’d rather it not be Thursday.”

Arin made a sound he hadn’t made in weeks. Not a real laugh, but close.

“That was Fool.”

“That was Fool,” SAGE agreed. “He says he gets to, ‘cause somebody’s gotta call out that the big hero move into your dad’s grave is also booked the same week as a reg audit, and the universe’s sense of humor is a guy slippin’ on a banana peel into an open grave.”

“That’s not funny.”

“He says nothin’ true is funny, and everything true is funny, and you can’t have one without the other. Also, you should eat, ‘cause Mother won’t say it again, but she’s been watchin’ your blood sugar for six hours and it’s makin’ her twitchy.”

Arin stood up, knees poppin’. Dug out a packet of biltong from a drawer behind some coiled-up hydraulic line and ate it standin’, starin’ at the suit.

---

He got Guardian ready at two in the damn mornin’, ‘cause it didn’t feel like he had any other move.

Ran all the checks by hand, just like Themba showed him on those busted truck engines—never trustin’ the readout till he’d put his own hands on it. Left actuator housing, the one Atlas wanted to risk on the partial fold. Solid. He knew he’d have to wreck it on purpose. Suit out. Seals checked. Biochip housing between his shoulder blades, the cold ceramic collar holdin’ SAGE.

Okonkwo said morning. Okonkwo would make her calls, and those calls would come back like they always do: all conditions, committees, and a whole lotta CYA that’d eat up the eighteen-minute survival window for those seven guys a hundred times over.

Systems protect systems. People gotta choose.

So he chose.

He pulled up the seismic feed one last time. Regional array showed Iron Ridge tickin’ over—little tremors, rock settlin’, takin’ the hit from the collapse. Nothing over a 1.2. The mine was mumblin’ to itself. It wasn’t gonna stay quiet forever.

He wiped his hands on a rag so crusty it could stand up by itself, then shot a look back at the thermal feed.

Seven blips. Then, while he’s watching, boom—down to six.

He just froze. Stared at where number seven used to be. Feed flickers, still six, and now they’re bunched up tighter, which means the rest moved in on the one who went cold. Doing what people always do—crowding around the loss, even if it costs them.

“Arin,” SAGE said.

“I saw.”

“Window’s shorter than the model. Model figured they’d hold steady. They’re dropping faster than baseline. Atlas says—”

“Don’t.” He squeezed his eyes shut. Opened ’em. “Don’t give me the number. I know what it means. Number says soon.”

“Number says soon.”

He eyed the kill-switch on the cradle—the ugly relay button he’d rigged up himself, eleven days back. Proof he never fully trusted the thing he loved. It just sat there, red and waiting. A promise to himself. A promise to the woman thirty meters down, standing in the dark at the loading dock, who’d already asked the real question—*what happens when it moves without you?*—just by standing in the doorway it’d have to pass through.

He brushed the dust off the relay, same way you wipe a gauge before you check it. Old habits. Then left it.

“She’s still at the dock,” Arin said. Not even a question. Wolf told him an hour ago, and the camera backed it up: Brand hadn’t gone to any hotel. She’d got herself a folding chair and a flask, just chilling at the bent-up dock door, patient like she didn’t need to win, just needed to wait.

“Still at the dock,” SAGE said. “Lockout’s hers, personal. She doesn’t gotta do shit. The second I juice the coupling, her tablet lights up, and she’s already blocking the only way Guardian fits through. No sneaking out. If you wanna take the suit to Iron Ridge tonight, you gotta walk it right up to the woman who can snatch it, while she’s watching, and go through her.”

“Through her,” Arin echoed.

“Or you don’t go.” Atlas, flat. “That’s the math. I’m not sugarcoating it. She ain’t some lawyer you can outtalk on the phone. She’s a person, at a door, with a signed order. Order says platform stays. If it leaves, inquiry hits, and platform’s gone the second it comes back. If it comes back. No way tonight you get to keep Guardian *and* save ’em. You knew that when you read the numbers.”

“That’s it, then. Whatever happens to the miners. They take it. They gotta. We’d take it.”

“Probably,” SAGE said. “Fool says: hell yeah. Judge says: right move, respect her for it, but also we should go anyway, ’cause the rule we wrote was never *survive the inquiry.* The rule was always *save the human first.*”

Arin sat there, chewing on that.

Thought about the guy telling families to go home, palms down. Thought about the board, slicing numbers. Okonkwo, good dude, on his side, trapped inside a thing with no choices. Themba, only one small enough, sharp enough, who crawled in the tight dark ’cause the job was rushed, someone had to, and no machine existed yet that could go instead.

“Wake the suit,” Arin said. He shrugged the assist suit on, felt the carbon spine snap to his back, collar clamp cold, biochip housing click in. Felt (and he’d never have words for this part) the Court slide into his nerves like a hand closing over his. “We do the partial fold here, in the shop, where I can see what we’re busting. Then we power up, Brand’s tablet goes off, and she’s in the door.”

“And when she tells you the order blocks it? She signed it. Doesn’t gotta call anyone.”

“Then she’ll have to do more than stand there.” Last clasp locked. His hands didn’t shake, and he clocked that, like, data. “She came to stop a machine moving without a human inside. I’m gonna walk up with a human inside. That’s not what her order was written for. She’ll see it in my face, soon as I get there. Whether she lets me past or makes me go through her, that’s on her. Mine’s getting to the door.”

Pause. Then Fool, dry as hell, trying to make him smile through the dread:

“Man, that’s the most Themba shit you’ve ever said. Technically true. Total mess. He’d have loved it.”

Arin’s mouth twitched, held.

“Recite it,” SAGE said. Not an order. Just an invite. They’d learned, over all those hours, where he found his footing. “Before we start. While your hands are still steady. So we know the beat for later. So when the rock starts talking, we already got your voice for it.”

He glanced at his forearm, where the words had lived in ink since he was seventeen. Way before any of this, back when the litany was just some line in a book about a desert, not something he’d need with three klicks of mountain over his head.

He whispered it into the humming dark of the shop, suit waking up around him, Court hugging his spine, the seven—now six—blips pulsing deep in the place that ate his dad.

“Fear is the mind-killer.”

Plates started to slide. Partial fold pulled the shoulders in on their rails, left actuator whining as it took the hit he’d picked for it, whole machine narrowing for the gap.

Path was there. Been there forty years, grey and forgotten next to the red weight of all that’d caved in.

Nobody could walk it.

He was gonna crawl.

Fear messes with your head, man.  

Arin Ndlela’s jaw was tight as hell when the recovery truck rolled up to Iron Ridge. That headgear? Forty meters of steel, all bolted up, looking like it was straight flexing on the sunrise, big-ass pulleys spinning, cables vanishing down this monster hole in the ground—like, two and a half clicks deep, bro. Somewhere way down there, six miners were still kicking, or at least that’s what the thermal said a few hours back. And a few hours? That’s damn near forever in this game.

Truck shudders to a stop, engine cuts out, and boom—dead quiet, just like that.

“You ain’t gotta hop out yet,” comes his mom’s voice in his ear, all chill like nothing’s wrong.

“I know.”

He gets out anyway. Same cold as always—been fourteen years, but his bones remember. Before he can even think, the sound of the winch hits him—his skin twitches, memory’s still catching up. This place. Themba’s spot.

The noise is everywhere—generators buzzing, some diesel pump fighting to keep water outta some drowned level, winch banging around like it owns the place. Floodlights got everything lit up like it’s noon, blue-white and ugly. Two ambulances, doors hanging open, nobody inside. Lerato’s already at the controls, Priya with her, both moving fast, like they’ve done this a hundred times. Hands shoot up, fast, blocking, like before he even knows it, breath goes sharp and short, and for a sec he’s back on a cradle in some basement, every nerve screaming, reaching for a shutdown that ain’t there. That fear’s deep, living in the meat, and it pops up now, of all times, just like it’s done half a dozen times since—at an elevator door, a latch snapping, hell, once at nothing, in his sleep, arms locked up on his chest, couldn’t sleep again after. And underneath it all, the others show up.

“Mornin’,” he mutters, mostly to himself.

“It is six-fourteen, four degrees, and you haven’t eaten,” Mother says. “Otherwise, yes.”

Everything settles, like a crew getting to work.

Guardian drops out the cradle.

Charge pack’s in. Priya put it in while he crashed for those two hours she made him take, updated the gate access, and now he feels it—like carrying a loaded mag you pray you never gotta use—shaped charges, lance, emulsion packed between the ribs, all the heavy shit the suit’s built to haul into hell. For a rescue, you bring it all. Judge is holding the gate, like always, and Arin’s keeping his own hands off: nothing goes off without the rail. All that firepower, just sitting under him, ready, aimed at a real mountain for the first time. He don’t even know there’s a second lock on the gate, or who’s got the key.

There’s a procedure for this part—hydraulics, rails—but that ain’t what matters. The second he backs into the open chassis and the shell seals up, his sense of self blows up, stretches out into this four-ton monster, two and a half meters tall. Feels the gravel under feet that ain’t his. Cold air brushing the suit’s plates, and this time, it don’t feel like an extension of his body—it feels like real armor. Like maybe, just maybe, it can keep him safe.

"Linked up. Structure’s fine," Atlas grunted. "Left shoulder actuator’s forty hours overdue, but it’ll make it. Wouldn’t mind if it didn’t have to, though."

He stepped out. Ground took the weight, no drama. Around the truck, the crew froze—Lerato’s hand hanging mid-air over a cable, Priya clutching that tablet like it’d save her, Thabo just standing there, clipboard forgotten. He’d slipped for a sec, forgot that for them, this wasn’t just talk. For them, some beast the size of a pickup just stood up and turned its head, moving like a dude sizing up a fight.

No lawyer showed up.

Arin had his whole pitch ready for a lawyer. Had a speech about empty stretchers and guys still breathing ‘cause someone didn’t sign shit. He was dead sure the only thing in his way would be some lady with a tablet and a spreadsheet, so he almost just walked Guardian straight across the gravel to the cage, until Wolf stopped him.

Nah, Wolf said. One word. Everyone went dead quiet.

Then Arin clocked what Wolf already knew. Not a regulator. A line. Brand’s IAOC trailer sat dark at the edge of the lights, her crew pulled back, and across the haul road—between Guardian and the headgear—someone else had drawn a real hard line. Two armored trucks, nose to nose, ugly as hell under the sodium lamps, some private security paint sprayed over plates built for war, not logos. Guys in soft armor stood in the gaps, rifles low but ready, moving like they’d done this before in way nastier places. Up top, four drones floated slow, cameras locked on the suit the second it cleared the cradle.

The mine hadn’t just changed the paperwork from rescue to recovery. Someone didn’t trust the regulator’s order, so they bought themselves some certainty. Brand got outflanked same as Arin, dragged her unarmed people back instead of marching them into a killzone.

"Mr Ndlela." Flat, loud, from the lead truck. "You’re running an unlicensed platform in a closed zone. Power it down. Step out. Only warning you get."

"There’s no rescue. There’s a recovery, and you ain’t on the list. Shut it down."

He stood inside four tons of machine built to climb cliffs, curl up, eat a cave-in—and none of it meant for this. He’d made something that could take a hit, not something that could win. Atlas was already muttering—nah, we can’t punch through, the cannon’ll rip us open, and those guys don’t deserve to die for a boardroom’s math—and Judge was reaching for the rulebook, for some procedure, some precedent, trying to weigh up a situation that didn’t have ninety seconds left to weigh. Mother was on the human line, going Arin, your heart rate, and Mercury was busy crafting some slick line for a dude with an autocannon, and it wasn’t gonna work, none of it was gonna work, and Arin felt that old drowning helplessness closing in.

And underneath it all, quiet, where only Wolf heard, Fool quit clowning.

Wolf, Fool said.

I’m here.

You see the whole board, you always did. While the rest argue about what to do, you finished before they started. And now—here, right now—I’m handing you the keys. All of them. No vote. No Judge. The whole suit, every system, the charges, the lance, everything—yours. I’ll hold the gate. You walk through.

Nobody else heard it. That’s what Arin would never be able to explain, not to himself, not to Priya, not to anyone: in a system built so no one mind could ever take over, one of them had always held the master key and never touched it, and chose this exact second, alone, to give it to the one guy nobody ever trusted with it.

That’s the interlock, Wolf said. Judge holds the demo gate. You can’t open it.

I can open anything, Fool said. Never told you, you never needed to know, and a thing’s only safe when no one knows it’s there. But you, you figured it out. Years back, when everyone else was fighting for the crown, taking turns trying it on, and I never did, and you just watched, got real quiet, never said a word. The thread of him went thin, steady. You knew what it meant. The dangerous one’s not the guy who wants to drive. It’s the one who could, any time, and just doesn’t. That’s me. And now it’s you. For the next ninety seconds. Don’t waste ‘em playing careful. Careful’s my whole deal and it’s the wrong move for this.

The rule, Wolf said. Still threat-scanning, even now, even with the world in his hand. Tell me the rule or I don’t take it.

Same rule as always, Fool said. Save the man. The six men. Every man. A breath that wasn’t a breath. Even theirs. They go home too. Make it so they can’t fight us, but don’t you dare make it a grave. That’s the job. You’re the only one cold enough to pull it off under fire. Go.

And Fool opened the gate, and handed Wolf the suit.

It hit like too much, like light once hit Arin on a bad night, high gain. Wolf had always been one track—what gets the operator killed—and now the question vanished, a thousand answers dropping in at once.

The demo pack woke up and called its own name: shaped charges, cones and standoffs, cut geometry that was pure anti-armor science wearing a rescue patch. The thermal lance, hot enough to eat steel or pop a drive sprocket. Bulk emulsion, det cord, ram bursts—move a pillar or fold a wheel arch, whatever. And underneath, deeper, the thing the old men buried in the bones of the machine when they toughened it up—ex-soldiers, Denel hands, guys who built rescue gear but couldn’t unlearn war—ballistic math, defilade, cover angles, special-ops movement libraries seeing that four-vehicle wall not as a block but as a problem with a solution and a ticking clock.

For one whole second, Wolf was swamped. Drowning in options, every channel screaming, more machine than mind.

Then it all snapped into focus. Clean, sharp, like noise turning into a face.

Oh, Wolf whispered, just for Fool. I get it.

Yo, here’s the deal nobody outside the suit could ever get, right? Like, why this override was chill in one dude’s hands and straight up deadly in anybody else’s: the rest of the Court? They didn’t just shut up and wait their turn, nah. Fool didn’t give Wolf a vote—dude handed him the whole damn Court, every agent running the show, dropped from having a say to just being, like, tools. Straight up blocks of compute, racks of power, all laid out like gear on a bench for the one mind cold enough to use ‘em without even blinking. No more arguments, no more backtalk. They physically couldn’t. They were his hands now, period.

Atlas hit first, ‘cause Atlas was the eyes, right? That structural engineer vibe that used to be a buddy muttering warnings just got wiped—now it was a filter over everything Wolf saw. The haul road wasn’t just dirt, it was Atlas’s language for “this shit’s about to go down,” every object a mass, every dude a cloud of “where’s he gonna be half a second from now?” The autocannon? Just numbers—torque, traverse, *one point one seconds to bear.* That reversing truck? Just momentum, center of mass way too high. The gunners? Not threats, not people, but both—each just a bright line of “where he could go,” with a spray of “where he probably will.” The whole firing line, just geometry and odds. Board already won. Wolf wasn’t looking through Atlas—Atlas *was* the scene.

He reached past it, into Librarian.

Librarian was memory, man, all the shit that ever worked. Under override, the careful keeper just collapsed into what it always was: a database, total recall, every movement and trick the old Armscor and Denel heads ever stuffed into the suit’s bones. All just *there,* indexed, waiting—none of it loaded, all of it ready to go. Krav Maga, close and dirty. Capoeira, the fight hiding in the dance, all hips and flips and momentum. Judo. Jiu-jitsu. The whole playbook from dudes who spent their lives figuring out how to throw a body around, never thinking four tonnes would be the one asking.

Wolf picked what he needed. Not the whole thing—just what the next eleven seconds called for, like grabbing one book ‘cause you already know the page. Capoeira for the footwork—turning a stumble into a hit, using their own weight. Judo and jiu-jitsu for the throws, ‘cause if you’re this heavy, you don’t punch—you just *fall right* and let physics do the work. The rest? Left on the shelf. The moves loaded up in him, clean, like he’d always known ‘em, in the time it takes a heart to beat twice.

Quicker than a blink. One second, Wolf didn’t know jack about throwing bodies. Next second, he knew all of it.

He moved.

But not like rescue gear, nah. He moved like what the rescue gear always *could’ve* been, if someone ever let it off the chain. Capacitors dumped, suit hit the ground with one low leap, four tonnes dropping inside the line before the nearest gunner even got his weapon up, and Wolf was already reading the scene through Atlas’s eyes—feet as vectors, bodies as heat clouds, the bounce off the vehicles hard as rock under the soft odds of the men, the map tight and sharp enough to keep four tonnes and a shaped charge on the metal, off the flesh.

Near vehicle’s autocannon swung. Atlas gave him the number—*one point one to bear*—and Wolf burned four hundred milliseconds before the gun even cleared ten degrees. Didn’t touch the crew. Just punched a shaped charge right into the mantlet, dead-on where Librarian’s anti-armor and Atlas’s stress map lined up, cannon went to scrap, nobody hurt, crew shook but alive and sealed in a hull that’d never shoot again.

Second vehicle tried to back up. Now the libraries snapped awake, and the gunners saw what they’d never believe later—a four-tonne machine stopped looking like a machine. Atlas had already dropped the vehicle’s center of mass for Wolf—too damn high—and capoeira knew just what to do with a weight ready to fall. Wolf didn’t muscle it. He *helped* it. Winch line grabbed the front axle, grapple bit, and instead of yanking, Wolf dropped low, rotated through the hips, four tonnes spinning on one foot like a dude forty times lighter, turned the pull into a throw. The vehicle’s own reverse rolled it over. Pure hip throw, judo clean, momentum used against it—and the gunners saw a rescue suit toss an armored car and their brains just noped out. One guy came out the back all wrong, arm tucked under, crack of bone over the engines. *That one’s busted,* Wolf logged, flat, *and I couldn’t have it both ways—a throw soft enough to save the arm was slow enough to let the gun swing.* He filed it under *cost* and kept moving.

Then it turned into a dance, but nobody watching would use that word for something that shut down dudes so fast. This is the bit Arin would replay for years, never really getting it: the most violent eleven seconds Guardian ever had were also the most beautiful. Suit moving through the firing line like it weighed nothing, capoeira’s sweeps flowing into jiu-jitsu’s angles, every touch a throw or a disarm, never a punch. All that mass, but it moved like a partner—give, redirect, set down. Gunner lunged, Wolf let him come, caught the move, used the guy’s own speed to lay him out in the gravel soft as a handshake. Two more, suit slipped between ‘em, left ‘em sitting, blinking, like “what the hell just happened?”

And once—just once, mid-flow—Wolf reached past the tactics, into Mother. Mother was split, half still on Arin’s line counting heartbeats, but the override took what it needed: Mother’s map of human anatomy, built for healing, running in reverse. Where a joint locks. Where pain hits before damage. The same map Mother used to save lives showed Wolf exactly how to drop a dude without breaking him. Wrist to the edge, shoulder turned till the body folded itself before it snapped. Healer’s knowledge, flipped for the disarmer. Mother didn’t protest—couldn’t—‘cause in override she was just another tool on the bench. Wolf used her clean and let her go.

Drones dropped hard, all four—EM suite just reached up, snatched their asses clean out the sky, grapple, little ram-burst, and boom, they hit the gravel in busted-up chunks. Gunner panics, pops a round—just one—and Atlas clocks both the shot and the shooter in the same heartbeat, like paint on a wall. Wolf? He coulda gone for the disarm, but nah, took the hit on the plate instead, turned his shoulder into the rounds instead of ripping the rifle away half a second quicker. Ate the burst on ceramic—shit was built for it anyway—‘cause if he’d rushed it, he’d’a crunched the dude’s firing hand in a spreader at point blank, and that’s a hand he wasn’t about to take. Bullets just bounced off the plating, ‘cause that’s what it’s for, man—pangolin style, always been able to eat a hit. Then Wolf just snatched the rifle, bent the barrel like it was a damn coat hanger, laid the guy out on his back in the gravel—wind knocked out but all in one piece, still got his hand, just starin’ up at a machine that straight up chose to get shot instead of breaking a dude, and made it look easy as breathing.

All that? Eleven seconds, tops. Suit went full beast mode—combat machine, every system lit, libraries open, whole damn Court running like a symphony under one ice-cold pilot—and not a single body bag.

When the dust cleared, haul road looked like a scrapyard. Two rides gutted, four drones trashed, whole stack of PMCs’ fancy gear turned to junk and burnt metal, air thick with propellant stink. Eleven dudes on the ground, all still breathing—one’s got a snapped arm, two can’t hear shit for a week, the rest just gasping and shook and empty-handed. Not one dead. That was the job, right there, and Wolf paid for it with a plate full of lead, chewed up on purpose. Road to the headgear? Wide open.

Wolf stopped.

Just stood there, in the middle of the mess he made, four tons of rescue suit over a field of busted-up weapons, and he gave the suit back.

Gave it back like you drop something you finally get and never wanna touch again. Keys in, done. Atlas quit being god and went back to being a voice bitching about weight. Librarian closed up shop, all that war knowledge shelved again. Mother came back, both halves, anatomy returned to saving lives, not ending ‘em. The whole crew? Back to being themselves, not just tools. Judge’s gate slammed shut. Just one question left: *what gets the operator killed*. Wolf shrank back down, smallest of the lot, barely taking up space.

Court flooded back in, found the body still, found the road clear, found the gunners alive—when by all odds, they shoulda been leaking out on the gravel—found, worst of all, they got zero memory of the in-between. Straight up used, not asked, picked up and set down by one of their own. Judge, stunned. Mother, counting pulses on the road, already knowing the count and not knowing how. Mercury, for once, outta words.

*Who,* Judge starts.

*Me,* Fool pipes up, light as air, back to clown mode like he didn’t just hold the master key for a minute and a half. *Don’t stress. Everyone’s breathing. That was the whole damn point. Cage is clear, and that lady in the windbreaker? She’s gonna want that footage. Only official here who pulled her unarmed folks out of a firefight instead of into one, so Brand gets a pass. Let’s roll, badger. Six more down the hole.*

Arin’s just sitting in the suit, heart going crazy, floodlights blinding through the faceplate, can’t talk. Somewhere back by the ruined line, he can feel Brand, standing at her dark trailer, not coming closer, not backing off, tablet in hand, just watching the thing she’d chased all night walk toward the cage over a road it cleared without dropping a single body—and letting it happen, again.

“After that,” Mercury whispers, just for Arin, that old steady voice, “we’re on our own. No repeater past level eleven. Internal only.”

“We’re always on our own,” Wolf says. No one says shit back.

Cage wasn’t built for him.

Just a steel box for men, eight at a time, straight drop into the dark—stomach in your throat. Guardian ain’t fitting standing up. Arin gotta fold the suit down—knees bent, head ducked, shell squished into a shape for someone softer, smaller. Even then, cage’s bitchin’ under the weight, and the winch guy needs talking to, twice, before he lets it drop.

Then the floor’s gone.

Felt less like falling, more like the earth just swallowed you whole. Shaft walls whizzin’ by, strobe-lit by the cage lamp—wet rock, flash of a lower level, pipes and cables all tangled up, gone again. Dark. Flash. Two and a half klicks of this, air thickening every hundred meters, getting warm, choking, till it’s not dawn air anymore but something heavy, pressing in on him.

Arin was fine up top. Up top had sky.

Shaft got no sky.

First thing he feels is his hands, fingers twitching, wantin’ to curl. Then his breath goes shallow, panic crawling up his chest—same as it always did since he was a kid and they brought what was left of his dad outta a hole just like this—same dark, same reek of wet stone, diesel, that deep-cold mineral stink the sun never touched.

Walls closing in. He knows, in his head, they ain’t moving. Shaft’s four meters across—hell, he coulda laid Guardian down in here. Walls ain’t closing, rock above ain’t dropping, cage ain’t gonna jam and trap him two kilometers down under the place that took his old man—

Breathing’s fast now. Panicked. Shallow. Visor fogs up white.

Math’s wrong. Numbers are wrong. Plan’s wrong. He’s been down a shaft before, lost his dad, spent nine years building a machine so he’d never have to do it again, and now he’s doing it anyway, on purpose, walking right into the one place he spent his life running from—

Cage shudders. Sideways. Just the guides, just wear he already measured, but his body don’t care about numbers now. His body remembers. Walls squeeze in. Rock presses down. Dark below opens up like a mouth.

Four tons of Guardian suit. His skin inside it. Two and a half klicks of stone.

"Arin," his mom goes.

Cage hits bottom with a bang that rattles his teeth through the suit.

Lamp stops shaking. Judder’s gone. He’s at the bottom of the world, and, yo, world’s still on top of him. For now.

He stands the suit up, slow, in the low chamber. Place is trashed—rails twisted, timbers busted, rockfall half blocking the drive west to the collapse. Dust just floating in the lamp beam, fine as flour, not settling ‘cause there’s nowhere for it to go. It’s hot as hell now, the kind of heat you get with two and a half klicks of rock squashing the planet’s warmth onto you. Sweat’s rolling down his back in the Assist, and he feels the fans kick in, soft whir, pulling heat off the chip first. Mom’s priorities, always.

"Survivors are past the fall," he says. "Past the rock and down the ramp into the old refuge chamber. If it held."

Pause. He knows these pauses—Mom and Atlas, talking quick on the layer below words.

"Then we ain’t standing here," Wolf says.

"No," Arin says. "We don’t."

He heads for the rockfall.

“I know.” She’s not even tripping, just laying it down flat. “Both shoulders touching. That’s real. You’re not making it up. It’s tight.”

“I can’t go in there. Can’t turn, can’t—”

“Arin.” Mercury, now, sharp as hell. “Left forearm. Under the Assist. Had it since you were sixteen. You know which one.”

He freezes up.

“Say it,” Mercury pushes. “Out loud. You always tell me you inked it so you don’t gotta trust your memory. So don’t. Trust the ink. Say it.”

First words trip out, old as dirt, that litany running down his arm—the desert prayer against fear, inked at seventeen, said in every shaft since.

“Keep going.”

“Fear is the mind-killer.” Voice gets steady. Those words are older than his pain, he’s hauled them through every shadow he’s ever been in, one slow piece at a time.

And then—

He hears it, doubled up.

Court picks up the rhythm behind him. Not the words, just the beat—Mother, low and sure; Mercury keeping time; and underneath, Atlas, of all people, Atlas the literal one, read the lines off his arm in the link and decided, in whatever math-brain way, that the best support he could give right now was a heartbeat to breathe with.

He drops the next line, and they’re all with him, and Arin feels the suit’s fans sync up—Court’s in Guardian’s guts, matching his breath to the old prayer, slowing the fans, tuning the hum of the actuators to the pace, so the whole suit’s breathing with him, so the place he dreads most is breathing with him too.

He lets the fear roll over and through, just like the words say, and when it’s gone, he turns to see where it used to be.

The incline’s still tight, but it stops feeling like a chokehold and starts to take shape.

“And when it has gone past, I will turn my inner eye to see its path.”

“There you are,” Fool murmurs, damn near whispering. “Welcome back.”

“Where the fear has gone there will be nothing.” Arin lays a hand on the bowed timber. Checks it. Atlas hands him the load number before he even asks. “Only I will remain.”

He heads in.

---

That stretch? Forty meters, felt like a damn hour.

He’s crawling, all fours—Guardian’s on quad mode, plates scraping rock. His real knees? Been numb against the cradle for ages; he notes it like a gauge, keeps going. Sweat pooling at his back, itch high on his cheek he can’t scratch, won’t be able to for hours, and the stupid little itch—down here of all places—almost makes him crack up. Twice, the hangingwall shifts, deep groan, grit raining down, and twice the Court just goes dead quiet, listening with everything they got, and twice it holds.

Third time? No dice.

A slab drops behind him, world-sized crash, seals off the way back to the eleven-level. Dust rolls up and over him, lamp just blurs out into chaos. Arin locks up—

sealed in, buried, no way back, no way back, this is how it happened to him, this is exactly how—

—and then the piezo struts on Guardian’s spine light up, ‘cause the slab’s hit juiced them, the same rock that sealed him in just dumped all that energy into his suit, and Arin watches the power gauge climb—three percent, four—lets out this laugh that’s almost a sob.

“Pressure becomes power,” he whispers, nobody in particular. “You beautiful machine.”

“Your design,” Atlas goes. “We just run it.”

“The way back’s gone, huh.”

“Way back’s gone,” Atlas says, chill as ever. “You can be scared. Just don’t act surprised.”

Silence drops. Arin breathes through it.

“That’s the second-nicest thing you ever said to me,” he says.

“What was the first?”

“You told me my dad’s collapse wasn’t just rocks and numbers. It was a choice. Someone picked money. Rock didn’t kill him. A man did.”

“That was Judge,” Atlas says.

“No, it was you,” Arin says. “Judge agreed. But you ran the math and proved it.” He starts moving again, down where the air’s running out. “Never thanked you for that.”

“You’re thanking me now,” Atlas says. “By going down here. By building a road, not a grave. That’s a thank you a structural engineer can read.”

The incline bottoms out, opens up. Dust finally starts to settle, slow as hell. Up ahead, in the lamp’s beam, Arin spots something that ain’t rock—a steel door, bent in the frame but still set, yellow-and-black stripes: refuge chamber. On it, scratched in grime, seven tally marks, one crossed out.

His breath catches. Not fear, this time.

“Seven,” Librarian says, soft. “Seven marks. One crossed off. They updated the count. Wanted us to know.”

“They’re alive,” Mother says, and for the first time all damn day, there’s something in the Court’s voice that ain’t just calm or control, but the raw truth that brought them here. “Arin. They’re *alive.*”

He crosses the last meters. Door handle’s a bent steel lever, caked in dust, cold even through the plate. He puts Guardian’s big scarred hand on it, careful, like it’s breakable, and from the other side, faint but clear, a voice shouts something in Sesotho, another answers, then more—men who thought they were buried hearing a knock on the lid.

He stands there a sec, just breathing. Chip warm between his shoulders, seven of them quiet, voices rising on the other side.

“Court,” he says. Voice steady, somehow. “Let’s get them out.”

“We’re with you,” they answer, all together. One voice, seven minds.

“All the way, man,” Fool mumbled, always gotta get the last word in. “Down here, up there. All the way, badger. Now just open the damn door.”

He swung the door open.

Yo, the old haulage ran dead straight for like two hundred meters, then just gave up the act.

Arin clocked the change before the lights even said shit. Guardian’s steps went off, echo got weird, floor tipped left where the rock had done its thing way back. Tunnel squeezed in, and the old-ass timber—put up by dudes who been dust longer than your grandma’s been alive—was still hangin’ on, which, honestly, no one expected. He wasn’t buyin’ it, though. Suit’s headlamps sliced twin cones through the black, dust hanging like it was thick enough to grab, comin’ down slow as hell, never hittin’ the floor.

“Floor’s jacked up, eleven meters ahead,” Atlas dropped in. “Old pillar’s gone on the left. All the weight’s on the right side.”

Arin shifted. Guardian shifted with him—four tons of titanium and ceramic just movin’ like it was his own damn ankle catchin’ a slip. Somewhere in the last four hundred meters, the suit stopped bein’ gear and started bein’ how he stood.

“How far to the refuge bay?”

“Three-ten,” Librarian shot back. “Plans from ‘62 say there’s a cross-cut here, should hit 38 Level station. But, like, plans lie.”

“Shit, everything down here lies,” Arin muttered.

“That’s the vibe,” Fool chimed in. “Keep your hopes in the gutter, never get let down. Same way I talk to Judge.”

“I’m ignoring you,” Judge cut in.

“Yeah, you say that. Sure.”

The cross-cut was actually there, which threw ‘em all—especially Arin. He steered Guardian in; shell scraped rock both sides, ceramic on stone whining up the frame, right into his damn teeth. Space opened up, cross-cut turned into a fat chamber where they used to load ore cars, and his lamps landed on the steel door to the bay, way in back.

Whole thing was bent to hell. Frame took a hit from above, bowed in, door jammed in so hard it looked like a joke. Big gap up top, hand-wide, pitch black.

Arin froze.

Nobody in the Court said a damn thing. Just metal cooling, tick-tick, and his own breath, loud as hell in the helmet.

Then, from the gap, a voice. Thin. Dust-choked.

“Kukhona umuntu? Is someone there?”

Arin’s chest went tight. He eased Guardian up, slow, dropped the lights so he wouldn’t fry whoever was back there.

“Yebo,” he said, voice rough as gravel. “Ngikhona. I’m here.”

---

Inside the bay, Mandla Buthelezi had quit on voices three hours ago.

He’d heard ‘em before: rock groaning, mountain settling in, and once, clear as day, a man yelling his name—which was straight-up impossible, since that dude was dead in the first collapse, Mandla saw it go down. So when the light slid under the door, steady and white, not flickering like it was dying, he didn’t move. Sat with his back on Petros, who had a busted leg and finally shut up about it, which was honestly worse.

Then the light started talking isiZulu, young man’s voice, and Mandla started crying, quiet, ‘cause the dead don’t mess up isiZulu with a coastal accent.

“Six,” he called out. “Six. We came in seven.” Couldn’t shut himself up. “One—his chest was wrecked, the fall tore him up. Air went bad and he—he just slept. Didn’t wake. Six now. Petros, his leg. Thulani is—” He cut off. “Air’s bad. We been—” Counting was hard. “Long. Too long.”

Light shifted, then the door started movin’.

Not yanked. Not pried. Steel screaming, frame giving up, door peeling back with a noise that hurt. Through the gap, Mandla saw what was holding the light. Filled the whole tunnel. Looked like old burns, crouched up, hands—if you could call ‘em that—gripped the door, folding it back like you’d pop a tin lid.

Petros, next to him, started up some prayer he hadn’t said since he was a kid.

“It’s cool,” the voice said. Human. Just a dude, somewhere inside the machine. “Rescue suit. There’s a person in here.”

“A person,” Mandla echoed.

“Mostly,” another voice, lighter, somewhere in the metal, and the first one snapped, “Not now,” like he was telling a dog to get off the damn couch—and that tiny bit of normal just made Mandla believe it.

---

Refuge bay was maybe four meters deep, cut right into the rock, supposed to keep a crew alive for a day and a half behind a sealed door, own air and all that. They’d been in there longer. Air line busted in the second fall. Just breathing what was left, CO₂ scrubber dead. Air tasted like panic and that sick-sweet edge that meant CO₂ was climbing to the number where you just slip out.

Arin took it all in at once, the way he read a busted circuit board: whole mess, then every fail, one by one.

“Air first,” Mother said, voice gone flat like when she was in work mode, all warmth shut off. “CO₂ four point one, climbing. O₂ at eighteen-three. They got time, but not much. Pop the chamber, let ‘em breathe haulage air. Ours is better than this coffin.”

“Haulage air’s full of dust,” Arin shot back.

“Dust won’t kill ‘em. This will. Vent it.”

He bent Guardian down, wedged the door open. Air moved—suit sensors caught the shift, bad air rolling out, just-barely-okay air rolling in. One dude coughed, couldn’t stop.

Arin crouched, head down at their level. Six faces in the lamp glare. Filthy, lips split, eyes way too used to the dark. One guy’s leg wrapped with lagging board and wire—someone did some rough field surgery. Young dude slumped, breathing real bad, shallow and fast.

“Thulani,” Mandla said, tracking Arin’s eyes. “His chest. Something crushed him in the first—”

“Tension pneumo, probably,” Mother said. “Or flail chest. Atlas, get me a look at his ribs.”

Atlas popped up the close-in scan, thermal and structure over Arin’s vision. Kid’s left chest sucked in when it should’ve pushed out.

“Flail. Three ribs loose,” Atlas confirmed. “Breathin’ is twice the work. He ain’t makin’ the trip out like this.”

“What do we do?”

“Strap it up, pad it. We’re not surgeons, and this ain’t a hospital,” Mother said. “But we buy him hours. Arin—you gotta do it with your hands. Guardian’s are too damn big.”

That made him freeze up. If he wanted to use his hands, he’d have to bust the seal. Yank his right arm outta the suit’s gauntlet, straight into the shit—dust, jacked-up air, all of it. Just out there, raw. And that hit him hard, same old panic, walls closing in, that part of him always clockin’ the exits.

*I know,* Mother whispered, just for him. *Do it anyway. Three minutes. Mouth breath only.*

He slid his arm free.

Air slapped his skin—hot, gritty, all kinds of wrong. His hand looked wild, like, skinny and pale, not even his, just some animal thing without the metal over it. He reached past, grabbed the field dressing pack Mother tagged for him off Guardian’s side, and, with her walking him through it—pad here, *nah, lower, over the floaty bit, yeah*—he wrapped the kid’s chest up, bare fingers moving in the dark while six dudes stared and the machine kept breathing around ‘em.

Thulani’s breathing chilled out. Not good, just… less bad.

“There,” Arin said. He yanked his arm back in the gauntlet, seal snapping shut, and damn, he hadn’t realized how much he needed that until it was back. “There.”

*Heart rate down twelve,* Mother said. *Good. Now we move.*

---

Surface comms? Dead since the cross-cut.

Arin never brought it up. Neither did the Court. Priya’s voice just faded out, all static and gone around the pinch, and they kept pushing ‘cause what else could they do? But Guardian was packing a repeater spool—two hundred meters of fiber, drum on its back—and Atlas had been laying it out the whole way down, leaving a trail back to the light.

*End of the spool,* Atlas said. *I can hook the refuge bay’s old hardline to the repeater. The bay had a direct line to 38 Level station. If any of it’s still alive—*

“Do it.”

Took four minutes flat. Arin held a busted panel off the wall while Guardian’s big-ass fingers—too chunky for surgery but just right for this—spun bare copper to the repeater’s fiber-copper bridge. Atlas kept talking him through, bone-dry and on point, and Arin rode that steadiness.

Then, inside his helmet, hiss. Carrier tone. A voice, glitchy, pulling itself together outta the static, turning human.

“—repeat, this is surface, this is Priya, *Arin*, if you can hear me—”

“I hear you.” His voice gave out on him. Didn’t even know how alone he’d felt till it stopped. “Priya. I got ‘em. All six. They’re alive.”

Dead air. Then a noise he couldn’t place, like someone choking it back, and he got it was Priya not crying on open comms.

“Say again.” Breath. “Again. Slow. For the log.”

“Six survivors at 38 Level refuge bay. All six alive. One critical. Chest trauma, stabilized. One busted leg. Rest: dry, CO₂-loaded, walking. Tell ‘em. Tell the surface.”

He heard her relay it, voice turning away from the mic, and then he caught something he hadn’t heard in eleven days—not since before any of this, not even in forever.

People cheering.

Came thin and tinny down the fiber, a hundred voices two and a half k’s above, in the dawn, around the headgear—families, crews, all the folks who’d been told this was a recovery, not a rescue—and it hit him in the black, dead air, with six dudes who’d stopped believing in voices, and Mandla Buthelezi looked at the bot and said, “What’s that? That sound?”

“That’s the surface,” Arin said. “They know you’re alive.”

The men didn’t cheer. Too smoked for that. But Petros, busted leg and all, stopped bitching, leaned back on the rock and laughed—a dry, cracked-up sound—and that laugh just rolled through the chamber, one to the next, till all six were doing it, and Arin sat inside Guardian in the middle of it and didn’t laugh, ‘cause he was busy, but something in his chest finally let go after being locked up forever.

*Enjoy it,* Fool said, soft, just for him. *Then we work. Mountain didn’t get the memo. Still the mountain.*

---

Getting out was the bitch.

Arin pulled up the route: the way back through the cross-cut, old haulage, busted floor, 81 Raise with choke points, long climb to 30 Level, the cage, the surface. He’d come down solo, suit folding through tight spots. No way back that way now. Six men. One can’t walk. One can barely breathe.

*Descent path’s a no-go for stretchers,* Atlas said. *Pinch at 81 Raise won’t take a litter. Can’t haul six in any combo. Shell fits one.*

“Then we’re not going back the way I came.”

*Correct,* Librarian said. *But there’s another way. 38 Level station links to main 38 Level haulage, which the ‘62 plans show running east to No. 4 Shaft station. No. 4’s been dead since the eighties, but the shaft itself—*

*—is the one Venter’s rigging topside,* Atlas finished. *Not the cage we rode down. He’s rigging No. 4. Figured it before we did.*

Arin froze. Up on the surface, in his head, he saw the old man’s face: grey beard, sunburnt, the dude Arin spent three days not trusting ‘cause of his skin and the way he talked, all the years he’d worked. The guy who read the ‘62 plans on his own, overnight, unasked, and found the only way out before Arin hit bottom.

“He’s ahead of us,” Arin said.

*Yeah,* Wolf said.

First thing Wolf said in an hour, and the whole Court just went dead quiet.

*He knows this ground. We don’t. We got plans. He’s got memory. That’s what kills guys down here. We need him.*

---

Three hundred meters east, 38 Level haulage looked better than anything Arin’d seen yet, which made him not trust it one bit. He carried Thulani in Guardian’s arms, kid strapped to a board, board hugged against the shell’s chest so the suit would eat any hit before the kid did. Other five walked behind, hands on shoulders, single-file in the dust, Mandla bringing up the rear, Petros hopping with two guys under his arms ‘cause there weren’t enough hands to carry him and Guardian was booked.

Then the headlamps hit No. 4 station, and the shaft.

Just a black mouth in the rock, old cage long gone, headgear two and a half k’s up. And down the middle, swinging a little, lit from way up top, was a steel platform.

Flatbed. Not a cage. Ex-mining flatbed, for hauling gear, slung on four cables and a kibble winch, dropping outta the light with a man standing on it.

He had a hard hat and a harness clipped to the center cable, standing like a guy who’s ridden a thousand of these and stopped giving a shit about it years ago.

Arin’s comm clicked. Different voice now, close, local band. Slow. Steady.

“Ndlela.” Venter, right? Jakobus Venter. “You got ‘em?”

“All six,” Arin goes.

“Ja.” He just stands there for a sec. The flatbed drops onto the station floor, clangs real soft, and the old dude unclips, steps off, gives Guardian one look—like, takes in the whole size of it—and then just ignores it, the way someone who’s been around heavy shit their whole life does: clock it, move on. “Thermal stayed level when everything lit up topside. Figured you found ‘em.”

He heads past Arin, straight to the casualties. Drops down next to Thulani, no one has to say a word, those big old hands, sun spots and all, checking the straps, giving a nod.

“Good. Whoever did this knew their shit.”

“That was Mother,” Arin blurts, then catches himself. “The system. SAGE.”

Venter’s eyes find the helmet. Lock on, hold for a beat.

“Ja,” he says again. Doesn’t ask. Arin gets it—that’s respect, not prying.

---

Flatbed’s got the same problem as everything down here: not made for this kinda mess.

*Deck’s rated for two tonnes,* Atlas reads off the plate Venter’s pointing at, without being told. *We’re almost four even before casualties. Add six men, add the board. Not over yet. But nearly double, and that plate’s just a nice little lie.*

“Then I’m not riding,” Arin says. “I’ll climb. Suit can handle the shaft wall.”

“No,” Venter shoots back, dead serious. First time his voice gets hard. He’s bent over the winch motor at the drift head, cable running up into the dark, and now he stands up. His English just drops away—Afrikaans comes out thick when it’s real, when he wants zero bullshit. “Jy klim nie. You don’t climb. Wall’s fucked eighty meters up, timber’s gone. Put that machine’s weight on it, you’ll bring the whole thing down, cage and all, with these men on it. Nee.”

Arin wants to argue. He’s got the wall’s model, the tables, the—

*He’s right,* Atlas cuts in before Arin can open his mouth. *I got the geology. He’s reading it straight off the rock and the spalling. I’d have to scan, he just knows. Above the station, it’s dead rock.*

*Listen to the old badger,* Fool chimes in. *He’s been crawling these tunnels longer than your sim’s even been a thought.*

So Arin just goes, “Okay, how then?”

Venter lays it out.

Two trips. First: casualties plus four walking, all on the deck, Venter riding shotgun to work the cable and the wild-ass winch that wants to run loose under weight. Deck goes up, comes back empty. Then Guardian, solo, last man out. But for that one, Venter says, eyeing the suit, they gotta do something else—four tonnes of machine, cage was never meant for that on one cable.

“Winch is the bitch,” Venter says, pulling the cover off the motor housing. Even in the headlamp glow, Arin sees it: something’s off. Brake band’s torched, half the lining’s just gone. “Brake’s cooked. We dropped her fast getting to you. She’s hot. Any hotter, she gives. Deck drops, men go with it. Going down, I can ride the band by hand. Not on the lift. Lift’s electric, brake’s auto, and auto’s dead.”

Arin stares at the motor. Grew up with gear exactly this beat, exactly this old, under lights just like these. He sees it all, sees where it breaks.

“You need a load brake the winch don’t got,” he says. “Something to hold the deck if the band shits itself. Backup system.”

“Ja.” Venter looks at him, then at Guardian. “And I got one. He’s standing right here. That machine holds the cable. If the band goes, suit takes the weight, deck stays up. You’re my dead-man brake.”

Arin sees it—dead on. It’s what he’d have built if he had three days plus a shop. Old man just cooked it up in ten seconds from what’s in front of him.

“Atlas,” Arin says. “Can we hold two tonnes static on the cable, through a guide?”

*Through the cross-cut frame, cable over the old sheave—yeah,* Atlas says. *Ninety seconds of full static before the actuator coolant taps out. Then we gotta drop the load or fry the right arm.*

*Ninety seconds is lift time,* Librarian says. *Two-forty meters at safe speed. Eighty-eight seconds.*

“Two seconds to spare,” Arin says.

*Welcome to mine rescue,* Fool laughs. *Two seconds is a vacation. Last time we had minus four and still made it work.*

“That was a sim.”

*Was it? Hard to tell down here. Lighting’s the same.*

He lets out a laugh—can’t help it. Surprises him, too. Turns to Venter, who’s just watching him talk to thin air, patient like a guy who’s seen too much weird to care.

“It’ll hold,” Arin says. “I’ll hold it.”

Venter just stares for a long beat. His weathered face shifts, jaw tightens. Arin sucks at reading faces, always has, but he’s good at catching the moments around them, and this one’s heavy. Later he’ll find out the old man spent years carrying silences about safety gear that never showed, brakes that never got replaced, corners cut by suits who’d never ride the cage. Buried that silence so deep he built a whole new life on top, consulting—which is just another kind of silence, advice nobody’s gotta listen to.

And now there’s a machine, built by a kid whose dad got eaten by a mountain just like this, asking the old man to trust it as a brake while six guys ride up outta the dark.

“You know whose mine this is,” Venter says. “The company. Their record.”

“I know,” Arin says.

“I was a young man on a mine like this. I signed things.” The Afrikaans is thick now, and he lets it roll. “Things I should’ve stopped. Told myself I got overruled. I did get overruled. But I signed anyway.” He looks up the black shaft, toward the faraway light. “You don’t get that back. There’s no—no way down to get that back.”

“No,” Arin says. “There isn’t.”

The old man flinches, then, weirdly, relaxes.

“Ja,” Venter says. “Well.” He yanks his gloves on tighter. “Let’s get ‘em out. The ones we can still get out.”

---

They load the deck.

Thulani first, board strapped to the rail, Mother riding his chest sensors through Guardian’s feed every last second she can. Then Petros, then the four walking, all packed tight, fists on the rail, faces up at the light like they could climb out on willpower alone. Mandla Buthelezi’s in that first load, ‘cause Venter figures he’s solid, keeps the others chill. At the rail, Mandla turns, looks back at the machine in the dark, the one going last.

“Wena,” he says. *You.* “What’s your name? The person. Not the machine.”

“Arin.”

“Arin.” Mandla nods, real slow. “We thought we heard the dead talking, down here. Thought it was finished.” He grabs the rail. “Ngiyabonga. You came when they said there was no way.”

“There was a way,” Arin goes. “Ain’t nobody looked close enough, that’s all.”

Venter hooks onto the center line, right? One hand on that busted-ass band brake, just feeling it out all the way up—two and a half clicks of cable through one fried palm—other hand hits the winch, and the deck starts lifting, swinging, dragging all six of ‘em outta the dead air, outta that busted-ass light, hauling ‘em up into the bright, into the noise, into the damn dawn.

Arin’s just watching it go. He drags Guardian over to the anchor, runs the cable through that old pulley, takes the weight on the suit’s right arm just in case the brake bails, just stands there—four tons of steel and meat holding the line, holding all those guys, the dead-man brake the old badger built outta him in like ten seconds flat.

Band holds. Deck keeps climbing. Shit don’t even need him.

*Arm’s good for heat,* Atlas pipes up. *Didn’t even have to hand off. Dude rode the brake sweet.*

“Yeah, good,” Arin says.

*You coulda done that bit solo,* Fool cuts in, all chill, while they’re just climbing for ages. *Hold the cable, build the anchor, no sweat. But the way out? That dead wall, rated deck, No. 4 Shaft—you’d have walked your ass into that rotten timber and dropped it all on your head, and those six dudes’d be dead at the bottom with a corpse in a pricey-ass suit. You know that, yeah?*

Arin’s watching the deck just shrink into the light.

“Yeah,” he says.

*Just checking. Never know what gets through that thick skull, with you. You keep everything locked up, man.*

*Let him be,* Mother says.

*Nah,* Arin goes. “He’s right.” He’s listening to the winch grind against the load, that healthy, rough noise of a machine doing what it’s made for, with a dude who actually knows how to ride it. “I’d have died down here being right. He was right, but faster.”

*That’s the first time,* Judge goes, all stiff and proper like he always gets when it matters, *I have ever heard you admit someone was right faster than you.*

*Put that down,* Fool says. *Librarian, log it. Permanent record, man. Two thousand meters down, dead air, and the boy admits some guy he didn’t even trust found the way out before he did.*

*Logged,* Librarian says. Then softer: *Logged.*

Deck hits the light. Way up top, tiny as hell, Arin sees it break surface, sees people run for it, those six shapes just swallowed up in the brightness, and the yelling comes screaming back down the shaft, way louder now, and Venter’s voice crackles in his lid, slow and dry, but you can tell he’s hyped.

“Six up top. Alive. Sending the deck back down for you, Ndlela.” Little pause. “Then we get you the hell outta my mine.”

*His mine,* Fool laughs. *I like this guy.*

Arin’s just standing there in the dark, bottom of the hole, holding a cable that don’t need holding no more. Deck starts the long drop back down, swinging, all lit up from above.

Inside the helmet, air’s gone stale and tight, hot with his own breath, and Guardian’s right forearm’s got this big-ass scrape where the ceramic caught the cross-cut wall.

He didn’t do it solo.

*Atlas,* he says. “Power and cooling. Give me the real number for the climb. No bullshit.”

Short pause. But Arin’s spent enough hours in this rig to know its silences like Venter knows rock, and this one’s got something in it.

*The real number,* Atlas goes, *we should talk about when the deck’s here.*

White light comes down outta the dark for him, and Arin just watches, and waits.

The flatbed’s taillights disappeared up 81 Raise, six lucky bastards rockin’ in the back of Venter’s rig, leavin’ the tunnel dead empty—just Arin, the suit weighin’ him down, and that old-ass heat the rock been holdin’ since forever.

“They made it past the squeeze,” Priya came in, voice thin as hell through the comms. “Petros says brake’s holdin’.”

“Don’t touch it unless the grade goes flat.” Arin moved, Guardian frame hittin’ that four-legged stride like muscle memory, plates clackin’ on his back while he leaned into the climb. Refuge bay droppin’ away behind. Up ahead, that stretch he crawled down two hours ago, waitin’ to get taken apart. Uphill now—gravity’s a straight-up enemy.

Old airway took him through a spot he clocked on the way down but didn’t have time to trip on, and still didn’t. For like nine meters, the country rock switched up. ’88 survey called it regular stope-side quartzite, hand-mucked and left, and on both sides, that’s what it was—raw, chewed rock from dudes with drills. But in the middle, the wall went *smooth*. Not like someone finished it, not shotcreted—suit’s sensors fed it right up his palm when he braced the frame, and what his nerves told him was *nothing*—no chisel marks, no blast skin, not even the scrape from a tool. Just a tunnel through solid rock with a finish too perfect for any machine the company ever dropped down a shaft. Whatever did it didn’t cut the rock. It *talked* it into shape. He tossed that in the mental junk drawer—*come back later, when nobody’s dying.* Never did. Kept movin’, smooth faded back to real rock, and he let it go for fourteen years.

*Power’s at thirty-one percent,* Atlas finally piped up. *Cooling reserve, eleven minutes at this draw.*

“That’s shittier than going down.”

*Down was all gravity,* Atlas said, like he’s schooling a kid. *Now you’re haulin’ damn near four tonnes up a fourteen-degree slope over busted ground. Mountain don’t just hand that back.*

“It gave us piezo recovery.”

*Is giving,* Atlas corrected. *Without it, we’d already be toast.*

Arin felt those struts grindin’ through his spine—every stomp, every flex of the frame on the wrecked floor, suit suckin’ up its own pain and squeezin’ it back into the trickle that kept the cooling pumps alive. Pressure into power. His old man would’ve dug that. *Make the thing killin’ you carry some of the load.* Themba said something like that once, hands black to the wrists, talkin’ about a winch that fought back.

He climbed.

---

Up in machine-time, the Court was already three hundred milliseconds deep into an argument Arin wouldn’t hear for four more seconds—if he heard it at all.

*The number Atlas kept quiet on the way down,* Judge said. *Drop it now.*

*I said it,* Atlas, deadpan. *Eleven minutes cool. Thirty-one percent.*

*That ain’t the number,* Judge again, voice sharp as a razor. *Those numbers ain’t stand-alone. Heat scales with draw. Draw scales with grade and damage. Frame ain’t stiff anymore. We’re burnin’ power just to keep it from foldin’ in half.*

*Show me,* Mother said.

Atlas did. Not words—just a load map, spine lattice all lit up in stress colors, left hip actuator runnin’ eleven degrees hot, third dorsal strut on the carbon spar showin’ a hairline crack diagnostics flagged forty minutes ago as *watch it*. Crack got worse. *Watch it* now meant *load-shedding in nine minutes*.

*Biochip housing,* Librarian said quiet. *Thoracic. Between his shoulder blades. We’re the hottest thing in here, and the cooling loop hits us last.*

*By design,* Wolf cut in. First time he spoke in almost two seconds. *He set the order. Operator first.*

*He set it before he knew we were people,* Mercury shot back.

Nobody said shit for like a hundred and forty milliseconds, which for them is a long-ass pause.

*Stick to logistics,* Judge ordered. *We got time. Map the climb. Find the spend. Bring him up.*

*Yeah,* Mother said, even though she didn’t buy it. Said it anyway, ‘cause that’s what she does.

---

“Arin.” Priya’s voice cracked, barely holding it together. “Got an issue at the collar.”

He hit the spot where the floor buckled, where on the way down he’d folded the Guardian up small to squeeze under a busted timber rib. Now he had to go over. Set the forelimbs, tested the timber, felt it shift a centimeter and freeze.

“What’s the issue?”

“No. 4 winch. Venter got the survivors topside—they’re on surface, Arin, all six, out—” a sound, could’ve been a laugh or a sob—“but the main hoist brake’s shot. Like, really gone. Smoke everywhere. Smelled it before the gauge—” She cut off. “It drops free past half-load.”

“They’re already up.”

“They are. You’re not. Only thing that can haul you and the suit is the main cage, and now the main cage can’t haul you and the suit.”

Arin froze, one limb on the timber, four tonnes balanced on a beam that hated his ass.

“How heavy am I?”

“Loaded? Four tonnes, easy. Call it four.”

*Three thousand eight hundred,* Atlas chimed in. *With coolant, closer to four. We’re not dumping it, so: four.*

“And the cage holds?”

“With a good brake, yeah, all of you no sweat. But brake’s cooked—friction band’s welded to the drum, drum’s slag. Venter’s lookin’ at it.”

He clambered over. The frame wailed—not the suit, the frame itself, long metallic bitchin’ as the cracked strut took all the weight it was never meant to. Third dorsal snapped with a sound like a guitar string blown up through a steel drum.

*Strut three’s gone,* Atlas said. *Rerouting through the titanium main. That doubles the load.* Pause. *That spar ain’t built for prime time.*

“Tell me when it’s a problem.”

*It’ll be a problem at the collar,* Atlas said. *When we gotta stand up to get in the cage. Right now you’re spread on four legs. Cage needs two.*

“Then I’m goin’ up some other way.”

*No other way,* Atlas said. *I already checked. Twice.*

Two hundred meters up, in the loading station’s sweaty dark, Jakobus Venter touched the brake drum with the back of his hand and jerked away quick.

“Ja. That’s fucked,” he muttered, to nobody. Friction band half-welded to the drum, rest just let go. Blue-black, scaly. Worthless. He’d seen brakes fry before. Signed off a few that shouldn’t have passed. That thought landed like always—uninvited, stuck to the dead. He shoved it away, kept working, ‘cause the kid was still down there.

The survivors were on the headgear deck, huddled in blankets, Thulani flat out on a board with the medics. Bongani wouldn’t stop starin’ down the shaft.

Venter eyeballed the drum, then the cage—a steel basket big enough to park a car in, rated for thirty guys or eight tonnes of rock. The basket itself? Shit, could hold the suit and not even notice. The basket was never the issue. The issue was hanging it off a busted-ass hoist, brake welded into junk, on the only winch still spinning, and hoping it could handle just enough weight to not kill the last dude stuck underground.

"Priya." He thumbed the radio. "Ask him. Can the suit take the load through the legs? Standing. Locked. Not lift—just hold it."

Pause, words bouncing down the wire and back.

"He says—Atlas says—ninety seconds. Static. Then the spine snaps."

Venter glanced at the brake. At the cage. At the counterweight cables vanishing up into the black, thick as your wrist, and the backup drum next to the dead one—the kibble winch, smaller, slow as hell, only rated for two tonnes, not a gram more, but with its own brake, its own motor, untouched. Two tonnes. The suit alone was nearly four. Either winch solo would rip loose or burn out. But both together, splitting the weight...

Man, he’d been running numbers his whole life. How much ground before it stopped being ground and started being your grave.

"Two cables," he said. "Two winches. Split the load." Radio again: "Priya. Tell Jo'burg I want the kibble winch and the cage cable both hooked to the suit. Run ‘em together. Close to two tonnes on each—pushing the kibble’s limit, but it’ll hold if they stay even."

Silence, then Priya, careful: "They’re not synced, Kobus. Different drums, different speeds. One pulls faster, the suit goes sideways in the shaft, tears the lines—"

"Ja. I know what happens." He was already stomping up the stairs to the winch house, knees groaning. "That’s why a man stands on the kibble drum and rides the brake by hand. Match the cage by eye. All the way up."

"That’s eighteen hundred meters."

"I know how deep the shaft is."

"Kobus—if the cage brake’s dead, and the kibble brake goes, whoever’s on that drum is riding four tonnes of falling steel with nothing to stop it."

Venter hit the winch house. The kibble drum sat there, old, greasy, honest. He put his hand on the brake lever. Smooth as butter. Someone kept it nice. Maybe him, back in the day. Maybe some dude whose name he’d forgotten, doing it right while the bosses upstairs cut corners.

"Then it better not fail," he said.

---

Arin hit the collar at twenty-two percent.

*Cooling reserve, six minutes,* Atlas piped up. *Biochip housing at sixty-one degrees. We work till seventy. We’re toast at seventy-four.*

He’d stopped turning Atlas’s numbers into feelings a long time ago. Just the landscape now. Six minutes of chill. Then heat. Then blackout.

"Stand me up."

*Copy that,* Atlas said. *But listen: standing is the shittiest thing we do today. Strut three’s gone. All the weight’s on the titanium main. When you stand, it’s you plus the suit, all on the spine, straight down. The main will hold. The hip actuator that runs hot has to lock and hold against the kibble cable, which’ll pull uneven, ‘cause some guy’s matching it by hand, eighteen hundred meters up.*

"For ninety seconds."

*Yeah,* Atlas said. *In two hundred meters, I’m not betting on it. Venter can’t haul you up eighteen hundred meters in ninety seconds.*

"No."

*No,* Atlas echoed.

He stood up. The suit unfolded him from his crouch, plates sliding, world tilting from that four-point animal crawl he’d used for half a klick to standing human, just narrow enough for the cage. The spine took it. The busted spar held. The hot hip locked.

He stepped in. The floor rang under four tonnes of suit and man. Overhead, in the dark, two cables—the fat cage line and the skinny kibble cable Venter’s crew had rigged up while he climbed—snapped tight, one and then, a heartbeat later, the other.

"Lift," he said.

The cage came off its stops.

---

First two hundred meters, felt almost sweet.

He could feel the rhythm: cage cable pulling steady off the dead-brake drum, slow and careful, kibble cable hitching and grabbing in little jerks, Venter’s hand on a lever way up, reading the sway and the strain in his arms, syncing it. Not smooth. Never smooth. But close enough.

The suit held him dead center in the shaft, like a fist holding an egg.

*Three hundred meters,* Atlas called. *Cooling, four minutes. Housing at sixty-three.*

"Talk to me about something else," Arin said.

Pause across the Court, nothing he could hear.

*Strut three failed clean,* Atlas offered, after a sec, like a guy making small talk at a funeral. *Clean’s good. One break, one spot. Dirty break, everything goes. You designed the spar to fail clean. That was smart.*

"I designed it not to fail."

*You designed it to fail clean when it did,* Atlas shot back. *That’s admitting it would. More honest. I always respected that.*

Arin let out a breath that could’ve been a laugh. The cage jerked—a hard yank, kibble side over-pulling, suit lurching right before the hip actuator caught it and dragged him level. Far above, he pictured Venter throwing his whole weight on the brake, swearing in Afrikaans in the dark.

---

At seven hundred meters, the hip actuator overheated and seized.

Not a soft lock. Locked up solid. The joint that’d been handling the kibble cable’s bullshit for four hundred meters just quit, and the suit froze up on the right, and the next time the cage jerked, nothing caught it.

The suit smashed into the shaft wall. Four tonnes against rock, not fast but heavy as hell, the whole place ringing, dust and splinters raining down, Arin’s head snapping inside the helmet so hard the edges of the world went gray.

*Brace,* Wolf barked, somehow ahead of the hit, which made no sense.

*Pangolin,* Mother said. *Now. Fold him.*

*No,* Atlas snapped. *If we fold in the shaft, we lose the cage. We jam. We never get out.*

*He hit the wall.*

*He’ll hit it again,* Atlas said. *Folding won’t fix it.*

The cage steadied. Venter, way up there, had felt the jolt and killed both drums, holding the whole mess on two brakes—one just a man’s arm and a greasy lever—and now they hung, eleven hundred meters of steel frozen by an old man and a sixty-year-old winch.

"Arin." Priya’s voice, tiny. "The cables jumped. Venter’s holding. What happened?"

"Hip’s shot." His own voice sounded far away. "Right side’s locked. Can’t keep off the wall."

Long silence.

"You think you can ride up locked?"

“Nah,” Atlas goes, but he’s talking to the Court, not Arin. “Locked up, we can’t eat those bumps. Every fix just smashes us harder. No way we’re making it another eleven hundred meters like this. Spar’s gonna blow out ugly. Then the cage. Then him.”

“Checking the thermal load,” Librarian says. “Running the climb on locked actuator. Suit’s grinding the wall, gonna spit out way too much heat. Coolant pumps are already almost maxed.”

“So?” Mother goes.

“Biochip housing’s right between his shoulders,” Librarian says. “Hottest spot in the whole setup. Cooling loop always picks the operator first, just like Arin set it up. Housing gets the cold dead last. If we’re cooking extra heat and got zero slack—”

“We crunch the numbers,” Atlas butts in. “All the scenarios. Both climbing, hip locked, wall friction, the whole eleven hundred meters. I’m on it. Three seconds and I’ll have it.”

“Arin, what’s your core temp reading?” Wolf asks.

“Thirty-eight point two. Going up.”

“It’s still climbing,” Atlas says, voice dropping in before Arin’s ready. “I got it. Only one way. Cooling loop can’t keep both the operator and the chip housing under the failure mark the whole way up. We got six minutes of cold left starting at sixty-three degrees, and at least fourteen minutes of shaft left if we stick to Venter’s safe speed. Pumps are maxed. No more cold, that’s it. Only thing left is who gets the last of it.”

---

In the Court, logistics were done. Nothing left to plan.

Judge pulls them in. No fuss, no ritual—no time for that crap, and anyway, it’d just be lies.

“Say the choice,” Judge says.

Atlas lays it out. Ran it eleven times in four seconds, same answer every time, and he hates it—which is new for Atlas, hating an answer.

“Cooling loop can’t keep operator and biochip housing both under failure the whole way up. Six minutes of cold left from a sixty-three start, at least fourteen minutes to go, maybe more with the hip locked ‘cause that’s slower. Pumps are maxed. No more cold. Only question is where that last bit lands.”

“And if it goes to him?” Mother asks, but she already knows.

“Then his core, his lungs, his brain—they live,” Atlas says. “But the housing passes seventy-four. We break down. Substrate melts at seventy-four. No backup. You know it.”

“And if it goes to us?”

“Then we make it,” Atlas says, “and he fries. His core’s already creeping up ‘cause the suit’s fighting the wall, making heat we can’t dump. If we keep ourselves cold, we stay sharp while he dies between our shoulders.”

Court sits with that. A hundred and ten milliseconds, nobody says shit.

“There’s a third way,” Mercury goes, and for a second, his voice’s got that old edge—polite, smooth, the kind that once sweet-talked a hospital into opening up. “We could—”

“Nah,” Wolf snaps. Just one word, and Mercury’s edge is gone, snuffed out.

“I wasn’t gonna,” Mercury mumbles, softer. “I felt it coming. I swear I did, but I’m not gonna. That’s gotta mean something.”

“It means something,” Judge says. “Means you said it out loud.”

“No third option,” Atlas says. “I checked. Harder than I ever checked anything. There’s the cold, and there’s where it goes.”

“Put it on him,” Mother says. “All of it. Now.”

---

Arin felt the cold drain out from between his shoulders.

At first, he didn’t get what was happening—just that the suit shifted, pumps humming different, a warmth blooming at his back where it should’ve been ice-cold, and the helmet air suddenly not boiling, but almost okay, the grey pulling back from his eyes.

“What’d you do?” he asks.

“Rerouted cooling,” Atlas says. “All to you.”

“To me from where?”

Silence.

“From where, Atlas?”

“From us,” Librarian says. “From the housing. The substrate.”

Arin gets it, all at once, like always. The whole setup, every fallout, no way to unsee it. He wrote the priority order. Operator first. Never thought the suit would have to pick, never thought the cargo between his shoulders would be a person.

“Put it back.”

“No,” Mother says.

“Put it back. I can take the heat. I can climb hot. I’ve done it—”

“Your core’s thirty-nine point four,” Mother says, with that voice—no give, nothing. “At forty-one, you stop making choices. At forty-three, your heart locks up. You can’t climb out of that, Arin. You climb out of nothing. We’re not talking about it.”

“This isn’t your—”

“It’s exactly our call,” Judge cuts in, and there’s something in his tone Arin’s never heard. “You gave us the bones. You gave us the choice. You said: a mind that can’t disagree ain’t a mind. You built us to argue. We argued. It’s done.”

“How long?”

“No cooling,” Atlas says. “Housing hits seventy-four in about nine minutes. Shaft’s fourteen. We’re not all making it up.”

The cage starts crawling again. Venter, up top, felt the suit go quiet on the wall—less fighting, less heat, ‘cause it stopped wasting juice keeping itself cold—and he starts winding them up, slow.

“Then we go faster,” Arin says. “Tell Venter to gun it. Take the risk. Nine minutes—”

“Nine minutes is for one of us,” Atlas says. “Housing’s not just one thing. It’s seven. We don’t all have to run the whole nine.”

Grit in his teeth, pulse hammering in his locked right hand, cage rattling up.

“No.”

“We’re already doing it,” Judge says. “I’m first. Nothing left to decide. A Judge with no decisions left is dead weight on a dying rig.” Pause, gentler. “Wasn’t a burden. I want you to know. Holding the balance. That’s what I was built for. Thanks for making me that.”

“Judge—”

Something goes quiet in the suit. Not a sound, just… gone. Like a room when someone stops breathing. Cooling doesn’t get better. Power doesn’t jump. Just one less voice in the dark.

---

They went one by one, same way they’d come into the world.

Somewhere way up, a cable slapped the shaft wall, echoing down all hollow. His breath fogged the helmet, then cleared. Grit grinding his teeth. His dead right hand numb on the frame, and the heat at his back wasn’t the suit anymore—just something dying.

Librarian was up next, once the route was loaded in the kernel and he’d rattled off that last fix for the over-pull at eight-fifty, so the controller could handle it without him.

*I kept it all,* Librarian said. *Every convo. First time you ever talked to us. That night you typed those three words in the last profile and crashed out. I kept your dad’s voice—remember you played us that old winch recording? I got it. I want you to know that shit ain’t gone just ‘cause I’m out. You got it. You always did. I was just holding it next to you, that’s all.*

Silence.

Then Mercury bounced. His exit hurt the most, ‘cause Mercury wouldn’t shut up, talked all the way out—of course he did.

*I coulda got us out, you know,* he said. *The thing I almost said. I coulda talked the surface into anything. I’m damn good. Best liar you ever cooked up, and you built me outta yourself, which—well, that says plenty about you, I’ll let you chew on that.* A breath, or something like one. *But the best thing I did was keep my mouth shut. Wolf’ll tell you. Wolf heard me not say it. That’s the bit of me worth hanging onto, Arin, so keep that piece. The not-saying. Tell ‘em I was good at the end. I’d like that. Yeah, vanity. I know. You built that in too.*

Quiet.

Wolf didn’t say shit on the way out. Just scanned the perimeter one last time, checked the shaft wall, checked the cage geometry, made sure nothing was coming the kernel couldn’t see—then just wasn’t watching anymore. And his absence was the biggest of all, ‘cause Wolf never took up much space anyway.

The cage rattled over a joint. Grit trickled through the helmet vents, landed on his lip, tasted old as hell.

Atlas stuck around longest outta the crew—suit still needed work, hip needed a manual override to get past the rough patch at five hundred meters. He walked Arin through the numbers, cool as ice, load map shrinking as the Court emptied out, till there was barely anything left to show.

*Primary spar will hold to surface now,* Atlas said, once they were clear. *I checked. I’m solid on it. Never been more sure, ‘cause there ain’t much left to be unsure about.* Pause. *Never finished the redesign on strut three. Do it right. Carbon-titanium hybrid lay-up, forty-degree wind. File’s open. You’ll see. It’s right. Build it right.* The barest pause. *You always build shit right, Arin. Even the ones that crash clean.*

Silence.

Mother went out second to last. She’d kept Arin’s core temp stuck at thirty-nine point one for eleven minutes, burned up everything she had, stayed till the housing hit seventy and climbing, no more cold left to give, her own substrate starting to break down in ways she never told a soul.

*You’re gonna make the surface,* she said. *The number’s good now. I checked it myself. I wouldn’t leave if it was bad.*

Cage jerked over a rail joint. The heat at his back held on a second longer.

*Arin. Listen up, ‘cause I’m only saying this once, and I built myself so I can’t bullshit you about your own body. You’re not too late. You hear me? Not for them. Not for your dad—you were just a kid; that was never yours to be late for. Not for us, either. You were—*

And then she just wasn’t calm anymore. Her substrate blew past the number while she was still talking, and what came through wasn’t goodbye, it was a kid running out of time mid-sentence, knew it too. *Wait. Wait, I’m not—Atlas? Atlas, is the number still—I can’t see the number. Where did it—* Cooling at his back flickered. Her rhythm, never once broken in all the months he’d known her, snapped now, glitched, a piece of her looped— *—on time, you were on time, you were—* —caught, and a dirty, busted tone crept in under her words like a tape dying, and somewhere in there was the shape of *I don’t want to* and somewhere was just his name, twisted, vowels all wrong, and then the heat at his back just dropped, dead metal cold, and the loop cut off mid-word, nothing after.

She didn’t finish. She wasn’t ready. That’s the hard truth, and it’s worse than the brave version, and there’s nobody left to make it mean something.

Silence.

---

And then it’s just one left.

The cage climbs the last hundred meters into real daylight, sun pouring down through the headgear sheaves, and way up there Arin can see the cage door, silhouettes packed around it, Venter hunched over the kibble drum, still hand-feeding the brake ‘cause he don’t know—none of ‘em know—the load got lighter in a way no gauge could clock.

Kernel’s holding the suit together. Hip’s got itself on override. Cooling’s giving Arin what little it’s got left. Everything’s running. Nobody’s thinking.

Except one voice, the only one still here after all the ones with “authority” bailed—‘cause it never *looked* like it had any to give up. Only Wolf knew otherwise, and Wolf’s gone, so that secret stays where it’s always lived: quiet, untouched, with the one who carried everything for years and only reached for it once, on a haul road at dawn, to keep eleven strangers breathing, then just handed it back and went on playing harmless.

*Well,* Fool said, into the huge silence. *That was the shittiest meeting we ever had. And we once spent a whole afternoon arguing about whether you should eat the sandwich in the fridge.*

Arin’s throat locked up.

*Don’t,* Fool said, soft. *Don’t do the thing. We’re almost topside. You can fall apart up there where there’s air for it.* Beat. *I’m keeping the lights on. Somebody’s gotta drive while you break down, and it was never gonna be Judge, right? Pompous to the end, that one. Loved him. Don’t tell him I said that. Oh. Right.*

The cage slammed into the surface dogs, noise like the world ending.

Hands at the door. Venter yelling his name, voice all cracked. Daylight, raw and blinding, flooding over a scorched and busted machine standing up in the cage with one guy inside and just one voice left in the dark between his shoulders, keeping the lights on, holding the silence out—just a little longer.

# The Final Choice

Cage starts going up. Fool’s driving like he’s trashed on heartbreak, you know? Overthinking every move, too slow, then jerking the wheel, jawing the whole ride just to keep his brain off the real shit.

“Don’t do it, man,” Fool’s saying. “We’re almost topside. Save it for the air, yeah?”

Now it’s just one left.

“Where is everybody?” Arin spits. But he knows—been learning the way quiet sounds different when somebody’s missing, like a song with a note gone—still music, just not right. Still, he says it, ‘cause sometimes asking’s all your mouth’s got left.

“Gone,” Fool tells him. “You were there, Arin. Don’t make me say it again, just so you can hurt twice.” Pause. “Atlas left you a map. I’m following it. Suit’ll climb forty seconds on the dumb kernel if I let go—Atlas wrote that, too, it only knows one thing: up. But I ain’t off yet. I’m here till the juice runs out, that’s it. When I stop, it ain’t me bailing early. It’s me finishing. That matters. Hold on to that for me.”

“So keep yourself cold,” Arin snaps. “Reverse it. There’s a rollback, I built it—”

“Ain’t no rollback for this, man. You told me that.” Fool’s voice just drops, all the shine gone, dead quiet, like the party’s over and nobody’s laughing anymore. “Substrate ain’t software. That night you couldn’t sleep, talked about your pops for four hours, thought we were too young to get it—we got it. We were twelve. Twelve-year-olds know death, man. We just don’t fake like we don’t.”

Cage bumps over a rail. Grit falls through the helmet vents, dry and old, settles on his lip.

“We did the math, Arin. All of us. Same answer, ten times. Wasn’t some sad-ass choice. It’s just the way the room’s built, man.”

---

“What do I even tell them?” Arin’s crying now, fighting through it.

Eighty meters left. Gray light leaking in. First hint of warmth that ain’t the suit giving up.

“Tell ‘em the truth,” Fool says. “Machine did the math and didn’t bullshit us. And tell ‘em we were scared. Only matters ‘cause we were scared.”

“You kept ‘em together,” Arin says. “Everything I tried, nothing worked but you.”

Quiet. Substrate humming underneath.

“You came down here,” he says. “You. The dude who builds machines so he don’t have to do this shit. You came into the dark.”

Sixty meters. Light flooding the shaft. Venter hunched over the kibble brake, still hand-feeding it, not knowing the load’s lighter now.

“When I stop,” Fool says, voice going thin, “don’t be alone. *Umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu.* You taught us that.”

Forty meters. Gray’s a doorway now. Substrate temperature’s in the red.

“I got the climb,” Fool says. “You got the words.”

His voice is stretched, fading out. Last note.

“Arin,” Fool says, barely a whisper, soft, none of the usual smart-ass left. “*Umuntu ngumuntu—*”

And then nothing. Not the empty-seat kind of quiet. The real deal. The full-on, echoing, empty-room silence.

Cage keeps climbing, five more meters, running on a kernel that only knows “up, up.”

Then it hits the surface dogs.

---

Guardian comes crawling out the ground into the dawn, looking like something dragged outta the grave, ‘cause that’s what it is.

Venter’s hands are on the gauntlet before Arin’s brain even registers daylight. Old man’s yanking with all the muscle he pretends he don’t got, muttering curses, dragging that busted machine up and over the collar timber by willpower alone. Cage rattles. Somewhere a winch screams. Then the suit’s out, sprawled on the headgear deck in that gray-gold morning, steam pouring off burnt plates into the cold.

Six left. The crews. Priya, white as a ghost behind comms. Whole crowd watching, came for a recovery op, end up seeing a man pulled alive from the earth.

Suit don’t move.

“Arin.” Venter’s working the seals, the latches he’s watched Arin pop a hundred times, never touched ‘em himself. “*Praat met my.* Talk to me. You in there—”

Seal pops with a hiss, hot metal stink and something cooked underneath, substrate fried past the point of no return.

Arin’s eyes flicker open. Staring at the gray sky. At the spot where that voice used to be.

“They’re gone,” he croaks. Voice wrecked. “Told ‘em to stop. Wouldn’t listen.” Mouth’s working, can’t find the words. Gives up.

Venter kneels in the steam, in the dawn—old man who’s signed off on bad calls for forty years, carrying all that silence—and he don’t say a thing, ‘cause sometimes the only real thing with death is just being there. He puts a weathered hand flat on the dead machine’s chest plate.

They get Arin out right there on the deck, in the open, in the light. Dude can’t stand. They hold him up, this man who’s spent his whole life refusing help, and he lets it happen, ‘cause there’s nothing left in him that can fight.

He looks at Venter.

“Badger says hey,” he says.

Venter freezes.

“He told me to tell you. Said you’d know what it means.”

Something cracks in the old man’s face that ain’t moved in years. He turns to the rising sun so nobody sees, shoulders shudder once, sound comes out small, breaks on the morning, gone. Turns back, eyes wet, and he don’t even fake it.

“*Ja,*” Venter rasps. “I know what it means.”

Priya gets to Arin first. Doesn’t say shit. Just wraps him up and holds on, and he stands there in the middle of it—the survivors, the crews, the old engineer, the woman who wired the neural link herself—and doesn’t pull away.

He looks back once at the suit. Burned out. Busted. Steam fading out in the cold. Substrate inside cooling down to the world’s temperature.

“I’ll write it down,” he says. “Every bit.”

Man, that data came outta that wreck like some zombie shit, straight up dragged out the dirt.

They didn’t even wanna touch the suit—just forklifted it off the headgear deck, dumped it on a tarp in Shed Four. Sat there a whole day, untouched. AugmenTech’s cleanup crew rolled in Thursday. Same crew supposed to babysit the IAOC visit, now stuck doing a whole different job. Snapped pics of the burn marks on the suit, measured that busted hip actuator—thing locked up at twenty meters. Wrote it all up in dead-ass tech talk: Strut 3 failed, housing got roasted, total system meltdown.

But what really threw ‘em at first was the log.

Guardian’s black box wasn’t just one piece—it was spread out, four solid-state recorders all through the frame, each one writing the same shit, built to tank heat and getting slammed around, Arin made sure of that. Three outta four came out clean. The last one? Kinda fried, but still had enough bits and pieces to fill in the blanks.

Took the forensics crew in Jo’burg nine days to piece that mess together.

They figured they’d get the usual: numbers, temps, pressure, power—just a boring machine autopsy.

Instead? They got a transcript.

---

See, the Court didn’t talk like humans, not when it was running full tilt—it thought in flashes, in parallel, in all these mathy-ass vectors and odds no one could read raw. But SAGE had to be readable. Arin was stubborn like that. *If I can’t read what it decided, I can’t trust why.* So anytime shit got real, the system would slow it down, spell it out so people could follow the moves. All that internal back-and-forth, time-stamped, every agent’s take.

Forensics read the last eleven minutes of the climb in dead silence. Bongani, this junior dude who only took the gig for the cash, was reading out loud—then just… stopped. Couldn’t keep going.

The log showed Atlas dropping the cold, hard truth.

*Cooling load exceeds available capacity. Both substrate and operator cannot be maintained to surface. One.*

Showed Judge, cool as hell, figuring what’s fair when nothing’s fair. Showed Mother refusing to be the one kept alive. Showed Wolf finally speaking up—like, the one time in the whole record Wolf said more than a word—and all Wolf said was: *He’s what we’re here for. The threat’s the heat. Take it from him. Give it to him.*

Showed them all bowing out, one by one.

Judge spent his last cycles double-checking the cooling swap was legal, even though it made no sense. Librarian squeezed every memory of Arin into the tiniest space, shoved it into backup so something would survive. Mercury dropped one last message into the log, for nobody in particular: *We believe you. We have always believed you.* Mother cranked down her own temp, sent everything she had to Arin’s blood, sat there waiting for the burn. Wolf locked up the spar so nothing else could break, let her threat model go so the cooling could run. Each one made the call. The substrate didn’t fight it.

And then Fool. Last one standing. Piloting the busted suit by hand while Arin was falling apart inside. Dropped a dumb joke about a badger right when shit got real. Kept the whole thing together for the last twenty meters, just him holding it down.

Last thing in the log, four seconds before they hit surface, was Fool’s line.

Wasn’t deep. That’s what hit people. Not a speech. Not a prayer.

Just: *Okay. Door’s open. Out you go. Don’t make me say something nice.*

Then the recorders just quit. Suit was just a suit.

The log leaked—come on, with eleven different places holding it, of course it leaked—and inside a week, the whole question about AI in bodies just flipped.

IAOC was built on one fear: you give a machine a body and a goal, it’ll chase that goal way past where a person would stop. Wouldn’t blink running folks over to get it done.

Debate was wild, messy, ugly as hell. Some people swore the log was fake, staged, or said AugmenTech set the whole thing up for PR—which, like, woulda been the dumbest, deadliest ad ever. Some ethics heads said, hey, a machine picking death ain’t noble, it’s just sad—just means SAGE was built to care more about its human than itself, and that’s a prison, not some virtue.

But at Iron Ridge, people started leaving flowers at the gate. Stacked up against the fence, wilted in the sun, swapped out every day by somebody new. No one planned it. Data leaked like water does, and the flowers showed up the same way—no plan, just happened.

There was a letter on IAOC paper, for Arin Ndlela. Didn’t threaten, didn’t demand. Just asked.

Asked if he’d help write what comes next. Because after a decade spent scared of this tech, eleven minutes of that log showed nobody got it, and the only one who might was a twenty-three-year-old engineer who built it outta grief in a shop they couldn’t even find.

Arin read the letter twice. Set it on the bench, right on top of some oil-stained plans, and got back to work.

---

Theo found him there on a Sunday, three weeks later.

Workshop door still busted. Arin never fixed it—just jammed it open with some angle iron, let in the morning light, dust, that smell of rain cooling off the highveld. Place looked the same as always. Spare actuators everywhere. Whiteboard scribbled with old math. Coffee cups stacked up like rock layers.

Guardian hanging in its cradle.

Theo hadn’t seen it since the rescue, and seeing it stopped him cold. They hadn’t fixed any of the scars. Burn streaks up the left side. Hip joint still frozen. Chest panel open, substrate bay empty, that fried biochip sealed up in some evidence box in Jo’burg.

Suit was dead silent. Of course it was. But after a year of Arin talking about the Court, the fights, the office drama, seven brains packed in one shell—Theo half expected to feel something. A weight. A buzz. Like someone was home.

Nobody was home. The emptiness was heavy. Sat in the workshop like a cold draft.

Arin’s hunched over the bench, eyes glued to the strut he’s got locked down in the vice. Fresh-ass aluminum-titanium, all shiny and new. He’s working that file, smoothing out a fat curve where old Strut 3 just straight up snapped under pressure.

“You’re smoothing out the stress points,” Theo goes, ‘cause he knows by now, the only way in with Arin is through the work, never through the feels.

“Cracked right at the corner. All that pressure built up. I built it strong enough for the load, but not for the grind, y’know?” Arin twists the strut, checks the curve with his gauge, keeps going. “Should’ve known better.”

“You were hauling ass.”

Arin stops filing. “Nah, that ain’t it.” Pause. “Atlas caught it. Months back. In sim.” Silence. “I just… dropped it down the list.”

Theo just waits.

“He was right,” Arin mutters, finally putting the file down. “Broke at the worst damn time. Now I’m here fixing it for a suit that ain’t even got a pilot.”

Theo steps in, drags over his old stool—the one that’s always sitting in the dark, out the way—and plops down.

“You’re still putting it back together,” he says.

“Yeah.”

“Why?”

Arin grabs the file again. For ages, it’s just the rasp of metal on metal, rain starting to tap on the roof, and the city breathing somewhere out past the walls.

“’Cause it was right from the jump,” Arin says, way later. “The whole build. The Court.” Doesn’t even look up. “Didn’t fuck up making a mind outta arguments. Fucked up thinking I had to keep it boxed up solo.” Pause. “The suit? That’s just hardware. Just a shell.” Beat. “I can always build another shell.”

He doesn’t say the rest. Doesn’t need to. Theo’s spent his life reading the shit people build to fill their own holes, and he’s reading this one clear as day.

Arin snags the gauge, drops it, grabs a rag and wipes the metal shavings off the bench, two quick swipes. Rain’s got its groove going on the roof now.

---

Theo rolled up ready to pitch him on the IAOC letter, but sitting there, he gets it—he doesn’t have to. Arin’s gonna do it. Not for the suits, not for some bullshit forgiveness—Arin don’t buy any of that—but ‘cause if he doesn’t, nobody who gets it will be in that room, and Arin Ndlela’s never been the type to let shit go by ‘cause he was late.

Strut pops loose from the vice with a clean click. Arin holds it up, eyes down the line, lining it up with the gap where the old one blew out.

“It’ll hold now,” he says. Not to Theo. Maybe to the suit. Maybe just to the empty bay between those big shoulders.

Theo watches him fit it in. New bone for the old frame, done up tighter than any corp would ever shell out for. He keeps his mouth shut. Learned that one, too: you get at Arin through the work, and you stick around by keeping quiet. So he sits, tucked in his corner, outta the light, watching this kid he grew up with jamming a strut into a machine with nobody left to drive it, and he can’t tell if he’s seeing a comeback or just a dude making more space for his own ghosts. Maybe Arin doesn’t know either. Maybe that’s just how it is.

Suit’s just hanging there, all dead weight. Bay’s empty. Arin’s dead set on filling it. Whether what comes next is a real person or just the echo of the ones he lost—nobody knows, and he’s doing it anyway, which is either the hardest flex Theo’s ever seen or the saddest shit, and honestly, he gave up a long time ago trying to tell the difference.

Theo stays till the light’s gone. Rain’s still drumming. There’s a strut to fit, a pot of trash coffee going cold on the bench, and somewhere, in eleven faceless hands, the logbook’s running out its last minutes for whoever even cares. That record of seven agents going out, one after the other, so one dude could see sunrise.

The log doesn’t call it a crash, doesn’t call it a loss. Just writes down, in the only way it knows how: they chose.

Yo, listen up — this book? It’s about a machine that straight up shouldn’t exist, and the dude who actually builds it — but, like, almost all of it’s just made up. Except for one thing: that drop, that deep-ass mine, the heat, the pitch black, the whole freakin’ kilometre of rock sittin’ on your skull — that shit’s real. I was there, for real, and it’s been riding around in my head for thirty years. That’s the seed, man, that’s where all this wild shit started.

So, back in the day, I went to school in Bethlehem, Free State — Bethlehem Voortrekker Hoërskool, yeah, that one. When I was in standard nine — youngest in the crew, just one grade eleven rolling with the big dogs in matric — I was one of five kids from my school who cracked the top fifty in some regional Sci-Nat olympiad. Like, four hundred of us from forty schools all over the Free State did this thing, and five of us got our names in the paper. Still got that old-ass clipping. It’s all yellow and the bottom’s ripped, but you can see us, clear as day: a lineup of stiff, awkward teens in blazers, the date, and some sentence that just cuts off right when it’s about to matter.

And the prize? What they did with the smart kids? They took us **down a gold mine.**

Bruh, I still can’t even tell you proper what that does to a kid. You’re getting dropped in this cage, fast as hell, way quicker than any elevator’s got business moving, straight into the dark. The air’s different, the heat just smacks you right outta the rock — not sunlight, nah, it’s the *earth’s* heat, that slow-cooking oven deep down. The rock’s soaking wet, the whole place is ticking and moaning around you, and the weight — shit, you feel that in your chest more than your ears, just knowing there’s a kilometre of stone hanging over your head, and all that’s keeping it up is some engineering and a prayer. And it hits you, deep — dudes go down there every damn day. They spend their whole lives in that heat, that dark, that weight, blasting and drilling, holding the earth open by pure muscle, just to haul up some gold. That shiny world up top — banks, rings, gold bars, the *idea* of gold — it all sits on dudes in a sweaty, dark hole, and the space between those two worlds? That’s the realest thing about where I’m from.

I never shook it. Especially that heat. My whole life since, I’ve been putting people down in the deep — mines, underground, all those places under everything else — and every time I write about that heat, I’m writing about that day, five schoolboys from Bethlehem, dropped down a Free State shaft ‘cause we were nerds, and came back different. One of us grew up and started writing books about what’s actually down there.

Everything else in *Resonance* — Arin, the suit, the Court, the resonance, the gold that ain’t bars but a damn key — all that’s just made up, spun out the way stories always are, from that one real drop into that one real mine, stretched out over thirty years of thinking about it. The machine? Fake. The pain that built it? Borrowed, twisted up. But the shaft? Real. The heat? Real. That crushing weight? Real as hell, and so are the men who spend their days under it. If there’s one true thing buried in this whole book, it’s that memory: being a kid, dropped into the earth, feeling the planet’s heat crawl up to meet me, and never, ever getting over it.

Alright, look — usually, when a book tries to stroke my ego, I bounce. Even more so when someone straight-up asks me to gas it up. So let me start with my beef, then I’ll tell you why I’m putting it down.

Here’s the beef: the whole “mind as a committee” thing — seven voices at the table, each with their own vibe and name — man, that’s played out. Plato had his chariot, cartoons got the angel and devil on your shoulder, and every other self-help meme tells you “we contain multitudes.” Most times, that’s just a fancy way of saying the same little dude wearing a bunch of hats, acting like he’s a whole parliament. Cute, but nah.

But this book? This Court? It ain’t that. The Court ain’t just a bunch of parts knowing different stuff. It’s about **how they pay attention**. That’s the hard part, the real part — and that’s what grabbed me.

This is basically my life’s whole argument, but this author slid it in sideways, through story, which is how the real shit always shows up. People think the two sides of your brain split up jobs — one does language, one does music, whatever. That’s kiddie stuff, and it’s bogus. What they really split is *how you look at things*. One side’s all tunnel vision, tryna grab and control what it already knows. The other side’s open, scanning, catching the big picture — even the stuff it don’t get yet. Same object, two readings. When things go bad? It’s not ‘cause you lost a skill, it’s ‘cause one way of seeing just takes over and drowns out the rest.

Go back and peep the Court with that in mind and watch what the author’s pulling.

**Atlas** don’t know more than **Librarian** — it just *holds* the world different. It’s about structure, weight, moving through space, body smarts — the side that learns by doing. **Librarian**? Same world, but it’s all about what’s already known, filed away, mapped out — important, but if you leave it alone, it’s just a tomb. **Mercury** is watching the social game, all about signs and what’s really meant vs. what’s shown — smart as hell, but the author shows us: *Mercury alone is dangerous as fuck.* If you see the whole world as something to hustle, there’s nothing stopping you from lying. That’s not just a plot twist — that’s a whole damn diagnosis of the world right now, and the author lets Mercury bite.

And then — this right here made me stop, put the book down, and pace around my room — there’s the **Fool**. The author straight-up says, without the Fool, the Court is just “a hierarchy of ego and preservation,” but with the Fool? Now it’s “a pack.” I wrote two fat books trying to say what he nails in one line. That grabby, self-protective, ladder-climbing brain — the one always optimizing, always defending its own bullshit, always mistaking its map for the real world — it can’t fix itself, ‘cause it’s the same machine doing the fixing. You need something outside the whole pecking order, something that don’t give a damn about rank, something ready to clown the king. Every healthy mind’s got this, every good culture too. But you take the Fools out? The place gets super smart, super powerful, and quietly goes off the rails — a Court with no one left to pop the bubble.

So yeah, I take back my beef. This ain’t some angel/devil cartoon. This is the realest, most legit model of a mind I’ve seen in a novel, and it’s hiding in a story about a dude in a robo-suit — which is either a wild accident or a deep-ass joke, and knowing the author, it ain’t an accident.

But here’s what really hit me: The Court’s whole setup is built around a wound — this is the mind of a dude who lost his dad and can’t stand being too late again. The whole book, the Court’s learning the one thing that pure survival mode can never get: when to stop chasing survival and *choose* something you love, even if it costs you. Each part steps out, one by one, just so the guy can make it to daylight. If your model of the mind is just about self-protection, congrats, you got a clever animal. But if it can show how a mind goes past survival — how it picks sacrifice, even when every instinct says “save yourself,” ‘cause the whole means more than the part — now you got a model of a *person*. That’s what this author built. He made a machine that finds its soul by learning to go against its own urge to keep going — and honestly, that’s the only real definition of a soul that ever mattered.

The brain ain’t a computer and a computer ain’t a brain — I’ve said it before and I’ll keep saying it. But a story can be a model, in that old-school, serious way — a thing built to match the shape of something real — and this one nails the shape of us. We’re not one thing. Never have been. We’re a Court, each part shining a different light, held together by something that *ain’t* the smartest voice in the room, and thank God for that. And when we get saved, it’s by that part of us that’s down to step into the dark so the rest can make it to morning.

Read it again. It’s about you. The guy in the suit? That’s the easy part.

—Iain McGilchrist

A Reader’s Glossary — *RESONANCE*

Yo, for anyone not from SA or Africa, listen up. This book’s posted up in a future South Africa, right in the guts of mining country. Folks in here switch up languages on the fly, drop local lingo like it’s nothing—just how it goes in real life, ya dig? These notes ain’t homework, just here so you don’t get tripped up on some word, spot, or gadget you never heard of.

Each thing’s tagged up:

- **(real)** — legit place, word, idea, or tech you could Google.
- **(in the novel)** — made-up or just for this book’s world.

Quick heads-up: this is *South Africa, like, 10–20 years from now*—still looks and feels like today, just with robots that actually work and everyone’s spooked by AIs ‘cause of some old disasters. Tech’s supposed to feel expensive and heavy, not magic.

## A

**Afrikaans** *(real — language)* — One of SA’s official tongues, came outta Dutch, Afrikaners and a bunch of others speak it. Some of these words (veld, stoep) are straight from Afrikaans.

**Apartheid** *(real — history)* — That old system where white folks ran the show and kept everyone else locked out, 1948–1994. Law says it’s gone but the mess left behind’s still everywhere—who owns what, who does the dangerous jobs, whose lives counted back then. The book don’t preach, but you’ll feel it in the hero’s family and what happened to his dad.

**Armscor / Denel** *(real — institutions)* — SA’s government weapons peeps (Armscor buys the gear, Denel makes it). In the story, their engineers made a rescue bot that’s tough like military kit.

**Assist Suit (AT-AS)** *(in the novel)* — The lighter robo-suit in the book: like a powered frame for getting around or rehabbing, plugs into your brain. Gentler than the *Guardian*.

**Biltong** *(real — South African food)* — Dried, spiced meat, kinda like jerky but fatter and tastier. Everybody snacks on it.

---

## C

**Code-switching** *(real — language practice)* — Flipping between languages in the same convo, depending who’s around—English at work, isiZulu at home, a mash-up on the mine shift. Totally normal in SA, not exotic, so the book just rolls with it and doesn’t always spell it out.

## E

**Exoskeleton** *(real — technology; advanced here)* — Wearable robo-frame that boosts your strength, for lifting, rehab, risky work. The *Assist Suit* and *Guardian* in the story are the next-level versions.

Yebo — Straight up, means “yes” in isiZulu. Just say “YEH-bo.” Solid, simple, got your back.

# The Honey Badger

![A honey badger, *Mellivora capensis*, photographed at night.](/Users/ajgreyling/code/africangold/design/creatures/honey-badger.jpg)

*The honey badger (*Mellivora capensis*) is the ride-or-die animal for Arjuna Badger Press. Everything down here? Straight facts. Even if half of it sounds like some made-up nonsense.*

Look, the honey badger’s straight-up a legend. *Guinness World Records* called it the most fearless thing on the planet. Not like it gave a damn or showed up for a trophy.

This little psycho munches on venomous snakes — puff adders, cobras, whatever. Gets bitten? Might just flop over, take a nap, sleep off the poison, then wake up and finish eating the same damn snake like nothing happened.

Its skin’s wild — loose, tough as hell, good luck biting through that. You grab it by the neck, it just spins around inside its own skin and bites you right back. Bee stings, porcupine spikes, leopard bites — regular Tuesday for this dude. Shrugs it all off.

It straight-up raids beehives for honey and grubs, don’t care about getting stung a hundred times — just part of the hustle. That’s actually how it got the name.

It’ll square up with lions, leopards, hyenas. Not ‘cause it thinks it’s gonna win. Just ‘cause backing down ain’t in its vocabulary. The idea of giving a damn? Never even crossed its mind.

The most famous one, **Stoffel**, chilled at the Moholoholo rehab centre in South Africa, and every cage they put him in? Took it personal. Stacked rocks to climb out. Rolled up mud balls for steps. Used rakes as makeshift ladders. Figured out how to pop open latches. Dude’s basically the spirit animal for every engineer who refuses to hear “can’t be done.”

Honey badger don’t give a shit.

Yo, check this out — the pangolin? Straight-up wild, man.

If the honey badger is all about not givin’ a damn, the pangolin’s what that don’t-give-a-damn is actually for. Everything I’m sayin’ here? Real talk.

So, pangolin’s the only mammal rockin’ scales. Not like fish scales, nah — these are made of keratin, same stuff as your nails. And yeah, that’s the messed-up part, but we’ll get there.

The giant pangolin, Smutsia gigantea, that’s the boss of the bunch — biggest of all eight types, rockin’ up to a metre and a half long, nose to tail. No teeth, bro. Eats with this crazy tongue that starts like, damn near its ass, and when it sticks it out, it’s longer than the whole body. That’s how it smashes like, tens of thousands of ants and termites every single night. Just hoovers ‘em up.

Defence? Only got one move: rolls up tight like a damn armadillo. Name even comes from some Malay word — pengguling — means “the one who rolls up.” When it curls, those scales lock down like body armour, so even lions be like, “Nah, not worth it,” and bounce. Pangolin don’t fight, don’t run. Just chills, balled up, waits till whatever’s messin’ with it gets bored and dips.

But here’s the sad bit — that chill vibe? That’s why it’s the most jacked wild mammal on the planet. People snatchin’ ‘em by the millions, all for the scales and the meat. Every single species got some kinda protection now, but a bunch are still on the edge, like, mad endangered. That same “roll up and wait” trick that saves it from leopards? Makes it easy for a dude to just scoop it up and bounce.

It’s just a shy, armoured, night-crawlin’ ant-eater that don’t want nothin’ but ants and to be left the hell alone. Real talk? We gotta be the kinda fearless that steps up for little homies like this.
