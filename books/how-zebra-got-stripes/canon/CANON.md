# How the Zebra Got Her Stripes — Canon

> A children's picture book for Arjuna Badger Press — the Children's Library shelf.
> An **original retelling** of a classic African *pourquoi* ("how it came to be") folktale:
> *how the zebra came by her stripes.* The text and phrasing are original. The folk-motif —
> a plain zebra, the long-grass dapple of light and shade, the herd that makes a place for her —
> is the heritage being honoured, not borrowed prose.

## The one-sentence spine
Long ago Zebra was one plain colour, proud and lonely for it; in a dry season she learns —
with the help of a watching child and the patient animals of the veld — that the very thing
that makes her *different* is the thing that lets her hide safe and finally belong.

## The source motif (heritage honoured)
The "where the zebra got its stripes" tale lives all across southern and eastern Africa, in
many mouths and many shapes. One old shape is harsh — a fight at the last waterhole, a fall into
the cold black ash of a burnt-out fire, the scorch-marks left as stripes. **We deliberately tell
the gentler, kinder shape**: no cruelty, no villain. The stripes are a **gift** — painted on
by the hands of the herd and the child, light-and-shadow made to wear — so that Zebra can melt
into the dapple of the long grass and never be lost again. The house rule holds: the real force
under the story is **pride and loneliness and belonging**, not a baddie.

## The story-under-the-story
On the surface: a magic explanation for a beautiful animal.
Underneath: **what makes you different is exactly what keeps you safe — and what lets you
belong.** A child who feels odd, or singular, or like they stick out, reads this and learns that
the odd thing is not a flaw to be hidden but a gift waiting to be worn. You do not belong by
becoming the same as everyone. You belong by bringing the one thing only you can bring.

## The reader
Read-aloud age 4–8; reads-alone age 6–9. One adult, one child, one lamp. The pictures must
carry the warmth with the sound off. Personalised keepsake: the child-hero is named with the
literal token `{{CHILD}}` everywhere; the body opens on a dedication line carrying `{{DEDICATION}}`.

## Cast
- **{{CHILD}}** — the human child of the tale. A herder-child of the veld who waters the
  animals at the pan and watches over them. Kind, watchful, unhurried. The only one who never
  laughs at Zebra for being plain. (Named with the `{{CHILD}}` token throughout — personalised.)
- **Zebra** — when the tale begins she is **one plain colour**, the soft grey-gold of dry grass,
  with no markings at all. Proud of being the most beautiful, lonely under the pride. She keeps
  to the edge of the herd because the edge is where the proud stand. She is not vain to be cruel;
  she is vain to cover up that she is afraid of being seen — and afraid of *not* being seen.
- **Tortoise** — old, slow, the one who remembers the oldest things and the gentle way of doing
  them. The keeper of the idea that becomes the gift.
- **Weaver Bird** — quick, bright, a hundred of him at once; the herd's small busy hands.
- **Lion** — not a villain. The honest danger of the open veld: the reason hiding is a gift,
  not a trick. He hunts what he can *see*, and the open is where the seen die. Drawn dignified,
  golden, never a cartoon menace.
- **The herd** — wildebeest, antelope, the other plain-coated animals who graze together in the
  safe muddle of the middle, where no one is too special to be missed.

## The gift (how the stripes come)
Tortoise remembers the oldest gentle magic: at dusk, when the low sun pours through the tall
dry grass, the whole world goes striped with **light and shadow, light and shadow** — bars of
gold and bars of blue-black laid right across the ground. The animals and {{CHILD}} gather that
very dapple — sunlight in one hand, shadow in the other — and paint it onto Zebra, stripe by
stripe, so she may *wear the grass itself.* When she steps into the long grass at dusk she
disappears — not because she is hidden away, but because she has become the place she stands in.
Lion's eyes slide right past her. And — the turn — when she walks back into the herd, the herd
can no longer tell where Zebra ends and the dapple begins, so she is never again on the lonely
proud edge: she is *inside*, one of many, safe in the muddle of the middle. Different made her
safe. Different let her belong.

## The refrain (a child can say it back)
> *Light and shadow, shadow and light —*
> *now you'll never be lost again.*

It recurs across the spreads — first as the magic of the dusk grass, then as the painting, then
as the thing the herd says over her, then as the closing blessing. By the third time, the child
reading along will say it before the adult does. That is the test of a good refrain.

## Spread plan (14 spreads)
1. Title spread — the wide dry-season veld at golden dusk; the pan, the long grass, the herd far
   off; {{CHILD}} small at the water's edge. Establish the place and the light.
2. Zebra in the old days — plain, grey-gold, *beautiful and alone*, standing proud on the lonely
   edge of the herd, a little apart.
3. The pride — Zebra tells the dapple-coated others she is the most beautiful because she is one
   pure colour; she does not see how the long grass swallows *them* safe while she stands out.
4. The dry season bites — the pan shrinks, the grass goes pale and tall, and out on the open
   gold, anything that stands alone can be *seen*. {{CHILD}} waters the animals at the last pan.
5. **HERO SPREAD — the danger.** Dusk. Lion on the open ground. The dapple-coated herd melts into
   the striped grass and vanishes; only plain Zebra is left standing, lit up, the one thing the
   eye can find. She is seen. (Honest danger, no gore — the tension is being *visible*.)
6. {{CHILD}} reaches Zebra first and does the kind thing — stands between, calls her low and
   quiet into the grass, but plain Zebra cannot hide; she has nothing of the grass on her.
7. The herd is safe; Zebra is safe-this-once but frightened, and for the first time not proud.
   She asks the question under the whole book: *why can everyone disappear but me?*
8. Tortoise remembers — the oldest gentle thing. At dusk the world goes striped with light and
   shadow; that is a coat, if you know how to wear it. The idea is born.
9. **COMPOSITE / making-spread.** The gathering of the dapple — {{CHILD}} and Weaver Bird and the
   whole herd catch bars of low gold sun and bars of blue-black shadow from the grass, hands full
   of light and shade. The refrain as a working-song.
10. The painting — stripe by stripe they lay the light and shadow onto Zebra's plain coat;
    gold, then dark, then gold. She holds very still. The refrain sung over her.
11. **The vanishing — the pay-off.** Zebra steps into the long dusk grass and *disappears* — she
    has become the place she stands in. Lion's gold eyes slide right past. Safe.
12. The belonging turn — she walks back to the herd, and now no one can tell where Zebra ends and
    the dapple begins. The proud lonely edge is gone; she is *inside*, one of many, missed if
    she were gone. Different made her safe; different let her belong.
13. {{CHILD}} and Zebra at the water at last light, foreheads close; the quiet private thank-you.
    The thing that made her odd was the thing that saved her.
14. Closing image — and that, the elders say, is why to this day every zebra wears the grass at
    dusk on her back: light and shadow, so she is never lost, and never alone. Final refrain.

## Art direction (house-consistent)
- Warm, painterly storybook look — gouache/illustrated-picture-book warmth, a little chaotic, a
  little funny, deeply tender. **Dusk-veld palette**: low honey-gold sun, dry-grass ochre,
  blue-black evening shadow, the deep glow of the African evening. The whole book is built on
  *light and shadow*, so the dapple of the long grass is the visual motor of every spread.
- The veld is **specific and dignified** — southern African bushveld/grassland, a windmill or a
  thorn tree on the horizon if it helps the place feel real. Never generic "jungle." Never caricature.
- Zebra changes across the book: plain grey-gold at first, striped and glowing by the end. Keep
  her face and shape consistent so a child tracks the *same* animal becoming herself.
- {{CHILD}} is an African herder-child of the veld — warm, specific, dignified. Drawn the same
  child on every spread (the personalised hero). Representation is non-negotiable house a11y.
- Lion is golden and dignified — the honest danger of the open, never a cartoon monster.
- Every spread must read emotionally with the sound off: a tired parent at 8pm should *feel* it
  before reading a word.

## Tone & rules for the prose
- Read-aloud rhythm. Short lines. Lots of air. The refrain a child can say back.
- **No villain.** The force under the story is pride, loneliness, the dry season, and the honest
  danger of being seen in the open — not cruelty. The gentle version: the stripes are a gift.
- Gentle but **honest** — never twee. Zebra's loneliness is real; her fear at being the only one
  seen is real; that is what makes the gift land.
- Sacred / initiation matter kept at the threshold — this is a folk-magic gift at dusk, not a rite.
- The badger-house wink, if any, stays a wink. Never explained.
