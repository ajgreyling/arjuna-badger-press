REAL PLACE: Puma Punku, part of the Tiwanaku complex, Bolivia, high on the Andean plain near Lake Titicaca. SITE MAP: The ruined monumental platform, scattered with immense red sandstone and andesite blocks, some weighing over 100 tons; the iconic H-blocks arrayed in rows, carved with sharp, geometric precision; deep-cut grooves and T-shaped channels where bronze cramps once joined stone to stone; wind-cut grass, thin air, distant snow on the Cordillera, the lake’s blue edge. CHASE GEOGRAPHY: From the rim villages and Lake Titicaca’s reed islands (where the quarry stones began), through the modern town of Tiwanaku, to the fenced archaeological zone and its off-limits vaults. TEXTURE OF WONDER: Stones so flat you can’t slip a razor blade between them, tool marks visible but enigmatic; the cold gleam of bronze in ancient grooves; the silence of the plateau, broken by wind and the calls of market sellers; the living presence of Aymara families who walk the ruins daily, bringing offerings. LIVING PEOPLE: Aymara communities, site custodians, Bolivian archaeologists, market women, local children playing among the stones; the government archaeologist who must balance preservation, tourism, and ancestral connection; the adversary’s polite, foreign-funded team, always one step ahead or behind, always watching.
