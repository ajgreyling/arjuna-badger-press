A found-family crew of neurodiverse misfits — led on the ground by Naila (ND metallurgist/maker) — heads to the high Andean plateau to chase the official story of Puma Punku: a site of massive, precisely-shaped stones and interlocking H-blocks, said by some to be "impossible" without lost technology, but by mainstream archaeologists to be a supreme feat of human craft. They're up against a clean-handed adversary: a well-funded foundation with an ideological agenda (to "prove" a lost super-civilization), racing to secure key artifacts and shape the narrative.

They play it straight: the official story gets its full due — the Tiwanaku culture, ancestral Aymara on their own ground, cut and fit these stones with stone and bronze tools, using templates, patience, and genius. The builders' real marvel? The cast bronze cramps, molten metal poured into precisely-carved grooves to bind the stones — a feat of high-altitude metallurgy and engineering, grounded and physical, not alien.

But when the crew tries to duplicate the bronze-cramp joinery — with the best modern knowledge, in the real thin air, with period materials — their "easy" version comes out a potato: the fit leaks, the bronze doesn't flow right, the channels crack, and the scale is daunting. The "easy" story is hard.

The hole: No one has experimentally, at full scale and altitude, shown how the Tiwanaku metallurgists achieved such consistent, tight-fit castings with the tools and resources known to be available. The real in situ cramps, alloyed just right, fit just so, remain a marvel of craft not fully reverse-engineered.

The wink: If the official story is right, why can't we repeat it cleanly, even with our advantages? If the fringe is right, why is every material in the ground exactly what we expect from this time and place? What is missing from the craft story we haven't seen?

The local — an Aymara/Bolivian archaeologist — out-reads the crew, sharing the site’s meaning as a living place, not a puzzle-box: the cramps are not just technical, but ceremonial, binding stone and intention, earth and sky, people and ancestors.

Jakobus, as always, reads the made thing: What is it for? The function is both physical and social — a key that locks the stones, a bond that survives earthquakes, a visible sign of mastery and memory, a map of what holds a people together.

Back-matter promise: Every official claim is cited and sourced; every unresolved marvel is real and checkable; every fringe claim is labelled as such. The reader is left in the draught between what is known and what is not — and the permission to say "maybe" is the book's gift.
