# Chapter 14 — The Second Attempt

The mould was warm this time. That was the lesson, and it had cost them a week to learn it.

Naila crouched over the channel they'd cut into the practice block — a slab of red sandstone Lucía had pointed them to in the rubble field, already split, already worthless, so no one could accuse them of carving into the past. The groove ran the shape of the in-situ cramps: that fat I, two square mouths joined by a waist. She'd traced the real ones with calipers until the custodian's assistant started giving her a look. The dimensions lived in her hands now. The trouble had never been the shape.

The trouble was the metal.

"Charcoal's good," Specs called from the bellows pit. They'd rebuilt the furnace three times, and the third one finally held heat the way the thin air wanted to steal it. At thirty-eight hundred metres the fire fought you. Every flame ran hungrier, leaner, the oxygen spread out and reluctant, and the crucible took longer to come up than the books — written at sea level, by people who'd never gasped walking to their own car — said it should. "She's near. Naila. She's near."

"Don't tell me near."

"I'm telling you near."

The fish, riding in the chest pocket of Naila's coat where it had no business being and no reason to talk, observed that *near* was also what the last cramp had been, right up until it wasn't, and that perhaps near was the word people used the way other people used *probably fine*.

She ignored it. She'd learned to ignore it the way you ignore your own pulse.

The first attempt — chapter of its own, that disaster — had failed because she'd treated the stone like a foundry would: cold mould, hot pour, let physics do the rest. The bronze had hit the cold sandstone and seized. Skinned over before it filled. They'd chipped out a thing that looked like a melted spoon, porous as bread, and the groove around it had spalled where the thermal shock cracked the rock. A potato. An honest potato. Rani had photographed it from four angles and filed it under *do not repeat*.

The locals had not laughed at them. That was the thing Naila kept turning over. A foundry crew at sea level would have laughed. Here the old man named Eulogio — Lucía's cousin's husband, a smith who still cast bronze for festival ornaments the way his father had — had only looked at the seized lump for a long moment and then mimed warming his hands at a fire. *The stone is cold,* he'd said, through Lucía, who translated with the patience of a woman who did it forty times a day. *You poured into a cold thing. Did your grandmother put bread in a cold oven?*

So now: preheat.

They'd built a fire of dung and brush in the channel itself and let it burn down twice, and Naila had her bare hand flat against the sandstone an inch from the groove, reading the warmth up through her palm the way she read everything, by feel, by the slow honest argument of materials. The stone was hot enough to make her want to pull back. Not hot enough to crack. That window — Eulogio had shown them with his hands how wide it was, and it was not wide.

The official story did not require any of this.

The official story, stated straight and stated with respect, was clean and it was sufficient. The Tiwanaku — ancestors of the Aymara who walked these stones every morning, who left coca leaves in the joints while the crew sweated over their furnace — had cut these channels with stone and bronze tools, no steel anywhere in the ground to argue otherwise, and into them they had poured molten arsenical bronze to lock block to block. U-shaped cramps. I-shaped cramps. Metallurgists confirmed the alloy. The channels were right there, mouth and waist, in stone you could touch. No lasers. No lost machines. No one from anywhere but here. A supreme feat of hand and patience and a thousand years of accumulated skill, and the marvel of it — the real marvel, the one that didn't need aliens to be astonishing — was the metal. People standing where Naila stood now, in this air, with this fire fighting them, had done it consistently, block after block, fit after fit, for generations.

That was the whole story. It was true. Naila believed it.

She just could not do it.

"Crucible's ready." Specs's voice had gone tight, which meant they believed it too. The graphite crucible came up in the long iron tongs glowing the deep orange of bronze at temperature, and the surface of the melt moved like something alive, like mercury's serious cousin, a skin of dross sliding as Specs tilted it for the skim. "Rani, leaves."

"Coca and a little tallow." Rani read it off the notes without looking up, the way Rani read everything, eyes on the paper because the paper didn't ask anything back. "Eulogio said the flux is — he didn't say flux. He said it *helps the metal forget itself.* I wrote it down exactly. I don't know what it means."

"It means it wets the channel," Naila said. "Lowers the surface tension so it flows into the corners instead of balling up. He's describing flux. He just doesn't call it that."

Across the rubble, on a fallen block worn smooth by five hundred years of weather and sitting, Dr. Lucía Quispe watched them with her coat collar up and her hands around a tin cup, and said nothing.

She'd been there an hour. She hadn't offered to help. That wasn't coldness — Naila had stopped reading it as coldness around the time of the first potato. It was something closer to courtesy. The kind you extend to people who have asked, finally, the right way: by failing in front of you and not pretending otherwise. Lucía would tell them what the cramps *meant* when she chose to, and not before, and that was hers to choose. She had told them once, flatly, *You keep asking me how. The how is in the museum. Ask the right question and I'll think about answering it.* Naila was still working out what the right question was. She suspected it wasn't a metallurgy question.

But Lucía had pointed them to Eulogio. That was a gift, and Naila knew the weight of it.

"Now," Naila said.

Specs poured.

There is a moment in a casting that you cannot describe to anyone who hasn't stood over one. The metal leaves the crucible in a rope, bright and shivering, and it goes into the dark mouth of the channel and for a second you can't see anything, you can only listen — to the hiss, to the way the stone takes the heat, to whether it accepts the metal or rejects it. Cold rock screams. This stone sighed. The bronze ran down the waist and filled the second mouth and rose to the lip and Naila watched it crawl into the square corners — actually *crawl in*, fill them, the tallow-and-coca flux doing its work, the metal forgetting itself just as the old man had said — and for the length of one held breath it was right.

It was right.

"Look at it," Specs whispered. "Look at it *go.*"

"Don't talk to it," Naila said. "Don't talk."

The fish noted that they were all talking, that the only one not talking was the woman on the rock, and that this seemed like data.

They let it sit. You always want to crack the mould early — every fibre of the impatient animal in you wants to *see* — and every smith who ever lived has learned the hard way to wait. Specs jittered. Specs's whole arc was the jitter, the hands that needed to be doing the next thing, and Naila put a hand flat on their forearm without looking and held it there until the jitter eased to a thrum. They waited while the bronze went from gold to straw to the dull liver-brown of cooling metal. They waited while the warmth bled out of the stone and the high cold of the plateau came back in around them, that thin blue cold that smelled of dust and far-off snow, and a market woman's call drifted up from the town below, and two children chased each other along the top of a fallen H-block as if it were a garden wall and not the most precisely cut object for a thousand miles.

"Closer," Rani said quietly. Rani had the calipers now, hovering, not yet touching. "The fill's complete. No porosity I can see on the surface. The first one had — that one was Swiss cheese. This one's solid."

It was the closest they'd come. Naila could see it without measuring — the cramp sat *in* the channel instead of shrinking away from it, the two mouths full and square, the metal kissed up tight against the sandstone walls. A week ago they'd had a melted spoon. Now they had a cramp. A real one. Ugly at the edges where the pour-gate had been, a flaw any of them could fix with a file, but a cramp, an actual lock of bronze sitting in stone the way the ancient ones did, and Naila felt the thing she always felt at the edge of getting it, the pull toward *we did it, it's solved, close the file* —

— and that was the pull Lucía was watching for. Naila understood that, suddenly, looking up and catching the custodian's eye across the rubble. Lucía wasn't watching the cramp. She was watching Naila's *face*. Watching to see whether the outsider would mistake the closer fit for the answer.

Naila made herself put the calipers down.

"It's closer," she said, carefully, to the air, to the fish, to the question she still hadn't found the shape of. "It's not the same. Look at the fit at the lip — there's a hair of a gap on the south corner. The real ones, you can't get a blade in. I can get a blade in this."

"You can get a *thought* in this," Specs said, deflating only a little, because Specs's recoveries were as fast as Specs's crashes. "But Naila. The first one didn't even fill. This one filled. We learned the heat, we learned the flux, we got the ore from — where'd Eulogio say the ore—"

"Local," Rani said. "Arsenical copper from the same belt the originals tested to. We got that right. We did everything right." A pause, the honest autistic pause that didn't soften the next part for anyone's comfort. "It's still wrong."

And there it was, the thing the books didn't close. Naila had read every paper Rani could find. Protzen and Nair on the architecture, careful and humane and full of love for the builders' skill. Vranich modelling the blocks in three dimensions. Janusek on the culture, the ancestors, the living thread. Not one of them — not one — published a full-scale workflow for this. Smelt to transport to pour to cool to fit, at this altitude, with this air, repeated to this consistency. The cramps existed. The alloy was known. The channels were measurable. The *making* of them, the actual sequence of a person's hands and a fire's behaviour and a stone's temperature on this exact ground, had never been cleanly reproduced and written down. Other ancient places had cramps — Egypt, Greece. Nobody had nailed *these*. The crew had the best modern knowledge, period materials, a master smith's guidance, the right ore, the right heat, and they had produced a thing you could slide a blade into.

Closer. Not it.

"Two days of preheat we couldn't have sustained for a whole platform," Naila said, mostly to herself. "How many cramps in this site? Hundreds. Each one a fire, each one a pour, each one this window of heat you can't miss. And they didn't miss. They didn't get *closer.* They got it." She looked at the children on the H-block, at the coca leaves brown in the joints from this morning's offering, at a fit so old and so good it had outlasted the empire that made it and three more after. "We're missing something. Not technology. Something in the *doing.*"

Lucía spoke for the first time in an hour.

"Now," she said, dry as the dust, "you're getting warmer than your fire."

Naila turned. The custodian was almost smiling, the tin cup steaming, her eyes on the cramp and then on Naila with that measuring patience.

"You think it's a temperature problem," Lucía said. "Or a flux problem. Or an ore problem. You keep it in your hands because your hands are clever and I respect that — Eulogio respects that, he doesn't take just anyone to the fire." A tilt of the head. "But you're casting it like a thing you make once and walk away from. Ask yourself who else was standing at that fire when the real ones were poured. Ask yourself what they were saying while the metal ran. You think it was *don't talk to it.*"

She didn't say more. That was hers to give or hold, and she was holding it, the way she'd held it all week, the door open just enough to let the cold draught through.

Naila looked back down at the cramp. It had gone fully dark now, the warmth all bled away, the south corner with its blade-width gap, the closest they'd ever come.

And as she looked, the stone spoke.

A sound like a knuckle popping, small and absolute, came up out of the channel — the bronze had cooled faster than the sandstone around it, pulled tight as it shrank, and somewhere in that argument between metal that wanted to be smaller and rock that wasn't ready to let it, something had to give. A hairline split ran out from the south corner across the lip of the groove, threading the stone, and the cramp shifted a sixteenth of an inch and sat loose in its bed.

Nobody moved.

Specs let out a long breath that fogged in the cold. Rani wrote something down. The fish, in Naila's pocket, said nothing at all for once, which was its own kind of comment.

Lucía rose from the worn block, brushing dust from her coat, and walked over to look down at the crack with neither triumph nor pity, only a long custodian's familiarity, as if she'd watched this exact thing happen to clever hands before.

"That," she said, "is the question I'll answer. When you stop asking it the way you're asking it." She set her empty cup down on the stone. "Come tomorrow. Early. Before the tour buses. I want to show you something, and it isn't a furnace."

She walked off toward the town, and the children waved at her from the H-block, and she waved back, and Naila stood over the cracked cooling thing they'd made — closer, so close, *not it* — and understood that the gap she could fit a blade into was not the gap that mattered.
