# How Fire Came to the People — Canon

> A children's picture book for Arjuna Badger Press — Children's Library shelf.
> An ORIGINAL retelling. The text and phrasing are our own; the heritage being honoured is the
> San / Khoi family of fire-origin "pourquoi" tales — how the people first carried fire home, and
> why ash is the grey blanket the morning's coal sleeps under. Inspired-by, not derived-from: no
> sacred or initiation matter is depicted; the wonder is kept at the warm, everyday threshold.
> This is a personalised print keepsake: the child-hero is named with the token {{CHILD}}.

## The one-sentence spine
In the cold time before fire, a small brave child {{CHILD}} and a clever mantis go to the
faraway keeper of fire to bring home one living coal — and learn that the greatest gifts must be
**carried gently and shared**, and that even leftover ash is a quiet promise of tomorrow.

## The story-under-the-story (the layer under the magic)
On the surface: how the people first got fire.
Underneath: this is a book about **carrying something precious without crushing it** — about how a
gift only stays alive if you guard it *and* give it away, and about ash, the humble grey leftover
that everyone overlooks, which is in fact the keeper of the next morning's flame and the food of
new green grass. A child learns: the small, the gentle, and the easily-missed are the things that
carry tomorrow.

## The reader
Read-aloud age 4–8; reads-alone age 6–9. One adult, one child, one lamp. The pictures must carry
the warmth before a child can read a word. A REFRAIN a child can chant back is the spine of the
read-aloud rhythm.

## The refrain
*Carry it gently. Carry it slow.*
*Cup your hands round it. Don't let it go.*

(Recurs across the journey-home spreads; a child learns to say it back. A final turn at the end
folds ash into the same promise.)

## Cast
- **{{CHILD}}** — the child-hero. A small, brave African child of the cold-time village. Kind,
  steady, more careful than fearless — which is exactly the right kind of brave for carrying a
  coal. The human child who goes and brings fire home. (Named everywhere with the literal token
  {{CHILD}} — never a hard-coded name — because this is a personalised keepsake.)
- **Mantis** — the animal helper, in the San storytelling tradition: a clever, twiggy praying
  mantis who rides on {{CHILD}}'s shoulder, knows the long road, and keeps the little one steady.
  Wise, dry-humoured, gentle. Never a trickster here — a guide.
- **The Fire-Bird** — the far, dangerous keeper of fire. Not a villain: fire is simply *hers*,
  high on the old mountain, and she is not cruel, only careful, the way fire is careful. She does
  not give the coal away to power; she gives it to a child who promises to *share* it.
- **The People** — the cold-time village: grandmothers, fathers, babies, all wrapped against the
  dark, waiting. Warm, specific, dignified. They are who the gift is *for*.
- **The Ash** — almost a character by the end: the soft grey blanket the coal sleeps under, the
  thing that looks like nothing and is the whole promise of morning.

## Why the keeper is not a villain (house rule)
The danger is **nature, distance, and care** — the long cold road, the wind that wants the coal,
the dark that wants the light — not an evil holder of fire. The Fire-Bird tests one thing only:
will this child *carry it gently and share it*? That is the true force of the tale.

## The journey is the heart
The going-to-get-it is short. The **carrying-home is the whole book.** Wind, river, slope, and the
long dark all want to put the coal out, and the only thing that keeps it alive is cupped hands, a
slow pace, the dry-grass nest in the clay pot, and {{CHILD}}'s steady breath on it. This is where
the refrain lives and where a child *feels* the weight of carrying something precious.

## The turn that lands the medicine
At home, the people crowd round the one little coal — and {{CHILD}}'s first instinct could be to
hoard it, to keep it safe and alone. Instead {{CHILD}} does the brave, generous thing: touches the
coal to dry grass, and to the next fire, and the next, so that one coal becomes many fires —
**a gift kept by being given away.** And in the morning, when the flames have gone, {{CHILD}} sees
the grey ash, thinks it is only leftover — and Mantis shows the secret: blow softly, and the coal
still lives under the ash. Ash kept the fire. Ash, scattered on the ground, feeds new green grass.

## The ending
Fire is the people's now — warmth, cooked food, light against the dark, and stories told around it.
And every morning the grey ash is banked over the coal like a blanket, and the last refrain folds
ash into the promise: what looks like leftover is tomorrow, waiting.

## The closing image
The whole village around the first fire under a huge starry African night; {{CHILD}} banking the
soft grey ash over the sleeping coal with cupped hands; Mantis on {{CHILD}}'s shoulder; new green
grass already pushing up at the edge of yesterday's ash.

## Tone & rules for the prose
- Read-aloud rhythm. Short lines, lots of air. The refrain recurs and a child can say it back.
- No villain. The danger is cold, wind, distance, dark — and the temptation to *hoard*.
- Gentle but HONEST: the cold time is really cold, the road is really long, the coal really can go
  out. Never twee about it. Earn the warmth.
- African veld/bush/mountain — specific and dignified, never generic "jungle," never caricature.
- Sacred/initiation matter stays at the threshold: no ritual depicted. Fire is wonder, not rite.

## Spread plan (13 spreads)
1. Title spread — the cold time: the village wrapped in the blue-grey dark, no fire anywhere.
2. Night meeting — the people shiver; {{CHILD}} hears that fire lives far away with the Fire-Bird.
3. {{CHILD}} sets out at dawn; Mantis hops onto a shoulder: "I know the long road."
4. The long road — veld, kopje, river crossed; the mountain growing closer.
5. **HERO SPREAD.** The Fire-Bird on the old mountain, all ember and gold; one living coal offered
   into {{CHILD}}'s cupped hands, with the one promise: *carry it gently, and share it.*
6. The clay pot and the dry-grass nest — Mantis shows how to cradle the coal for the road home.
7. The wind — wants the coal; {{CHILD}} turns a shoulder to it, cups closer. (Refrain.)
8. The river / the cold dark — the light shrinks to a thread; {{CHILD}}'s slow breath keeps it. (Refrain.)
9. Home in sight at last — the village, the waiting people, the coal still a tiny live glow.
10. The giving — the temptation to hoard; instead {{CHILD}} touches coal to grass, fire to fire:
    one coal becomes many fires. The whole village warm and gold.
11. The first true night of fire — cooked food, light against the dark, faces, the first stories.
12. Morning — only grey ash left; {{CHILD}} thinks it's nothing; Mantis: *blow softly* — the coal
    still lives under the ash. The secret of ash revealed.
13. Closing image — banking the ash over the sleeping coal under the great starry night; new green
    grass at the ash's edge; the final refrain folds ash into the promise of tomorrow.

## Art direction (house-consistent)
- Warm, painterly storybook gouache/watercolour — soft visible brushwork, gentle edges, deeply
  tender, a little wondrous. Firelight palette: ember orange, honey gold, warm ochre against the
  deep blue-grey of the cold time and the indigo of the African night. The single coal/flame is
  the recurring point of warm light in almost every spread — the eye should always find it.
- {{CHILD}} is a Black / San-descended African child of the veld. Mantis is a real praying mantis,
  twiggy and characterful, never cartoon-bug-eyed. The Fire-Bird is glorious, not frightening.
- Every spread must read emotionally with the sound off — a tired parent at bedtime should *feel*
  it before reading a word. The African veld, kopje, river, and big sky are specific and dignified.

## Heritage note
This honours the San / Khoi family of fire-origin stories — among the oldest told on earth, around
the oldest hearths — in which fire is carried from a far keeper and ash is understood as the
fire's gentle keeper and the soil's feeder. Mantis (/Kaggen in San tradition) appears here only in
his warm role as a wise helper, with no sacred or ritual content depicted. The phrasing and story
are original to Arjuna Badger Press; the motif is the heritage being honoured.
