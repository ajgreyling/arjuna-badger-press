The real Great Serpent Mound, Adams County, Ohio: 1,300 feet of earthwork coiled atop a forested ridge, its head pointed at the summer-solstice sunset. The mound sits on the rim of a meteorite impact crater, the ground rolling and strange. The air: summer cicadas, the smell of warm grass, the hush before dusk. The site map: the serpent’s spiral tail, seven coils, the oval “egg” at the head, the overlook trail, the conical burial mounds nearby, and the visitor center (half-museum, half-gift shop, now threatened by the foundation’s planned “World Heritage Experience” complex). Geography of the chase: the crew’s battered van on the back roads, the river lowlands below, the foundation’s surveyors in pressed polos with drones and clipboards, the local’s quiet home on the reservation edge. Texture of wonder: the shadow of the coils at dusk, the sense of being watched by the land itself; the way the horizon and sky seem to bend at sunset. Living people: tourists, field-tripping schoolkids, local farmers, tribal elders who don’t come up the ridge but know the stories in their own ways; the adversary’s project managers, earnest and eager to “celebrate” and “preserve” but always with an eye on the brochure. The world is living, contested, and never a backdrop.
