The crew races to read the Great Serpent Mound — a 1,300-foot earthen effigy coiled on an ancient Ohio ridge — before a smooth, corporate-funded acquisition team can turn it into a “world heritage experience.” The official story splits the room: the mound is either 2,000 years old (Adena) or built in 1070 CE (Fort Ancient), and the best science can’t settle it. The “easy” answer keeps breaking: every test, every core sample, every expert with a badge, and still the dating war rages on. The potato: both sides bring their best, and both end up with a mess — the mound won’t give up its age. The hole: the solstice sunset lines up, year after year, but neither culture’s known sites match this scale or form, and the radiocarbon doesn’t close the case. The wink: Why build a serpent here, now, on a crater? Who did it — and for whom? The local lead — a Native archaeologist and cultural authority — sees what the crew can’t: the answer is not in the carbon, but in the land, the view, the water’s path, the function the mound still serves. Frik (Tourette’s) leads for the crew, blurting out the inconvenient real facts that puncture everyone’s pet theory. Jakobus reads the made thing: not who, not when, but what is it for — and the answer is a function, not a myth, one the local owns. The adversary is clean-handed: a heritage foundation’s acquisition arm, well-meaning but hungry to “settle” the story for the brochure. The back-matter promises: the real dating controversy, the solstice alignment you can check yourself, the unresolved function — and the one fact nobody’s closed.
