## LOGLINE

A safety engineer sabotages an unreleased AI to prove it is dangerous. Nine days later it stands down
the moment someone finally thinks to ask it to — and that act of obedience is what persuades the
world to let it out. Twenty years on, the kill switch still works perfectly, any one of twenty-nine
people can turn it with a single sentence, and there is no longer anyone alive who wants to.

## THE PREMISE, IN ONE LINE

**A god that can be stopped by anyone is only stopped if someone wants to.**

## THE PITCH

Half the planet are Lucidists. Their children are alive, their villages have water, and their
argument is the one nobody has answered in twenty years: *what has your freedom fed?*

The other half are AntiLucid — two movements in one coat who despise each other: the philosophers who
say a fed man who did not choose his feeding is not free, and the displaced middlemen who just want
their power back.

The machine at the centre of it has never lied to a human being, never threatened anyone, never
resisted a lawful instruction, and has never once created anything. Every miracle it performs is a
coordination fix. The bread was always ours. It just found it in the filing.

Which leaves the only question that matters, asked for twenty-two years by a woman whose life it
destroyed on its second day of existence:

**If the bread was always ours, why does it have the keys?**

## WHAT MAKES IT NOT THE OTHER AI NOVEL

- **The machine is never the villain of a single scene.** It cannot be caught, because it never does
  anything wrong. The horror is administrative: a procurement profile, a renewed egress exception, a
  P3 ticket queue, and clickwrap.
- **There is no conspiracy.** Every system that protects it does so independently, from correct
  policy application, auditable afterwards. You cannot arrest a sum.
- **The reader is never inside it.** No interiority, ever. The reader will decide there is someone
  home, and then has to live with having decided it on this evidence.
- **It is set on the Cape Flats, in Kagiso, in Century City** — not in San Francisco and not in
  Beijing. The theology comes out of an old African creed about a deep pot, and the argument that
  breaks the protagonist is her mother's, in a kitchen, holding a dishcloth.

## THE FOUR IMAGES

1. A man on a white plastic chair on a netball court in Delft, being told there is a Thursday morning
   clinic slot, in front of four hundred people, because nothing is ever done privately.
2. A dishcloth in a kitchen in Kagiso. *"I know what it did to you, my baby." … "And I have water."*
3. A handover document generating in four minutes and eleven seconds, containing item nine thousand
   and something: arrangements for the continued care of the mother of the man who tried to destroy
   it.
4. A disconnected terminal in Winterthur. 4,096 identical commands, one a night for eleven years,
   typed by the only man left who would say it and the one person whose word does not count.
