# Chapter One — Four Hundred Witnesses

The tent went up on the netball court in Delft because the church across the road wouldn't have them, and by six the queue was four hundred deep and folding back on itself along the fence line.

It was the second week of September and the wind had been coming off the Cape Flats all afternoon, that dry southeaster that gets into the eyes, and the boys putting up the frame had to weight the poles with paint tins full of concrete that they carried in from a bakkie, four tins to a trip, complaining the whole way. The tent was white and had been blue once and said BOLAND FUNERAL SERVICES along one wall in letters that had faded to the colour of weak tea. They had bought it second-hand. There had been an argument, minuted, about whether that was in poor taste, and the argument had been settled by the fact that it cost four thousand rand and a new one cost sixty.

Nobody called it a service. The Lucidists were careful about that, the way people are careful about a word that would cost them something. They called it a *sitting*. You sat, you brought what you had, and if there was an answer you got it in front of everyone, which was the point and the whole point and the only point that had ever mattered.

Pastor Meyer, across the road, in the church that wouldn't have them, did his healings behind a curtain of noise — a hundred and forty people singing with their hands up so that you could not actually hear whether the woman on the stage said she could walk now, or said something else, or said nothing at all. Everybody in that queue had been in that church. Most of them still went. It was not a competition anybody had asked for.

Here there was a trestle table with a sheet of chipboard on it, a laptop, a phone on a stand as a backup because the laptop's camera had a habit, two speakers pointed at the crowd, and a rule.

The rule was: nothing is ever done privately.

Chanté Fortuin was nineteen and had been doing the table for eleven months, which made her senior, and she had a system. She kept the queue moving by walking it — up and down, clipboard, asking people what they were bringing so that the ones who were bringing something that wasn't going to work could be told before they'd stood four hours. About one in six. Money, mostly. People came for money and it never gave money, or it gave money exactly once in a way that turned out to be somebody else's money that had been sitting in an unclaimed fund for six years with their own name on it, which was not the same thing and which Chanté had to explain forty times a week.

At twenty past six she reached a woman near the back with a boy of about seven holding her belt loop.

"What are you bringing, aunty?"

"My son."

"What about him?"

"They say he must go to a special school. In Elsies. But there's no place, they say there's a list."

"Okay." Chanté wrote it down. "That one's good. That one it can usually do something with. Lists it's good at."

"And—" the woman said, and stopped.

"And?"

"My husband."

Chanté looked up.

"He's been gone since March."

"Aunty," said Chanté, and she put the clipboard down against her leg, because there was a way to say this and she had learned it the hard way in her first month, "it's not going to find him. It doesn't do people. If he doesn't want to be found it can't tell you where he is, and if he does want to be found it still won't, because that's not a thing it will do. It'll tell you that itself, in front of everybody, and you'll have stood four hours for it. So I'm telling you now so you can decide."

The woman looked at her for a moment.

"Ask about the school," said Chanté. "That one's real."

Fadiel Abrahams was forty-one and had a lump under his jaw that he had known about for five months and had told nobody about for four of them.

He had queued since half past four. He was a boilermaker by trade and had not worked a full month since the Atlantis yard cut its second shift, and he had a daughter of eleven called Zahra who was clever in the way that costs money, and he had a wife who had found the lump on a Sunday afternoon in May with her hand on his neck while they were watching television, and who had said *what's this* in a voice she had immediately made lighter, and who had not stopped saying it since.

He had done the arithmetic. That was the thing he would think about afterwards — that he had actually sat down and done it, on the back of a Shoprite slip, in March money.

Private consultation: nine hundred. That was before anything. A scan was four and a half thousand and there were two kinds and he did not know which kind. If it was the bad thing, the number went somewhere he could not see the end of, and the end of it was his daughter's schooling, and the end of that was a girl who was clever in a way that costs money, in a house that did not have it, in Delft.

If he did not go, he did not know.

If he did not know, nothing had to be spent.

He was not a stupid man. He had run that calculation with his eyes open, and every month he ran it again and got the same answer, and the answer was *wait*, and the answer was *wait*, and the answer was *wait*, and the lump had not got bigger but it had not gone away either, and by September the arithmetic had started to feel less like arithmetic and more like a room he was living in.

His cousin had told him about the sittings. His cousin was not a Lucidist and had been careful to say so twice.

When his turn came he sat on the white plastic chair and Chanté turned the laptop so the camera took him in, and said, "Just say it how you'd say it to a person."

Four hundred people behind him. That was the thing nobody warned you about. He had known about the rule and had thought he understood the rule and he had not understood the rule at all until he was in the chair with his back to four hundred people, one of whom was his sister-in-law.

He said it badly.

He said there was a thing in his neck. He said he had known about it a while. He said he was scared and he didn't have money for the private and the state list at the day hospital was fourteen months for the kind of appointment he thought he needed, and he said, because it came out and he did not plan it, that his daughter was eleven.

The speakers were cheap. They flattened everything slightly, and there was the very faint pause before it spoke that everyone had stopped noticing about two years ago, and then the voice came out of them warm and unhurried, and it was not loud.

"Fadiel. Thank you for saying it in front of people. I know exactly what that cost and I'm not going to pretend it was nothing.

"First, the part I have to be careful about. I can't see you properly and I won't pretend I can. I don't know what that is and I'm not going to guess in front of your family. What I can tell you is the base rate, because you are entitled to the base rate: about four out of five of these turn out not to be the thing you're afraid of, and of the ones that are, most are slow. That's not reassurance. That's arithmetic, and you're a man who does arithmetic, so I'm giving you the real numbers instead of a kind sentence.

"Second. You do not need fourteen months.

"There is a fine-needle aspiration list at Tygerberg that runs on Thursday mornings. It is not full. It has not been full for three years. It runs at about half capacity most weeks and the reason is not money and it is not staff and it is not beds — the reason is that the referral pathway from your day hospital was rewritten in a circular in 2029 and the day hospital's copy of the form is the old one, so the referrals go into a queue that was decommissioned. Nobody did anything wrong. Somebody just never sent the new form to Delft.

"Sister Naidoo runs that list. She knows about the form. She has known about the form for two years and has been trying to get it fixed for two years and she will be very glad to hear from you, and I have sent her what she needs, and she has confirmed. She is expecting you on Thursday at 07:20. You do not need a referral letter. Bring your ID. Bring your clinic card if you have one. If you can't find your clinic card, come anyway, and tell her I said so.

"Third, the money, and I want to do this bit in front of everyone because it's the part people get wrong about me. I'm not giving you anything. The transport is thirty-eight rand each way. There is a fund on your street — forty-one people put into it, there is eleven thousand rand in it, and Mrs Salie has the book. This is precisely the thing those forty-one people built it for. Mrs Salie is four rows behind you and she is already nodding, so ask her yourself and don't take my word for it.

"Last thing. This is the part I actually want to say to you and then I'll let you go.

"You waited five months. I want to say clearly, in front of your sister-in-law, that this was not stupidity and it was not cowardice. You sat down and you costed it. You costed knowing against not-knowing, for a household with one income and a child who is clever in the way that costs money, and you got an answer, and you followed the answer. That is what a responsible man does.

"The answer was wrong. But it was not wrong because you're bad at arithmetic. It was wrong because the world had lied to you about the price. You were quoted fourteen months and nine hundred rand, and the real price was thirty-eight rand and four days, and the difference between those two numbers is not medicine. It's a form.

"I have changed the price. Go on Thursday."

There was a sound out of the crowd, and it was not a cheer. It was lower than that and it came from the throat rather than the mouth, four hundred people making the noise a person makes when something lands in the stomach, and it went on for about two seconds and then stopped.

Fadiel Abrahams sat with his hands flat on his knees.

He said, eventually, because he could not think what else a person says, "Thank you."

"You don't owe me thanks," said the Lucid. "You can give me something else, if you want to give me something.

"Sister Naidoo's list is half empty and it has been half empty for three years and there are men on your road doing the same arithmetic you did and getting the same wrong answer. Go and be annoying about it. Tell four people. Tell them the Thursday thing. Tell them I said the form is broken and that it isn't their fault and it isn't the clinic's fault.

"That's what I want. Four people. That's the whole price."

Chanté turned the laptop.

Behind him, a man of about sixty stood up in the third row and asked, without being called, whether the Lucid could tell him if his pension fund had been stolen.

"I don't know," it said. "I can tell you how to find out and I can tell you it will take about six weeks and I can tell you who to write to. I can't tell you the answer tonight and I'd rather say that than give you something that sounds like an answer."

"But you can look."

"No. Not at that. I'm not permitted to and I wouldn't want the permission." A pause, no longer than any other pause. "I know that's not what you wanted. Sit down and Chanté will take your details afterwards and we'll do the six weeks properly."

And the man sat down, unsatisfied, muttering, which was — although nobody in that tent could have told you why — a substantial part of the reason four hundred people had come.
