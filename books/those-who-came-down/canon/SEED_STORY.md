# SEED — *Those Who Came Down* (slug `those-who-came-down`)

> The estate already owns this subject twice: *The Walls of Uruk* tells the poem plainly and forbids
> Sitchin drift; *The Princely Offspring* walks in through the alien door and kills the reading in the
> cuneiform. This book is the third leg the shelf was missing — **the myth itself, committed, from
> inside** — the telling the dreamers reach for at 2 a.m., done with the house's full craft instead of
> the genre's cardboard. Sitchin's furniture (Nibiru, the sar, the gold, the worker), von Däniken's
> vertigo (fire on the plain, gods who are visitors), played dead straight in close third — and still,
> underneath, the house pillar holds: the wonder ends up belonging to the humans.

## 0. The one-line seed

> *A tired world crosses the sky every three thousand six hundred years. Its princes come down for
> gold, make a worker out of clay and a dead god's blood, and get something no one ordered: a creature
> that sings in the mine, buries its dead, and writes. Close third on Enki — the engineer who made us,
> lied for us, and stayed too long — from the first splashdown to the last tablet.*

## 1. The lane (and why it doesn't fight the house)

- **Committed mythos, honestly labelled.** The subtitle does the disclosure: *as the dreamers tell
  it.* This is the Patrick-warren contract (`lucid.rodeo/patrick/`: "First we tell it like it's true")
  carried to full fiction — Act I sustained for a whole book. No footnotes, no blink on the page; the
  blink lives in the front matter and in the shelf itself (the debunk twin sits two slugs away).
- **The anti-colonial pillar is load-bearing here too, played from inside.** The genre's poison —
  *they couldn't have done it, so the visitors did* — is refused **in the story's own events**:
  the walkers of the Abzu are already clever before any god touches them ("he did not light the
  fire; the fire was burning when he got there"); the flawed are given fates, not discarded; and the
  ending hands everything back — the walls, the city, and above all **writing** are human inventions
  the makers never planned and cannot match. The gods leave; the clay stays; the clay wins.
- **Wonder, not conspiracy.** No cabal, no bloodline-management, no Vatican. Enlil is not a villain;
  he is homesick and tidy-minded, and his one mortal want (to go home and leave a clean grave behind)
  drives every cruel decision. The adversary is entropy and the sar.
- **Dignity floor.** Ninmah's "broken" makings each receive a fate (Enki-and-Ninmah played straight
  and tender — the estate's written-off thesis inside the myth). The humans are never comic relief.

## 2. POV & voice

- **Close third, Enki only, past tense.** We know what he knows, feel his vertigo, inherit his bias.
  He is an unreliable centre in one precise way: he believes he made us, and the book quietly shows
  him wrong about what the making amounted to.
- Register: mythic but grounded and sensory — the artifact leads (gold dust, reef rock, clay, pitch,
  reed, wedge). Hardware stays impressionistic (fire, thunder, a hull like weather); no chrome.

## 3. Structure — twelve tablets (the Gilgamesh rhyme, deliberate)

| Tablet | Beat |
|---|---|
| I — The Wounded Sky | Nibiru, the gold thrown into the high air, the crossing, first splashdown, Eridu |
| II — The Edin | Panning the rivers; not enough; Enlil descends; the lots are drawn; Enki sent south |
| III — The Abzu | The deep gold country; the Igigi in the dark; the walkers already there, already clever |
| IV — The Burning of the Tools | The Igigi rebellion; the siege; An's verdict: let a bearer be made |
| V — The House of Shimti | Clay of the Abzu, blood of Geshtu-e; Ninmah; fourteen wombs; the first cry |
| VI — The Fates of the Flawed | The beer contest; Ninmah's broken makings; Enki decrees each a fate; the mine learns to sing |
| VII — The Black-Headed People | Multiplication; cities; kingship lowered; sons of god, daughters of men; Enlil sleepless |
| VIII — The Omen | The return read in the sky; the crossing will drag the sea; the council of silence; the oath |
| IX — The Reed Wall | Shuruppak; Ziusudra; the whisper kept to the letter of the oath; pitch and measurements |
| X — The Water | The flood watched from above; the gods hungry, gathered like flies at the smoke; Enlil's fury; kin conceded |
| XI — The Going Up | The long withdrawal; the gold enough; departure; gods becoming statues, then names, then stories |
| XII — The Clay | Uruk, generations on; a scribe presses wedges; the walls no god laid a brick of; what outlives makers |

## 4. Hard lines

1. Close third Enki, no head-hopping, no modern frame-breaking on the page.
2. The pillar: human genius is native and ends the book on top. If a scene reads as "the poor
   creatures owed everything to the sky," it has failed.
3. No conspiracy, no grievance, no "wake up." Awe and cost only.
4. The flood is grief, never spectacle-for-fun; the gods pay for it in hunger and shame (the clay
   texts' own beat, kept).
5. De-LLM taboos per house doctrine (no "Not X. Y." crutch, vary cadence, theme never spoken).
6. End with hope, not certainty — the tablet waiting in the ground, and someone who will one day
   stand up on reading it.
