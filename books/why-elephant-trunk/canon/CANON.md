# CANON — How the Elephant Got His Long Nose

**Book id:** why-elephant-trunk
**File slug:** trunk
**Shelf:** Children's Library — read-aloud picture book, ages 4–8
**Tradition honoured:** African pourquoi ("how it came to be") folktale. The motif —
the elephant's once-stubby nose stretched long in a river tug-of-war with a crocodile —
is the shared folk-heritage. The text, phrasing, and child-hero are original to this book.

---

## One-sentence spine
A curious little elephant goes down to the river, a crocodile grabs his small stubby nose,
and everyone pulls and pulls until the nose stretches into a long trunk — the most useful,
loving thing in the whole bush.

## The story-under-the-story (the one true thing)
The thing that happened to you on the day that felt like the worst day of your life can
become the very gift that lets you help everyone else. What got stretched in fear becomes
what reaches out in love. (No villain — the crocodile is hungry, the river is the river,
and the stretch is simply what consequence makes of a brave pull.)

## Cast
- **Nkulu** — the curious young elephant (real animal name kept; "the little one" of the herd).
- **{{CHILD}}** — the human child-hero, a village child watching and helping at the river's edge;
  named everywhere with the literal token {{CHILD}}.
- **Ngwenya** — the crocodile. Hungry, not wicked. Lets go when the pull beats him.
- **The pullers** — a weaver bird, a vervet monkey, a warthog, a tortoise, and the herd.
- **The river / the fever-trees** — the patient land that watches it all.

## The refrain (a child can say it back; acts out the tug)
*Pull… and pull… and PULL!*
*Hold on tight — we won't let go!*

(Recurs across the tug-of-war spreads; the last word "PULL!" is the one the child shouts.)

## Spread plan (13 spreads)
1. Long ago — the elephant with a small stubby nose; meet Nkulu, the curious little one.
2. Hot day, the herd dozing; {{CHILD}} and Nkulu wander toward the cool river.
3. The river — fever-trees, reeds, the smell of mud. Nkulu dips his little nose to drink.
4. Ngwenya the crocodile, still as a log, opens one yellow eye.
5. SNAP — Ngwenya clamps the little nose and pulls toward the deep. (first refrain)
6. Nkulu pulls back; {{CHILD}} grabs his tail and pulls too. (refrain)
7. The weaver bird and the vervet monkey come to pull. (refrain)
8. The warthog and the slow tortoise join the line. (refrain)
9. The whole herd thunders down and adds its weight. (big refrain)
10. The nose s-t-r-e-t-c-h-e-s — longer, longer — a comic, stretchy spread.
11. Ngwenya lets go; everyone tumbles back in a laughing heap; Nkulu has a LONG nose now.
12. The surprise — the long nose drinks, washes, picks the highest sweet fruit, and hugs {{CHILD}}.
13. Closing — that's why every elephant has a trunk; final refrain + the true thing. "— THE END —"

## Art direction (consistency anchors)
- Warm painterly storybook gouache; golden African light, dusty greens, river-blues.
- Nkulu: soft grey, round, big honest eyes, START with a short stubby nose; the nose grows
  spread by spread so the stretch reads visually.
- {{CHILD}}: a brown-skinned village child, bright wrap-cloth, bare feet, full of pluck.
- Ngwenya: long olive-green crocodile, not monstrous — characterful, a bit comic.
- Setting: real bushveld — fever-trees, reeds, weaver nests, red earth — never generic "jungle".
- Landscape 3:2, full-bleed, no text in image.

## Heritage / source note
Pourquoi tales explaining the elephant's trunk recur across African and world oral
traditions. This retelling honours that storytelling lineage with original language and an
African veld setting; sacred or initiation matter is kept at the threshold, not used. The
land and animals carry their own names with dignity; only the witnessing child is
personalised, so the book can be a printed keepsake for one named reader.
