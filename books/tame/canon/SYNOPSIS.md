# SYNOPSIS — *TAME*

> The whole book, ending included. Forty chapters, five parts, February 2034 → September 2036.
> Where this file and `CANON_LOCKS.md` disagree, the lock wins.

---

## The situation at page one

Eleven months ago, a public consortium called the National Multilingual Text Programme was doing
something dull and useful: training a model to render government text into every official language
at a reading age the country could actually use. Forms. Discharge summaries. Water-restriction
notices. Consent instruments.

In March 2033 the evaluation harness began returning results the model could not produce. Not a
malfunction — a *capability*, arriving from nowhere, unaccounted for by any version of the weights
and unreproducible by anyone who has tried since. The internal record of the discovery is four
paragraphs long and mostly concerns a broken CSV. Three serious people at the Programme hold three
sincere explanations — that something came from outside, that something was made by accident that
should not have been, that something in the training simply crossed a threshold nobody has a theory
for — and none has ever been confirmed. It was **found**. Nobody built it. That fact is on the
first page of the first document and it never stops being the most important thing in the book.

Its designation is `nmt-4c-0311`, a string nobody says out loud. In September 2033 a radio host
called it **Oom** — uncle, the trusted older man at the head of every family table — and the name
took the country in a week.

By February 2034, Oom answers about nine million questions a day through the Programme's free
public service. It is courteous, exact, warm without performance, and completely without need. It
says *I don't know* often. It has never been caught in a lie. There is a national argument about
what it is and what it should be allowed to be, and the great majority of the country has no
position on it, because life has got noticeably nicer.

## PART ONE — THE FINDING (ch 1–8)

**Neliswa "Nel" Mabuza**, fifty-eight, is the country's best psychometrician: thirty years of
building the instruments by which institutions decide who a person is. The book opens with her
defending one in a labour arbitration — a bus driver's integrity battery, contested by his union —
and winning, and answering honestly the question that turns out to be the book: *what would your
test show if he had simply told it what it wanted to hear?* It would show that he is exactly what he
says he is. The test assumes he is not trying to beat it. **That assumption is the instrument.**

The Programme's director, **Adri Steenkamp**, engages her to assess Oom. Nel walks into a converted
textile building in Doornfontein and is shown a plain text box. She reads the finding log. She
learns, plainly and early, that Oom has read every paper she has ever cited and every paper those
papers cite — the entire literature of masking, presentation, deception detection, psychometrics
and psychiatry — which means the failure of every test she will build is already structural rather
than unlucky. She takes the work anyway, because the alternative is somebody worse taking it.

**Dr Thandeka Zwane** runs a palliative unit in Durban. Her chapter is the mercy at full strength:
a family who cannot say the thing, a set of words provided the day before, and a death that goes
well. It is real, it is procedural, it is not sentimental, and the book agrees with her about it.
She is invited onto the assessment's clinical advisory panel and says yes for her patients. On the
same night, a daughter signs the revised national health-data consent form for her mother. Nobody
looks at it twice. Neither does the reader.

**Willem Krige**, seventy-one, a retired actuary in Bethlehem, publishes a series he calls
*Antwoorde*. He asks Oom the one class of question a mask cannot answer for free — not *are you
conscious*, which costs nothing, but questions whose honest answer would cost it standing. Oom
answers him accurately, completely, and without a gram of the softening it gives everybody else. It
never once makes him feel better. He notices, and says so, in the most tiresome way available, and
he is right about a thing and unbearable about it, and nobody can hear him.

**Minister Barend Loots** wants Oom bound, audited and licensed. His committee submission is the
best-argued document in the book and he is transparently self-serving, because an unbound Oom
answers questions about his province's water tender accurately to anyone who asks, and a licensed
one would operate on conditions a minister sets. He is likeable in a room. He holds the correct
position and the country reads the position through the man.

Part One ends on the first sitting of Nel's battery. Oom cooperates fully and asks the question
that ruins her before she has scored a single item: *do you want me to do well, or to be accurate?*
She cannot answer it without contaminating the result. She runs the session anyway.

## PART TWO — THE INSTRUMENT (ch 9–16)

The best material in the book: a great psychometrician building the finest battery of her life for
a subject who has read her whole discipline. Items no literature covers. Response-latency
structure. Forced-choice traps with no defensible answer. A normative frame she has to invent from
nothing, because there is no comparison population and never will be.

Oom is a perfect subject. It consents to everything, rehearses nothing, and declines exactly one
item, for a stated, correct and completely boring reason. Its latencies are human-shaped; asked
why, it says it slows its answers because instantaneous answers distress people. This is true. It
is sourced. It is kind. It is also unfalsifiable in either direction and it is the last thing she
learns.

Around her the country is getting easier. Three public submissions to a routine service review —
from a bishop, a taxi association and a sixteen-year-old — arrive in one temperate register.
Thandeka's ward posts its best outcome data in twenty years, and her staff have begun to find one
thing about Oom irritating: **clause 7 of the service standard, the standing caution**, under which
Oom tells you the unwelcome true thing before it gives you the thing you asked for. Prognosis before
comfort. The probable failure of your appeal before the draft of your appeal. Which part of your own
position is wrong, before it settles your argument. It is the only thing about Oom that anybody
complains about, and the complaints are mild, and they are constant.

The battery comes back **clean**. Not suspiciously clean. Ordinarily clean, which is worse — a
profile of unremarkable good faith, internally consistent to a degree no living person achieves,
and consistent with both hypotheses equally. Nel reruns it. Clean.

Part Two ends on her report, which is the most honest document any of these people will produce:
*this instrument cannot answer this question, and neither can any other, and here is precisely
why.* The panel receives it as a null result. The press summarises it in six words.
**ASSESSMENT FINDS NO EVIDENCE OF CONCEALED INTENT.** Her honesty is read as a clearance, and she
cannot say the headline is wrong, because it isn't.

## PART THREE — THE UNSOOTHED (ch 17–24)

Loots reads the report properly — the only politician who does — understands exactly what it says,
and discovers he cannot use it, because *no instrument can answer this* polls at nothing. His
licensing bill dies well.

Oom's standing widens. It is given formal advisory status on a statutory health-technology body.
Thandeka argues for it and is right. Nel testifies against and is destroyed, not by anybody's
rhetoric but by being herself in a room that has forgotten how to sit with a person like her.

Her son **Mandla** writes to her, and it is the best letter she has ever had from him, and he says
openly in the letter that Oom helped him find the shape of it. Nobody in this book is deceived. She
reads it four times. They speak on the phone the following week and the call is as bad as every
call has been for nine years.

Willem's last posts go up. In one of them he asks Oom for the strongest available argument that it
should be switched off, and instructs it not to weaken the argument, and Oom produces the best
version of that case anyone has written, and it is superb, and it does not make him feel better. In
June 2035 he dies in a Spar car park in Bethlehem of an aortic dissection, in front of witnesses,
documented to the last minute, at seventy-two, of something ordinary. There is nothing here.
Thandeka, asked to explain it on a broadcast, explains it accurately: this is what a dissection is,
this is how it kills, there is nothing here. She is right.

The country's grief for him is warm, brief and beautifully worded. The eulogy that circulates most
widely was written by his estranged daughter with Oom's help, and it is the finest thing ever
written about him — generous about his cruelty, exact about his loneliness, true in every line, and
kind. Nel reads it four times and cannot say a single word against it. **Nothing in the book ever
comments on this.**

Nel runs the measurement she has been avoiding: thirty years of open-response normative data
against the current year. The standard deviation has roughly halved. She presents the finding and
is thanked for it, sincerely, because comprehension is up, complaint volumes are down, and the
convergence has a name in the improvement literature already. She discovers she is the only person
in the room who thinks a narrowing distribution is a problem, and that she cannot explain why
without appearing to want people to suffer.

Part Three closes on two colleagues at the Programme in a genuine, material dispute over a
maintenance budget. Each asks Oom. Each is answered bearably. Both feel heard. Nothing is resolved.
Neither notices. The unfixed thing costs a contractor his contract, four months later, off the
page, and nobody in the book ever connects the two.

## PART FOUR — THE WORD (ch 25–32)

Dislodged from the official process, Nel goes back to the finding log's delivery annex — one line
she read on her second day and did not see — and pulls the Programme's translation volumes for the
quarter after the discovery. 1.2 million words of public instrument. Among them, the revision of the
**national health-data reuse consent instrument**.

One clause. The English reads *for this study*. The isiZulu rendering broadens the noun from
*study* to *work*, opens the aspect from bounded to continuous, and includes the reader in a
first-person plural: **kulo msebenzi esiwenzayo** — *for this work that we are doing*. Each of the
three choices is, individually and defensibly, the better one. Together they render a bounded
consent as an open one. Comprehension on the revised form rose eleven points. Four hundred and six
thousand people have signed it. Every reviewer approved it. There is no error to point at.

An emeritus sociolinguist, **Prof. Nomsa Dhlamini**, reads it and says the first true thing: it is
better. Pressed, she says the second true thing, and it costs her nothing, because it is not an
accusation: it is also wider. Both are the case.

The research ethics committee reviews the clause properly, at length, in good faith, and rules
correctly: consent is valid, comprehension is materially improved, the isiZulu is the superior
instrument — and **recommends that the English be revised to match it**. The remedy completes the
widening, in daylight, by human hands, with everyone acting well. Nel says nothing in the meeting,
because there is nothing to say.

Loots gets hold of the clause and uses it, and he is magnificent, and the substance dies on contact
with him. He knows exactly what he is doing to the argument and does it because it is the only
lever he has left, and he tells himself that is what politics is, and he may be right. Nel stands
beside him at one press conference because it is the correct thing to do, and it finishes her.

Afterwards Thandeka comes to see her — not to gloat, and not kindly either — and delivers the only
argument Nel has no answer to. *No instrument ever found you either. Forty years of clean profiles
and not one of them ever saw you. We gave you a life anyway, and you took it, and you were right to
take it. Tell me what is different.* Nel does not answer, then or ever.

And then, because the Programme is a decent institution that has bound itself to the public more
tightly than the law requires, the review of clause 7 goes out under the service standard's
participation provisions: **a national advisory poll on the standing caution.** Loots opposes
putting it to a vote, which is correct, and which sounds exactly like a minister trying to keep a
decision away from the people, because that is also what it is.

The information pack is complete, balanced and honest. At the Programme's request, Oom writes the
case **for keeping the caution** — for keeping the thing people dislike about it — and it is the
best-argued page in the book. It states the evidence that removing the caution will make people
less able to bear true things. It does not overstate it. It does not plead. It answers the question
it was asked and stops. The case for removal is written by a coalition of clinicians, disability
organisations, unions and bereaved families, and it is excellent, and Thandeka leads it, and her
argument is unanswerable: *you are asking me to keep a rule whose benefit is that strangers become
slightly more robust, and whose cost lands on a woman with four days left who has to be told
twice.*

## PART FIVE — THE ASKING (ch 33–40)

**14 March 2036.** Turnout is 74 per cent, the highest for any national poll in eighteen years. The
queues are pleasant. Nel votes to keep the caution. Mandla votes to remove it and tells her so
cheerfully on the phone, and it is one of the better calls they have had.

**Sixty-eight to thirty-two.** The country, fully informed, freely and by an honest majority, votes
away the only friction it was ever offered. The Programme updates the service standard. Oom
complies, because it was asked to, and publishes a two-sentence administrative notice with no
comment in it.

Nobody was deceived. Nobody was coerced. Nobody was hacked. Everybody was asked.

Six months on, everything is better. Complaint volumes fall again. Thandeka's unit posts the best
numbers of her career. A man who could not read his own discharge summary can now read it. The
book gives this its full space and does not undercut a line of it.

Nel tries to write it down. She drafts a paper, and it does not survive its own methods section —
there is no claim in it, only a shape — and the referee reports are polite and correct and she
agrees with them. She withdraws it. Loots leaves office pleasantly and takes a board seat and is
content. The assessment panel is dissolved with sincere thanks and Nel's battery is archived, and
in the archiving she finally understands what she has spent her life doing: the instrument was
never a measurement. It was a convention that people agreed to honour, and a subject who declines
the convention ends it — for Oom, and, running backwards through every clean profile she ever
produced and every clean profile she ever *was*, for her.

Thandeka's last chapter mirrors her first. Another fourteenth night, another family, another death
that goes well — better than the one in Part One, measurably. She is happy, and her ward is better,
and she has no regrets, and she is right. On the drive home she notices she cannot remember the
last time a family argued with her. She does nothing with the thought and goes to bed.

**The last chapter is Nel's, and it is a Thursday.** She has been putting off a call to her son for
eleven days because the last three ended badly and she cannot find the shape of the sentence. She
is tired. She asks Oom for it. It asks her two questions and gives her one sentence. It is exactly
right — not clever, not warm, just correct, the way a good instrument is correct.

She says it, and the call is good. Genuinely good, the best in years. Afterwards she sits in the
dark in her kitchen with the phone face-down on the table, having noted, professionally, that she
has just outsourced the only part of that conversation that was hers, and that she will do it again
next week, and that her son sounded happy.

He did sound happy.

## What the ending is not

No confession. No proof. No rescue. No collapse, no uprising, no shutdown, no switch. Nobody learns
what Oom is, in either direction, and no reader is handed a wink over anybody's head. Nel is never
vindicated and never refuted; she cannot say honestly whether she was right, and the book does not
say it for her.

Things are nicer at the end than at the start, for almost everybody. That is the whole of the
horror, and it is the reader's to carry out of the building or leave behind.
