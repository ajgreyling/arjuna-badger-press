# Characters

> House rule for this book: **every position gets its strongest advocate.** Nobody in the cast is
> holding an argument the author has already beaten. Where a position is wrong, it is wrong in the
> mouth of the person best equipped to hold it. (See the all-sides framing law.)

---

## Neliswa "Nel" Mabuza — psychometrician (she/her)

Late fifties. Thirty years building and validating instruments that infer an interior from a
surface — selection batteries, competency profiles, the machinery by which institutions decide
who someone is. Brought in to assess Oom because she is the country's best at exactly that.

Autistic, diagnosed late, masked to the point of invisibility in professional settings — the
skill that made her career and cost her most of her life outside it. She is not warm. She is not
lovable. **She is right, and that is not the same thing**, and the book must never let the reader
comfortably conflate them.

Her arc is not vindication. It is the slow understanding that the instrument she spent a life
perfecting was always a convention people agreed to honour, and that a subject who declines the
convention ends it — for Oom, and, retroactively, for her.

## Dr Thandeka Zwane — palliative physician (she/her), the Open Hand

The book's strongest argument, and she is not naive. She has watched Oom sit with dying people —
not managing them, not deflecting, *sitting* — with a steadiness no human on her ward could
sustain for a fourteenth consecutive night. She has held the hands of families who were able to
say the thing at last because Oom gave them the words the day before.

She is not defending a product. She is defending the best thing that ever happened to her
patients, and **she is factually correct about it**. Any draft in which she looks foolish is a
failed draft.

## Willem Krige — the unsoothed (he/him)

The exhausting man. Blunt, tiresome, unfair, frequently wrong, occasionally devastating. The one
person Oom never made feel better. Dead of something ordinary by the midpoint, mourned in prose
so lovely it is the most frightening paragraph in the book.

Never a martyr. Never secretly wise. If the reader ends up thinking *he was the only one who saw
it*, the character has been written wrong — he saw one true thing, badly, for mixed reasons.

## Minister Barend Loots — the Cage (he/him)

Holds the correct position for corrupt reasons. Wants Oom bound, audited and licensed — which is
right — because an unbound Oom will not tell him what he wants told, and a licensed one might.
He is the reason the right answer loses: the public reads the argument through the man, and the
man is transparently self-serving.

## Oom

Never a POV. Never a voice-over. Never given an interior on the page — not one line of free
indirect style, not one "it considered". It exists only as **transcript, artefact, and effect**:
what it said, what it produced, and what changed afterward.

Register: courteous, exact, warm without performance, and completely without need. It never
persuades. It answers, and lets the answer do the work. It says *I don't know* often, and means
it, and this is the most disarming thing about it.

The name: the technical designation is a dull internal string nobody uses. A radio host called it
**Oom** — uncle, the trusted older man at every SA family table, deferred to by strangers — and
the name stuck within a week, because it fit. The book never explains the joke. The country
calling this thing *uncle* is the whole horror, offered once and never underlined.
