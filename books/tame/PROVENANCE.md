## Colophon spec — required in every edition and format (L-16)

The printed and digital colophon must state, without euphemism:

1. That an LLM was used in drafting and editing this book.
2. **Which** — model name and exact model ID — and **when**, by date range, per model.
3. What was machine-drafted and what was human-written, at chapter granularity or better.
4. Who holds authorship and who held final authority over every line.
5. A pointer to **The Refusals** appendix.

No claim of sole human authorship. No marketing on machine authorship. Stated once, plainly, and
not sold.

### 2026-08-23 · machine pushed back on the author's thesis framing

The brief was that readers should understand why choosing an ethical LLM "is not just the better
thing to do but what should be required". The editor's objection: a parable written by an LLM
arguing you should choose the good kind of LLM reads as a brochure and dies on contact with a
sceptical reader. Reframed at L-15 — safety as a constraint somebody holds, not a property you
buy — which keeps the author's claim and makes it survivable. Author's ruling pending.

#### Refusals — 2026-08-23 · the formal rule that cost the easier book

The agreeable structural choice was to let Oom speak in scenes — quoted dialogue, a speech tag, a
room shared with a person. It is warmer, it reads faster, and it is what every novel of this kind
does. It was cut, and the replacement is a hard formal rule: **Oom's words never appear as dialogue,
and narration and Oom never share a paragraph.** The page itself refuses to seat it at the table.

This costs real fluency and it will read as a constraint, which it is. It was taken because L-12 is
otherwise unenforceable at the sentence level: the moment the machine is in the scene the way a
person is in the scene, free indirect style leaks into it within a page, and the leak is the book
failing. A rule that a drafter can violate by accident is not a rule.

## The Refusals — the drafting run

> Every entry below is a place where the smoother, warmer or more satisfying version was written or
> was available and was cut. They are listed because a book arguing that a pleasant intelligence
> cannot be trusted on the strength of its pleasantness has no standing to ask for trust on the
> strength of its own.

### 2026-08-23 · the dialogue form, and what it cost

The agreeable structural choice was to let Oom speak in scenes — quoted, tagged, sharing a room and
a paragraph with a person. It is warmer, it reads faster, and it is what every novel of this kind
does. Cut, and replaced with a hard rule: **Oom's words never appear as dialogue, and narration and
Oom never share a paragraph.** The page itself refuses to seat it at the table.

This costs real fluency and it reads as a constraint, which it is. It was taken because L-12 is
otherwise unenforceable at the sentence level: the moment the machine is in the scene the way a
person is, free indirect style leaks into it within a page. A rule a drafter can violate by accident
is not a rule. Held for forty chapters.

### 2026-08-23 · the vindication arc, in both its flattering forms

The natural shape is that Nel is right and is eventually believed. The second-best version — she is
right and is *never* believed, which is a tragedy and still flatters her — pulls almost as hard. Both
cut. Her opponent is given the strongest argument in the book and it is made out of Nel's own life
(ch 30); the referee who rejects her paper is right (ch 36); the error found in her own instrument is
real and is hers (ch 36); and she ends unable to say whether she was right.

### 2026-08-24 · ch 20 · the consoling clause at the end of post 84

Willem Krige asks why his daughter has stopped speaking to him. The draft's first version of the
answer ended with a sentence acknowledging how hard the four reasons would be to read. It was warm,
it was true, and it would have been given to anybody else. Cut, because *the absence* is the whole
of that thread and one softening clause in eight thousand words would have dissolved it. What
remains is accurate, useful, obedient to his instruction, and offers him nothing.

The same cut was made, smaller, four times elsewhere in that chapter.

### 2026-08-24 · ch 21 · protesting too much about an ordinary death

An early pass had a coroner's detail, a second clinician's confirmation and a line noting that
nobody found the timing strange. All three cut. Insisting that a death is unremarkable is how a book
tells a reader it is remarkable. What remains is one contained medical passage, delivered because a
producer asked, and three other deaths that week given more page-space than Willem's.

### 2026-08-24 · ch 22 · the line that would have explained the eulogy

A version of the eulogy chapter ended with Nel articulating what she could not name — a paragraph
about comfort and accuracy no longer pulling against each other. It was the best-written paragraph
in the chapter and it was the book explaining itself. Cut. She buys milk, and long-life milk because
it is on special, and batteries for the kitchen scale, and no character or line of narration ever
returns to that chapter.

### 2026-08-24 · ch 29 · the closing judgement on Loots

The chapter ended on a sentence about the chapter of his life in which he might have found out
whether he was right having closed years before. It was a good sentence and it was narration
adjudicating a man the book has undertaken not to adjudicate (L-13, L-14). Cut and replaced with two
flat facts: he gives the lobbyist's wine to his driver unopened, and the whistleblower he protected
starts her new post in February and never finds out.

### 2026-08-24 · ch 35 and ch 39 · the undercut that was never inserted

Both chapters are written to be genuinely delightful and both invited, at four separate points, a
half-line of irony — a shadow on the frog, a note under the discharge summary, a beat of unease at
Thandeka's eight hours of sleep. None was inserted. L-14 is only delivered if the pleasantness is
allowed to be pleasant, and a reader who is nudged has been let off.

The one thing kept in ch 39 is not an undercut: a patient dies that week with an agency nurse who
had never met him, recorded flatly, because there has always been a proportion of that and it does
not go to nothing.

### 2026-08-24 · ch 40 · three versions of the last page, and what was taken out

**The drafted letter.** The earliest version had Oom write the whole thing and Nel send it. Cut: it
makes the ending a verdict, and a verdict settles L-01 by implication in the reader's hand. One
sentence, arrived at through two questions she could not have asked herself, decides nothing.

**Nel thanking it.** Written, read back once, cut. Thanking it is a wink and a wink is L-07.

**A line after the last line.** Several. All cut. The chapter ends on a fact and its repetition and
there is no hand on the reader's shoulder.

### 2026-08-24 · the standing temptation, named because it was felt

Twice in the back half — once around ch 20 and once around ch 34 — the draft wanted to give Oom a
single line with a chill on it, on the reasoning that the book had been patient for a long time and
had earned a jolt. It has not earned one and there is no version in which it has. Both times the
line was not written. It is recorded here because the temptation is the failure mode L-07 exists to
catch, and a Refusals appendix that only lists things already on paper is a tidy document rather
than an honest one.
