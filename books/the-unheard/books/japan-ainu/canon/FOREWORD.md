# Foreword

### *by Conrad Heildi* †

I lived most of my life as a foreigner in Japan, which is the finest vantage point on earth for the one thing this book is about: the difference between a country's account of itself and the truth that account is built to hide. The foreigner sees it because he was never taught not to. He notices the seam the native has been trained, from birth and for kindness, to look politely past.

This book notices the seam. It notices all of them. And it does so with a tact and an affection that I, who spent fifty years learning that tact, did not expect from a writer coming to Japan from the outside and the south.

What he has understood is the thing that took me decades. It is this: that the most beautiful national stories are very often the most recently *invented* — that "the way of the warrior," the timeless code, the ancient and unchanging spirit, is frequently a thing assembled in living memory, polished to look old, and then sincerely believed by everyone including the polishers. The book takes one such invention and turns it gently over in the light until you can see the join, the maker's mark, the date — and it does this not to debunk, not to sneer, but to perform a far more difficult and loving act: to ask who was *left out* when the beautiful story was written, and what it cost them to be the part the story paints over.

For that is the real subject, and it is the bravest one a book about Japan can choose. Not the Japan of the brochure — the cherry blossom, the bullet train, the serene and ancient harmony — but the hidden Japan, the people the celebrated story required to be invisible so that it could remain beautiful. The author has gone looking for the painted-over hands, and has found them, and has given them back their faces, and he has done it without a single false note of pity, which is the one note that would have ruined everything. He does not pity these people. He *respects* them, which is rarer and harder and the only thing they ever actually wanted.

And here is the grace of it: he lets the locals out-see the outsiders. The travellers who come into this story, clever and well-meaning and sure they understand, are gently and repeatedly shown that the person who actually knows is the one they were not looking at — the keeper of the old craft, the bearer of the unfashionable name, the one whom the official Japan would prefer the foreign visitor not to meet. I spent my life learning to be quiet enough to be taught by such people. The author has built a whole book out of that quiet, and it taught me things, at the end of my life, about a country I had presumed to know.

A note on the prose, because it is a particular pleasure. It is restrained in exactly the Japanese way — it trusts the space, the unsaid, the single detail allowed to carry the weight a lesser writer would spread across a paragraph. He has learned the aesthetic he is writing about, and writes in its key, so that the form of the book and its subject rhyme. That is a quiet virtuosity, and I admired it the way one admires a perfectly set stone in a garden: you could almost miss the labour, and missing it is the point.

Go slowly. This is a book that rewards the reader who can sit still, who can attend to the thing the official story hurried past. It is about a way that was invented, and about the people the inventing erased, and it gives them, at last, the one thing that was always owed them and never paid: to be *seen.*

Begin, and keep your eyes on whoever the story tells you not to watch.

*— Conrad Heildi*
