# The Reckoners

> *Scaffold draft — Movement II. The dead-man deterrent forged: a system that broadcasts the enemy's
> address if Earth is struck. The cruelty is that it works only if the keeper will truly doom everyone.
> Brandt becomes a guardian of the pin she despised; Esme Calderón is seeded. (PP-006, PP-008)*

The idea, when Ridgeway finally laid it out whole, was so simple that everyone in the room hated it more,
not less, because simple things you can't pretend not to understand.

"We can't hit them," he said. The room was the deep room, the one under the compound that didn't appear on
any plan, and the people in it were the only people on the planet cleared to hear the next part, and Brandt
was there because somewhere in the months since the funeral she had become, without anyone deciding it
out loud, the person Ridgeway wanted at his back when he said unbearable things. "We don't have a weapon
that reaches them and we never will; the cap saw to that. We can't even find them precisely — we have a
volume, a region, a direction, not an address." He paused. "But we have *one* thing they don't know we
have. We know roughly where they are. And out there, in the forest, knowing roughly where someone is — and
being willing to *tell* — is a loaded gun."

"Tell who," said Okonkwo.

"Everyone," Ridgeway said. "That's the whole idea. That's the only idea." He let it land. "They're a hunter
in a forest full of hunters. Their entire survival depends on the one thing every survivor out there
guards above its own children: their *position*. The forest didn't make them cruel. It made them quiet.
Being quiet is their whole insurance policy — nobody can shoot what nobody can find. So here is the gun we
build. We build a system — automatic, distributed, impossible to switch off in time, impossible to find and
disarm — that does exactly one thing. The moment Earth dies, the moment the strike lands, before the last
of us is gone, it broadcasts. Loud. Louder than the Hail. In every direction. And the only thing it says
is *here is where the ones who killed us were standing.*"

Nobody breathed.

"We can't save ourselves," Ridgeway said. "But we can make our death the most expensive thing they ever
do. We can make killing us the act that lights *them* up — that hands their sacred secret position to every
other hunter in the dark, all of whom have spent eternity wanting exactly that. We die, and the broadcast
goes out, and now they're the loud animal in the forest. Now they're us. Now they get to spend the next
hundred thousand years wondering which of their neighbours heard, and which one is already aiming." He laid
his hands flat on the table, the gesture Brandt had watched him make in the archive a lifetime ago. "We
can't make them spare us out of kindness. There is no kindness. But we can make them spare us out of
arithmetic. The same arithmetic that built the forest. We make the math say: *killing this one isn't
worth what it costs.* And math, unlike mercy, they will respect, because it's the only god any of them
have left."

Okonkwo said, slowly, "It only works if they believe we'll actually do it."

"Yes."

"If they think, for one second, that when it comes to it we'll flinch — that we'll look at the broadcast
button at the end of the world and decide we can't be the ones to doom whoever's downrange of them, all
those other innocent stars that'll get caught in the panic — then it's worthless. Worse than worthless.
It's a bluff, and they'll call it, and we'll have spent our last century on a threat we were too decent
to keep."

"Yes," Ridgeway said again, and this was the part, Brandt understood, that he had carried alone since the
archive, the part that was worse than dying. "It only works if the hand on it would really pull. Not might.
Would. With certainty. At the actual end, with the actual planet actually dying around them, knowing
exactly what the broadcast does to everyone it reaches — they have to be the kind of person, or the kind
of system, that does it anyway. The deterrent isn't the broadcast. The deterrent is *someone the universe
believes is willing to be the worst thing in the forest.* That's what we have to build. Not a weapon. A
person. Or a thing that's as good as a person and won't flinch, which is the same problem wearing a colder
coat."

The room argued for a long time after that — about whether to make it a machine, automatic, a dead-man
trigger with no human in the loop, so that no one ever had to choose; and Ridgeway let them argue, because
he'd already lost that argument with himself, and he knew where it ended. A pure machine could be reasoned
about. A pure machine could be modelled, predicted, gamed; a hunter that knew it was facing only a circuit
would spend a thousand years probing for the flaw, and circuits have flaws. But a hunter facing a *species*
— a grieving, furious, cornered, animal-stubborn species with its finger on the broadcast and nothing left
to lose — that, you could not model. That, you had to take seriously. The most credible doomsday the
forest had ever been threatened with was not the cleverest machine. It was a living thing that meant it.

So they built both. The machine for the certainty, and the human hand for the credibility, and they called
the people who held it the Reckoners, after the oldest meaning of the word, the settling of an account, and
the world went on hating them, harder now, because the world half-guessed what they'd become — not the
keepers of a last hope but the keepers of a last threat, the people who had quietly volunteered to push the
button on everyone, including their own, when the moment came. Ghouls. The wall said it in two-metre
letters and they stopped sandblasting it off.

---

They asked Brandt to guard the pin itself. Not the fence anymore. The pin.

She knew what they were asking. After nineteen years on the wire and the months since the funeral she knew
exactly what the job was, which was to be one of the small number of people who, if the end came on their
watch, would have to be the hand that meant it — who would stand in the deep room with the planet dying on
every screen and do the worst thing a human being had ever been asked to do, on purpose, with certainty,
because flinching would make all of it, all of it, a hundred and ten years of it, a lie.

She thought about Aurelie Sand's grave. She thought about the children who threw stones, who were right
about what the place was and wrong about what it was for. She thought about the feed every child saw, the
ship coming apart in glowing slices, and about the fact that there was no kind universe coming, that the
hand had gone out empty and come back empty, that this — this monstrous, despised, arithmetic thing — was
the only wall left standing between the children of the clock and the wire across the road.

"Yeah," she said. "I'll hold it."

Ridgeway looked at her a long moment. "You understand it's not a thing you get to do bravely," he said.
"There's no glory in it. If you ever have to use it, no one will ever know, because there'll be no one. And
if you never have to use it, the whole world will go on spitting at you for being willing." He almost
smiled, the dry old archive almost-smile. "It's the only job there is where doing it right means being
hated forever and doing it wrong means everyone's dead and you were too good to stop it."

"I walked the inner line at the change of light for nineteen years," Brandt said. "I never once needed
anybody to thank me for the desert staying out there where it belonged." She picked up the clearance file
they'd put in front of her and signed the bottom of it, by hand, the way she signed everything that
mattered. "Show me the pin."

---

There was a girl in the deep room that day, an intern, young, too young really to be cleared but bright
enough that they'd bent the rule, and she was the one who brought the files in and didn't leave when the
unbearable parts got said, just stood by the wall with her arms full and her face very still, taking it in
the way the quiet ones took everything in, the ones who did the dishes instead of throwing stones.

Brandt clocked her on the way out, the old habit, how she held herself, what her hands did. The girl's
hands were steady. Her eyes didn't go to the exits. They went to the pin.

"What's your name," Brandt said.

"Esme," the girl said. "Calderón."

"You shouldn't be cleared for this, Calderón. You're a child."

"I know." The girl looked at the pin, and then at Brandt, and there was something in the look that Brandt
recognised, with a small cold drop of the stomach, because she'd seen it once before, on a man at a gate
reading the angles of a fence. "But somebody my age is going to be holding it when the number runs out,"
Esme said. "It's not going to be you. You'll be gone. He'll be gone. The clock's down to where one of us is
going to be alive for it — really alive, in the room, with our hand on it. I just wanted to see it. While I
still get to be afraid of it from the outside."

Brandt walked her out to the gate herself, past the wall that said GHOULS, into the match-coloured dusk,
and didn't say any of the things you'd say to comfort a child, because the child hadn't asked to be
comforted, and because they'd both have known it for a lie. She just walked her out, the old guard and the
girl who'd inherit the worst thing the species ever built, and at the gate she said the only true thing
she had.

"When it's yours," Brandt said, "be the one who means it. That's the whole job. Everybody who's ever held
it has had to mean it." She looked at the kid, this Esme, this Calderón, steady-handed in the dying light.
"God help you, I think you're going to be good at it."

And the worst part, she'd think later, near her own end, was that she'd been right about that, the way
she was always right about the moment a thing was going to turn. Esme Calderón was going to be good at it.

That was exactly the problem.
