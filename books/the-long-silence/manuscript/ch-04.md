# Meridian Reach

> *Scaffold draft — Movement I. We meet the ship and her people, quickly and warmly, because we are about
> to lose them. The chapter ends a breath before the cut.*

The *Meridian Reach* was not a warship and not a marvel. She was a working hull, forty years old, a
medium-haul tender that ran the long quiet road between the inner stations and the deep relay the array
kept out past the orbit of the cold planets, and she ran it the way a bus runs a route — on a schedule,
with the same corners, carrying the same dull freight of people and parts and the small human cargo that
keeps any frontier from going feral: mail, in the old sense; coffee; a violin somebody's daughter had
asked for; a box of seeds labelled in three languages; the relay crew's replacements, and the relay
crew's relief, going home.

She carried forty-one souls that run, which her captain knew because she counted them every departure, by
hand, off a paper list, because she did not trust a screen with a number that mattered and a screen had
once, early in her career, lost her a man for six hours by rounding him off. Her name was Imani Sowande
and she had run this road for eleven years and she counted her people by hand, forty-one, and signed the
bottom of the list, and that was the ship leaving.

It is important to know a few of them, because nobody else ever bothered to, after.

There was a kid named Petar on his first deep run, nineteen, so seasick the first day on the long
acceleration that the older hands had bet on the hour he'd give up and ask to be put off at the inner
station, and he hadn't asked, he'd just gone green and gripped the rail and done his shift, and by the
third day he had his colour back and a kind of stunned grin every time he looked out at the dark, the
grin of someone who has just understood that the thing he signed up for is real and is *enormous* and is,
incredibly, his now too. The older hands stopped betting against him. One of them, a woman named Reyes —
no relation she knew of to the Ada Reyes whose unread warning was at that moment three hundred and forty
years from its zero and filed in a building the kid had never heard of — started saving him the good seat
at mess, the one by the port where you could see the relay's beacon once you got close, a steady green
star that meant you were nearly there.

There was the cook, Olufemi, who hated his job and was magnificent at it, who fed forty-one people out of
a galley the size of a closet and complained the entire time and would have walked into vacuum for any one
of them. There was the relay engineer going out, Tan, who had a violin in the hold for his daughter and a
list in his head of the things he'd say to her in eight years when his rotation ended and he came home for
good, and who practised the saying of them, quietly, in his bunk, getting them right. There was the relay
chief coming home, Massri, who had done four rotations and was done, properly done, who had stopped
dreaming about the relay and started dreaming about a garden, a small wet green ordinary garden on the
mountain near the array, and who lay in his bunk on the long road in and let himself, for the first time
in eight years, believe in the garden.

They were not remarkable people. That was the thing the relay watch could never make anyone understand,
afterward, in the flat language that was all there was afterward. They were a bus full of ordinary people
on a route, carrying a violin and a box of seeds and a kid's first astonishment, and they were three days
from the green star, and they were, every one of them, exactly as alive as you are.

---

Weeks before, in the dark along that road, the snow had done its work.

Nobody saw it. There was no one to see it; that was the point of the road, that it was empty, that ships
had run it for thirty years and met nothing because there was nothing to meet. The microprobes came in
off the long fall from the outer dark, too small to flag and too many to track even if anyone had been
tracking, and they did the one simple thing they had crossed the void to do. Two of them, working as a
pair, anchored themselves a precise distance apart across the road — across the exact volume of space the
*Meridian Reach* would occupy at a specific second of a specific day, the way you lead a bird — and
between them they drew out a single thread.

The thread was carbon, one molecule wide, and it was longer than a mountain and thinner than a question,
and it could not be seen by any instrument the ship carried because there was nothing of it to see; it
turned no light, cast no shadow, returned no ping. It was not a wall. It was not a mine. It was a line, a
geometry, a piece of pure intention drawn across an empty road and pulled taut and left, with the
patience of a thing that has done this before, to wait for a working hull on a schedule to keep its
corner.

It had no malice in it. This is the hardest thing to hold and the truest, the thing Ada Reyes had
understood from the data and never managed to make a committee feel: there was no hatred anywhere in it.
The thread did not want anything. The hunter that strung it did not hate the *Meridian Reach*, had never
heard of Imani Sowande or her paper list or the kid by the port or the violin or the garden on the
mountain. It had heard a loud animal in the dark, long ago, and it had reached ahead of itself, lightly,
to be sure — and being sure, for a hunter in a forest full of hunters, looked exactly like this: a line
drawn across a road, costing almost nothing, keeping its appointment, while the hunter that drew it had
long since turned its attention elsewhere, to other roads, other loud and frightened things, because the
forest was wide and the work was never done.

---

On the relay, the green beacon turned, as it had turned for thirty years.

On the *Meridian Reach*, Olufemi was swearing at a pot. Tan was getting a sentence right. Massri was in
his garden, behind his eyes. The captain had counted her people that morning out of habit, off the paper
list, forty-one, and signed nothing because there was nothing to sign, the ship was already underway.
Petar had the good seat at mess, and through the port the green star had grown, in three days, from a
point to a steady lamp, near now, nearly home, and he was looking at it with the stunned grin, the one
that meant the enormous thing was real and was his, and he said to the woman named Reyes who'd saved him
the seat, "How long now?"

"Tomorrow," she said. "We'll see the dish by tomorrow."

The ship held its corner, on schedule, the way it had for thirty years, and crossed onto the road, toward
the green lamp, at speed, and ahead of it in the empty dark the line it could not see waited exactly where
a mind had decided, a long time ago, that it would be.
