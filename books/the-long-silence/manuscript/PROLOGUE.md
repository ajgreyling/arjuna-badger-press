# Prologue

They threw a party for the loudest thing our species had ever done, and the catering was very good.

There was a marquee on the lawn of the institute with the sides rolled up because the evening had decided,
against the forecast, to be kind. There was a string quartet that nobody listened to and everybody was
glad of. There were two hundred people who had spent their careers learning to hear a whisper from the
far side of nothing, and tonight they had come to celebrate the opposite — the night the institute would
stop listening and, for eleven minutes, *speak*: a single transmission aimed up the throat of the galaxy
at a power no civilization on this rock had ever managed, a hello with the whole planet's name written
under it.

The man who had spent nine years arguing the world into letting him send it stood near the bar with a
glass he had not drunk from, and he was happy. It is important to be fair to him, because everything that
came after has made him easy to hate, and he did not deserve to be easy. He was not a fool. He had run
the objections to ground one by one, in committee rooms and in the press and across a dozen languages,
and the objection that the dark might be dark on purpose he had answered the way a reasonable person
answers it: that a universe old enough and wide enough to grow a mind like ours, twice or ten thousand
times, could not be so cheaply afraid; that silence was not strategy but absence; that the absence was
the loneliest fact there was and he would rather spend his one life trying to end it than crouching under
it. People had wept when he said that. People had voted for it. He believed it the way you believe the
floor will be there when you swing your legs out of bed, which is to say he did not believe it at all — he
simply had never once been made to.

At the console a young woman whose name nobody at the party would remember in a hundred years, though the
species would owe her the most terrible debt of all, watched a countdown that was not yet the countdown
that mattered. Eleven minutes of the planet talking. She had checked the aim four times. It was correct.
She would think about that for the rest of her life: that the aim had been correct.

He found her there because he wanted to thank someone real, and she was the realest person in the
building. "Big night," he said.

"Big night," she agreed, and did not look up.

"You don't sound sure."

She watched the numbers. "I keep thinking," she said, "that we built the most sensitive ear in the
history of the world, and the first thing we're going to do with it is shout."

He laughed, because it was a good line and because he liked her, and because he had answered this exact
worry in front of a senate and could answer it in his sleep. "If there's anyone out there to hear us," he
said, "don't you think they'd want to know they're not alone either?"

She turned then, and looked at him, and he would remember her face longer than he remembered his own
children's, though he did not know that yet.

"That's the part that frightens me," she said. "I think they already know exactly how alone they are. And
I think that's why it's so quiet."

Outside the quartet finished a movement and a few kind people clapped. The numbers reached the small
threshold and crossed it, and across half a continent a ring of dishes that had only ever leaned in to
listen tipped their faces up together and, for eleven minutes, sang.

You could not hear it. That was the strange thing she carried out of the marquee that night, past the
laughing people and the warm forgiving dark. The loudest thing the species had ever said, and on the lawn
you could not hear a sound. It went out perfectly silent, in every direction that mattered, at the speed
of the only thing faster than regret, toward a forest that had been quiet for a very long time, and for
very good reasons, and that was about to hear us coming.
