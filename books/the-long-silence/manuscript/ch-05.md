# The Cut

> *Scaffold draft — Movement I detonation. The cable-cut, kept at full calibre. Witnessed from the relay,
> on the feed, helpless. The cleanness and the delay are the horror. Never caption the awe.*

The relay watch that night was a man named Aldous Park, who was sixty-one and bored and four hours from
the end of a shift in which the most interesting thing that had happened was that the coffee machine had
finally, after eight years of threatening it, died.

He had the *Meridian Reach* on the long approach feed, because you always had the inbound on the feed,
not because you expected anything but because a captain liked to raise the relay and say something dry and
human across the gap and you liked to say something dry and human back. Sowande had raised him an hour
ago. *Tell Massri his garden's still here,* she'd said, *the goats haven't found it yet,* and Park had
said he'd tell him, and that the coffee machine was dead, and she'd said good riddance, it made coffee
like a threat, and that had been the whole of it, two people on a quiet road being ordinary at each other
across a hundred thousand kilometres, and then she'd gone back to her ship and he'd gone back to his
boredom and the feed had sat there showing him the *Meridian Reach* growing, slowly, frame by frame, into
the lamp on the approach.

She was beautiful on the feed, in the plain way working things are beautiful. Lit up inside, all her ports
warm, forty years old and forty-one aboard, holding her line down the road toward the green star, near
now, nearly in.

Park was thinking about the coffee machine.

He saw the ship cross something.

That was all it was, at first, and afterward, in every interview, in the inquiry, in his own head for the
nineteen years he had left, that was the phrase he came back to and could never improve, because it was
exactly and only what he saw. The ship crossed something. There was no flash. There was no impact, no
shudder, no debris, no sound — there was never going to be a sound, but the mind expects one and its
absence is its own wrongness. The *Meridian Reach* simply passed through a place in the dark, a line he
could not see and would never see, and for one second she was exactly as she had been.

For one second nothing was wrong.

He would give evidence, later, that he had not even leaned forward. Why would he. The ship was whole. The
ports were warm. She was holding her line. One second.

In the second second she began, very gently, to come apart.

It was not an explosion. He needed people to understand that and they never quite could, because the word
for a ship ending is *explosion* and this was the opposite of an explosion, this was something patient and
geometric and almost tender. She came apart along lines. Parallel lines, evenly spaced, a few metres
apart down the whole length of her, as though a hand had taken a working hull and a violin and a box of
seeds and forty-one people and drawn them, all together, slowly, through the finest comb that had ever
existed — and the ship did not shatter, she *separated*, into sheets, into clean bright slices that began,
without hurry, in the absence of any force he could see, to slide apart in the dark.

And the lights stayed on.

That was the thing. That was the thing Aldous Park carried to the end of his life and could not set down.
The lights stayed on in the slices. For a moment — two, three seconds, an eternity that the feed recorded
and that the inquiry played and replayed until people wept and then stopped a person could be made to
watch it — the *Meridian Reach* hung in the dark as a stack of glowing cross-sections, each one lit from
within, each one perfectly, surgically clean, and in the cross-sections, in the cut-open warm-lit slabs
of the ship, you could see, if you were a man at a feed who could not look away, the inside of her. The
corridors in section. The galley in section. The mess in section, and the good seat by the port, and the
shapes in the seats. All of it open to the dark now, all of it still lit, all of it sliding apart so
gently, so evenly, that for those few seconds it did not look like dying at all. It looked like a drawing
of a ship. It looked like the most careful thing anyone had ever done.

Then the dark got in.

The lights went, not all at once but in a kind of falling sequence as the air left and the cold came and
the slices drifted out of true, and the green star of the relay went on turning behind them as it had
turned for thirty years, indifferent, on schedule, and the debris of the *Meridian Reach* spread out
along the road in neat parallel sheets, evenly spaced, the tidiest wreck in the history of the species,
the kind of wreck that does not happen by accident, that cannot happen by accident, that announces in
every clean millimetre of itself that it was *done*, by something, on purpose, with no more effort or
malice than it takes to draw a line.

Park understood what he had seen about half a second after the feed had already shown it to him, which is
the cruelty of a feed, that it knows before you do. He understood it the way you understand a death in a
language you don't speak, the meaning arriving ahead of the words. He had time to think, with terrible
clarity, *there was a wire,* and then he had time to think nothing at all for a while.

He did not raise anyone. There was no one to raise. You do not raise a ship that is a row of sheets. He
sat in the relay room with the dead coffee machine and the turning green star and the feed still running,
still dutifully showing him the slow neat scatter of forty-one people, and he did the only thing left to
a man at the far quiet end of a road, which was to be the one who saw it, so that it had been seen.

---

It took the inquiry a long time to say the word, because the word implied a hand, and a hand implied the
thing Ada Reyes had named in a report no one read, three hundred and forty years from its zero: that the
future was not only coming but *already here*, already reaching ahead of itself with something small and
patient, already drawing lines across the species' empty roads. There had been no weapon aboard anything.
There had been no fleet, no enemy, no battle. There had been a working hull on a schedule, and a line in
the dark, one molecule wide, that cost the thing that drew it almost nothing, and that the *Meridian
Reach* had crossed at speed on a Tuesday — Park always remembered it was a Tuesday, and was never able to
say why that was the part that broke him — on her way home, three days from the green star, with the
coffee machine dead and the goats not yet in the garden.

After that, the number on Ada Reyes's clock stopped being a number.

For three hundred years it had been an abstraction, a horizon, the kind of thing a species built to fear
snakes could agree was real and then forget by Friday. Now there was a feed. Now there was a thing every
child eventually saw, the slow clean separation of a warm-lit ship into sheets, the lights staying on, the
shapes in the seats, and no child who saw it ever again believed the dark was lonely rather than
dangerous, or that being found was the end of being alone.

It was the end of something else. The species understood, finally, sitting in front of the tidiest wreck
it had ever made, what kind of forest it had been shouting into. It understood that the thing in the dark
did not need a fleet to end you. It needed a line. It understood that there was no malice anywhere in
what had happened, and that this was infinitely worse than malice, because malice can be reasoned with and
a geometry cannot, and a thing that ends you with a line it does not even stay to watch you cross is a
thing that has done this many times, on many roads, to many loud and frightened animals, and felt nothing,
and moved on.

And somewhere in the long dark, on schedule, costing almost nothing, the hunter that had strung the line
had already turned its quiet attention elsewhere, because the forest was wide, and the work was never
done, and it had four hundred years — three hundred and forty, now — to be sure, and it was a patient
thing, and it knew, the way the species was only now beginning to know, exactly how the silence was kept,
and by whom, and why.
