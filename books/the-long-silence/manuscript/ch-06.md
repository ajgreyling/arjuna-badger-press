# The Children of the Clock

> *Scaffold draft — Movement II opens. A century on. The countdown is the water everyone swims in. The
> deterrent's keepers are hated. Sgt. Nadia Brandt, the ballast voice, introduces the Reckoners from the
> outside — and reads the strange new man they've just pulled in.*

A hundred and ten years is long enough that the end of the world becomes a municipal department.

Nadia Brandt had policed the fence around it for nineteen of those years, and she still couldn't say the
department's name without a small private twist of the mouth, the way you'd say the name of a relative who
did something the family didn't talk about. The Reckoners. Bureau of Continuity, on the brass plate,
because somebody a long time ago had decided that a planet could not be expected to walk past a sign that
said what the place actually did. Continuity. As if it were a records office. As if the people behind the
fence weren't sitting on the single worst idea the species had ever had, polishing it, keeping it warm,
waiting to use it.

She walked the inner line at the change of light because she liked the inner line at the change of light,
when the floods hadn't come up yet and the desert went the colour of a struck match and you could, for
about eleven minutes, pretend you worked at an ordinary place that ordinary people didn't spit at through
the wire.

People spat at the wire. That was a thing you got used to, in the job. Not every day. But enough that the
maintenance crew had a standing schedule for the east gate, where the protestors gathered, where somebody
had painted GHOULS in two-metre letters that had to be sandblasted off every spring and that Brandt
privately thought they should just leave, because it was, depending on how you held it, accurate.

The children threw the worst of it, which was the part that got to her. Not because children are cruel —
children are always cruel, that's not news — but because of the specific shape of this cruelty, which she
had watched curdle over her nineteen years into something she didn't have a clean word for. These were the
children of the clock. Every one of them, every human being now alive, had been born under a number. None
of them had asked for it. They had come into a world that already knew the date of its own funeral, more
or less, give or take, and had been told the date roughly the way you'd tell a child about death in the
abstract, as a far thing, a someday thing — except it wasn't abstract, it had a feed, the feed every child
eventually saw, the old grainy one of a ship coming apart into glowing slices with the lights still on, and
once you'd seen that you understood that the someday thing had a *texture*, and the texture was a wire you
couldn't see drawn across a road you had to cross.

You raised a whole species under that, and you got — what Brandt walked past every morning. You got
children who threw stones at a fence and meant them. You got the ones who'd decided the only sane response
to a funeral you couldn't cancel was to live like the band on a sinking ship, gloriously, loudly, now, and
who made astonishing art and astonishing trouble and burned bright and short. You got the other ones, the
quiet ones, who'd gone the opposite way, who got up and did the dishes and paid the mortgage that was
still, somehow, after a hundred and ten years, due on Friday, because the animal stubbornness Ada Reyes had
named was real and load-bearing and the only thing that had kept the lights on this long. And you got, at
the dead centre of it, behind Brandt's fence, the third kind: the ones who had looked at the funeral and
decided to do something about it, and whom everyone else hated for it, because the something was unbearable.

The cap was why they hated, mostly, if you traced it back. A hundred and ten years and the species had not
moved. Could not move. Brandt was no scientist but she'd absorbed the shape of it the way you absorb the
shape of anything you stand next to for two decades: there was a ceiling on the deep physics, a tidy
invisible ceiling, and every generation of clever frightened children threw themselves at it and came back
down with beautiful clean wrong answers, and after a century of that a species starts to feel it in its
body, the not-rising, the running-in-place. They had cities you wouldn't believe and medicine that bordered
on the miraculous and they could not, would not ever, build the one thing that might have let them fight
or flee, and they knew exactly why — there was a hand on the page — and the knowing was worse than not
knowing, and they needed somewhere to put it, and they put it on the fence. On the ghouls. On the only
people in arm's reach who'd admitted out loud that the funeral was real.

Brandt understood the spitting. She just thought it was aimed wrong. You don't spit at the undertaker
because somebody died.

---

The new one arrived on a Tuesday, which she'd remember, later, because of the old story about Tuesdays
that everyone at the bureau half-knew and nobody fully believed, the one about the first cut having come on
a Tuesday and the number having stopped being a number after.

He came through the east gate in an ordinary car with ordinary plates, no escort, which was already
strange, and he got out into the spitting distance and just stood there a moment looking at the GHOULS on
the wall with his head tilted like a man reading a menu, and the protestors, who could smell bureau on a
person at forty metres, started up, and he didn't flinch and he didn't hurry and he didn't do the thing
the smart frightened ones did, which was pretend not to hear. He listened to it. He looked like he was
*adding it up*.

Brandt clocked him from the gatehouse the way she clocked everyone, which was a habit older than the job —
how a man holds himself, what his hands do when he thinks no one's watching, whether the eyes go to the
exits or the people. His eyes went to neither. His eyes went, she realised, to the *angles* — the line of
the fence, the spacing of the floods, the geometry of the place, as though the compound were a problem
written down and he were checking somebody's work. She'd seen a lot of types come through the gate.
Frightened types, fanatic types, the careerists, the broken ones who came to the bureau the way some
people come to a war, looking for a reason. She had not seen this type. This was a man looking at her fence
the way the thing out in the dark must have looked at a road.

"You the new analyst," she said, when he reached the booth. Not a question. She'd had his file an hour.
Ridgeway. No service record, no movement record worth the name, which at the bureau meant either nobody or
the most somebody there was.

"I'm told that's the word for it." He had a dry voice, quieter than the noise behind him, so you had to
lean in, which she suspected was deliberate. "What do you call it, on this side of the wire?"

"We don't call it anything good." She found his pass on the manifest and didn't hand it over yet. "They
been spitting at you the whole walk up?"

"Since the gate."

"Bother you?"

He considered it like she'd asked him something that mattered, which nobody at the bureau ever did. "No,"
he said. "It's the only honest thing anybody's said to me since I took the job. They understand exactly
what this place is. They're the only people for forty kilometres who do." He took the pass when she
finally held it out, and looked at it, and looked back at her, and said the thing that decided her on him,
the thing that meant she stopped thinking of him as a file and started, against every instinct she had for
self-preservation, to watch him the way you watch weather coming. "You walk the inner line at the change
of light," he said. "I saw you from the road. Most people would walk it when the floods are up. You walk
it when you can still see the desert." He almost smiled. "You're not afraid of the dark out there. You're
afraid of the lights in here. So am I. We're going to get along."

Then he went in through the gate the species had built to hold its worst idea, past the wall that said
GHOULS, and Brandt stood in the booth with the spitting going on behind her and the match-coloured desert
going dark and thought, with the flat certainty she usually saved for the moment before a thing went bad:
that man is going to do something, and we are all going to have to live with it, and the worst part is I
think it's going to work.
