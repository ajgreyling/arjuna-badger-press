# The Quiet Sabotage

> *Scaffold draft — Movement I. The Net's first effect: it is already here, and it is keeping us stupid on
> purpose. The technological-explosion fear, dramatised. Ada ages; the relay begins.*

The physics broke before anyone admitted the physics was broken, the way a marriage ends a long time
before the conversation about ending it.

Ada heard it first from a man named Devraj, who ran one of the big detectors on the far side of the world
and called her at an hour that was nobody's reasonable hour in either time zone, which was how she knew it
was real. They had met twice at conferences. He was a careful man, the kind who underclaimed on purpose,
and he was not careful on the phone that night.

"My numbers are lying to me," he said.

"Everyone's numbers lie to them. That's why we have error bars."

"Not like this." She could hear him not-sleeping, the particular wakefulness of a man who has checked a
thing forty times. "The error bars are fine. That's the problem. The error bars are *beautiful*. The data
is clean and tight and reproducible and it is *wrong*, Ada, it's wrong in a way that's consistent, and
consistent wrong is the one thing nature doesn't do. Nature is sloppy. This is tidy. Something tidy is
sitting in my detector telling me a tidy lie."

She didn't laugh, which she would remember as a small mercy she had managed to extend to one person before
the years made her hard. "Send me the runs," she said, and he sent her the runs, and she spent a week
with them the way she had once spent a week proving the timing hadn't drifted, and at the end of the week
she had the same kind of answer she'd had then: not the answer she wanted, the other one.

Devraj was right. The detector was being lied to. So was every detector like it, on every continent,
whenever it reached past a certain energy — and only past that energy, which was the tell, the fingerprint
of intent. Below the line, the universe behaved. Above the line, where the deep questions lived, the
questions you had to answer before you could build anything that might let you fight a thing in the dark
or run from it, the results came back clean and tidy and subtly, surgically false. As if someone had read
the species' physics textbooks, found the chapter that came next, and quietly torn it out, leaving the
page numbers intact so you wouldn't notice the gap until you went looking for what should have been there.

It took her another month to say the word *sabotage* out loud, because the word implied a saboteur, and a
saboteur implied that the thing four hundred years away had a hand here already, now, in the room, and
that was a different and worse fact than a clock. A clock you could ignore for a mortgage. A hand in the
room you could not.

But that was what it was. She drew it out the way she drew everything out, on the back of something, in a
flat hand: if you were a hunter, and you had found a loud animal in the dark, and you were four centuries
away at the speed of your own arrival, what would you do *first* — before you came, before you struck,
the very first thing? You would make sure that when you got there, the animal had not grown teeth. You
would not wait four hundred years and gamble that a clever, frightened, fast-learning thing stayed
exactly as clever as it was the day you found it. You would reach ahead of yourself, somehow, with
something small and patient and already moving, and you would press your thumb gently down on the one
place the animal might grow — its deep physics, the only soil teeth grow from — and you would hold it
there, lightly, for four hundred years, and let the animal exhaust itself running in the same circle,
certain it was about to break through, never breaking through, until you arrived.

That was the part that finally frightened her in the fast way, the snake-in-the-grass way her species was
actually built to feel. Not the death four centuries off. The thumb. The thumb was now.

---

She tried to tell them. This is the part the histories got least wrong and cared about least, because it
made nobody a hero and it had no scene in it, only the long grinding friction of a true thing meeting a
world that had a mortgage due.

She wrote it up. She wrote it up well, which was its own kind of error; a worse writer might have
frightened someone. She laid out the energy threshold and the consistency of the lie and the chain of
reasoning from the loud animal to the gentle thumb, and she ended it with a single line she meant to be
the most important sentence she ever wrote, and it was, and no one read it for nineteen years, which by
then she had learned to expect:

*We are not running out of time. We have been quietly forbidden to use the time we have.*

The committees received it. The committees received many things. Hers went into the body of received
things, and the world, which had agreed four hundred years was far away, found it very easy to agree that
a sabotage no one could see, of experiments no one but a few thousand people understood, in service of a
threat no one would live to meet, was not the most pressing item on a planet that still had to feed
itself by Friday. They were not wrong to feed themselves. That was the trap of it, the same trap as the
mortgage, the same trap as the Hail: every individual choice was reasonable, and the sum of the reasonable
choices was a species standing very still while a thumb pressed down on the one place it might have grown
strong enough to live.

Devraj kept working. That was the other thing she carried out of those years, the only warm thing. He
kept walking into the room with the lying detector and asking it the next question and writing down the
beautiful, tidy, false answer, year after year, not because he thought he'd break through — he had stopped
thinking that — but because, he told her once, the day everyone stopped asking was the day the thumb won,
and he would be damned if he'd hand it that day for free. He died still asking. A lot of them did. There
was a whole generation of physicists, Ada came to think, who spent their entire careers as a kind of
honour guard for a question they were not allowed to answer, standing watch over an empty page, and the
species never built them a single statue, because what would it say.

---

She got old at the array. She had not meant to; nobody means to; you look up and the noodles are someone
else's now and the desk that wobbled has been replaced by a desk that wobbles differently and the people
on the night shift call you by your last name with a small weight on it that means they have heard the
stories and don't quite believe you were ever young.

There was a young one, near the end, sharp the way she had been sharp, who sat with her one slow night
under the same tea-coloured sky and asked her the question they all eventually asked, which was whether
she thought there was any hope.

Ada thought about the thumb, and the clock, and the four hundred years that were now three hundred and
sixty, and the page that should have had the next chapter on it.

"I think," she said, "that we are very good at aiming, and very bad at deciding what to point at the sky.
And I think the one thing nobody's tried yet is being quiet." She tapped the desk, once, with one finger,
the way she had tapped a maintenance log a lifetime ago. "We made the most sensitive ear in the history
of the world. We should have used it to learn how the quiet ones stay quiet. Instead we used it to shout,
and then we used it to listen to our own echo coming back as a clock." She smiled, which surprised them
both. "Write that down somewhere they won't read it. It's traditional."

She did not live to see the reply's first physical envoy reach the inner system. She did not see the
microprobes, too small and too many to find, seed themselves through the dark around the planet like the
quietest snow. She did not see what the snow built, weeks before the *Meridian Reach* ever left port — a
single line, drawn taut and invisible across a road that ships had used for thirty unbothered years,
waiting with the patience of a thing that has done this before and knows exactly how much time a loud and
frightened species will waste before it learns to look where it's going.

She had drawn the bubble correctly. She had read the clock correctly. She had named the thumb. She had,
like the woman at the console before her and the watch that would come after her, gotten the aim exactly
right, and been, like all of them, exactly too quiet to be heard over the sound of a species still
shouting at a sky that had long since stopped pretending not to listen.
