# The Reply

> *Scaffold draft — Movement I. The reply arrives, and is not a reply. Ada Reyes; Marcus Hale present. The
> countdown begins and never stops.*

The reply came on a Tuesday, which Ada Reyes would later think was the most honest thing about it. Not a
date you would carve into anything. A Tuesday in the low season, the plateau gone the colour of weak tea
under a sky that hadn't decided to be winter yet, and most of the array idling through a maintenance
window while two thirds of the staff were down the mountain getting their teeth cleaned and their cars
serviced and their ordinary lives lived.

She was not even looking for it. Nobody was looking for it; that was a thing the documentaries always got
wrong afterward, the breathless reconstruction of the heroic watch. There was no watch. There was a
woman eating cold noodles out of the carton at a desk that wasn't hers because hers had a wobble, running
a diagnostic on the deep array's timing because the timing had drifted four nanoseconds and four
nanoseconds was the kind of thing that ruined a month of somebody's data if you let it ride.

The diagnostic came back clean. The timing hadn't drifted. Something had arrived.

She put the carton down.

It is worth being precise about what she saw, because everyone who came after her got it slightly wrong,
softened it into a message, a voice, a face. There was no message. There was a pulse — narrow, coherent,
artificial past any argument, the kind of signal that nature does not make and cannot fake — and it was
not aimed at the array. That was the first wrong thing. It was aimed *past* the array, at a point a little
off the plane of the planet's orbit, the way you would aim not at a man but at the place a man was going
to be. And it was not one pulse. It was the leading edge of a sequence, and the sequence was counting.

She sat with it for forty minutes before she called anyone, and she was never ashamed of those forty
minutes, though people implied she should be. In forty minutes she did the only useful thing anyone did
that whole year, which was to understand it before she had to explain it.

It was counting down. The intervals between the pulses were shrinking by a fixed ratio, a clean
geometric decay, the most unmistakable structure a mind can put into a signal — not *here is a thought*
but *here is a clock*. And the clock was long. She worked the decay out to its zero on the back of a
maintenance log, checked it twice because the number was absurd, and got the same answer both times. The
clock ran down in a little under four centuries.

Four hundred years, give or take, before whatever was counting reached zero.

She felt, sitting there, an emotion she had no name for and would spend the rest of her life failing to
describe to people who hadn't felt it. It was not fear, exactly. Fear is fast. This was slow, and it had
a horizon in it. She thought of her mother, who had been dead six years. She thought of a child she did
not have and now understood, with a flat certainty that startled her, she was never going to have. She
thought: everyone I will ever love will be dead before this number runs out, and so will everyone they
love, and so will everyone after that, for sixteen generations, and the last of them will stand exactly
where I am standing and watch this same clock hit zero, and there is nothing I can do tonight except
write it down correctly.

So she wrote it down correctly. Then she called the director, who called the people the director called,
and by morning the mountain was full of cars again, and Marcus Hale was on the first flight in.

---

He arrived looking like a man who had been told a joke he hadn't found funny yet, certain there was a
punchline that would set it right. Ada had never met him. She knew him the way everyone knew him — the
clear voice, the kind face that had spent nine years on screens explaining why the dark was only lonely
and never dangerous, the man who had made the case for the Hail and won it. She had voted for it herself.
She thought it was important to remember that, later. She had been one of the hands that went up.

They put him in the briefing room with the senior staff and the math on the wall, and he read the math,
and Ada watched him read it. He was not stupid. That was the thing she kept coming back to. A stupid man
she could have forgiven faster. He looked at the geometric decay and the four-hundred-year zero and the
little detail that the signal was aimed not *at* us but at the volume of space the inner system would
occupy, the way you lead a bird, and he understood all of it, and then he said the thing that hope makes
intelligent people say.

"It's a reply," he said. "It's contact. It's them telling us they heard, and when they'll arrive."

"It's a countdown," Ada said.

"To a meeting."

"To something." She did not raise her voice. She had decided in the forty minutes that she would never
raise her voice about this, that the only authority she had was to be the person in the room who said the
flat thing flatly. "You don't send a clock to someone you want to meet. You send a clock to someone you
want to know exactly how much time they have."

The room was quiet in the particular way of a room where everyone has just heard the true thing and is
waiting to see whether they're allowed to agree with it.

"You don't know that," Hale said. And he was right, technically; that was the cruelty of the chain she
had not yet learned the name for. She didn't know it. He didn't know it either. Neither of them could
know it, and the not-knowing did not split the difference down the middle into a comfortable maybe. It
sat there between them, and it would sit between Earth and the thing in the dark for four hundred years,
unverifiable, and a species that bet its life on the kind reading of an unverifiable stranger was a
species that had not understood the stranger was thinking the exact same thoughts about *it*.

"I sent the Hail," Hale said, to no one, and it was the first time Ada heard the change in his voice — the
hairline crack that would widen over the years she knew him into something she came, against all her
intentions, to pity. "I argued for it. People voted because I asked them to."

"You did," the director said, not unkindly.

Hale looked at the wall, at the clean decaying intervals, at the number. "If it's a threat," he said,
slowly, the way a man tests a tooth he is afraid is broken, "then I'm the one who told them where we are."

Nobody answered him, because the kind answer and the true answer were the same answer, and it was yes.

---

They argued for three weeks about whether to tell the world, which Ada thought was the second most absurd
thing the species did that year, after sending the Hail in the first place. You could not un-tell the
world. The signal was loud and clean and aimed at the sky; every serious array on the planet would have
it within the month, and the third-rate ones within the year, and there was no committee on Earth with
the authority to make four hundred years of arriving death into a secret. The only question was whether
the people who had found it first got to choose the words, and even that, Ada knew, was a vanity. The
words wouldn't matter. The number would do all the talking, the way it had done all of hers.

In the end they told the truth, more or less, in the careful denatured language of official truth, and
the world did what worlds do: it panicked for a season, and then it got up the next morning and went to
work, because the number was four hundred years away and a mortgage was due on Friday. That was the part
the doomsayers never accounted for, the sheer animal stubbornness of ordinary life, its refusal to be
cancelled by anything it couldn't see. It was, Ada came to believe, both the best thing about her species
and the thing that was going to kill it. They could not stay frightened of a horizon. They were built to
fear the snake in the grass, not the season turning, and what was coming for them was a season.

She went back to the array. There was nothing else to go back to that she trusted. The clock ticked down
its first imperceptible increment, four nanoseconds closer, and somewhere off the plane of the orbit, in
a volume of empty space that a mind in the dark had picked out the way you pick the spot a bird is flying
to, the future kept its appointment, patient as weather, and did not hurry, because it had done this
before, and it knew exactly how much time a loud and frightened thing could waste.
