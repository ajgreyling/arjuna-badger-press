# The Brightening

> *Scaffold draft — opening of Movement I. Establishes the Analyst, the array, and the broadcast-folly as
> data: the moment humanity's loudness becomes legible as a thing that can be seen, and therefore aimed
> at. Cast names are placeholders pending CANON_CHOICES.*

The map was wrong, and it took her three weeks to understand that the map was not wrong, the planet was.

It hung on the wall of the correlation room the way it had hung there for nine years before she came: the
sky in radio, the whole bowl of it flattened out and false-coloured, the galaxy a smeared bright spine
down the middle and everything else the deep cold blue of things that did not transmit. You learned to
read it the way you learned to read a familiar face. The bright spots were old friends — the engines at
the cores of distant things, the dying stars that screamed in wavelengths you could not see by. The blue
was the dark, and the dark was, on this map, simply the absence of anyone shouting.

There was a spot on it that had no business being there, and the spot was them.

She had found it the way you find most true things, by accident, while looking for something duller. A
calibration run, eighty years of archived sky folded together to clean the noise out, and the noise had
refused to clean. It sat there in the folded data, low and broad and unmistakable once you saw it, a
warmth where the map said there should be cold. She had assumed an error in the instrument. She had spent
a week proving it was an error in the instrument, and at the end of the week she had a proof that it was
not, and she sat in the correlation room at two in the morning and looked at the small ugly bright thing
and understood what she was looking at.

It was the planet. It was them. It was eighty years of everything they had ever broadcast — the
television and the radar and the chatter and, eight months ago, eleven triumphant minutes of a deliberate
hello — all of it folded together and seen from the only honest distance, which was far enough away to
forget the content and keep only the fact. From far enough away you could not hear what they were saying.
You could only see that they were saying it, loudly, in a place where the map said no one said anything at
all.

She thought, with the flat clarity that came to her sometimes at that hour: we are the only light on in
the street.

It was not even a frightening thought, at first. It was a *funny* one. She nearly laughed. All that money,
all those dishes, the most sensitive ears the species had ever ground and polished and pointed at the
dark — and the loudest source in the sky, once you stepped back far enough to see straight, was the back
of their own heads. Like a man cupping his hand to his ear to catch a far-off footstep, unaware he is
standing on a stage, under a spotlight, in front of a theatre he cannot see into, having just announced
his name.

She made herself stop laughing because the next thought arrived underneath the first one and it was not
funny at all.

The eighty years of leakage had gone out at the speed of light. The hello eight months ago had gone out
at the speed of light. Light was fast. Light was also, across the distances the map described, *slow* —
slow enough that the oldest edge of their loudness had travelled eighty light-years and no further, a
little bubble of noise expanding into the quiet at exactly one second per second, no faster, and anything
that lived beyond the skin of that bubble had not heard them yet.

But everything *inside* it had.

She pulled the catalogue without quite deciding to. How many stars inside eighty light-years. The number
came up and it was not a small number and it was not a large one; it was the worst kind of number, the
kind you could imagine. A few hundred. A few hundred porches inside earshot of a planet that had spent
eight decades learning to listen and not one single hour learning to be quiet, that had built the most
exquisite ear in its history and used it, first, to make sure the whole neighbourhood knew it was home.

If anyone was out there. If anyone was listening. If the quiet meant anything other than empty.

She looked at the bright spot that was her whole world and she thought about the woman at the console
eight months ago, the one the broadcast people had quoted in their celebration: *don't you think they'd
want to know they're not alone?* And she found she could not stop thinking the obvious answer, the one no
one had said into a microphone, the one that sat in the folded data like a temperature:

That depends entirely on what they are.

She did not sleep. By the time the early shift came in she had the temperature mapped, the bubble drawn,
the catalogue of porches sorted by distance, and a single line typed at the top of the report that she
would later wish she had had the courage or the cowardice to delete, because it was the first true thing
anyone on the planet wrote down about the situation they were actually in, and no one read it for another
nineteen years:

*We are not listening for them. We have been advertising to them. The only open question is whether the
forest is empty — and we have just done the one thing that would tell us.*

The reply, when it came, did not come for a long time, and was not a reply, and the woman who wrote the
report did not live to read it. But she had drawn the bubble correctly. She had, like the woman at the
console before her, gotten the aim exactly right.

That was the thing about her whole species, she would think, near the end, when there was at last nothing
to do but think. They were so very good at aiming. They had only ever been bad at deciding what to point
at the sky.
