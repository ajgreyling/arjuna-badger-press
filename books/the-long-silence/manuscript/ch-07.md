# The Shape of the Forest

> *Scaffold draft — Movement II, the anti-sag danger zone. The Dark Forest law, deduced clause by clause —
> but UNDER PRESSURE, through argument with people who need him to be wrong, never as a lecture. Cole
> Ridgeway; Brandt present as the human floor. Contains the one partial, contested near-statement of the
> law (PP-009).*

They gave Ridgeway the archive, which everyone treated as a punishment and which he took, Brandt saw, as a
gift.

The archive was a hundred and ten years of how-we-might-survive, and it was the saddest room in the
compound, sadder than the cells where they kept the pin, because the cells at least held something that
worked. The archive held everything that hadn't. Every defence the species had dreamed up since the night
the number arrived: the fleets, the bunkers, the arks, the schemes to hide the planet, to move it, to dig
in, to run, to fight, to *talk* — a century and a tenth of the most desperate ingenuity a frightened
species could produce, every file of it stamped, eventually, in the same flat word. Unviable. Unviable.
Unviable. Like a wall of headstones for ideas.

He read it the way Brandt had seen men read a confession they already knew the shape of, looking not for
the news but for the place the lie was. She'd been assigned to him — half bodyguard, half minder, the
bureau never said which because the bureau never said anything — and so she sat in the archive while he
read, weeks of it, and she watched a man do the thing she'd never seen done before, which was take the
worst news in the world apart to see why it was true.

The young ones came to argue with him. That was the part she hadn't expected. The bureau was full of clever
frightened children grown into clever frightened adults, and they had spent their careers on the viable
ideas, the *almost*, the next fleet, the next scheme, and here was this dry quiet man in the headstone
room telling them — not unkindly, that was the maddening thing, he was never unkind — that none of it could
ever work, and they came in singly and in pairs to prove him wrong, and Brandt sat in the corner and
learned the shape of the forest from listening to him refuse to let them off.

"It's a numbers problem and the numbers are with us," said one of them, early, a sharp woman from the
fleet office named Okonkwo who'd built three of the headstones herself. "There are billions of stars. The
overwhelming majority of anything out there will be too far, too slow, too uninterested to ever matter to
us. We're a needle in a hayfield. Why does anyone come for the needle?"

"Two reasons," Ridgeway said. He didn't look up from the file. "And you already know the first one,
because you live under it. Why does anyone come for the needle? They came for us because we *announced* the
needle. We sent up a flare and rang a bell and then wondered why the field noticed. That's not the forest
being cruel. That's us being loud." He set the file down. "But that's the old mistake, and it's done, and
flagellating about the Hail won't save anyone now. The second reason is the one nobody in this building
wants to finish thinking, and it's the one that matters." He finally looked at her. "Sit down. This takes
a minute and you'll want to be sitting."

Okonkwo sat. Brandt, in the corner, did not, on principle, but she leaned.

"Start with two things you can't argue with," Ridgeway said, "because everything ugly comes out of two
things nobody can argue with. One: anything alive wants to stay alive. Not 'usually.' Not 'most.'
*Anything* — because the ones that didn't, aren't here to be discussed. Survival isn't a value out there.
It's a filter. Everyone left standing in the dark is, by definition, descended from the ones who put
staying alive ahead of everything else, every time. That's not cynicism. That's just what's left after a
few billion years of everything else dying."

"Fine," said Okonkwo. "Granted. Everyone wants to live. That's an argument for leaving each other *alone*."

"It would be," Ridgeway said, "if there were enough room. Here's the second thing you can't argue with.
Everything that's alive and clever *grows*. It uses more, reaches further, wants more room and more matter
and more energy, every century more than the last — you've watched your own species do it for a hundred
thousand years. But the universe doesn't grow to match. The matter that's out there is the matter that's
out there. Finite shelf. Infinite appetite, multiplied by however many hungry clever things are sitting in
the dark." He let that sit. "Two facts. Everyone wants to live. Everyone, forever, wants more than there
is. That's the whole engine. Everything else is just the gears turning."

"That's competition," Okonkwo said. "Competition isn't extermination. We compete with rivals on this
planet without murdering all of them."

"Because you can see them," Ridgeway said, and here his voice changed, dropped, got careful, and Brandt
felt the room change with it. "That's the gear nobody wants to look at. On this planet, when you meet a
rival, you can read them. You can see their face, hear the lie under the words, watch what they do over
time, build the slow thing we call trust because you can *check* it, day after day, in the same room. Take
all of that away. Now your rival is a hundred light-years off. Anything you learn about them is a century
out of date by the time it reaches you, and anything you say to them is a century out of date by the time
it lands, and there is no room — there will *never* be a room — where you can both stand and read each
other's faces and find out, the slow safe way, whether you can let your guard down."

He stood, then, and walked to the wall of headstones, and put his hand flat against a drawer of them.

"So you have to *guess*," he said. "About a stranger who wants to live as badly as you do, and wants more
than there is as badly as you do, and whom you cannot read and cannot check and cannot ever meet. You have
to guess whether they're kind. And here's the trap, the actual trap, the thing that makes the dark what it
is: it doesn't matter what *you* decide. Because they're guessing about *you*, with exactly the same
nothing to go on. And you can't tell them you're kind in any way they can believe, because that's exactly
what something that wasn't kind would also say. And they can't tell *you*. And you both know the other one
knows that. And it goes round, and it doesn't cancel out into a nice safe maybe — it *compounds*, every
loop, into something neither of you can ever climb down from. Two strangers in the dark, each one perfectly
aware that the other might be reasoning their way, right now, to the only move that's certainly safe."

"Which is," Okonkwo said, very quietly, because she'd gotten there.

"Which is to shoot first," Ridgeway said. "While the stranger is still weak enough to kill. Before they
grow. Because that's the last gear, and it's the one that turns 'maybe' into 'now.' A thing that looks
harmless today might not stay harmless. You found *us* harmless — a loud animal that can't even hold its
own physics together. But you can't bet a century of flight time that we *stay* harmless, because clever
frightened things grow teeth fast when you give them four hundred years and a reason, and by the time your
weapon crosses the gap we might be something that crosses back. So you don't gamble. You don't wait to find
out if the harmless thing stays harmless or the silent thing stays silent. You end it now, cheaply, while
it's cheap, and you don't even hate it, and you don't stay to watch, and you go quiet again, because the
one thing worse than a stranger in the dark is *being* the stranger who just lit himself up by taking a
shot."

The archive was very quiet. Somewhere far off the floods came up, and the high windows went from
match-colour to nothing.

"That's the forest," Ridgeway said, and it was the only time Brandt ever heard him say it as a whole thing,
and even then he said it like a man laying down a weight he didn't want, with all the qualifiers a careful
man keeps, none of which made it any lighter. "I think — and I'd give a great deal to be wrong, and I've
spent six weeks in this room trying to be — I think every civilization that lasts long enough to do this
math arrives at the same place. Stay silent. Stay hidden. And if you ever find someone who didn't get
quiet in time, deal with it before it grows. Not out of malice. Out of arithmetic. The kind ones died of
their kindness a long time ago, and what's left is a sky full of very old, very patient survivors, all of
them holding still, all of them listening, all of them having learned the one lesson the loud don't live to
learn." He looked at the drawer under his hand. "We just rang the dinner bell. And then we spent a hundred
and ten years filing schemes to survive being heard, when the only thing that was ever going to save us was
to never have made a sound."

Okonkwo said, "Then we're already dead. If you're right we've been dead since the Hail and everything
since is just the clock."

"No," Ridgeway said. And he turned around, and for the first time since the gate Brandt saw something move
behind the dry careful nothing of his face, something that wasn't hope, hope was for the children who threw
stones, but was hope's harder older cousin, the thing you build when hope's gone. "If we can't win and we
can't run and we can't hide and we can't talk — and we can't, I've read the whole wall, it's all here —
then there's exactly one thing left. We can't make ourselves safe. But we might be able to make ourselves
*not worth it*. And that's a different problem, and it's the only one on this wall nobody's solved, because
it's the only one that requires us to be willing to do something worse than die."

Brandt, in the corner, who had spent nineteen years guarding a thing she'd called a ghoul's idea, felt the
floor of her stomach go, because she understood before Okonkwo did, the way she always understood the
moment a thing was about to turn, that he was talking about the pin. The worst idea the species ever had.
The one behind her fence. And that the dry quiet man in the headstone room hadn't come to add to the wall.

He'd come to finish it.
