# Foreword

This book owes a debt it cannot repay and will not hide.

Across three novels published between 2008 and 2010, Liu Cixin built the most serious account our genre
has of a frightening idea: that a universe full of life would not be full of friends. He called the shape
of it a dark forest — every civilization a hunter moving silent between the trees, certain that the only
safe stranger is a quiet one and the only certainly safe stranger is a dead one. He reasoned it out the
way you'd reason out a proof, from two plain premises that nobody can argue with, and the conclusion came
down like a blade. Then he spent three books making you feel it: the patience of a watcher that can reach
across light-years, the single object that crosses a system and ends a fleet without slowing down, the
long cold arithmetic of a species learning that it had been loud in a place where loud gets you killed.

*The Long Silence* is not those books. It does not retell them, borrow their people, or move their
furniture into a new room. What it borrows is the engine underneath — the theory, which belongs to the
form now and not to any one set of words: that survival is the first need, that matter is finite, that
goodwill cannot be verified across the dark, and that a civilization clever enough to broadcast its
position has therefore made the one mistake the universe does not forgive. The names here are ours. The
world is ours. The watcher and the weapon and the bleak deterrent that holds the line for a hundred years
are ours, built to different physics. The thesis is meant to rhyme. The thesis is the only thing meant to
rhyme.

There are two things this book is also trying to do, and it should say them plainly, because they are the
reason it exists at all. The first is to read as English — written in it, not carried into it. The
original came to most of us in translation, and for all the skill of that work, some of it kept the grain
of another language, the feel of a thought first had elsewhere. This one is built to have been born here,
on the tongue it's printed in. The second is to never be dull. Even an admirer will tell you the source
had stretches that stalled, where a marvellous idea was explained at you instead of done to you. This
book treats every page as a debt to the reader's attention, and means to pay it.

Arjuna Badger Press makes work this way on purpose, and says so on purpose. The house has written an
unlicensed homage before — a *Warhammer 40,000* novel that claimed nothing it had no right to claim, and
a book after Philip K. Dick that took his machine and never his text — and the rule is the same one again:
take the engine, never the words; disclose the debt; do not pretend the idea was yours. You are holding a
craft exercise that wants very badly to also be a real book. Whether it earns that is for you to decide,
which is the only honest way it could be decided.

— *Arjuna Badger Press*
