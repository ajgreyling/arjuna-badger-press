# Chapter 12 — The Impossible Season

The whiteboard had come from the state office, wheeled out on protesting casters into the field station's back room, and Tobias had been waiting for it the way other people wait for a meal. He wiped it down twice though it was already clean. He uncapped a marker, sniffed it, capped it. He had been carrying the timeline in his head since the levee, and his head, when it carried a thing too long, started to ache with the wanting to put it down.

"Okay," he said. "Okay. So."

"He's been like this since the diner," said Mara, the fixer, from the doorway, arms folded, watching him the way she watched everyone — for the tell, the seam. She found his soothing rather than alarming. His tells were honest. "Let him build it."

Dr. Lemoine sat in a folding chair near the window with a cup of coffee gone cold an hour ago. She was the reason they were all here and not at a hotel bar — the state-site archaeologist, born forty miles south, with a grandmother who had picked cotton within sight of these ridges and a doctorate that said in dry institutional language what the grandmother had said plainer: *this is ours, and you are guests.* She had agreed to watch Tobias do his thing. Agreeing to watch was not the same as agreeing.

Tobias wrote a number at the top of the board. **238,000.**

"Cubic meters," he said. "That's Mound A. Just the bird mound. Not the ridges, not the smaller mounds, just the one. Twenty-two meters tall." He drew a lopsided shape that was meant to be a bird and was, in fact, a potato with intentions. Nobody corrected him. "Kidder's number. WashU. It's the published figure."

"It's the published figure," Lemoine agreed. Her voice gave nothing.

"So." He drew a line. Under it: **basket = 30 to 50 lb.** Under that: **call it 40.** "A basket load. That's what they carried. No wheel — no draft animals — no shovels even, not metal ones. You scoop the dirt, you fill the basket, you carry it on your back or your hip or your head, you dump it, you walk back. That's the whole machine. That's the entire technology."

"That's the entire technology," said the archivist, Wen, from the corner where she had three books open and a fourth balanced on her knee. "Kidder's wording is that it was moved 'basket by basket.' The microstratigraphy shows the loads. You can see them. Individual dumps. Men, women, children — the load sizes vary, the smaller ones are kids."

"Children," Lemoine said, and now there was something in it, a small weight. "People forget the children. They picture a labor gang. It was families."

Tobias paused, marker hovering. He liked it when a fact had a person inside it. He didn't always know what to *do* with the person, but he liked knowing they were there. He wrote **kids too** in the corner, smaller, and felt better.

"So here's the math," he said, and the room settled, because this was the part he was good at and everyone knew it. The numbers steadied him the way water steadies a level. "238,000 cubic meters. Fill weighs — call it loess and clay, mixed, wet, because it's Louisiana and it's always wet — call it 1,600 kilos a cubic meter. That's two-fifty, two-sixty million kilos of dirt. Round it. Quarter of a billion kilos. Moved by hand."

The LENS — Cole, who had not lifted his camera in twenty minutes, which for him was a long fast — let out a low sound that wasn't quite a laugh.

"Forty pounds a basket is eighteen kilos," Tobias went on. "So you need —" he wrote, the marker squeaking — "fourteen million basket loads. Give or take. To make the one mound."

"Fourteen million," Mara repeated. She was doing her own math, the kind that didn't go on boards. Fourteen million of anything was a logistics problem, and logistics problems were where the money lived, where the grift lived, where she lived. "And the timeline."

This was the part Tobias had been holding longest, and his hand actually trembled a little setting it down.

"Ninety days," he said. "That's the outside. Kidder says months — thirty to ninety days for the main build of Mound A. Because of the dirt." He turned to Lemoine, almost pleading, wanting her to confirm the beautiful terrible thing. "Because there's no weathering between the layers. Right? That's the whole point. If you stop building — if you walk away for a wet season, even for a few weeks of Louisiana rain — the top of your dirt pile gets a *skin*. It rains, it washes, it dries, little gullies, a crust, leaves blow in and rot. You'd see it in the stratigraphy. A rain-wash surface. A pause-line."

"You'd see it," Lemoine said.

"And it's not there." He said it like a confession. "Twenty-two meters of fill and there's no pause-line. None. The dirt went up wet-on-wet, fast, continuous. You can't *fake* not-raining in northeast Louisiana for the time it takes to do this slow. So it wasn't slow. The ground itself is the clock, and the clock says one push. One season. They started, and they did not stop, and then the mountain was there."

Silence in the back room. Outside, thunder shouldered along the horizon, theatrical, on cue, and Cole's hand twitched toward the camera and didn't go.

Wen turned a page. "Kidder compares the soil engineering to Roman concrete," she said quietly. "Chinese rammed earth. They mixed the fills on purpose — different soils, to control shrink and swell. So it wouldn't crack itself apart when it dried, wouldn't slump in the wet. Three thousand years and it's barely eroded. That's not a pile. That's a designed material."

"They knew their dirt," Lemoine said.

---

Here is where the chapter ought to tell you it can't be done, and walk away satisfied. The crew had assumed the same. So they had tried.

Tobias flipped the whiteboard to its second face, where, two days before, he had recorded the experiment they were all still sore from. They had marked off a small bank by the cane on the back forty, gotten permission, gotten baskets — real woven ones, sized right, weighed — and the four of them plus two grad students and Jakobus had spent a morning being a society without kings.

"We moved," Tobias read off the board, in the flat voice he used for results he didn't like, "in three and a half hours, with healthy backs and bottled water and a wheelbarrow we agreed not to use — about nine cubic meters. Six people."

"Felt like more," Cole said.

"Felt like a great deal more," said Mara, who had blisters under her rings.

"Nine cubic meters," Tobias said. "Six people. Three and a half hours. Scale it." He didn't even write it; he'd done it in his head at two in the morning and not slept after. "To do 238,000 cubic meters at our rate, six people work for — call it ten thousand days. Twenty-seven years. So you don't use six people."

"You use the crowd," Mara said. The official story's whole load-bearing wall. *You don't need a king. You need a big-enough crowd and a reason.*

"You use thousands," Tobias agreed. "Say two thousand workers, swapping out, eight hours a day, ninety days. Now the per-person number is — sane. It's hard but it's sane. A few hundred basket-loads a day each. People have done harder for a wage." He set the marker down. His relief was visible, a release in the shoulders. The math *closed.* It actually closed. The skeptics were right: no magic needed. Just bodies.

And then, because he was the kind of mind that could not leave a closed door without checking it was really a door, he picked the marker back up.

"Except," he said.

Lemoine's eyes came up from her cold coffee.

"Two thousand people," Tobias said slowly, "in one place, in the Louisiana summer, for ninety days. With no farms." He looked at Wen. "There's no agriculture. Right? Tell me I have that right."

"No agriculture," Wen said. "No maize, no field crops, no granaries. Hunter-fisher-gatherers. That's not contested. That's the load-bearing strangeness of the whole site — it's monumental and it's *forager.* The two things aren't supposed to go together. Monuments are supposed to wait for farms and kings."

"So nobody's storing grain to feed the build," Tobias said. He was speaking faster now, the pattern lighting up across his vision the way it did, the wrongness of it, the gap where a gear should mesh and didn't. "You can't bank calories without farms. So you're feeding two thousand people *out of the swamp,* in real time, for ninety days, while they're too busy hauling dirt to hunt and fish full time. That's — that's not a building problem anymore. That's a *supply* problem. That's the part the dirt-math hides. Moving the earth is the easy part once you have the crowd. Getting the crowd *there.* Holding them. Feeding them. Out of nothing but what the land gives, and the land doesn't give two thousand people's dinner for a season, not without — without—"

He ran out of words and stood there with his hand against the board.

"Without somebody making them," Mara finished, soft, and her face had the look it got when she found the seam in someone's pitch — not triumph, something colder, more careful. "That's the move every consortium makes, by the way. When you see a big coordinated thing, you assume a boss. You assume extraction, a man at the top taking his cut of the labor. Because that's the only way *we* know how to get two thousand people to do something hard for ninety days." She nodded at the board, at the absent king. "It's how the Heritage people read this place, too. They look at Mound A and they see a CEO. They see a project they could have managed. They want to find the boardroom."

"And there isn't one," Lemoine said.

"There isn't one," Mara agreed.

---

They had laid it all on the board now. The quarter-billion kilos. The fourteen million baskets. The pause-line that wasn't there. The dirt that outlasted Rome. The crowd the math demanded. The food the land couldn't bank. And nowhere — nowhere in the post rings the magnetometer had found ringing the plaza, sixty-three meters across, nowhere in the cooking-ball middens, nowhere in three thousand years of held-together engineered soil — a palace. A throne. A storehouse. A scribe's mark. A name.

Tobias turned from the board to Lemoine, and his face was open in the way that made strangers underestimate him and friends protect him.

"So the data says yes," he said. "It's real. Ninety days. The ground proves it can't have been slow, and the math proves a crowd that size can do it, and the soil proves they knew exactly what they were doing. I came here thinking I'd find the hole in the *can.* And there isn't one. They could. They did. I believe it now, completely." He swallowed. "I just don't understand the *why.* Two thousand people walk in from — everywhere, the stone's coming from a thousand kilometers, this is a continental thing — they walk in, they raise a mountain in a summer without a king to make them or a god's-honest harvest to feed them, and then —"

"And then they walk away," Lemoine said.

"And then they walk away," Tobias echoed. "No one inherits it. No dynasty builds on it. It just — it gets *finished,* and then everyone goes home, wherever home was, and it sits there for three thousand years holding its shape in the rain because they engineered it to, holding a shape nobody's living in." He spread his hands. "I can tell you how. I can tell you how to the cubic meter. I cannot tell you the *reason,* and the reason is the only thing that makes any of the rest of it move."

He had laid the whole engine on the table, every part labeled, every measurement cited, and the engine had no ignition.

Lemoine set her cold coffee down on the windowsill. She stood, and she walked to the board, and she looked at it the way you'd look at a child's drawing of your own house — fond, and aware of everything it left out. She picked up the eraser. She did not use it. She held it a moment, then set it back in the tray.

"You did good work," she said, and she meant it; it wasn't a dismissal. "Better than the consortium's people, who never once asked about the rain-wash. They went straight to the visitor's center floor plan." A dry beat. "Numbers don't scare you. That's rare. Most folks who come through here, the numbers are the thing they want to be scared of. They *want* the impossible. It feels better than the truth, which is harder."

"Which is what?" Mara asked.

Lemoine looked out the window, where the thunder had moved on without spending and the light was going long and gold across the outermost ridge, the one a kilometer and a quarter from edge to edge, soft as a sleeping animal under the grass.

"That they could," she said. "That ordinary people, with baskets and their own backs and their children working beside them, decided to, and did, and the deciding is the part I can't hand you off a whiteboard." She turned. Her eyes went over each of them, and rested longest, oddly, on Cole, on the lowered camera. "We know *how* they built it. To the basket. The how is on the board, and you got most of it right." A pause she let sit. "What called them here, and why they stopped — that's not in the dirt. The dirt is honest about how. The dirt has nothing to say about why."

Tobias looked at the board. At the closed math. At the open thing the closed math sat inside, like a perfect engine bolted to no road.

"So I had it backwards," he said slowly. "I thought the ninety days was the impossible part."

"The ninety days is the easy part," Lemoine said. "The ninety days is just arithmetic and willing arms." She picked her coffee back up, found it cold, made a face, kept it anyway. "The impossible part is what was worth ninety days to two thousand people who didn't have to come, weren't made to come, and never came again."

Outside, the gold deepened and the cane went to shadow. Cole's hand found the camera at last and did not lift it. Nobody told him to.
