# Chapter 11 — Negotiations

The diner in Epps had a laminated map of the parish under the glass on every table, and somebody had spilled coffee on West Carroll a long time ago, so the whole northeast corner of the county wore a brown stain shaped vaguely like a question mark. I kept finding my thumb on it.

Caldwell — that was the consortium's man, Heritage Development, the name embroidered on his polo in a green so soft it was practically an apology — had taken the corner booth and ordered for the table before any of us sat down. Sweet tea, fried catfish, hush puppies. "You have to," he said, smiling, when I raised an eyebrow at the plates already coming. "It's the best in the parish. I'd be doing you a disservice."

He wasn't wrong about the catfish. That was the thing about Caldwell I clocked in the first ninety seconds, the thing I'd been hired by nobody to clock but couldn't stop clocking anyway: he was never wrong about the small stuff. The small stuff was where you built trust. He knew the waitress's kids' names. He knew the high school had lost three teachers to budget last year. He knew, to the dollar, what the state's interpretive budget for Poverty Point had been cut to, and he said the number out loud with the specific grief of a man who'd memorized it because it made his pitch.

I do this for a living, more or less. Not the fixing — the reading. You spend enough years being the kid who couldn't stop watching the room because the room kept changing the rules on you, and you stop being able to turn it off. Everybody at this table was performing. The trick was knowing what each of them was performing *for*.

Caldwell was performing *plausibility*. That was his whole product.

"Here's the shape of it," he said, and laid a folder on the coffee-stained map, square to the table edge, the way people do when they want you to feel that the thing inside is already settled. "Forty million over six years. A new visitor center — and I mean a real one, climate control, a theater, a café. Trained interpretive staff. Marketing reach we can actually measure. Right now this place gets, what." He looked at Dr. Ardoin. "Forty thousand visitors a year?"

"About that," she said.

"We can get you two hundred thousand."

The young engineer, who'd been silent and folding his straw wrapper into smaller and smaller squares, said, "Two hundred thousand people on those ridges."

"On managed paths," Caldwell said, smooth. "Boardwalk. You protect the resource *by* channeling the traffic. That's preservation, not despite the visitors — *because* of them."

It was a good line. I watched Dr. Ardoin not react to it, which is its own kind of reaction.

She was the state-site archaeologist, and she'd grown up forty minutes from here, and she had the stillness of somebody who had heard a great many people explain her own place to her and had decided, long ago, to let them finish. Her family had been in this parish since before it was a parish. When she walked the ridges she didn't walk them like a guide; she walked them like a kitchen, the way you move through a room you don't have to look at to cross.

The Lens hadn't lifted the camera. That was something. She had it on the table, lens cap on, and she was watching Caldwell's hands the way I was, except she was watching them for the frame and I was watching them for the tell. His hands were clean. Genuinely. That's what I want you to understand about him, because it's what made him dangerous: there was no villain in those clean hands. He believed every word.

"Let me say the part nobody likes to say," Caldwell said, leaning in. "This is one of the great wonders of the ancient Americas, and almost nobody on earth knows it exists. You've got six C-shaped ridges out there, a kilometer and a quarter across the outer one. You've got Mound A — twenty-two meters, two hundred and thirty-eight thousand cubic meters of earth, and the kicker, the *kicker*—" he pointed at the engineer like he'd already guessed which of us would care most, and he'd guessed right — "is that they think it went up in *months*. Maybe ninety days. Built by people with no kings, no farms, no writing. No metal. No wheel. No animals to pull a thing. Hands and baskets. That's it."

"That's it," the engineer agreed, very quietly, and I knew that tone. That was the tone of a man who'd tried to move dirt last week and had feelings about it.

We had, in fact, tried. Out behind the field station, with a marked plot and good shovels and a borrowed split-oak basket the curator swore was the right size, and a stopwatch. We moved earth from a borrow pit to a mound footprint, thirty-to-fifty-pound loads, the way the microstratigraphy says they did it — men, women, and the children too, you can read the kids in the soil, the small uneven dumps. We did it for four hours in real Louisiana June, and the gumbo clay grabbed the shovel like it had opinions, and the basket strap cut a stripe across your shoulder that you felt for two days, and at the end of four hours the engineer ran the numbers and his face did a thing.

Because the numbers don't go the easy way. That's the potato. Everyone wants the math to be friendly — big crowd, lots of hands, of course you can raise a hill. But you start multiplying. Two hundred and thirty-eight thousand cubic meters. A loaded basket is maybe an eighth of a cubic meter, generous. You're walking it up a growing slope in heat that makes the air shimmer over the cane. You need the dirt *dug*, and you need it *carried*, and you need the carriers *fed*, and the feeding of a few thousand foragers who grow nothing is its own mountain, an invisible one, built out of fish and nuts and whatever the river gave that season. We did the back-of-the-napkin and the napkin said: you'd need thousands of people, all of them, every daylight hour, for the whole of those ninety days, and not one of them deciding on day forty that they'd rather be fishing.

"And nobody made them," the archivist said. She'd been reading the menu like it was a footnote. "That's the part the brochure won't fit. No chief. The leadership's read as situational — somebody organizes the build and then it dissolves. There's no palace in the ground. There's no throne. There's no big house where the big man lived." She set the menu down. "There's a kingdom's worth of *work* and no king to point at."

Caldwell loved this. You could see him loving it. "Right! And that's the *story*. That's what sells. 'They built it together, no bosses, no kings—'" he spread his hands, framing an invisible banner — "'the first democracy in the Americas.' People eat that up."

"Did it," I said.

"Did it what?"

"Did it dissolve, or did they just walk away." I tapped the coffee stain. "Microstratigraphy says no weathering surfaces between the fill layers. No rain-wash, no pause. They didn't build it over generations and let the slopes settle and come back. They went *bang*, all at once, no breaks, and then—" I shrugged. "Three thousand years of nobody on the hill, and it's barely eroded, because they mixed the soils on purpose so it'd hold. They engineered it to *last*. And then they didn't stay to use it."

Dr. Ardoin looked at me for the first time like I'd said something she'd let me have.

"Kidder compares it to Roman concrete," she said. "Chinese rammed earth. Deliberate. They controlled the shrink-swell so it wouldn't crack itself apart. Three thousand years and Mound A's still got its shape." A beat. "We know *how*. Down to the basket. We can read the kids' loads in the dirt."

"And what it's for?" Caldwell asked, and he asked it lightly, the way you ask a question you've already decided is rhetorical, the way you ask it because you intend to be the one to answer it on the brochure.

Dr. Ardoin sipped her tea.

"That's a long conversation," she said.

That was all. *That's a long conversation.* And it landed in the booth like a flat stone going into still water, no splash, just the going-under and the rings.

Caldwell took it for deflection. I took it for a door she'd decided not to open in front of him. There's a difference, and you learn it by watching who *doesn't* talk and clocking what their hands do while they don't. Her hands stayed around the tea glass. Steady.

He pivoted to the folder. This was the part I'd been waiting for, because in my experience the strings always come about eleven minutes after the number.

"The center would carry the interpretation," Caldwell said. "Unified narrative. You've got — and I say this with respect, Doctor — you've got *eleven* different stories out there right now. The solstice people, the trade-route people, the 'we don't know' people. From a *visitor* standpoint that's chaos. You give somebody a clean line they can take home. 'A gathering. A wonder. Built together.' We'd want approval on the materials, obviously, brand consistency—"

"Approval," the archivist repeated, gently, to her water glass.

"Editorial alignment," Caldwell said. "And on the access side — there'd be premium experiences. Sunrise tours of Mound A. We'd cap general admission on event days. That's *conservation*. And a profit share back to the parish, real money, not somebody's grant cycle that gets killed in committee." He said *killed in committee* with a small flinch, like a man recalling a funeral.

I added it up while he talked, the way I always add it up. They'd fund the place and own the sentence. They'd protect the ridges by deciding who got to stand on them. They'd give the parish a cut of a story about people who took no cut from each other.

And here's the thing I won't pretend: it wasn't a bad deal. That's what kept getting me. The center would be real. The boardwalk would save the ridges from a thousand careless feet. Forty thousand visitors a year is a slow death by indifference. Caldwell wasn't selling poison. He was selling a *frame*, glass and climate-controlled, and the price of the frame was the picture.

"The solstice alignment," the engineer said abruptly. "You'd put that in the theater?"

"Centerpiece," Caldwell said. "People love an astronomy hook."

"It's contested," the engineer said. "Haag and Purrington don't even agree where the center of the site *is*. You move the center, you move every sightline. There's no consensus the aisles point at anything." He frowned at his shredded straw wrapper. "If you put it on a screen with no asterisk, you've decided a thing the field hasn't."

Dr. Ardoin's mouth moved, the smallest amount. Not a smile. The cousin of one.

"That," she said to the engineer, "is exactly the problem with a clean line."

And there it was — the gap between them, the consortium and the keeper, laid out plain over the catfish. Caldwell needed a sentence that would fit on a wall. Dr. Ardoin had spent her life inside the part that won't fit on a wall: the *we don't know*, held not as a failure but as a thing you protect, the way you'd protect a spring. He thought the mystery was a marketing problem. She knew it was the asset.

She wasn't going to say that to him. She was going to let him keep believing he was being generous.

"I'll take this to the council," she said, touching the folder but not taking it. "And to the elders. It's not mine to sign. It's not the state's, either, not really." A look around the booth, including us, excluding nobody, which is its own kind of authority. "People here have to live with whatever the place becomes. So they decide."

"Of course," Caldwell said. "Of course. That's exactly the partnership we—"

"It'll divide them," she said.

She said it not to him. She said it to the window, where you could see, past the gas station, the low green line of the floodplain going on toward the river.

"Some of them've watched this parish lose everything that ever brought a paycheck," she said. "School, the mill, the young people. Forty million is forty million. There's families'd take that money and not blink, and I won't say they're wrong to want it. And there's others—" she paused, and I watched her choose the word, and the word she chose was small — "others remember when the highway came through, and what it cost, and what the brochure said it'd give us that it never gave."

The booth was quiet. Even Caldwell, who I'd have bet money was incapable of three consecutive silent seconds, let it sit.

The Lens lifted her camera. Slow. She didn't shoot Caldwell, and she didn't shoot the folder. She shot Dr. Ardoin's hands around the sweating glass, and through the window behind her the green line of the bottomland, and I knew without seeing the frame that it would say more than anything the four of us had said all afternoon — a woman holding still while other people decided how loud to make her home.

"Take your time, Doctor," Caldwell said, finally, and he meant it warmly, and that was the worst part. "There's no rush. The offer holds."

But there was a rush, and we all knew it. Offers like that hold exactly as long as the man who made them needs them to.

She walked out ahead of us into the heat. The cicadas were going like a downed power line. She stopped in the gravel lot and looked east, where you couldn't see the ridges from here but you could *feel* them, the way you feel a held breath in a room.

"You want to know what it's for," she said. Not a question. She'd clocked me clocking, the whole lunch. She'd out-read me reading her, and let me think I was the one with the eyes. "Everybody who comes wants that. The aliens people. The democracy people. Now the forty-million people."

"I want to know," I said, "what happens to *you* if they get the sentence."

She looked at me a moment longer than was comfortable, which I've found is the look people give you when you've surprised them by aiming at the right thing.

"Come back Thursday," she said. "Council meets at the church. You can sit at the back."

She got in her truck. The door had a dent in it the exact shape of nothing in particular, just years.

Behind me, through the diner glass, Caldwell was paying the check and learning the busboy's name.
