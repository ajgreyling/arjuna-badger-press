# Chapter 14 — The Walk-Away

The light at the end of a Louisiana day did something to the ridges that no daylight could. It came in low and yellow off the cane, and the six nested arcs that Mara had spent two weeks failing to see — flat, gentle, near-invisible swells that the brochures kept calling *monumental* — caught the rake of it and stood up out of the ground like a thing remembered rather than a thing measured.

She had the camera up before she'd decided to. That was the old reflex, the one the series kept asking her to lower. Some part of her had spent a war learning that the moment you most wanted to look away was the moment worth the frame. She didn't trust the reflex anymore. She trusted it completely. Both were true and she had stopped trying to choose.

Through the viewfinder the ridges held their curve. C after C after C, six of them, opening toward the bayou like cupped hands, the outermost a kilometer and change from horn to horn. You could stand on one and not know you stood on anything. You had to get the sun this low, this kind, to feel the swell of it under your boots — three thousand years of dirt that the river had spent every wet season since trying to take back, and the dirt just sat there. Held its line. Refused.

Darnell Boudreaux walked ahead of them along the crest of Ridge 3 with his hands in his jacket pockets and the unhurried gait of a man on ground he'd been walking his whole life. He was the state archaeologist here, which was the smallest true thing you could say about him. His grandmother's people had fished this water. He'd read more of this place than anyone alive and he wore it like he wore the jacket, easy and without ceremony.

"You're chasing the gold light," he said, not turning around. "Everybody does. The geophysics boys, the drone people. Always at sundown."

"It's the only time the place lets you see it," Mara said.

"It's the only time you let yourself."

She let that sit, because it was probably correct.

Behind her, Tomás — the engineer, young, head down, walking the ridge crest the way he walked everything, like the ground owed him an answer — was counting his own footsteps under his breath and converting. He'd been doing the math for days. He couldn't stop doing the math. It was how he loved a thing.

"Two hundred thirty-eight thousand cubic meters," he said, to nobody. "Just Mound A. The ridges on top of that. Thirty to fifty pounds a basket." He stopped. Started again. "If you put a thousand people on it — a thousand, all day, every day —"

"You can't put a thousand foragers on it every day," said Petra. The archivist had a paperback going soft at the spine in one hand, her thumb in it, marking a place. "Foragers don't have a granary. There's no storehouse. No silo of corn to feed the work crew through the dry season because there's no corn. There's no corn anywhere on this site, in any layer, for the whole run of it." She looked up. "That's the part nobody films."

That was the part nobody filmed. Mara knew it cold now, two weeks in. The official story was the marvel and the marvel was honest: people who hunted and fished and gathered, who planted nothing, ruled no one, were ruled by no one — and they raised this. They moved a quarter of a million cubic meters of earth in baskets carried on the hip, basket by basket, no wheel to roll it, no ox to pull it, no bronze to dig it, the dirt going up the slope on the backs of men and women and — the microstratigraphy was clear on this, the small loads tucked between the large ones — children. A whole society's worth of children carrying their thirty pounds up a hill that became a mountain.

And then the skeptic's good clean answer, which Mara had come to respect the way you respect a thing that won't let you off the hook: *you don't need a king for that. You don't need kings or crops. You need a crowd big enough and a reason good enough.* No mystery. Just people, deciding together, and following the deciding all the way up.

She'd believed it. She still believed it. The trouble was the dirt.

—

They came off Ridge 3 and crossed the plaza, and here the fish in Mara's head — the one that heckled, the one that had kept her alive in places that wanted her dead by refusing to let any official anything go down whole — the fish surfaced and said: *flattest haunted nothing you ever saw, isn't it.*

It was. The plaza was a grass field. A man could mow it. And under it, the magnetometry had found rings — circles of postholes, big ones, twenty-five meters across, sixty-three meters across, timber set in arcs by people who then pulled the timber up or let it rot, leaving only the dark stain in the dirt for a machine to read three millennia later. Nobody knew what the rings did. Petra had read the papers. The papers said: rings.

"You stand here at the right time of year," Tomás said, turning slow, sighting down the gaps in the ridges like a man lining up a shot, "and the sun's supposed to come up through the —"

"Supposed to," said Petra, before he could finish. "Haag put the center one place. Purrington put it somewhere else. Move the center, you move the alignment. They don't agree on where the middle of this thing is, so they can't agree on what it points at." She found her thumb-marked page without looking. "There's no consensus on the sky here. There's people who want one very badly."

Tomás lowered his arm. He didn't sulk. He just filed it, the way he filed everything, into the place where the answers weren't yet. Mara loved him for the lack of sulk.

Darnell had stopped in the middle of the grass and turned to watch them argue, and there was something in his face — not amusement exactly. The patience of a man who'd heard the sun-through-the-gap theory from forty earnest visitors and would hear it from forty more and would not take it from any of them, because the not-knowing was his to keep too.

"Y'all want to know what it points at," he said.

"I want to know why they left," Mara said.

It came out of her before she'd weighed it. The light was going. The cane had gone from gold to the color of a held breath. She lowered the camera.

—

Because that was the hole. Not the moving of the dirt — the dirt was a marvel and the marvel was answerable, even if the answering was hard. The hole was after.

Kidder's work on Mound A was the thing she couldn't put down. Twenty-two meters of fill, and when they cut into it and read the layers the way you'd read tree rings or ice cores, they found — nothing. No nothing. No weathering surfaces between the loads. No rain-wash. No silt skin that says *here the work stopped and a wet season came and the exposed dirt sat and took the weather before the next load went on*. There was none of that. Layer met layer with no time between them. Which meant the mountain went up without a pause. Without a winter. Without a *we'll come back next year and finish*.

A matter of months. Thirty to ninety days. A whole hill in a season, all hands, all at once, no breath taken — and Kidder reaching for the only comparisons big enough to hold it, Roman concrete, Chinese rammed earth, the deliberate engineered mixing of soils so the thing would shed water and hold its shape for three thousand years. Which it had. Which it was doing right now, under her boots, in the dark coming on.

And then they walked away.

That was the part that stood the hair up on Mara's arms in a way the war hadn't managed in years. You gather thousands. You feed them — somehow, with no farm, no store, the river and the woods and the nut harvest stretched over a crowd that should have stripped this floodplain bare in a week. You raise a mountain in ninety days. And then there is no king to crown, no palace to build around, no heir to hand it to, no granary to defend. The leadership that ran the build — and something ran it, you don't move 238,000 cubic meters by accident — the leadership just *dissolves*. Goes back to being people. And the crowd scatters back into the woods and the water, and the rings rot, and the mound holds its line through three thousand years of seasons while the society that raised it does not hold together past the season that raised it.

*Built to last,* the fish said. *By people who didn't.*

Mara turned that over and didn't like how clean it sat. She distrusted clean.

—

They walked out toward the western edge as the last of it went. Darnell led, and they followed in a loose line along the dike, the bayou somewhere to the left making the small sounds water makes when it's deciding whether to rise.

"My grandmother," Darnell said, "would bring us out here when we were small. Not to teach. We didn't do teaching. She'd just walk us along the ridge and not say much." He stopped at the edge where the engineered ground gave way to the river's claim. "People come and ask me why they left like it's a thing went wrong. Like a building burned down. Like there's a body to find."

Mara had the camera at her hip. She did not raise it. The series kept asking her to lower it and here, now, she lowered it.

"They feed thousands with no farm," Tomás said. He'd stopped doing the math out loud. "They raise a mountain in ninety days. And there's no —" He stopped, hunting the word with that literal precision of his, refusing any word but the right one. "There's no *more*. After. No bigger thing they were building toward. They just — stop. At the top."

"That's the part your machines can't read," Darnell said. Not unkind.

"Kidder read the layers," Petra offered, quiet. "He can show you there was no pause. He can't show you why there was no second one."

"No." Darnell looked out at the water. "He can't."

Mara watched him in the failing light — the man who out-read every one of them on this ground, who knew which papers were sound and which were somebody's wishing, who could have told them the soil recipe and the basket count and the trade routes that brought stone here from a thousand kilometers off, debated in the detail but continental in the fact of it, gray flint and steatite and copper moving across a third of a continent into this one bend of one Louisiana river. He knew all of it. And she could see him deciding, the way she'd seen men decide things in worse places, what he would hand them and what was not theirs.

"You want me to tell you why they walked away," he said.

"I want to know if you know," Mara said. Which was the honest thing, and she watched it land.

He was quiet a long time. The bayou talked to itself. Up behind them in the dark the ridges held their six soft curves and Mound A held its twenty-two meters and the whole engineered field of it held the line it had been built to hold, water shedding off the mixed soils the way it had shed for three thousand summers, the dirt refusing the river one more night.

"I think," Darnell said slowly, "that they didn't build it to keep."

Mara waited.

"That's the thing y'all can't get past. The consortium can't get past it either, but worse, because they need it to be a thing you keep so they can sell the keeping." He turned to face them, and his face was almost gone in the dusk, just the shape of a man on his own ground. "You came here for the why. The why's not mine to give you, because I don't have it, and the part I do have —" He stopped. Started again, choosing. "What I have is: it wasn't ours to keep. It was never the keeping. They came, they made the thing, and the making was the thing. Then they let it go back to being a place."

"And the mound?" Tomás again, soft now. "It lasted three thousand years. They engineered it to last."

"Yeah." Something moved across Darnell's face that might have been a smile. "They built it to outlive the keeping. That's not the same as building it to keep."

Mara felt the floor of the thing drop out from under her and refuse to fully open — the maybe sitting there in the dark, unanswered, the draught of it cold on her face. She did not raise the camera. There was no frame for it. That was the point. The local had handed them exactly as much as was his to hand and not one grain more, and the not-more was the gift.

The fish, for once, said nothing.

—

They were halfway back along the dike, the site behind them gone fully to silhouette, when the phone in Mara's jacket woke up. Then Petra's. Then a third, somewhere, Tomás patting his pockets.

The same email. Mara read it walking, the screen too bright in the dark, ruining her night vision the way bright things always did.

It was from the consortium's man — the project lead, the charming one, the one Mara's whole wiring screamed *grift* at and who genuinely, she'd decided, believed every word he said, which was worse. The subject line was warm. *Excited to move forward.* The body was warmer. They'd secured the state's preliminary sign-off. The interpretive center renderings were attached — glass and reclaimed timber, very tasteful, a gift shop, a path that kept visitors to the path. The funding was real, generous, more than the site had seen in a decade of cuts. And the close, the soft close, the close that her body knew before her mind did:

*We'd like to bring this to the descendant community council Thursday for a decision. We feel strongly the window for partnership is now — opportunities like this don't hold.*

Three days.

Mara stopped on the dike. Ahead of her in the dark Darnell had stopped too, his own phone lit in his hand, his face turned down to it, unreadable.

The water on the left made its small deciding sound.

*Window for partnership is now,* the fish said quietly, reading over her shoulder the way it did. *Opportunities like this don't hold.*

No. They never did. That was always the line. That was always exactly the line — said by the man who needs you to decide before you've finished looking, the same way every man who'd ever taken a thing from people who built it had needed them to decide before they'd finished looking.

She raised the camera then. Got Darnell in the last of the light, the phone glow on his face, the dark field of three thousand years behind him that had been built to outlive its own keeping, and now had three days to find out if it would have to.

"Thursday," Darnell said, to none of them, to the water. He put the phone away. "They want it Thursday."

"They want it before we've read it," Mara said.

"They want it before *we* have," he said.

And that, she thought, lowering the camera, was the whole thing. That was always the whole thing.
