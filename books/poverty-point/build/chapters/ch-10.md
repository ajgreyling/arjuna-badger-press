# Chapter 10 — Trade and Distance

The drawer stuck on its runners, swelled with August, and when Demetria worked it loose it came with the small thunder of stone on metal — a tray of grey points and blades and broken bits, each in its own cell of foam, each with a number in pencil so faint it had to be read at an angle to the light.

"Don't lift them out," she said. "Read them where they lie."

Sol — the archivist, who lived for exactly this kind of permission — leaned over the tray with both hands behind his back like a man at a museum case, which, he supposed, was what this was. The field station's collections room smelled of the river that wasn't far off and of the particular mustiness of card catalogs that have outlived the people who filled them. A box fan turned its head from side to side and refused to commit.

"These two," Sol said. "Grey, but — wrong grey. Cooler."

"Burlington chert." Demetria didn't look up from the logbook she was squaring against the table edge. "Mississippi Valley. Up by where Missouri and Illinois shake hands."

"And these are darker."

"Dover. Tennessee." She turned a page. "That blue-grey by your thumb is Fort Payne. Also Tennessee, but a different shelf of it." A pause, the kind she used like punctuation. "And the orange one your engineer's about to touch with his nose is Edwards Plateau. Central Texas."

Eli — the engineer — had in fact lowered his face to within a few inches of the orange blade and now froze, hands clasped, nose retreating. "I wasn't going to," he said.

"Everybody was going to."

Sol felt the shape of it land before he had the words. He pulled the parish map from the wall in his head — the river, the bluff, the gumbo flat — and then he started pulling threads off it, north and east and west, the way you pull lengths off a spool. Missouri. Tennessee. Texas. He had read the inventories on the flight in; reading a number on a page was one thing. Reading the stone in the drawer, with the river audible through the wall, was another.

"How far," he said. It wasn't really a question.

"Burlington's eight hundred kilometres if a crow flies it, and the crow doesn't flat-pack." Demetria finally looked up. She had a way of meeting your eye that made you check your own posture. "The crow also doesn't carry forty pounds of chert. Some of these raw materials come from a thousand kilometres out. Stone, not finished tools. They brought in the rock and made the blades here, on the ridges, and the debris tells us so — the flakes, the cores. You don't haul a thousand kilometres of debris. You haul the good stuff."

Veronique — the lens — had been quiet by the window, which was where she went when a room was telling on itself. Now she lifted the camera, not to her eye, just to her chest, the gesture of a woman deciding. "May I."

"The drawer, yes. Not the labels."

The shutter, when it came, was almost apologetic.

---

Here is the official story, and it is a good one, and it ought to be told with the lights up.

Three thousand years before the present, on a low rise above Bayou Macon, people who farmed nothing and crowned no one ran a continent's worth of trade through their front door. Stone came from the upper Mississippi, from the Ouachitas, from the Tennessee hills, from the far Texas plateau. Soapstone vessels came up from Georgia and Alabama. Galena — lead ore, for its bright weight and its shine — came from the river country to the north. And copper came, the soft red metal hammered cold into beads and ornaments, copper that for a long time everyone said came from the Great Lakes, a thousand miles and a continent's spine away.

No carts. No pack animals. No road in the sense you'd put a milepost on. A man, a woman, a basket, a canoe, a river, a season. Down the Mississippi, you could ride the current most of the way. Coming back up, you paid for every mile with your shoulders.

The skeptic's correction is healthy here, and you should hear it from her own mouth, because the skeptic is right: nobody needs a king to want a pretty blade. Trade doesn't require an empire; it requires desire and a route. People have always passed things hand to hand and hand to hand again, each exchange a small local fairness, the object travelling further than any single trader ever did. You don't need to imagine a man from Texas walking to Louisiana with a sack of rock. You need to imagine a hundred handshakes, each one short, the stone moving while the people mostly stay home.

That's the elegant answer, and it is probably most of the truth, and it deserves its applause.

Sol gave it the applause. Then he stood in the collections room with eight hundred kilometres of Burlington chert in a foam cell and felt the elegant answer leave a little water on the floor.

---

"Show me the down-tools," Eli said, which was not English but was the way he asked the question that mattered. *Show me where the easy story has to do the actual work.*

So they took it outside, because Eli could not think with his hands empty, and the field station had a sandbox of sorts — a tarp, river sand, a few of the replica baskets the education program used for school groups. He filled a basket to what Demetria allowed was a fair load. Thirty-five pounds. He hefted it.

"Hand to hand," he said. "Fine. So the stone moves in short hops. But the short hops still add up to a thousand kilometres of total carrying, just spread across people. Somebody hauled every kilometre. The kilometres don't get cheaper because you split them up. They get *survivable*."

"That's the whole trick of it," Demetria said. She had come out to watch, arms folded, and watching was a thing she did with the full weight of her attention, which was how you knew she was the one who'd out-read you on whatever came next. "Survivable isn't free. You move galena north to south. The Mississippi helps you. Now bring the copper the other way — upstream, or overland through people who may or may not feel like trading today. The river's not your friend going up. The river's the bill."

Eli set the basket down and looked at the tarp like it had insulted him. "I keep trying to make the network *cheap*," he said. "It won't go cheap. It'll go *possible*. Those aren't the same number."

"No," said Demetria. "They are not."

The fish, if it had been there, would have said: *funny how 'possible' keeps showing up exactly where you wanted 'easy.'* The fish was not there. But Sol heard it anyway, which was its own kind of haunting.

---

Inside, Sol had found the copper.

A single bead, in its own cell, dark with the patina of three millennia, no bigger than a knuckle. He read its number, found the number in the logbook, found in the logbook a citation in someone's tidy hand, and felt the floor tilt very gently the way it does when a fact you were sure of turns out to have grown a footnote while you weren't looking.

"This is sourced," he said. "Somebody ran the chemistry."

"Somebody ran the chemistry on a lot of it." Demetria came to stand beside him, and for the first time her voice carried something other than instruction — a kind of dry pleasure, the pleasure of a person watching an outsider arrive at the place where the easy version breaks. "For years the story was Great Lakes copper. It's a good story. It's a long line on the map, Lake Superior to Louisiana, and it makes the network look as big as it actually is."

"And?"

"And then people did the isotopes and the trace elements, on this material and material like it, and some of what everyone called Great Lakes copper turned out to be southern Appalachian. Closer. Different source. The line on the map didn't vanish — it moved." She let that settle. "The network's still continental. It's still astonishing. But the details — *which* copper, from *where*, by *which* hands — those are argued over in print, right now, by people who are not done arguing. You want to be careful which line you draw bold."

Sol looked at the bead. Eight hundred kilometres of chert he could trust. The copper had taught him caution in a single sentence.

"So the distance is real," he said slowly. "But the *route* is debated. The achievement's not in question. The map is."

"The map is *drafts*," Demetria said. "It's always drafts. People write me letters wanting a single clean diagram with arrows. I tell them: I can give you the stone, and I can give you the chemistry, and I can give you the argument. I can't give you the arrows. The arrows are where the honest people disagree."

She closed the drawer. It stuck, and she let it stick.

---

Veronique had drifted to the far wall, where someone had pinned a continental map with pins and thread — the education version, the one with the bold arrows, the one Demetria had just disowned. She photographed it anyway. Then she stepped back and photographed the empty middle of the room, the box fan, Demetria's hands flat on the closed drawer.

"You don't trust the arrows either," Sol said to her, quietly, because she'd taught him to read a thing by what she didn't shoot.

"I trust the stone," Veronique said. "I trust her hands on the drawer." She lowered the camera. The light caught the old burn-scar of the war in her, the thing the series kept asking her to put down. "Arrows are the part somebody added to make it a story. The drawer's the part that's true." A beat. "Doesn't mean the story's a lie. Means somebody wanted it to land harder than it earns."

Eli, coming back in with sand on his shins, said the thing none of them had said. "Wait. If the network's that big — Texas, Tennessee, Mississippi Valley, Appalachia, Georgia soapstone — then the gathering and the trade are the *same question*."

The room went still in the way a room goes still when the literal-minded one says the load-bearing thing.

"Say it again," Sol said.

"To raise the mound you need thousands of people in one season," Eli said, talking with his hands now, plotting it in the air. "To run a continental trade network you need contact across the whole eastern half of the country. What if those aren't two facts. What if the people who came to build also came to *trade* — or came to trade and *then* built — and the gathering is the thing that makes both possible. You don't need a king to move the stone *or* the dirt. You need a reason for everyone to be in one place at one time. The reason is the same reason."

Demetria looked at him for a long moment. It was not the look of correction. It was the look of someone deciding how much of the floor to give away, and to whom.

"That," she said, "is a question I'm allowed to enjoy and not answer." She tapped the closed drawer once. "We have the stone. We have the distances. We have the chemistry, with its fights. What we do not have — what I do not have, what Kidder does not have, what nobody pinning arrows to my wall has — is the invitation. We don't have the thing that said *come*. We have everybody arriving. We don't have the word that called them."

"And the going," Sol said. "You don't have the word that ended it either."

"No," said Demetria. "I don't have that one at all."

She did not reach past it. That was the thing Sol kept returning to, later — how she stood exactly at the edge of what the ground would tell her and did not lean over. The stone was hers to read and she read it cold. The *why* she held in an open hand, and the open hand was a decision, not an absence.

---

The turn came, as it always did, by phone.

Sol was the money-reader, the fixer, the one whose wiring had been built in rooms where you learned to hear the grift under the courtesy or you didn't eat. So when his phone lit with the link someone in the parish had forwarded — the consortium's new slide deck, leaked the way these things leak, eager — he read it the way Demetria read chert. By the cool grey of the wrong word.

The slide was beautiful. A continental map, the very one Demetria had just disowned, arrows and all, rendered in gold. Across the top, in a font that cost money:

**POVERTY POINT: AMERICA'S FIRST GLOBAL HUB.**

And below, in the body, the phrases that made Sol's stomach do the slow drop it did when a true thing was about to be sold:

*A 3,000-year-old network spanning a continent. A heritage of connection. In partnership with regional and international stakeholders, the Heritage Development Consortium will unlock Poverty Point's global story — and its global value.*

He read it twice. He handed the phone to Demetria without a word, because it was hers before it was his.

She read it once. Her face didn't change, which was worse than if it had.

"They took the arrows," she said.

"They took the arrows," Sol agreed. "The ones you won't draw. They drew them in gold and put a fence around them."

"*Global,*" Demetria said, and set the word down on the table like a chert blade with a label she didn't trust. "The network's continental. It's real. It's argued over by honest people. And they've turned the argument into a *brochure*." She slid the phone back. "You know the cruelest part?"

Through the wall, the river kept paying its bill, the way it had for three thousand years, the way it would whether or not anyone bought the slide.

"It's not even a lie," she said. "It's worse. It's the true thing, with the open question sanded off and a price tag glued where the question used to be."

Outside, the box fan turned its head, considered the room, and declined to commit.
