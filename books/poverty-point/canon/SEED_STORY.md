A monumental earthwork city rises from the Louisiana floodplain in a single, fevered season — not by a king’s command, not by a farming state, but by a gathering of foragers who leave behind no palaces, no writing, and no heir to the throne. The official story is a marvel: hunter-gatherers, without agriculture or hierarchy, build Poverty Point’s six great ridges and the 22-metre Mound A — 238,000 cubic meters of earth, basket by basket, in possibly as little as ninety days. The skeptics say: you don’t need kings or crops, just a big-enough crowd and a reason. The “potato” is literal: when modern teams try to move that much dirt — even with daylight, plans, and healthy backs — the math goes sideways, and the hill grows slow, not fast. But Kidder’s microstratigraphy says otherwise: no weathering, no rain-wash, no pause — just a single, relentless push, all hands, all at once. The hole: how does a society with no chiefs, no farms, and no written word summon thousands, feed them, and raise a mountain in a summer — and then walk away? The wink: what did they gather for, and why did they stop?

The chase: the crew races to read what Poverty Point was for — before a well-funded “heritage development” consortium (the adversary: profit, not conspiracy) moves to privatize, extract, and repackage the site’s story for modern gain. The local who out-reads them is a Louisiana state-site archaeologist, with deep roots in the descendant community, who holds the answer and the right to withhold it. The ND lead is the crew’s MONEY/fixer — the one who reads the grift, the extraction, and the modern stakes, not just the ancient puzzle. Jakobus, as ever, reads the made thing — the engineered earth, the basket-load math, the soil that holds its shape when all else washes away.

Back-matter promise: every measurement, date, and engineering feat is real and cited; the rapid-build evidence is peer-reviewed and open; the continental trade network is genuine but debated in detail; the “impossible” is not magic, but the residue — the speed, the gathering, the walk-away — remains. The official story earns its awe; the hole remains open. The function — what it was for — is the local’s to say, and the reader’s to wonder.
