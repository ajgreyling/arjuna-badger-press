# Chapter 18 — The Wink

Edith had photographed a hundred cars that did not come down a road. She knew the grammar of it. A car that meant to arrive committed to the descent; a car that meant to *watch* paused at the lip of the slope, where it could see the whole awning and the truck and the lane in one frame, where it could leave faster than it came.

This one paused.

She did not lift the camera. That was the discipline now, the thing she had been building rib by rib all month — the hand going to the bag by reflex and then *stopping*, lying open and empty on her own knee. A month ago she'd have had the long lens on the windscreen before she'd finished thinking about it, would have had the plate, the driver's silhouette, the second man's elbow on the door. A month ago she'd have called that *witnessing*. She'd called a lot of things witnessing.

"Don't," she said, very quietly, to nobody, and meant *me*.

Yusuf had stopped laughing. He read money, and money read like a man, and the man in the passenger seat of a car that idled at the top of a rise in the Nineveh plain in the long gold of the afternoon was the kind of man Yusuf had once arranged hotels for. He knew the shoulders. He knew the way a man sat when the work was somebody else's hands but the watching was his.

"That's a look-and-go," Yusuf said. "Somebody's confirming a location."

Narin did not turn around. She was rolling the tablet — the school-text, the one with the beer rations and the line about *Dubsiga, your servant* that Bashir had laughed out loud at three days ago — back into its acid-free tissue with the unbothered hands of a woman who had spent twenty years putting fragile things back into boxes while the world did its worst around her.

"Then it is confirmed," she said. "Now we move it before they decide they are confident."

Out on the lane the man at the truck did not look up. He was crouched at the front wheel with the cayenne tin balanced on his knee and a strip of inner tube in his teeth, doing something to a valve that he had decided was more urgent than the car at the top of the rise. He had, in fact, decided this with the precise timing of a man who had clocked the car before any of them, and had then chosen — visibly, lazily, in full view — to look at a valve instead. He hung the weeping water bag back on the mirror. The wet canvas caught the light. From the rise it would read as *peasant; truck; nothing.* That was, Edith understood, the entire point.

---

The thing she had learned to call *the win* did not look like a win.

It looked like a school gymnasium in the lower town of Erbil with bad fluorescent light and a folding table and four people from the Directorate of Antiquities of the Kurdistan Region — two women, two men, one of them not yet thirty and visibly the smartest person in any room he ever entered — and a list. The list was the win. The list was forty-one inventory numbers, and against thirty of them somebody had handwritten a provenance and a museum and a date, and against eleven of them somebody had written *EN ROUTE / RECOVERED / VERIFY*.

Narin's crew — and it was, by now, plainly *Narin's* crew, the way a thing becomes someone's not by claiming it but by being the one who knows what it's for — had handed over a notebook, a card of microSD, and a single afternoon's worth of reading that turned a crate the network had been moving as *Sumerian astronomical archive (UNTRANSLATED) — significant* into a crate that the verify-column now described, in dry Kurdish bureaucratic shorthand, as *administrative; barley; lexical; school exercise.*

That was the whole murder. Not a gunfight. A reclassification.

"You understand what you have done," the young one said, the smart one, in English, because Bashir had switched them into English so the foreigners could follow and then immediately regretted the courtesy because it cost the man some of his music. "You have made it worthless."

"We have made it *cheap*," Narin said. "Which is the only thing that has ever protected anything in this country. The expensive things leave. The cheap things stay home and get read by students."

Edith had her camera on the table, lens-down, both hands flat on either side of it like a woman keeping a lid on a pot. She had asked. She had asked the young man and the two women and the older man, in turn, through Bashir, in three languages, the same flat question: *may I photograph the list, not your faces, only the list, and only if it helps and never if it hurts.* The older woman had looked at her for a long time. The older woman had been in Mosul in 2014 when the network's predecessors had filmed themselves taking sledgehammers to the museum there for a different streaming audience, and she knew exactly what a camera was for, and she had said: *the numbers. Not the room.*

So Edith shot the numbers and not the room. Forty-one of them, in dry light, no faces, no flag, no folding table, no anguish. A document. It was the best photograph she had taken in nine years and she would never sell it and that was, she had begun to suspect, the same fact.

---

The streaming-doc people heard about the reclassification on a Tuesday.

Yusuf heard about *them* hearing about it, because Yusuf still had two numbers in his phone from the wrong years and one of them texted him a single line — *the Mesopotamia ep is going dark, segment 4 cut, you anywhere near this?* — and Yusuf sat very still under the awning for about a minute and then put the phone face-down on the cloth and pressed his palm on top of it like Edith pressing her lens to the table, like the whole crew had become a set of people who knew what to do with the things in their hands now, which was mostly to leave them where they were.

"Segment four," he said. "That's the gold one."

Naila looked up from her ledger. She had been doing what she did, which was the only honest kind of arithmetic in the whole business — converting the network's grey-market price-sheet into base-ten and then watching the numbers refuse to be astronomy. "The gold-mining one," she said. "The visitors-came-for-the-gold one." Her mouth did a small flat thing that was, on Naila, a grin. "It's the same gold every time. African gold, anunnaki gold. It's always the gold, with these people. They cannot imagine someone coming a very long way for anything but gold, because gold is the only reason *they* would come."

"That," said Bashir, delighted, pen in the air, "is the most savage sentence anyone has said this entire month and you said it about *accountancy*."

"It is accountancy," Naila said. "The myth is the markup. I've shown you the spreadsheet."

She had. Twice. The seam of the whole thing, laid out in a column: a tablet read as *school text* sells for the price of a school text; the *same tablet* photographed badly, lit blue, captioned *DECODE THE ANUNNAKI STAR MAP THEY DON'T WANT YOU TO SEE*, and then *never actually translated*, sells for forty times that — and, crucially, gets read by no one who would ever notice it says *Dubsiga, your servant, two measures of barley.* The woo was not a side-effect. The woo was the *packaging.* Keep the question loud, keep the tablet untranslated, keep it moving. A translated tablet is just a tablet. A *mystery* is a commodity.

"So the doc dies because the tablet got read," Yusuf said. He took his hand off the phone. He looked, Edith thought, like a man who had walked a long way to a door he was sure led outside, and opened it, and found the actual sky on the other side, bigger than the painted one he'd been heading for, and was still standing in the doorway adjusting his eyes.

"Segment four dies," Narin corrected, gently, because she corrected everyone gently and absolutely, "because southern Iraq filed an injunction about chain-of-custody and the production company's lawyers looked at the inventory and decided the *story* was now a liability instead of an asset. The tablet did not kill it. *The provenance* killed it. People keep wanting the magic to be the reading." She tapped the rolled tissue bundle. "The reading is wonderful. But the thing that saved this object is paperwork."

---

It was Mira who saw the second thing, the larger thing, the thing none of their mouths had said.

She had been watching Narin's face for three days — not the tablets, *the face*, the way Narin's face did one thing when she talked about the Flood and a completely different thing when she talked about the Exile, the way a particular stillness came over the lower half of her face whenever the conversation got near the genealogy, near the awful and beautiful fact at the centre of everything: that the oldest stories in the Bible her people had carried out of Nineveh and Babylon, the Flood and the garden and the tower, were in conversation with stories a thousand years older, written down on the very clay they were now arguing over, and that the *route* of that conversation ran straight through the Babylonian Exile — through the conquest, through the deportation, through the worst thing that ever happened to her people's ancestors becoming the soil in which the words took their final root.

Mira had read it on her without a word being said. And now she signed it, badly, lovingly, her hands finding the shapes the crew had been teaching each other all month — *you. don't. say. it. out loud* — and pointed two fingers gently at Narin, and waited.

The crew went quiet. They had learned, finally, this month, to go quiet *for* Mira and not *around* her — to let her finish, to not talk over the slow careful sentence with three faster ones, because the entire month she had been talked over by everyone including herself and the worst of it was how normal it had felt, how *expected*, and then one afternoon Bashir had simply stopped mid-word with his hand still up and turned to watch her hands and said *go on, finish,* and something had shifted in the room that nobody narrated.

Narin watched the hands. Mira repeated it. *You read the clay out loud all day. The genealogy. You don't read that one out loud.*

Bashir started to translate it and Narin lifted one finger — *I have it* — because of course she had it, because she read faces too, everyone in her family read faces, you did not survive a minority on the Nineveh plain without reading faces, and she sat with it for a moment under the awning with the kettle ticking and the diesel-and-dust smell and the man outside doing nothing to a valve.

"No," she said at last. "I don't read that one out loud."

She turned the rolled tissue over once in her hands.

"Because the West has only ever known two ways to hold this," she said. "One: *the Bible is true and unique and dropped out of the sky, and these older clay versions are demonic counterfeits or coincidence.* Two: *the Bible has older sources, therefore the Bible is a fraud, a plagiarism, a fairy tale for the gullible, and we clever people have caught it out.* And both of those are the same insult wearing different hats. Both of them think the only thing a story can do is *be true or be stolen.*" Her face did the thing Mira had been watching for three days. "My people did a third thing. My people *did the third thing for a living.*"

She set the bundle down.

"Genesis knows the older stories. It is not hiding it. It is *arguing* with them. The Babylonians say the world was made when a god split a goddess's corpse like a fish — combat, blood, the strong god butchers the chaos and props the sky on her ribs. And the Hebrew writer takes that whole world of furniture, the *tehom*, the deep, the cognate of her very name, and builds a creation where there is no fight at all. No corpse. No butchery. A *word.* 'Let there be.' The Babylonians say humans were kneaded out of clay and a murdered god's blood to *carry the gods' baskets* — made to toil, made to die, slaves with a divine spark for a back. And the Hebrew writer keeps the clay — keeps the dust, *you are dust* — and changes the *one thing that matters.* Not slaves. *Image.* The same clay. A completely different verdict on what the clay is *for.*"

The kettle ticked.

"That is not theft," Narin said. "That is the bravest thing a conquered people ever did. They were marched a thousand miles into the empire that destroyed them, sat down in the shadow of its ziggurats and its myths and its priests, and instead of breaking, they took the empire's own creation story and rewrote it into an argument *against* the empire. *You* say we are slaves of the gods? We are the image of God. *You* say the world is a battlefield? The world is a sentence, spoken on purpose, called good. They out-wrote Babylon *in Babylon.* With Babylon's own bricks." She looked at her own hands. "How could I read that out loud as if it were a *catch?* As if I had *gotten one over* on my own grandmother? It is the most sophisticated act of survival in human literature and the West keeps trying to make it into either a magic trick or a forgery, because the West cannot conceive of a third thing, which is a *people refusing to die by taking the worst thing that ever happened to them and turning it into scripture.*"

Nobody touched it. They had learned that too.

After a while Frik — who had been very still, who had a tic going in his left shoulder that the crew no longer pretended not to see and no longer made into a problem — said, in the flat blurted voice that came when something was too true to be sat on:

"They didn't lose."

Everyone looked at him.

"The Babylonians," Frik said, and his hand jumped once, and he let it. "Conquered them. Burned the temple. *Won.*" The shoulder again. "But we don't read *Enuma Elish* on Sunday. They read—" and here the echolalia caught Narin's own word and threw it back, helplessly, exactly, "—*image. Image. Image.*"

It was the truest thing said under the awning all month and it had come out sideways, the way the truest things came out of Frik, the involuntary verdict that nobody could un-say or paint over, and Narin looked at him with the particular respect she gave the tablets.

"Yes," she said. "That is exactly it. The empire turned to dust. The argument with the empire is still being read in a thousand languages this morning." She almost smiled. "That is the joke at the bottom of everything. Nobody intended it. But it's there."

---

So where, then, was the hole. The Why Files always had a hole, and Bashir — who loved holes, who had spent a whole career living in the gaps between languages where the puns lived — went looking for it, because a story that closes is a story you don't trust.

"All right," he said. "Then what's still open. Genuinely. Not woo. Not aliens — the aliens are dead, we killed them in the clay, *nibiru* is Jupiter and the *sar* is a number and poor Mr. Dubsiga got his desk turned into a starship. Not that. What's the thing we *honestly don't know.*"

Narin thought about it properly. That was the other thing about her — she did not pretend to certainties she didn't have, which was the rarest quality in anyone the crew had met since the Beast first rolled north.

"The *how,*" she said. "We know the Hebrew primeval history is in dialogue with Mesopotamian literature. That's not a guess, that's a library. We know the Flood, the garden, the tower, the great long-lived ancestors, the *clay,* all have older cousins in the cuneiform. We know the Exile is the great pressure-cooker where so much of it got finalised. That much is mainstream, bulletproof, you can read the tablets yourself, George Smith stood up in 1872 and read a Babylonian flood off a broken tablet to a room full of Victorians who very nearly fainted, it is *done.*"

"But," said Yusuf.

"But the *exact mechanism.* The wiring. *Who* sat *where,* with *which* tablet, *when,* hearing it told *how,* and decided to keep the dust and change the verdict. Whether a Judean scribe read Akkadian in a Babylonian school, or whether it came down through generations of oral telling, or some braid of both that we will never untangle because the looms are gone. *That* we do not have. The fact of the genealogy — solid. The blood-vessels of it — gone." She spread her hands. "The connections are real and the details are mostly lost. That is the honest position. Anybody who tells you they know the precise channel is selling you something. And anybody who tells you the channel isn't *there* hasn't read the tablets."

"That's the hole," Bashir said, satisfied, settling back.

"That's the hole," Narin agreed. "And it is a *good* hole. It is a hole you can spend your life in and come out richer. It is not a hole you fill with a spaceship."

---

The car at the top of the rise had gone, at some point. Edith hadn't seen it leave and was glad, in a new way, not to have a photograph of its leaving.

There was a different thing she did have, though it was not on any card and never would be, because she'd had the lens at her hip when it happened and had — deliberately, with a small ache that she understood now to be the correct ache — not raised it.

Out on the lane, the man from the truck had gone, an hour ago, up the slope to where Narin's uncle had set a second awning by the wall, a low stretched square of canvas the colour of weak tea, and the two of them had sat down on a pair of plastic chairs that did not match, and the uncle had made tea in the small loud way of a man who has made tea ten thousand times, and they had passed *one glass.* One small clear glass of orange tea, back and forth, the way the man had once passed a cup in a place none of them had been, the way the uncle passed it to anyone he decided was *people.* They had not talked much. The man did not have the words; the uncle did not have the man's. They had sat in the long gold light at the edge of the Nineveh plain, two men who could not read a single sign off the clay, passing one glass, while the people who *could* read it argued under the lower awning about how the world had taught itself to write.

Edith had watched it for a full minute through nothing but her own eyes. It was the most photographable thing she had seen in nine years and she had let it go through her and out the other side and kept it the only place a thing like that survives, which is not a card. The network's people, wherever they'd been parked with their good glass, had never gotten a frame of him either. Three weeks of watching and the man had never once been still in the open with his face up at the right moment. The light loved him and the lens never landed.

When he came back down the slope he had the cayenne tin out and a small fold of the always-clean hanky pressed to a nick on his thumb from the valve, and he didn't come in under the awning — he stopped at the edge, in the lane, where the story's *meaning* couldn't reach him and he didn't want it to.

"We can run them to Erbil at first light," he said. To Narin. Only to Narin. *The locals own the win and the win is hers.* "The crate goes with your directorate. We're just the truck." A beat. "*Spas,*" he said, in Kurdish, badly, the courtesy handful, and the uncle behind him barked a laugh at the accent and said something back that made even Narin smile.

Then he went to check the water bag again, though it did not need checking, and the camera did not follow him, because the camera belonged to the crew, and the crew were under the awning, where the last of the light was going amber on the rolled white bundle of a four-thousand-year-old worksheet about barley.

Narin picked it up. She held it the way you hold the thing itself and not the price of the thing.

"Roll the tape," Bashir said, because someone had to, and they were all a little drunk on tea and survival.

"There's no tape," Naila said, not looking up. "There never was a tape. There was clay." She closed her ledger. "Which is better. Tape rots in fifty years. We've still got the homework of a man who was bad at fractions in 1700 BC."

And the question hung there in the amber light, unanswered, allowed to stay unanswered — not *were they aliens,* that was dead and buried in the original language, but the bigger one, the one with the room in it: *what were they for, the princely offspring, the made-from-clay, and who taught Genesis to bake its bricks the Babylonian way, and whose hand* — somewhere, off every lens, in some quiet office where the loud tablets got promoted and the quiet ones disappeared — *had been deciding for years which of these stories the world was allowed to hear at all.*

Nobody answered. That was the whole art of it.

Out past the awning, southern of here, beyond the checkpoints and the longing and the line on the map, the real cradle lay in the dusk — Ur, Uruk, Eridu, the mounds where writing was born — in the hands of the people who lived there now.

Narin set the bundle down and reached for the kettle.

"Tea," she said. "And then I'll show you the one with the joke in it. It only works in Sumerian."
