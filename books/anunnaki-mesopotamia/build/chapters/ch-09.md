# Chapter 9 — The Real Evidence

Naila woke before the heat did, which was the only honest hour in Erbil. She had learned this in two days. The light came up grey-gold over the citadel and the air still held the night's coolness like a held breath, and for maybe forty minutes a person could think without their own sweat arguing with them. She took the thin folder out to the awning table while the others slept, because she wanted to read the sealed-envelope story alone before Narin narrated it. She wanted to find the shape of it herself. She did not want to be *shown* the pattern. She wanted to *catch* it.

The marginalia in this folder was in the script Bashir didn't know — Syriac, Narin had said, her grandmother's hand, a woman who had copied scholarship by lamp the way other women copied recipes. Naila couldn't read the Syriac either. But she could read the photocopied English under it, and she could read the structure, and the structure was what made her sit very still with her tea going cold.

It went like this.

A man named Henry Rawlinson, an army officer, had spent years hanging off a cliff in Persia. The cliff was called Behistun, and three hundred feet up it, a Persian king named Darius had carved a brag the size of a billboard: I am Darius, I crushed the liars, the god Ahuramazda gave me the kingdom. He'd carved it three times, in three languages — Old Persian, Elamite, Babylonian — because he wanted everyone in his empire to read it, and the three languages were three doors into the same room.

Naila's chest did the thing it did when a system clicked. Three statements. Same content. Different code. If you could read one, you had a key to the others. It was *parallel text.* It was the most beautiful kind of proof there was, because you didn't have to trust the man who carved it; you only had to trust that he'd said the same thing three times, and that arithmetic — the brute, unbendable count of which sign sat where — would hold him to it.

She heard the awning rustle. Mira, barefoot on the packed earth, hair flat on one side from sleep, reading Naila's face before Naila said a word. Mira tapped two fingers near her own eye — *you're seeing something* — and sat, and Naila turned the page so they could both look, and that was a whole conversation.

The rest of the house woke in pieces. Yusuf with coffee he'd made wrong and drunk anyway. Bashir with a notebook already open, as if he slept holding it. Frik in last night's shirt, hands going, jaw working on a word that hadn't decided to come out yet. Edith last, camera bag over one shoulder by reflex, the strap-mark already worn pale into the canvas. Out past the gate the Beast sat with its hood down now, and a low African vowel-music drifted off it — Jakobus, working a wet rag over the windscreen against the dust, asking the morning for nothing.

Then Narin came out with the real coffee and the rest of the folder and the morning began.

---

"He nearly died for it," Narin said, before she sat. "Rawlinson. Behistun. People skip that part. He's swinging off a rope on a cliff three hundred feet up, copying a Persian king's victory speech sign by sign, and the only ladder to the Babylonian section is gone, so he hires a Kurdish boy — a local boy, mind you —" a small, dry stress on *local* that landed on every foreigner at the table at once "— who free-climbs across the rock face with a paper squeeze and copies what no adult could reach. So when people tell you cuneiform was *cracked*, remember a Kurdish kid with no rope is in that sentence, and nobody knows his name."

Edith's hand moved toward the camera bag. Stopped. Did not.

Naila watched her stop. So did Mira.

"Old Persian came first," Narin went on. "Forty-odd signs, alphabetic-ish, the easy door. They guessed at it from the proper names — you find *Darius, Xerxes, Hystaspes* in the king-lists, you find the same shapes on the rock in the right places, you start matching sounds. Slow. Years. Arguments. But the Persian gave them the key, and the key turned the Babylonian, because Darius had said the same thing three times." She set a hand flat on the table. "And *that's* where it gets ugly. Because Babylonian — Akkadian cuneiform — isn't forty signs. It's six hundred. And the same sign can be a syllable, or a whole word, or a silent marker telling you what *kind* of word is coming. One sign reads four different ways depending on its mood."

"Polyvalence," Bashir said, soft, like a name he was glad to hear out loud. "It's not a code. It's a *language wearing* a code."

"It's a nightmare," Narin said. "And here's the part the believers should sit with. By the 1850s you had four men in Europe all claiming they could read it. Edward Hincks, an Irish parson stuck in a country rectory — possibly the best of them, never got the credit. Henry Rawlinson, the cliff man, all bluster. Jules Oppert, German-French, brilliant and prickly. And William Henry Fox Talbot — yes, *that* Talbot, the photography one—"

Edith's head came up.

"—he invented the negative and then got bored and learned dead languages," Narin said, and let the small smile happen. "And the field had a problem. Because four men reading the same broken tablet and getting four different stories — that's not a science. That's a séance. That's everybody seeing the face they brought with them."

Frik's hands went still. His mouth opened. "*That's the fringe,*" he said — flat, blurted, not to anyone. "Four men. Four stories. Pick one." His face coloured at having said it. Nobody moved to make it a thing.

"Yes," Narin said simply, like he'd answered a question correctly. "That's exactly the fringe. So in 1857 they did something the fringe has never once done in its life."

She slid a single page to the centre of the table. A photocopy of a printed report, Royal Asiatic Society, the long S's of old type.

"Somebody had dug up a clay prism — a barrel of baked clay, eight sides, covered in the annals of an Assyrian king, Tiglath-Pileser the First. Eight hundred lines. A king bragging about his wars and his hunts and the temples he fixed, which is what kings did, it was the only thing they ever did. And the Society had a brilliant, nasty idea." Narin's eyes had the brightness now that Naila had learned meant the gate was open all the way. "They printed four copies of the prism. They sent one to each man. Hincks, Rawlinson, Oppert, Talbot. *Translate this. Independently. Tell no one. Seal your answer in an envelope and send it back to us unopened.*"

The table was very quiet. Out at the gate the rag stopped on the glass, then started again.

Naila felt the shape lock. This was the thing she'd caught at dawn and hadn't had the word for. "Blind," she said. "It was blind. None of them could bend toward the others. None of them could see the answer and steer the glyphs to meet it."

"It was a *trial,*" Narin said. "It was the cradle of the whole discipline putting its own neck on the block. Because if the four envelopes came back four different stories, that was the end. Then cuneiform was a séance, and everyone reading it was a fraud or a fool, and you could fold the whole field up and go home. They *risked it.* That's the part. They were willing to be proven liars in public."

"And?" Yusuf said. He'd stopped writing. He had a habit, Naila had noticed, of going still when money or proof was on the table — the part of him that used to evaluate whether a thing was real for a buyer.

"The twenty-ninth of May, 1857," Narin said. "They opened the four envelopes in front of a committee. Four men. Four sealed translations of eight hundred lines of an arrogant dead king. And they *matched.*" She tapped the page. "Not perfectly. There were disagreements — a number here, an unclear sign there. They were honest about those; it's *in the report.* But the substance? The king, the campaigns, the gods, the structure, the sense of it? The same. Four men in four rooms had read the same dead tongue the same way and not known they agreed until the envelopes opened."

She let it sit.

"That," Narin said, "is the moment cuneiform stopped being a guess and became a *reading.* That is the receipt. When somebody tells you the scholars just *invented* a way to read the clay — that they're all in on a story — you tell them about the envelopes. Because the men who made this field earned it by agreeing to be caught if they were wrong. They submitted to the seal."

---

Naila was already running it. Couldn't not. The arithmetic of it, the cleanness.

"It's reproducible," she said. "That's what it is. It's the whole thing science is. You hide the answer from the people generating it, you let them work alone, and if they land in the same place by separate roads, the place is real — not the road, the *place.*" She heard her own voice going faster, the flat fast cadence the others had stopped flinching at. "It's a control. They built a control. They didn't trust *themselves,* that's the genius, they didn't trust their own desire to be right—"

"Naila." Bashir, gently, two fingers tapped on the wood — their version of *land it.*

She landed it. Breathed. "It's a good system," she said, quieter. "It's a really good system."

"It's the *opposite* system," Bashir said, and now he leaned in, and the polyglot's loneliness went out of his face the way it always did when language stopped being a border and started being a key. "Think about what the other method is. The fringe method. You decide first — the gods were astronauts, say. You *know* the answer before you open the envelope. Then you go to the clay and you find the signs that fit and you blur the signs that don't. You bend the glyph to the story." He turned his hands over, empty. "These four men did the inverse. They made it *impossible* to bend. They sealed the story away from the glyph. They made themselves blind on purpose so the clay could speak."

"Say the other one," Naila said. "Say nibiru."

Bashir almost laughed. "*Nibiru.* Yes. The famous twelfth planet. It's a real Akkadian word — *nēberu.* You know what it means? It means a crossing. A ferry-point. The place you cross a river. The Babylonians used it as the name for a celestial *position* — the point a planet crosses at its high station." He spread his hands. "Most often: Jupiter. You can watch it. It's up there every year. *Nēberu* is the crossing-point of *Jupiter,* a thing a Babylonian could see with his own eyes from a rooftop." A beat. "It is never — not once, not anywhere — a death-planet on a thirty-six-hundred-year orbit full of gold-miners. That sum was done by reading the word as a story you brought with you, and never opening the envelope."

"And the *sar,*" Frik said. He'd been holding it, Naila could tell — the over-reach he'd punctured at the museum, days ago now, that had cost him a flinch then too. He got it out clean. "The three-thousand-six-hundred. They call it the *orbit.*" His hands flared and settled. "It's a *number.* Base sixty. Sixty times sixty. It's how they *counted.* It's not a clock for a planet, it's — it's *math homework.*"

"It's their sixty," Narin said. "We still have it. Sixty seconds, sixty minutes, three hundred and sixty degrees. We are standing in their arithmetic right now and we don't even feel it. *Sar* is a *big round number* the way we say *a million* — and someone read it as a doomsday calendar." Her mouth twisted, but not cruelly. "The men who guessed read forward from a story. The four men in 1857 read backward from the clay. One of those methods built a discipline that can read a dead king's grocery list. The other one sold a lot of paperbacks."

The cruelty wasn't in it. Naila noticed that, the way she noticed everything. Narin wasn't sneering at the paperbacks. She'd said it almost tired.

Yusuf had gone quiet in the particular way he went quiet, and Naila watched Mira watch him, because Mira had clocked it first. He turned the RAS page around with one finger, slow.

"It would've been easier if they hadn't matched," Yusuf said, to the page. "For them. Four different answers, you can argue forever. Argument is a market. As long as nobody's *sure,* everybody's selling." He didn't look up. "These four men closed the market. They made it boring. They made it *settled.* And settled doesn't sell."

Nobody asked him what he meant. Naila understood it sideways anyway — that he was talking about the paperbacks, and not only the paperbacks. That he'd been the kind of man who profited from things staying loud. That the man across the table from himself five years ago would've hated this room.

---

The heat had its hand on the day now. The awning's shade had pulled in tight. Out at the gate Jakobus had gone from the windscreen to the radiator, and the Beast made its small sounds, and twice his shadow crossed the gravel and didn't come closer.

Narin folded the RAS page back into its sleeve, and the gate in her face changed key. The teaching gate closed. A harder one came down.

"Now," she said. "The next part isn't a story. It's logistics. And it's where I find out whether you're tourists."

Edith's camera bag was on the table. Narin looked at it, then at Edith, until Edith moved it onto the bench beside her, off the wood. It was not subtle and was not meant to be.

"You want to go south," Narin said. "All of you, even if you haven't said it. You've got the cradle in your eyes. Uruk. Ur. Eridu. The first city, the first writing, the first wheel — it's all *down there,* in the alluvium, where the two rivers used to meet the sea before the silt moved the coast. That's the real thing. That's where the clay we've been reading came *out of the ground.*" She let it ache for half a second, because it was aching in her too. "And we are *not going there.* Not on this trip. Maybe not on any trip you're part of."

"Why?" Bashir asked. No challenge in it. He of all of them knew what a border cost.

"Because south is Baghdad's writ, federal Iraq, and that's a different set of permits, a different security picture, a different *decade* of trust I'd have to spend to take seven foreigners with cameras down there safely, and I won't spend it on a road trip." She didn't raise her voice. That was the thing — she never raised it. "Up here is the Kurdistan Region. The KRG runs its own antiquities, its own roads, its own answer to the question *who is this person and why are they near the tablets.* It's the safe north. It's the part the West is allowed to visit. And even *here* I had to vouch for you. With my name. To people who remember every foreigner who ever came to look at the clay and decide what it meant *for* us."

The diesel-and-dust grain of it sat in the room now, the way it had sat under the whole trip — that they were not on a set, that there were forms with real stamps, that a man named Yusuf had spent three weeks of phone calls and one favour he didn't explain to get them an awning to sit under at all.

"There's a tablet moving," Narin said. Flat. "You should know that's why I let you this close. Not the lecture. The lecture's just so you don't embarrass me. There's a tablet that came out of the south in '03 — out of the museum, or out of a dig nobody licensed, I don't know which yet — and it surfaced two months ago in a sale catalogue. A streaming outfit's involved. The kind that makes the pretty documentaries. And the catalogue copy calls it—" her jaw set "—it calls it *possible evidence of pre-Sumerian astronomical knowledge.* Which is the new way of saying aliens without saying aliens, so the lawyers stay calm."

"What's it actually say," Naila asked. She had to know. The arithmetic of the thing was already itching.

"I've seen one photograph. Bad light." Narin's mouth did something complicated. "It's a school text. A *practice* tablet. A scribe-student copying out grain measures and a list of beer rations for temple workers. *Dubsiga* — a name, a foreman maybe, *your servant so-and-so.* It's a kid's homework, three and a half thousand years old, and somebody is going to sell it for the price of a flat by calling its number-columns a star map." She looked around the table. "And here's the obscene part, the part that should make you angry. The *louder* the lie, the *safer* the theft. A tablet called *proof of ancient astronauts* is worth more on the grey market — and *nobody who could read it ever gets near it,* because the only people in the room are buyers who want the story. If they called it what it is — a beer-ration list — some Assyriologist would walk past the case, read it in four seconds, and ask where it came from." She let that land hard. "The myth is the markup. And the myth is also the *camouflage.* It's the best cover a smuggler ever had. Make it about aliens, and you've made it *un-investigable,* because everyone serious looks away and everyone who looks is in on the wanting."

Edith had gone very still. The 2003 in her was on her face — Naila couldn't read it the way Mira could, but she could see the cost of it, the held breath. Edith had photographs of the museum that year. The sacks going out. She'd never said it but it was in the room every time the looting came up, and Narin knew it was in the room, and that was half of why the camera bag had had to go on the bench.

"And the other lie's just as useful to them," Narin said, softer now, and this was the part Naila hadn't seen coming. "Not just the believers. The *shruggers.* The clever ones who say *oh it's all just myth, who cares, it's all made-up stories anyway.*" She looked, briefly, at no one and everyone. "That's the best cover of all. Because the believer at least *wants* the tablet to be real. The shrugger doesn't care if it's real or stolen or sold or smashed, it's all *just old superstition* to him. You want to move a crate of looted clay through customs? Don't hire a smuggler. Hire a cynic. Hire a man who thinks none of it matters." Her voice didn't rise. It got quieter, which was worse. "Both of them — the one who thinks it's spaceships and the one who thinks it's nothing — both of them help the crate move. The only person who stops the crate is the one who thinks it's *exactly* what it is. A boy's homework. Sacred and ordinary and *somebody's.*"

The Beast's engine turned over out at the gate, caught, settled into an idle. Jakobus had it breathing again. The water bag swayed on the wing where it had been hung to weep and cool, and the music had stopped because the engine was the music now. He didn't come in. He never came in for this part. He was outside making the thing that would carry them, and he left the inside of it — the language, the meaning, the grief, the *who decides* — to the people whose it was.

"So that's the gate," Narin said. "It doesn't open south. It opens *here,* it opens *slow,* and it opens only as far as I say, and I can close it whenever you make me regret it." She looked around once more, and something underneath the hardness was almost — not soft, but *level.* Equal. "You read pattern. You read tongues. You read faces and money and the thing a man blurts when he can't help it. Fine. Good. I needed people who read. But the place is not a puzzle for you to solve and leave. There are people *living* on the cradle right now, in the alluvium, in the dust, going to school, getting married, burying their dead, over the top of the first city anybody ever built. They are not scenery. The West made my ancestors into scenery once. Into a B-movie. I will not hand them to you to do it again."

Nobody spoke. Frik's hands were still. Even Naila's were.

Then Mira moved.

She'd been watching the whole time — not the page, not the tablet talk, *Narin* — and now she lifted one hand and signed something slow and careful and entirely wrong, the crew's terrible handmade dialect, and Bashir started to translate it and stopped, because Narin's face had changed before the words arrived. Whatever Mira had read in her — the thing under the hardness, the thing the lecture was built to cover — Mira had seen it, and let Narin see that she'd seen it.

Narin didn't answer. She looked at Mira for a long moment, and the room understood, in the way a room does, that they had walked up to an edge and that the edge had a name and that Narin was not going to say it.

Naila looked at the third folder, the one with the grandmother's Syriac in the margins. The pun was in there. *Tomorrow,* Narin had promised. The Lady of the Rib.

But the pun lived in the same book as the Flood and the clay and the genealogy, and Naila understood, watching Mira watch Narin, that the joke was going to cost the woman who told it more than any of them had thought a joke could cost.

Mira lowered her hand. And kept her eyes on Narin's face, reading the thing the mouth wouldn't say.
