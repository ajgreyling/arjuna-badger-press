# Chapter 21 — The Restoration

The light at Ur comes up the colour of the brick it falls on, so that for the first hour of morning you cannot tell where the ziggurat ends and the day begins. Edith had been told this by three different people and had not believed any of them. Now she stood on the lowest of the great staircases with the camera dead in her hands and believed it completely, and did not raise the lens, because there are things the lens makes smaller by trying.

She raised it anyway. It was the job.

Through the viewfinder the staircase climbed in three flights toward a platform that no longer held the shrine it was built to hold — the shrine was four thousand years gone, the god with it — and the brick went up in courses laid by men whose names were pressed, some of them, into the very bricks under her feet. *For Nanna, his king, Ur-Nammu, king of Ur, king of Sumer and Akkad, the one who built the temple of Nanna.* Huda had read it to her yesterday off a brick the size of a paving stone, running two fingers along the wedges the way a person reads a face they have known a long time, and the reading had taken eight seconds, and Edith had spent the afternoon thinking about the eight seconds.

"You're shooting the steps again," Omar said, behind her, not moving, which for Omar was a kind of event.

"I'm shooting the joins."

"That's the steps."

"It's where two days of work meet," Edith said. "Whoever laid the lower course went home, and somebody else came back the next morning and started where he stopped. You can see the seam." She lowered the camera. "I like seams."

---

The official story, the one the foundation's people would have filmed here if they'd gotten the permit — and they had tried; Archer had tried three times, charmingly, with letters on heavy paper — was simple and shining and had been told ten thousand times. *They could not have built this. Look at the scale. Look at the precision of the brick. Look at the alignment to the cardinal points, the casing, the engineering of the core. A Bronze Age people, mud-hut people, slave people — they were given this. Someone came down and showed them how.*

It is a beautiful story. It has been believed, in one dress or another, by clever people and kind people, by your uncle and possibly by you at two in the morning, and the camera does not sneer at it, because the camera has stood under the thing and felt the want it answers — the want to make the impossible make sense by handing it up to the sky.

So play it straight. The ziggurat at Ur is one of the best-preserved in Iraq. Its lowest stage was reconstructed in the twentieth century on the ancient core, and the core itself is genuine third-millennium-BCE work: a solid mass of mud-brick faced with a skin of kiln-fired brick set in bitumen, the whole thing pierced with weep-holes so the rain could drain and the mass not burst. Ur-Nammu raised it; his son Shulgi finished it; the Neo-Babylonians under Nabonidus rebuilt the upper stages a millennium and a half later, and Nabonidus left an inscription complaining, essentially, that the old kings had not built high enough.

Now the potato.

Go and make a brick.

Not buy one. Make one, the way they made these — the facing brick, the fired kind. You need clay, and the clay must be cleaned of stone, and you need a temper to keep it from cracking as it dries, chaff or sand in the right proportion, and you need a mould, and you need to know the proportion of water by feel because no one will write it down for you, and you need to dry the green brick *slowly*, out of the direct sun or it skins over and the inside stays wet and it splits, and then you need a kiln, which means you need fuel, and southern Mesopotamia has no forest, so the fuel is reeds and dung and whatever you can float down the river, and you need to know the firing curve — too fast and it shatters, too cool and it stays mud — and the temperature you are reaching for is north of nine hundred degrees, held, in a structure you also had to build, and you are doing this not once but several million times, because the ziggurat at Ur, by conservative count, swallowed brick in the millions, each one a small accomplishment of chemistry that a literate scribe somewhere logged into a ledger of grain paid to the workmen who made them, by name, with their rations.

It is so much harder than a spaceship. That is the secret nobody tells you. The spaceship is *easy.* The spaceship lets you off. The brick is the thing that costs.

---

Huda came up the steps at nine with eleven students behind her and a roll of cloth under her arm, and Edith began, quietly, to work.

The students were from the university at Nasiriyah, four women and seven men, the youngest maybe nineteen, all of them in the bad shoes of people who walk a long way to learn things. Two carried a folding table. One carried a box of the small acid-free trays the conservators use, and in the trays, packed in foam, were tablets — not the famous ones, not the Flood, nothing from behind the glass in London, but local finds, administrative clay, the receipts and contracts and schoolboy exercises that are the true literature of the place: nine hundred and ninety percent of all cuneiform ever excavated is somebody's accounts.

Edith photographed the box being set on the table. She photographed a girl's hands lifting a tablet free of the foam — the careful two-handed lift, the way you'd lift a sleeping animal. She photographed the tablet itself, a palm-sized thing the colour of weak tea, and the wedges across it, and she did not know what they said and that was the point of the frame: *here is the thing, and here is the not-knowing, and over there is the woman who knows.*

"Receipt for barley," Huda said, reading over the girl's shoulder without taking it from her. "Two gur, given out by a man called Lu-Nanna, to a man whose name is broken, in the third year of — " she tilted it to the light " — Ibbi-Sin. Witnessed. Sealed." She looked up at Edith. "Thirty-eight hundred years ago a man counted his barley and somebody wrote it down so no one could cheat him. You want the gods who came down from the sky? *That's* them. The men who invented the receipt. The first people on Earth who decided a thing should be *written so it could not be lied about later.*" A dry beat. "Try doing that with a campfire and a memory."

Naila had come up without anyone hearing her and was crouched at the table now, one finger hovering over a second tablet, not touching. "May I?"

"You'll read it with your hands," Huda said. It was not a question. The two women had taken each other's measure on the first day and arrived at something that was not friendship and was better than friendship for the work — a mutual refusal to perform. "Go on. Tell me what it is before you turn it over."

Naila set two fingertips on the clay and closed her eyes, and Edith lifted the camera and then did not press the shutter, because there is a frame you do not take, and this was one — the woman who reads the dead language with her skin, reading, with the woman who owns the language watching to see if she's right.

"School text," Naila said. "It's a list. It's — repetitions. Someone practising the same sign." Her finger moved. "Over and over. A child."

"A child," Huda agreed. "Learning to write the sign for *king.* Badly. Look — " she turned it for the camera now, deliberately, giving Edith the shot " — see how the third one runs downhill? He was tired. He was four thousand years ago and he was tired and he wanted to go outside." She set it down with great gentleness. "This is what they took from us. Not the gold. Not the spaceships. *Him.* The bored child learning to write. They looked at this — " she lifted her chin at the ziggurat going up gold behind them " — and they could not let it be the bored child's people who built it, so they gave it to aliens, and the aliens were a kindness to themselves, you understand, because if aliens built it then nobody has to feel the size of what they erased."

Omar, very quietly, in Arabic, said something Edith did not catch, and Huda answered him in Arabic, and the students laughed, and Edith did not ask for the translation because some of the room is not yours.

---

Here is the hole, and it is small and it is hard as a kiln-brick and you can check it tonight.

The skeptics won. They were *right.* There is no Planet Nibiru; the word means *crossing-place* and it is Jupiter, and you can watch it cross tonight if the sky is clear; the seal called VA 243 that the genre swears is a map of the solar system is a dedication, in plain cuneiform, to a man called Dubsiga; the Anunnaki are *the princely offspring,* divine functionaries, and they no more flew here in ships than the receipt for barley did. The clay killed the aliens. That part is over, and the people who killed it were doing honest work.

But go and read the *museum label.* Go and read the documentary, the good one, the responsible BBC-voiced one that debunks Sitchin properly. It says: *Sitchin was wrong. Nibiru is Jupiter. The end.* And then it stops. And in stopping, it walks the reader straight back out the door past the actual treasure without turning the lights on it — because the documentary, too, finds it easier to win the argument it was having than to face the stranger one underneath: that the people whose receipts these are, whose tired child these are, also wrote the older draft of the Flood, of the man made from clay, of the garden — and that draft is on tablets a thousand years older than the Hebrew one, and the Hebrew one *knows it,* answers it, argues with it, line by line. The debunkers closed the file marked ALIENS so neatly that nobody checked the much larger file underneath, the one marked GENESIS, the one with the most-read book on Earth inside it and a Mesopotamian return address.

That gap — between how completely the alien story was closed and how completely the real story was left lying in the foam, unread, on a table at the foot of a ziggurat — that is the hole. It is not a maybe about spaceships. There are no spaceships. It is a question about who was allowed to be the author of the human beginning.

"Why," Huda said — and she was not asking Edith, she was reading the broken receipt again, turning it, *what is this for* — "do you think they wrote the man being made from clay? Their man. The one in *Atrahasis,* a thousand years before anybody's Adam."

Mira, who had been silent on the lower step the whole hour, looked up. "To explain why we work," she said. "The gods are tired of digging the canals. So they kill one of their own and mix his blood with clay and make us, to carry the load." A pause. "We're made to do the labour the gods got tired of."

"Yes," said Huda. "And in Genesis?"

"He's made to keep a garden," Mira said slowly. "Same clay. Same breath. But he's — given the work as a gift first. Then it becomes a punishment. It's the same story arguing with itself."

Huda looked at her for a long moment, and something passed across her face that Edith caught and held in the frame forever: not surprise that the outsider had read it — they had all read it — but a flicker of permission given. *You may say that. You read it right.* And then she took it back into her own hands where it belonged. "Close," she said. "But the Hebrew writer is not copying. He's *answering.* He grew up in our libraries — his grandfathers were marched down to Babylon, they sat by the canals their conquerors made them dig, and they read our story of why men dig canals, and they said: *no. Not for tired gods. Not as slaves to the labour. For a covenant. For a God who walks in the cool of the day and calls you by name.*" She set the tablet down. "It's the most defiant thing in the whole Bible and nobody reads it that way, because to read it that way you have to admit our story came first, and that admission has been very hard for a great many people for a very long time."

Nobody said anything. The fish, who had been suspiciously quiet, surfaced. *"So you're telling me,"* it said, *"the slickest plagiarism case in human history is actually the Bronze Age's most savage subtweet."*

"I'm telling you," Huda said, not missing a beat, "it's a son arguing with his father about what a man is for. You can decide which one is right. That part was never mine to hand out."

---

She unrolled the cloth at the end of it, on the platform, with the day full and white now and the brick gone from gold to bone. There was no microphone. There was no foundation, no logo, no donor wall. There was a woman and her students and a table of receipts at the top of the oldest school in the world, and Huda said a few words in Arabic that Edith did not record because Naila, beside her, shook her head once — *not this* — and Edith lowered the camera and held it against her chest and let the words go out over the desert untaken, which was the only true thing the camera did all day.

Then the students each took up a tablet, the bored child's, the cheated farmer's, and carried them — not to a crate bound for a foundation's climate-controlled vault in a city that is not this one, but to a van bound for their own museum at Nasiriyah, fifteen kilometres up the road, where the labels would be in Arabic first.

Jakobus was at the bottom of the steps when they came down, where he had been the whole time, in the shade, having mended the van's failing door-latch with a strip of brass and a screwdriver while the others were up top, so that the tablets would not be jostled on the road. He handed the keys to the eldest student — a serious young man named Karim who would, Huda said, outlive all of them in the work — and said something short, and the young man laughed, and Jakobus stepped back.

He was always stepping back. It was the whole of him. He had read the latch and the road and the foam and the weight, and he had handed the rest — the meaning, the moment, the why — to the people whose meaning it was, and now he leaned on the van and watched them load their own past into their own vehicle to take to their own house, and his face had nothing on it but the small satisfaction of a door that would not bang open at speed.

Edith took one last frame. Not the ziggurat. The open van, the brass latch catching the light, eleven young people of Ur lifting the clay their ancestors fired, and at the edge, going out of frame, the backs of the crew, walking away.

Up the road, in Baghdad, Archer was on a phone he kept calm, learning that the permit would not come, that the narrative had a custodian now and her name was on the labels, and that the story he had wanted to *take* had instead been *handed* — over his head, past his money, into the only hands that were ever going to keep it.

The van pulled out. The dust came up the colour of the brick.

Whether the bored child knew he was building the foundation of every beginning we'd ever tell about ourselves — that one stays on the table. Read it with your hands. Decide.
