# Chapter 11 — The Beast on the Plain

The Beast did not have headlights so much as a strong opinion about the dark.

Edith learned this somewhere past the last village with a name she could pronounce, when Jakobus killed the mains at a wave from the old man on the motorbike ahead — a cousin of a cousin of Narin's uncle, she'd been told, which here meant the same as a passport — and the world went to moon and starlight and the small amber pilot of the dash, and the truck kept moving anyway, downhill into a basin of grass and stone, on a track Edith could not see and Jakobus apparently could.

She had her camera in her lap. She had not lifted it.

This was the discipline now. She felt it in her hands like a held breath: the want of the body to bring the eye up and frame the thing, the click that resolved the world into something she owned, and underneath it the new muscle she was building, slow and against grain, that let the want pass through her and out the other side without her hands moving.

Beside her, in the middle of the bench, Naila had her knees up and a folded printout against them and a penlight in her teeth, reading. She read in moving vehicles the way other people sat still in chairs. The light made a coin of orange on the paper and threw the shapes of cuneiform up onto the underside of her chin.

"Stop here," Narin said, from the back. Quiet. Not to Jakobus — past him. The cousin had stopped his bike at the lip of the basin and was off it, holding something to his ear, a phone with no signal that he was using as a watch. "We walk the last part."

"How far," Yusuf said. He had the look he got near money. Not greed. The opposite of greed — the face of a man reading a room for the exits, who had once been very good at being the danger and was now only good at smelling it.

"Eight hundred metres. Maybe less." Narin opened the door before the Beast had fully stopped. "The site is over the rise. They work it at night because there are no inspectors at night. There are no inspectors at night because the inspectors are in Baghdad arguing about whose budget pays for the road, and the road washed out in March."

"And we're here," Bashir said, "because—"

"Because I want to see it with my own eyes before I file a thing nobody reads." She was out, on the grass, a dark shape against darker land. "And because Hozan says they brought a lorry tonight. Lorry means they are loading. Loading means it is leaving. Once it leaves, it is gone. There is no after. There is only the photograph of the hole where it was."

Edith heard that and felt it land on her like a hand.

She got out.

---

The grass was waist-high and dry and full of the small constant noise of insects, and it smelled of dust and something green underneath, crushed where they walked. Jakobus came last from the Beast, and Edith watched him do the thing he did — the thing she had stopped trying to photograph because it never came out, because there was never enough of him in the frame to say it was him. He took a tin from his breast pocket. Cayenne, she knew now; he'd dusted the truck's air intake with it at the last fuel stop, against something, she hadn't followed the logic and hadn't asked. He pressed the lid back down. He set off behind them, not leading, a half-shape that the starlight refused to commit to, his shape forever about to become a bush or a fence-post or nothing at all.

The cousin Hozan led. Then Narin. Then the rest of them strung out in the grass like a bad idea.

"Down," Hozan said — Bashir relayed it, low — and they went down into the grass, and over the rise the night had a colour to it, a sick electric orange, and a sound: a generator's flat industrial drone, and under it the chunk and scrape of metal in earth.

Edith came up over the lip on her elbows beside Naila and looked.

It was not a dig.

She had photographed digs. She knew what a dig looked like: the string grids, the trowels, the little flags, the unbearable patience of a person on their knees with a brush, the whole grammar of *we will be here for years and we will write down everything.* This had the grammar of a quarry. Floodlights on poles run off the generator, throwing everything into hard orange and black. Three trenches torn straight down through stratigraphy that someone had spent five thousand years laying — she could see it in the lit walls of the cuts, the bands, the dark line of an old floor, the paler line of an old flood, all of it sheared through like a cake by people who only wanted the cherry. Men in the trenches. Men on the spoil heaps with sieves. A lorry, canvas-sided, backed up to the largest cut.

And the sacks.

That was the part. That was the part she would carry. Not the violence of it — she had a high tolerance now for the violence of it, that was the wound, that was the thing the doctor in London had a word for. It was the sacks. Rice sacks, the woven plastic kind, white going grey, the kind you saw stacked outside every shop in the bazaar with flour and bulgur and dried limes in them, and men were carrying them two-handed up out of the trench and swinging them up into the lorry, and the sacks were full of the cradle of writing.

"They're packing tablets in grain sacks," Naila said. Flat. The penlight was off. "Like potatoes."

"Don't," Bashir said, very gently, automatically, because the word had landed wrong in the dark, and then he heard himself and almost laughed and didn't.

Naila didn't laugh. She had gone still in the particular way Edith had learned to read — not the still of calm, the still of a system loading too fast. Her hands were flat on the dirt. The orange light, the generator's drone that never resolved into rhythm, the figures moving without pattern, the smell of diesel: Edith watched the whole night arrive on her at once and saw her decide, by some private arithmetic, to stay.

"Count them," Edith said, low, into the side of Naila's head. Giving her a fence to hold. "The sacks. I can't from here."

A breath. Then: "Nineteen in the lorry. Four at the lip. Two men filling. They've been at it—" the smallest pause, the eyes flicking, doing the thing she did that was not magic, that was just counting faster than the rest of them could "—the spoil heap is wrong for one night. They've been here a week. Minimum. Look at the lorry's tracks, there's a rut, they've parked it there before, the rut has dried."

"A week," Yusuf said, and the word had a whole career in it. He'd come up on Edith's other side. "A week, this close to the line, undisturbed. Somebody's paying for that. The quiet isn't free out here. Somebody bought the quiet."

"Who," said Bashir.

"That's the wrong question." Yusuf's voice had gone very even. "*Who* you can't find. *How* leaves a trail. How does a sack of school-texts about beer rations turn into money? It can't, that's the thing, nobody loots Sumer for the beer rations." A breath. "So you make it about aliens."

Down in the orange light a man straightened from the trench holding something up to the floodlight, turning it, and even at this distance Edith could see it was a cone — a clay cone, a foundation deposit, a thing a king had buried in a wall four thousand years ago so the gods would know who built it — and the man wasn't looking at the writing on it. He was looking at the shape. Whether it would *photograph*.

"There it is," Naila said. "That's the markup."

"What is?" said Edith.

"He's not selling a text. He doesn't read it, he can't read it, the buyer can't read it. He's selling a *story.*" Her voice had found its rail and was running on it now, fast and certain and almost happy, the way she went when the pattern came clear, the cost forgotten in the click of it. "A clay cone in a museum case with a card that says *Foundation deposit of Ur-Nammu, c. 2100 BCE, recording the building of a temple* is worth — what, Yusuf?"

"On a clean market with provenance, a few thousand. Without provenance, on the grey, nothing, because no auction house will touch it."

"Right. But the *same cone*—" and she had it now, the whole shape of it, and Edith felt the grief of the thing pour out of her sideways as delight, because that was how Naila grieved, by understanding "—the same cone, in a streaming documentary, lit blue, with a synth chord, and a man in a leather jacket saying *but mainstream archaeologists CAN'T EXPLAIN how a Bronze Age culture* — what's underneath, the lighting, do it — *and what if this symbol here is in fact a star map* — that cone is worth a fortune. Because now it's not a building record. Now it's a clue. Now it's *contraband from the gods.* And the beautiful part—"

"The ugly part," Edith said.

"The ugly part," Naila agreed, "is that the story makes it *more* sellable and *less* likely to be read by anyone who'd notice it just says a man's name and that he built a wall. The lie is the lock. You can't read a thing that everyone's been told is unreadable."

Frik had been silent the whole walk, which Edith had learned to clock the way you clock a held breath. He was on his stomach, fists working in the grass, jaw tight with the effort of holding, and Edith watched him lose it the way it always went — not a choice, a sneeze, the body overriding the man.

"*Beer rations,*" he said. Too loud. Then his own hand to his own mouth, eyes shutting, the small flinch of a man who'd spent forty years apologising for his own throat. And then, lower, helpless, the truth coming out the way it came out of him, sideways and exact: "Some scribe — Dubsiga's cousin — counting out the *beer*. For the wall crew." A breath. "And these *cunts* are selling him as a spaceman."

It rang in the grass. The generator ate it. Nobody flinched but Frik, who flinched at himself.

"Yeah," Yusuf said, simply, and put a hand flat on Frik's back, between the shoulder blades, and left it there. "Yeah. That's it. That's exactly it."

And Frik went still under the hand, and the night held.

---

Narin had not said anything for a while.

Edith found her a little apart, lower on the slope, on her knees in the grass with her hands open on her thighs, watching, and the watching was a different kind than the rest of theirs. The crew watched the way you watch a thing you've come to understand. Narin watched the way you watch a house burn that you grew up in.

Edith went to her on her knees, slow, telegraphing it, the way you approached a person who'd asked you to ask first. She did not bring the camera up. She left it on its strap, dead weight against her sternum, and she felt the want of it like a tooth.

"They're not even good trenches," Narin said. She didn't look round. "Look. There. The wall — see the line, the pale line, mud-brick — they've cut straight through a wall to get to the floor below, because the floor below is older and older sells. They've destroyed the building to get the bricks out of the way of the floor. The building was the context. Without the building, the floor is just—" her hand turned over, empty "—objects. Loose objects. A man finds a tablet in a floor and you can read the room. You know it was an archive, you know what was kept here, you know who they were. A man finds the same tablet in a sack and you know nothing. You have a *thing.* They are making things out of a city."

"I know," Edith said. Then, because it was true and she had decided to stop hiding behind being a professional: "I was here in 2003."

Narin turned her head at that. Just her head.

"Not *here* here. Baghdad. The museum." Edith kept her eyes on the orange trenches. "I photographed the looting. Day three. They were carrying things out in — the same. Sacks. Cushions. A man with the Warka mask wrapped in a — I never found out if it was a curtain or a flag. I photographed all of it. It ran on the front of three papers. It won a prize." She made herself say the next part. "I have never once stopped that man. In any of the pictures. I have a lot of pictures of him leaving and not one of him not leaving, and the pictures are very good, and I have thought, for twenty years, that taking them well was a kind of — that the beauty was a kind of—"

"Absolution," Narin said.

"Yeah."

Narin looked back at the trenches.

"It's why I didn't want your lens near my grandmother's hand," she said. "You understand. Your prize. Their grief, your prize. The most honest thing the West did with my country was photograph it leaving."

There was nothing to say to that that wouldn't be a defence, so Edith said nothing, which was the new muscle, and it hurt, and she let it.

Below them, the floodlights threw two long shadows up the slope, because a man with a clipboard — a *clipboard*, out here, the banality of it — had walked into the light, and he was not a labourer. Clean shirt. He held up a tablet, an actual tablet, oblong, dark, and turned it under the floodlight, and he had a phone up, and he was photographing it where it was, on site, the trench wall behind it, for someone, somewhere, who wanted to see it *in the ground* before they paid, because *in the ground* was the story, *in the ground* was the markup, *just discovered, never before seen, what the experts don't want you to know.*

Edith's hands came up.

She did not decide it. The camera was up, the eye was to it, the floodlight had given her everything — the trench, the clean shirt, the dark tablet held to the light like an offering, the lorry's canvas catching orange behind, the *whole sentence* of it composed in the frame the way it only ever composed itself maybe twice in a career, the picture that explains the thing, the picture that runs everywhere, the picture with her name under it.

She had the focus. She had the man's face turning a half-degree more into the light. She had the tablet's cuneiform catching as a row of small hard shadows. Three stops down, the floodlight wouldn't blow. One frame. Quiet shutter. Nobody would hear it over the generator.

*The picture that makes my name,* she thought, with the cold clarity of the body that has done this a thousand times, *and the dig coordinates are in it. The wall behind him is in it. Hozan walks these hills. Narin's uncle pours tea three valleys from here. This frame names this place, and the network has a media arm, and a media arm reads the news, and the news would run this, and they would read where it ran, and they would come for the people who could only have stood here.*

The frame was perfect. She held it.

She felt Narin not moving beside her. Not reaching for the camera. Not telling her. Trusting her, or testing her, the two being the same thing here.

She lowered the camera.

She let the man's face turn the rest of the way into the light unphotographed. She let the perfect frame dissolve back into ordinary dark. Her hands were shaking, which she noted and did not stop. She put the lens cap on. The small click of it was the loudest thing she had done all night.

"I'll get the lorry," she said, low, "when it's on the road. No site. No faces. Plate, if there is one. Make of truck. Direction. That's the trail, that's what Yusuf can run. The picture that catches *them* and not you."

Narin looked at her for a long moment in the dark.

"Yes," she said. "That one."

It was not absolution. Edith knew it was not. But it was the first frame she had ever *not* taken because of what the taking would cost someone else, and the not-taking sat in her chest like a coal, hot and clean, and she understood — no, she did not understand, that was the crutch, she would not do that — she *felt*, in the meat of her, that the wound and the cure were the same shape. Beauty as absolution had killed something in her. Maybe witness without consuming would grow it back.

---

The generator changed pitch.

Everyone on the slope felt it before they understood it — Naila's hand was on Edith's wrist, hard, before Edith had registered the change — and then the floodlights died, all of them, and the basin went to moon and the lorry's reverse-lights, red, and there was shouting, fast Arabic, fast Kurdish, a man's voice over it that had authority and no patience.

"They're spooked," Yusuf breathed. "Something spooked them. They're cutting it short. Leaving."

"Did they see us?" Bashir.

"No." Naila, certain. "They saw their own perimeter. Somebody's man up on the far rise stopped checking in. Their pattern broke. They don't know why their pattern broke, they only know it broke, so they run."

And up on the far rise, where the network's perimeter had stopped checking in, there was — Edith brought her old long lens up, the night-glass, slow, no shutter, only to *see* — there was a shape on the skyline that the starlight would not commit to. A shape that was about to become a fence-post, or a juniper, or nothing. It moved along the ridge against the grain of the wind, unhurried, and where it passed, a man with a phone to his ear lowered the phone and looked at it and looked at it and could not, by his whole posture, by the cant of his shoulders that Mira three feet away was already reading and would tell them later, *resolve* it into a man. The network had cameras on that ridge. Edith would learn that, after, from Yusuf, who would learn it from the trail. The network had three trail-cameras on that ridge and a man with a phone, and not one of them, that night, got a clean frame of whatever walked their line and made their watcher come down off the ridge to start his own engine.

She did not photograph it either. She would not have got it. Nobody ever got it.

"They left a man," Mira signed, fast, her hands catching the moonlight, and Bashir voiced it under his breath for Narin, *they left a man, the one with the phone, look how he keeps looking back, he didn't see a person, he saw a* — and Mira's hands stopped, and started, finding it — *a wrong. He saw a wrong and he can't say what.*

The lorry's engine caught. The canvas sides shuddered. Nineteen sacks plus four, Naila had said, twenty-three sacks of the first writing on earth, of grain-counts and god-lists and a scribe's complaint about his fee and somewhere in there, statistically, a school-text where a boy had copied out the flood, the older one, the one with the different mountain and the different birds, the one that was a thousand years old before anyone in Judah was carried off to Babylon to hear it — twenty-three sacks of it grinding away north and west across the dark plain toward the seam in the country where the safe north thinned out and a sack of cradle became, on the far side, *contraband from the gods.*

Edith got the lorry on the road, on the rise, against the last grey of the west. No site. No faces. The plate was mudded over, which told its own story. The make of the truck. Two reflective triangles on the tailgate, one cracked. The direction. The trail, handed to Yusuf, who took the camera screen and looked at it and said, quietly, "Okay. Okay. That I can follow. That I know how to follow," and there was, under the professional flatness, the smallest live thing — a man who had once moved crates like this for the wrong side, who had needed, more than any of them, for the lights in the sky to be true so that his old work might have been about something bigger than money, and who was here instead, in the grass, on his stomach, finding that the real work — *follow the cracked triangle, follow the mudded plate, follow the road that washed out in March* — was the bigger door after all.

---

They walked back to the Beast in the new dark, the generator silent behind them, the basin returned to its insects and its five thousand years of patience.

Naila walked beside Edith and was quiet, which after a thing like that was its own weather, the system unloading, and Edith did not fill the quiet. Then, near the truck, Naila said, to no one, to the grass:

"It's not even the worst of it. The selling. That's just money, money's stupid, money I almost understand." She stopped. Her hands found each other. "The worst of it is — they took those people's name off their own work. A man wrote *I, Ur-Nammu, built this wall,* he carved his *name* into it so the gods would know him in five thousand years, and the gods waited, and we dug him up, and instead of saying his name we said — *aliens did it.* We looked at the most patient act of authorship in human history and we gave the credit to the sky."

Nobody answered her. The Beast's water bag, hung again under the wheel-arch, had begun, very faintly, to weep.

Jakobus was already at the front of it, hood up, doing the thing with the torch in his teeth, separate, a half-shape, the man no camera could hold. Narin went to him — to the truck, to the open hood, the only one of them who could — and stood a moment, and said something low, three valleys from her uncle's awning, in the old tongue, the courtesy handful, and he answered with the same handful, and shut the hood, and wiped his hands on a hanky that came out clean and went back clean, and they got in.

"North and west," Yusuf said, from the back, watching the cracked triangle on his little screen go dark as the lorry crested out of range. "Toward the line."

"Toward the line," Narin said. And then, after a while, with her grandmother's notebook somewhere in her bag and her whole burning country in the back of her throat, quiet enough that only Mira, watching her mouth in the dash-light, was sure of every word: "We have to stop thinking of it as *stealing.* That's not what it is. That's not big enough for what it is."

Bashir half-turned. "Then what is it?"

Narin looked out at the dark plain going by, the grass, the old floors under the grass, the names carved into walls so the gods would remember.

She didn't answer yet.

The Beast carried them on into the night, headlights off, toward the seam.
