# Chapter 2 — Crossing-Point

The flat over the kebab shop on Caledonian Road had two exits and Naila had counted them before she took her coat off. The front door to the stairs. The fire escape past the bathroom, the bolt stiff with paint, twelve seconds to clear if she'd oiled it, which she had, last Tuesday, with the WD-40 Yusuf kept under the sink for the Beast's hinges three thousand miles away. Two exits. The number sat where it needed to sit and let the rest of her come into the room.

The rest of the room was the crew, and the file.

The file was good. The file was the best thing she owned. It lived in a green box-folder soft at the corners, and tonight it lived open on the table between the teapot and Frik's elbow, and she had laid it out in the only order it could go in, which was the order of the clay itself: oldest underneath, the fiction on top, so you peeled down through two thousand years to get to the man.

"Lights," Mira signed, and pointed at the standing lamp, and Bashir got up and angled it off the ceiling so it stopped glaring on the laminated sheets. Mira sat where she could see all their faces and the table both, which was the chair Naila would have wanted and had given her without a word the first week, because the woman who reads faces gets the chair that reads faces.

"Okay," Yusuf said. He had the look he got when a thing was finally about to be free. "You said you had the planet that isn't a planet."

"It's a planet," Naila said again. "I keep telling you. It's a very good planet. You can see it tonight."

She turned the top sheet over.

She had thought, for years, about how to do this without doing the thing she hated. The thing she hated had a sound to it, a particular curdle, the sound of a clever man on a stage saying *and of course it's all nonsense* with his chin up, getting his little laugh. She had watched a hundred hours of that man, that voice, on a hundred channels, and she had hated him as much as she hated the warm credulous one, because they were the same coin spent two ways, and neither of them had ever once read the clay.

So she did not start with *nonsense*. She started with the want.

"Here's the story they're selling," she said. "And it's a good story. I want to say that first, because I'm going to take it apart, and I don't want anyone in this room thinking I'm sneering at it. I'm not. It's a *beautiful* story. A planet called Nibiru, the twelfth planet, swings in on a long elliptical orbit — three thousand six hundred years to come round — and the last time it came round, the beings on it landed in Mesopotamia, and they were our gods, the Anunnaki, and they made us. Out of an ape and their own blood. To dig their gold."

"To dig their gold," Yusuf repeated, very quietly, and Naila heard the weight in it and let it stand. He'd wanted that story once. Maybe he still half-wanted it. That was allowed at this table.

"And it explains everything," she went on. "Why we got smart so fast. Why every culture has gods coming down. Why the King List gives the old kings these insane reigns — twenty-eight thousand years, you can't reign twenty-eight thousand years unless you're not really human. It explains the gap. It makes us important. Somebody came an unbelievable distance, for us." She put her finger flat on the top sheet, on the printed diagram, the planets in their pretty arc. "That's the want. Hold onto the want while I read you the clay. Because the want is the only part that's real, and it's *ours*, and I'd never take it off you."

Frik's foot was going under the table, heel-toe, heel-toe, a small private engine. He had the under-sheet pulled toward him already, the photograph, the one with the cuneiform on it. His lips were moving over nothing.

"Nibiru," Naila said. "Start there. It's the whole brand. It's on the book covers, it's the channel name, it's a tattoo on at least four men I've argued with online. So. The word's real. It's *attested*, it's in the texts, you can look it up." She slid the next sheet out — her own transliteration, her own hand under the printed line, because she didn't trust a printed transliteration she hadn't checked. "*Né-bé-ru.* From the verb, *ebēru.* To cross. To ford a river. A *nēberu* is a crossing-place. A ford. A ferry-point. The spot where you get across the water."

Bashir made a sound, not quite a word, the small glad noise he made when a word opened its hand. "It's a *place*," he said. "Not a body. A function. The crossing."

"The crossing," Naila said. "And in the astronomy texts — and Mesopotamia had real astronomy, centuries of night-after-night records, the best naked-eye sky-watching in the ancient world — *nēberu* is what they call the point where a planet crosses. Where it sits at a particular station. And the planet they hang it on, mostly, the one called the crossing-point —" she let it land soft "— is Jupiter."

She watched it go round the table. She loved this part and she made herself love it slowly.

"Jupiter," Yusuf said.

"Marduk's star," Naila said. "Babylon's god, up in the sky as the biggest brightest planet there is, and they called it the crossing-point because of where it sat in their year. That's *nibiru.* That's the twelfth planet. It's Jupiter. And here's the thing —" she got the next sheet, the one she'd printed off the planetarium site that afternoon "— it's up right now. Tonight. East, after dark, brightest thing that isn't the moon. You can walk down to the canal and look at the twelfth planet. It's been crossing every single year for the entire history of the human eye. It never went away. It never comes *in.* It's just there, doing exactly what it's done, and somebody read a Babylonian word for *the ford* and built a returning death-star out of it."

"Is it ever," Frik said, fast, his voice catching the way it did, "is it ever attached to the Anunnaki. In the texts. Is the crossing-point ever the Anunnaki's planet."

That was the whole game in one sentence, and he'd asked it the way he asked everything, like the question had jumped him from behind.

"No," Naila said. "That's the cleanest part. Never. Not once. The whole structure — Nibiru-is-the-Anunnaki's-homeworld — there's no tablet where those two things touch. You've got a word for Jupiter's crossing over here, and you've got the Anunnaki over there, and the bridge between them was built in the nineteen-seventies. On a typewriter. In English."

Mira had been watching the file and watching Naila's mouth and watching Yusuf's face, all three at once, the way she did, and now she lifted her hands. *And the Anunnaki,* she signed. *What's the word actually mean.* Bashir voiced it under his breath for the room, a beat behind her hands, because that was the rule they'd settled into — Mira led, the voice followed, never the reverse.

Naila reached for the word like reaching for a warm thing.

"This is my favourite," she said. "*Anunnaki.* *A-nun-na-ki.* The princely seed. The princely offspring. The *ki* on the end is *earth* — the offspring of the prince, of An, the sky-father, brought down to earth. The high gods' noble children. That's what the word *says.*" She paused. "And here's what nobody puts on a book cover, because it ruins the brand. The word *drifts.* Over two thousand years it slides. By the late period the Anunnaki aren't even the headline gods anymore — they get demoted, sort of, they become the gods of the *underworld.* The judges of the dead. The princely offspring start out as the bright nobility of heaven and end up as the bureaucracy of hell, and that drift took twenty centuries, and you can *track* it, tablet by tablet, because these people wrote everything down." She sat back. "That's not a spaceship crew. That's a word doing what words do over two thousand years of a living religion. They turned a theology into a flight manifest. You can't do that to a word that was alive that long. It's like reading 'host' on a Communion wafer and deciding the early church meant an *army.*"

Frik's foot stopped. He had the photograph an inch from his face. "Three thousand six hundred," he said. "The orbit. The *sar.*"

"The sar," Naila said, and now she got out the pencil.

This was the part she could do with her body, and she liked the parts she could do with her body, because pattern in the hand was quieter than pattern in the talk. She turned a sheet over to its blank back and drew a wedge. One clean wedge, pressed-in at the head, tailing off.

"Base sixty," she said. "They counted in sixties. That's why we have sixty seconds and sixty minutes and three hundred sixty degrees in a circle — that's *them*, that's still on your phone right now, four thousand years later. And in base sixty, you've got signs. A unit. A ten. And a big one." She drew it — the *sar*, the great circle-sign, fat and certain. "Sixty sixties. Three thousand six hundred. It's a *number.* It's the numeral for three thousand six hundred, the way that —" she tapped the blank corner "— a comma and three zeros is *thousand.* It's notation."

She turned back to the file and pulled the King List sheet, the famous one, the one that always got quoted.

"And here's where the alien case eats itself," she said. "Because they *love* the King List. The huge reigns, the antediluvian kings ruling tens of thousands of years — proof, they say, that the early rulers weren't human. And the reigns are written *in sars.* This king reigned ten sars. Eight sars. The numbers are colossal and that's the whole thrill of it." She put the pencil down. "So you can't have it both ways. Either the sar is a number — three thousand six hundred — and the reigns are just very big numbers, big the way myth makes everything big, a kingship measured in cosmic units to say *the old world was vast and we are small.* Or the sar is a planet's orbit. It can't be a planet's orbit in one sentence and a count of years in the next sentence on the *same tablet.*" She let it sit. "It's a number. It was always a number. It's the number the priests used for *king reigned a very long time* and the number a modern man pointed at and said *orbit.* He needed an orbit. The text was just doing arithmetic."

"The sar is a number," Frik said, and it came out of him almost gentle, almost like relief, the truth let off its leash with nowhere bad to run. "Not an orbit. It's the number."

"It's the number," Naila said. "You absolute disaster. Yeah."

Mira's face did the thing it did, the corner of it, and she signed *we know we love you* and Frik caught it this time and ducked his head and was, for one second, in a room that wasn't a problem.

Yusuf had gone quiet in the deep way. He was looking at the King List sheet, at the impossible reigns, and Naila knew the look because she'd worn it herself, years back, in the bad small flat before this one, when the file was thin and the want was thick. He'd had the long orbit on his wall once. The thirty-six-hundred-year clock. The countdown. It had been a door out of an ordinary life, and she'd just put a pencil through it, and she did the next part for him without saying it was for him.

"The want's still real," she said, to the table, to all of them, to him. "I want to be really clear. The man who built this — he wasn't a con man. I've read everything he wrote. He *loved* this stuff. He sat with the texts longer than almost anyone alive who wasn't paid to. He wanted us to be wanted. He wanted somebody to have crossed a vast dark for *us.* That's not stupid. That's the most human thing there is. The aliens are a way of not being alone." She tapped the file. "It's just not what the clay says. And what the clay says is *worse,* and that's the thing I can't get over, that's the thing I keep coming back to —"

"Show them VA 243," Bashir said softly. "Show them the man."

So she did the last one. The reach's crown jewel. The cylinder seal in Berlin, the little stone you rolled across wet clay to make your signature, four thousand years old, the one that's on the cover of the most famous book in the genre because it has, in its top corner, a star with dots around it — and somebody counted the dots and said: *the solar system. Carved before they could have known. Proof.*

"This is the one that does it for people," she said. "Every time. It's a *picture* of the solar system, they say, with the right number of planets, on a seal from four thousand years ago. And I get it. I do. It looks like a sun with planets. You want it to be that."

She laid the seal photograph down, and beside it, her own line, her own hand under the signs, slow and certain.

"It's a seal," she said. "A signature stone. And these things almost always say whose they are — they're *property*, they're *I am so-and-so, servant of such-and-such god.* That's most of what's written on the planet, by weight, in this entire civilisation. Not prophecy. Not star charts. *Receipts. Names. Whose this is.*" Her finger moved along the wedges. "And this one has an inscription, a little panel of cuneiform, and the inscription is a dedication, and it gives a name. *Dubsiga.*" She said it carefully. She always said it carefully, this name, because nobody else ever had, for four thousand years, until a few scholars and now this table. "It says, more or less — this belongs to Dubsiga, and below it the name of the man who *owned* the seal, who's presenting himself before his god. A servant before his lord. That's the scene. That's what the picture is. Not a star map. A man kneeling. Bringing his offering. Saying *I am yours.*"

"And the dots," Frik said. "The sun and the dots."

"A star symbol," Naila said. "A standard one. Divine. You put a star to mark *this is a god, this is the holy thing.* The dots are dots. It's decoration around a god-sign on a man's signet. Somebody counted the dots and found his solar system in another man's name-stamp." She sat very still. "His name was Dubsiga. He had a god he answered to. He had a seal made, beautiful work, and he rolled it in the clay so people would know his word was good. And four thousand years later we took his stone and rubbed his name off it and wrote *solar system,* and put it on a book, and made him scenery. That's the part I can't — that's the —" The word didn't come. She let it not come. "We had a whole person in our hands and we turned him into a UFO."

The kebab shop below sent up a gust of frying onion through the floor. Somewhere out on Caledonian Road a bus knelt and hissed. Nobody said anything for a moment, and the not-saying was the right size, and Mira held it open with her hands in her lap, not signing, just letting the room be quiet on purpose, which was a thing only Mira could do at this table, make a silence that meant *yes.*

"That's the case," Naila said at last. "All of it. There's more — there's always more, there's the bit where someone reads a school exercise about beer rations as a cargo manifest — but that's the floor. The crossing-point is Jupiter. The princely offspring are gods whose name drifted for two thousand years. The orbit is a numeral. The solar system belongs to a man named Dubsiga. Every single 'decode' is a bad translation. Every one. You just have to read it in the language it was written in, because that's the only honest place there is. You can't catch the cheat in English. The cheat lives in the gap between the languages and it dies the second you close the gap."

She had said it. The whole tower, down, in order, with love. She felt the clean of it, the particular relief that was hers and not like anyone else's relief — the relief of a thing that *resolved*, a transliteration with no slack in it, every sign accounted for, the ledger balancing to the wedge. It was the best feeling she had. It was better than being right. It was being *exact.*

And then it turned over in her, the way it always did, into the thing she couldn't flatten.

"But," she said.

"But," said Bashir, who knew. He was smiling the loneliest happiest smile in the building.

"The real thing is more astonishing than the fake thing," Naila said. "That's what nobody warns you about when you go to debunk this. You go in thinking you'll find dust and you find — God. The real texts out-wonder the fiction so badly it's almost rude. We make people from clay and a god's blood. Not to be loved — to *work.* To bear the gods' toil so the gods could rest. There's a flood and a man told to build a boat and a bird sent out to find land — a thousand years before Noah, in the same shape, in the same part of the world." She heard the next thing come up and she said it the careful way, the way that was true and didn't take anyone's God off them. "Not copied. Not identical. Different boat, different mountain, different birds, different days. But the same *room.* The same conversation. People telling each other the world's oldest story across a thousand years and a couple of languages, handing it down, arguing with it, changing it." She spread her hand over the whole file, the fiction and the clay together. "The fiction flatters us. *You were designed. You were wanted. Somebody crossed the dark for you.* And the real one says, *you were made out of mud to do the work nobody else would do, and you die, and you knew that, and you made the most beautiful writing in the history of the world about it anyway.* That's braver. That's so much braver. The fake one's a hug. The real one's a *people.*"

She stopped because her throat had got tight in the place it got tight, and she didn't perform the tight throat, she just waited it out, and the crew waited it out with her, which was the whole reason there was a crew.

Yusuf reached over and turned the King List sheet face-down, gently, the way you'd close someone's eyes. He didn't say it was for himself either. He said: "So why doesn't anyone *know* this. I had the long orbit on my wall for four years. Four years. And the man who could've told me in one sentence — *the sar's a number* — that man's got no audience and the orbit's got eleven million views."

"Because the fake one's a better watch," Edith said, from the windowsill, where she'd been sitting with the camera dark in her lap. She'd shot the file open on the table earlier, asked first, and then she'd stopped. "I framed alien tablets for a glossy outfit once, years back, before — I know exactly how you light a school-text about beer to make it look like it came off a saucer. It's not even hard. You underexpose, you go low and raking so the wedges throw shadows, you never, ever show the line that says *this is Dubsiga's.*" She turned the dead camera over in her hands. "The truth's a wide shot in flat light with a footnote. Nobody buys the footnote."

"There's a thing, though," Bashir said slowly, and they all looked at him, because Bashir went slow only when a thing had been bothering him sideways. He had his thumb on the photograph of the Flood Tablet — he'd carried the image out of Room 55 in his eye the way he carried words. "The doc crew today. They had a *list.* I saw the call-sheet on the producer's clipboard when Yusuf was charming her. The tablets they're shooting this whole series. And it's all —" he searched for it "— it's all the loud ones. VA 243's on it. The 'twelve planets' one. Every object that *photographs* like a mystery." He frowned. "And the Flood Tablet's on it but only the part that says *the gods sent the flood.* Not the part Smith actually wept over. The story. Just the woo-shaped slice of the story."

"That's just what sells," Yusuf said. "We just said that."

"Maybe." Bashir kept his thumb on the clay. "But I've watched three of these series get made now, doing the money for one of them. And it's never the *same* tablets that go quiet. It's always the boring true ones. The disproving ones. The school-text about beer rations — the actual evidence, the dull stuff that kills the case the second somebody reads it. Those don't get lost loud. They just —" he opened his hand "— stop being available. Stop getting filmed. Slide off the list. Every time. Like somebody keeps choosing which ones the world gets to see, and the choosing all leans one way."

The onion-gust came up again through the floor. Naila looked at the green folder, soft at its corners, the whole tower laid down in order, and felt the pattern try to assemble itself and refuse — too few data points, a shape with the start of a shape but no closure, the worst kind, the kind that itches.

"That's a coincidence," she said, because it was the honest thing to say. "Probably. Three series isn't a sample."

"Probably," Bashir agreed, and nobody at the table believed it all the way, and nobody could prove a single inch of it, which was exactly the trouble.

Yusuf's phone buzzed on the table, walked an inch, stopped. He read it and his whole face changed register. He looked up, and there was the grin from the museum steps, the talked-the-producer-down grin, but under it something straighter.

"Speaking of which," he said. "He's back tomorrow. Wants the file, Naila. The whole tower. Because we're not staying in London with it."

"Where," said Mira, reading it off his mouth before he aimed the word at her.

"Erbil," Yusuf said. "There's a woman there who can do to my pattern what Naila just did to the orbit. Reads the language. Reads the *meaning.* And she's already said no to us once."

Naila looked at the two exits, front and fire-escape, and then at the file, and then — past the kebab-shop window, out over the orange wash of the road — east, where Jupiter was up, crossing, doing what it had always done, while down here the question of *who keeps choosing what we get to see* sat on the table with no answer and the start of a shape.

"Then we'd better pack the clay," she said, "in the right order."
