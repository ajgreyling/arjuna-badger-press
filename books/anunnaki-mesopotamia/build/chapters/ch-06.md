# Chapter 6 — What the Word Doesn't Mean

Frik woke before the muezzin, which in Erbil meant before everything. He lay on the thin guest-room mattress and let the call come up out of the city the way it had come up every dawn since they'd landed — first one minaret, then a second leaning on it half a beat late, then the whole bowl of the old town singing slightly out of tune with itself, and he didn't have the words for any of it, but he had the shape, and the shape held him still. That was the gift nobody believed about him. The tics ran on noise and clenched on silence and went quiet, sometimes, inside a rhythm large enough to forgive a man for being one more arrhythmia in it.

Then his right shoulder snapped up to his ear, twice, hard, and the spell broke, and he was just a man on a floor in a country that hadn't asked for him.

He got up. He drank the water from the clay jug Narin's uncle kept on the sill, which tasted of the jug, and he went out into the courtyard where the light was the colour of weak tea and Jakobus was already crouched by the front wheel of the Beast with the canvas water bag weeping its slow dark stain into the dust beneath it, doing whatever he did to a vehicle that was older than most of them and ran better. The man didn't look up. He lifted two fingers off the wheel rim, which was hello, and went back to it. Somewhere in the truck a cassette was playing something with a marimba in it, very low, keeping time with nothing.

"Morning," Frik said, and then, because the morning quiet was too clean and his throat needed the load off, "*morning, morning,*" twice more, lighter, getting it out.

Jakobus tipped his head. The cayenne tin came out of his shirt pocket, was offered, was waved off, went back. None of it remarked on. That was the thing about the truck and the man at it: nothing Frik did out here was a problem to be solved. It was just weather. The crew was the first room he'd ever been in where his body wasn't the most interesting thing about him, and he'd have crawled across the bazaar on the broken glass of his own teeth before he'd say that out loud.

---

They worked in the long room off the courtyard, the one Narin's uncle used for storing the good rugs and the dates and a forty-year-run of agricultural-ministry calendars he would not throw away. Narin had cleared the long table. On it she'd laid, side by side, three things: a printout of VA 243, big, the famous one, the seal with the little gods and the little circle of dots that a man in New York had once called the solar system; a fat reference grammar with a cracked spine; and her own laptop, open to a photograph of an actual tablet, dun-coloured, fingernail-marked, with the cuneiform standing up off it in the slant light like a field of struck matches.

Naila stood at the head of the table with her arms folded close, not because she was cold but because the room had six people in it and the door was at her back and that arithmetic was always running. Yusuf had put himself by the door without being asked. He did that now. He'd done it since the roof.

"Show me the orbit," Narin said.

She said it without heat. She said it the way a teacher says a thing she has said before and will say again and would, if she were honest, like to stop having to say.

"The sar." She tapped the printout, the little dots. "This is the place people start. Three thousand six hundred. The orbit of the planet that comes back every three thousand six hundred years. So." She slid the grammar across to Bashir, who caught it without looking, the way he caught everything, and then she pulled the laptop toward Naila instead. "You read numbers. Read me that one."

Naila came off the wall. The table was a thing to do with her hands and her eyes and it pulled her in past the count of the doors. She bent over the screen.

"Sexagesimal," she said. "Base sixty." Her finger hovered, not touching. "It's not a quantity that means *years*. It's a digit in a place-value system." She looked up. "Sixty squared. Thirty-six hundred is just — it's *the round number*. It's how a kid in a scribal school writes a big tidy figure, the way we'd write a thousand or a million. It turns up everywhere. Loan periods. Grain. Gods reigning before the Flood — this king ruled a *sar*, this one ten *sars*, and they didn't mean he was on the throne for thirty-six thousand years, they meant *a very long time, a clean impressive number.*" She straightened. "You can't take a digit and bolt a telescope to it. It's a *ten*. It's a *ten* with a hat on."

Edith had her camera up — quiet, low, the lens an extension of her cheekbone, the way she held it when she'd already asked. She'd asked Narin yesterday, formally, in the courtyard, *may I photograph the work and not the room,* and Narin had looked at her for a long time and said *the work, yes. Not my uncle. Not the calendars,* and something had been agreed there that the rest of them only half saw. So now Edith framed the table and the tablet and Naila's clean unjewelled hands and she did not frame Narin's face, and the not-framing was its own kind of statement, made over and over, a hundred small refusals an hour.

"Now," Narin said. "Nibiru."

She let the word sit. It was a word the whole world thought it owned now — doom-planet, hidden planet, the planet the network's other channel ran a special on every eighteen months, *Nibiru: Is It Coming?*, with a render of a brown gas giant looming over a screaming coastline.

"It's a real word," Narin said. "That's the part people don't expect. It is *in the texts.* Right here." She pulled up a column of glyphs. "*Né-bé-ru.* From the verb *to cross.* It's the crossing-place. The ford. Where you ford a river, where the ferry runs — that's a *nibiru.*" She looked round at them. "And in the astronomical texts, when a planet reaches the point in the sky where it *crosses* — its station, the place it turns — they call that the *nibiru* of that planet. It's a *position.* Anything can have one." A small, dry pause. "The one they wrote about most, the *nibiru* that became a proper name almost, the king of the crossing-places —"

"Jupiter," Bashir said. He'd looked up from the grammar. There was a particular pleasure on his face that Frik had learned to watch for, the pleasure of a man who'd just felt a word click home in a slot it had been cut for three thousand years ago. "It's Jupiter."

"It's Jupiter." Narin almost smiled. "Marduk's planet. The biggest light that isn't the moon or the morning star. They watched it cross every year. *Every year.* You can watch it tonight, off the citadel wall, if the haze lifts. It is the least hidden object in that sky." She spread her hand flat on the table. "It was never a twelfth planet. It was never coming for us. And it was *never*—" and here she touched the printout of VA 243, the little gods, the little dots — "*never once, in any text, in any list, attached to these.*"

"The Anunnaki," Naila said.

"The Anunnaki." Narin took her hand back off the seal. "Different word. Different file. Different shelf. The man who made all this famous put the planet and the gods in the same sentence and the sentence has been travelling under its own power for fifty years. But the *clay* never put them together. He did."

---

It went very quiet in the long room. Frik felt the quiet land on him the way it always did, a pressure, a thing his nervous system would have to pay for, and he held still and breathed through it and watched the dust turn in the slant of light over the table.

Because here was the floor tilting. He could feel it tilt. He'd come in through the front door like everybody did — he'd watched the network's stuff, the gold-mining gods, the bred-from-apes, the whole gorgeous machine of it — and now a woman in a rug-room in northern Iraq had taken a screwdriver to it in its own language, gently, professionally, the way you take apart a thing you respect even when it's wrong, and the planet was just Jupiter and the orbit was just a *ten with a hat on*, and the question that came up through the hole where the aliens used to be wasn't *were they real* anymore.

It was: *then who, and why did we want it so badly.*

"VA 243," Yusuf said, from the door. His voice was careful. "The solar system seal. The dots. If it's not — what is it."

Narin pulled the printout to the centre. "It's a dedication."

She said it so plainly that Frik watched it not land, watched the whole alien architecture of the thing fail to fall just because a fact had been spoken, because facts didn't work like that, never had.

"There's an inscription on it," Narin said. "Two lines of cuneiform. People who talk about the solar system never read the two lines, because the two lines are the boring part, and the boring part is the whole point." Her finger moved along it. "*Dub-si-ga.* A name. A man's name, or a title — a high official, a scribe-prince. And then: *your servant.* Somebody had this seal cut and dedicated to a person named Dubsiga. *To Dubsiga, from your servant.* That's it. That's what it says. It's a business card. It's a man telling another man, *I'm yours, here's my mark.*" She looked up and her face had gone, for a moment, entirely undefended. "And the little dots aren't planets to scale. There's no astronomy in the proportions, there's no orbit, the spacing is wrong for the solar system and right for *decoration.* It's a star and the moon and stars, the way you and I would draw a sky. The kind of sky a child draws. The kind a god stands under." She sat back. "It's a man, kneeling, with his mark, in front of a god, under stars. It's the most ordinary devout thing in the entire collection. That's why nobody photographs the inscription."

And it was at that moment — Frik would think about this for a long time, about the timing of it, the way his body knew before he did — that the man on the floor near the door said it.

There was a person in the room nobody had named yet. Apsû had sent a producer-fixer with them, a young man called Deniz, fluent and bright and embedded, who'd been quiet and useful all morning and who was, Frik understood now, *of the network*, in the soft way the network was of everything, a clean-handed cheerful young man who believed, who actually believed, who'd grown up on the channel and the renders and the looming brown planet. And Deniz had been getting more and more wound through Narin's whole patient demolition, leaning forward, and now he couldn't hold it.

"Okay but," Deniz said, "but the sar — the thirty-six hundred — that *is* the orbit, though, right, because the King List, the reigns, they match the —"

"The sar is the *orbit*—" Frik said.

It came out of him entire and finished, in Deniz's own cadence, in Deniz's own rising hopeful pitch, the echo arriving a quarter-second behind the original and landing dead on top of it like a second foot in the same footprint — *the sar is the ORBIT* — and the whole room heard it for what it was, which was the sentence held up to the light, the sentence detached from the man and rotated once in the air so everyone could see clean through it.

The echolalia. It took whatever was loudest and gave it back. And what had been loudest, just then, was an honest man reaching for the thing he needed to be true, and Frik's nervous system had photographed the reach and printed it large.

Nobody laughed. That was the mercy of this crew, the thing Frik had crossed a continent to find — nobody laughed, and nobody hushed, and nobody looked at *him*. They looked at the sentence, hanging there, *the sar is the orbit*, with the question mark filed off it by the flatness of the repetition, so that it stopped being a hope and became an X-ray of a hope, and you could see in it exactly where the bone was broken.

Deniz heard it. You could see him hear it. His mouth was still half-open on his own version of it and there was his own want, played back to him in another man's broken voice, with all the *but* and the *though* and the *right?* burned off, just the naked claim, *the sar is the orbit*, and it sounded — out there in the air, separated from him — like exactly what it was. Like a thing somebody needed. Not a thing that was so.

"Sorry," Frik said. His shoulder jerked. "Sorry. It does that. It grabs—" his head ducked, twice — "grabs the loud one."

"No," Yusuf said quietly, from the door. "Don't be sorry."

Because Yusuf had gone very still. Frik watched him, because Frik watched the men by doors. And what was on Yusuf's face wasn't triumph and wasn't the cheap-scorn-on-the-believer thing the network would have shot in close-up — it was *recognition,* the bad soft kind, the kind a man wears when he sees his own old face on a stranger.

"It's a good thing to want," Yusuf said. He was talking to Deniz now, but he wasn't, really; the room understood that he wasn't, really. "That's the part nobody says. It's a *good* thing to want. You want it to be bigger than rations and tax-lists and a man named Dubsiga leaving his card. You want somebody to have come down. You want it to mean we were *chosen* — designed, planted, watched. You want the dust to not be the whole story." He turned the dead phone over in his hand, a habit now, a worry-stone. "I wanted it too. I wanted it like a door. You're in some warehouse job or some — some ordinary nothing life, and the alien story's the one door in the wall, and you'd be a fool not to want the door." His jaw worked. "I'm not laughing at you. I spent ten years walking through that door. It costs more than you think and it doesn't go anywhere, but God, it's the prettiest door in the building."

Deniz didn't say anything. He looked at the printout, at the little kneeling man and his little card and his little homemade sky, and something went out of his shoulders that wasn't faith exactly — Frik wouldn't have called it faith, faith wasn't being touched here, that wasn't what this room was for — but was the *flattery* going out of it. The thing Yusuf had named. The story had told Deniz he was chosen and the clay was telling him he was a man, made of clay, like the man on the seal, kneeling under a sky he'd drawn himself.

Mira had been at the far end of the table the whole time, where she could see every mouth, her chin tipped, reading. She'd missed none of it — Frik knew she'd missed none of it, she got more of a room than any of them, she got the part under the words. Now she lifted her hands and signed something to Bashir, who'd been learning, badly, eagerly. He frowned, parsing, then said it for her, low, to the table:

"She says — the want is the real subject." He watched her hands again. "She says nobody's wrong here. She says—" his eyes flicked, careful, getting it right — "the only liar in the room is the one who *sells* the want."

And that landed in Frik's chest harder than the orbit had. Because the want wasn't the lie. The want was the most human thing on the table. The lie was somebody monetising it.

---

They broke after that. Narin's uncle came through with the tea on a dented tray, the glasses too hot to hold, and outside in the courtyard Jakobus had got the second water bag rigged over the awning and the shade under it was ten degrees cooler than the lane and they drifted to it the way the crew always drifted to whatever shade the man had built without being asked. He poured nobody's tea. He'd taught them, somewhere back along the road none of them had been on with him, that you let a man pour his own. He'd taken his shades off in the shade. He said, to Narin's uncle, two careful words in Kurdish, badly pitched, and the old man laughed and corrected him and Jakobus said them again, worse, and the old man laughed harder and it was, Frik understood, a thing the man did on purpose — got the word wrong so the local could own being right. *Sawubona with the mouth,* Bashir had called it once. *He greets you by handing you the win.*

Naila stood at the edge of the awning's shade with her tea, near the open arch of the courtyard, where the doors were the whole world and not just a wall at her back, and Frik went and stood near her, not too near, and said nothing, which was the company she could take.

"It's cleaner than the lie," she said, after a while, not to him exactly. "The real one. The clay one." She was looking at nothing, at the angle of the citadel up the hill. "The lie says we're special. The clay says we're tired. Made to be tired. Made out of mud and a dead god's blood to do the gods' work so the gods could rest." She turned her glass. "That's a worse story and a better one. I keep doing the arithmetic on it and it keeps coming out *braver.*" She glanced at him, fast, sideways, the way warmth came off her, never head-on. "You did a good thing in there."

"Doesn't feel like a thing I *do,*" Frik said. "Feels like a thing that does me."

"Same," Naila said, which from her was practically an embrace, and they stood in the shade and didn't look at each other and were, both of them, fine.

---

It was Yusuf, in the end, who asked the question.

He'd helped Narin's uncle carry the empty tray back and he came out into the white lane light wiping his hands and he stopped in front of Narin where she stood under the awning with her own tea cooling, and he asked it the way you ask a thing you've earned the right to ask, which is humbly.

"So what were they," he said. "If they weren't planets and they weren't astronauts and the orbit's a ten with a hat on. The Anunnaki. What *were* they. What were they *for.*"

It was Jakobus's question, really. *What is it for.* It had been hanging over the whole table since London. But it wasn't Jakobus's to answer and the man knew it; he didn't even look up from the strap he was checking. The question went to the woman it belonged to, the way everything in this country was supposed to and so rarely did, and Frik watched the network's whole machine — the renders, the looming planet, the gold-mining gods, the fifty years of a sentence travelling under its own power — wait, in that hot lane, on what an Iraqi Assyriologist would do with her own ancestors' word.

Narin didn't answer.

She set her tea down on the low wall. She went back into the long room, and they heard her moving among her uncle's shelves, and she came back out with a different printout — not VA 243, not the famous one, nothing the network had ever sold. A column of dun clay, struck-match glyphs, older-looking, harder.

"Anunnaki," she said, "is not a what. It's a *who*, and the who *moves.* Over two thousand years the word slides under your feet like wet shale, and where it ends up is nothing like where it starts, and the slide is the whole story, and *nobody* who's selling you a planet has ever once watched it slide." She held the new sheet against her chest, not yet handing it over. "You want to know what they were for. You're asking the wrong tense. Ask me what the word *did.* Ask me what it *became.*"

She turned the sheet around.

The light came across it and the glyphs stood up off the clay, and at the top of the column, set apart, a single word waited to be read — older than the King List, older than the seal, older than the man named Dubsiga and his little homemade sky.

"Start here," Narin said. "*The princely offspring.* That's where it begins."

She held the page out flat, into the white lane light, and waited for them to come and read it with her.
