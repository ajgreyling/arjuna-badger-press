# Chapter 1 — The Most-Read Story on Earth

The thing under the glass was the size of a cigarette packet, and it had outlived every empire that ever sneered at small things.

Naila stood at the rope in Room 55 and did not look at the streaming crew yet, though she could hear them — the soft authoritative murmur of a man being recorded saying something he wanted very much to be true. She looked at the tablet. K.3375. Sixteen lines a side and a corner gone, the surface the colour of weak tea, the wedges packed so tight they read like a field of barley flattened by one careful wind. She let her eye fall into them the way she fell into a train timetable or a folded map: not reading, the cuneiform sat just past the edge of her Akkadian, but counting. The columns. The breaks. The rhythm of a scribe who had done this ten thousand times and was bored and excellent, both at once.

Most-read story on earth, the little label said, more or less. It was not bragging.

Behind her the city did what cities did, eleven million people pressing against the museum's quiet, and she kept her back to the room and her hand loose at her side and breathed through the part of her that wanted to count the people too. There were too many. There were always too many. The trick was to find one fixed true thing and stand near it. The tablet was a fixed true thing. The tablet had been a fixed true thing for two and a half thousand years.

"Roll it from the wonder," said a voice she would come to hate, gently, in a way that took her a moment to even register as hostile. "Don't open on the woo. We earn the woo."

She turned her head a degree.

The doc crew had set up across the room near the relief of the dying lion, which was either ignorance or cheek. A presenter in a charcoal henley, three days of designed stubble, the kind of warm low voice that sells a mattress. A DP with a gimbal. A producer in expensive trainers murmuring into a headset. And the light. They'd brought a single hard source and stood it low and raking, so it skidded across the tablet's face and threw every wedge into a long blue shadow, and the surface that was the colour of weak tea went the colour of a planetarium ceiling. Pinpricks. Constellations. A star chart for anyone who already wanted a star chart.

Edith saw it first. Naila felt her arrive at her shoulder before she heard her — the particular stillness of a person who looks for a living, gone very quiet.

"That's a key light," Edith said, low. "At about thirty degrees. You light a coin like that to read the date. You light *that*—" a tilt of the chin at the tablet "—like that to make a punter see Orion."

"It's the most-read story on earth," Naila said. "And they've lit it so nobody can read it."

"That's not an accident. That's the job." Edith's hands stayed at her sides. She had a camera on a strap and she was not lifting it, and Naila had learned, in the few days she'd known her, that Edith not lifting the camera was its own kind of sentence.

The presenter took a breath. The producer counted him in with two fingers.

"This," said the warm voice, and the room hushed for him because that is what the room is trained to do, "is the Flood Tablet. Most of you were taught it's a Bible story. Noah, the ark, two of every animal. What they *don't* teach you—" he let it land, the *they*, the whole conspiracy of teachers and curators and the people who'd kept the lights on in this building for two centuries "—is that this clay is a *thousand years older* than your Bible. And it doesn't just talk about a flood. It talks about the gods who *made* that flood. The Anunnaki. The ones who came down from the sky."

Frik made a sound.

He was three feet to Naila's left, hands jammed in the pockets of a jacket he'd zipped to the throat because crowds made the tics worse and he knew it, and the sound that came out of him was not a word, just a hard click of the soft palate, twice, and then his shoulder ticked up toward his ear and stayed a half-second too long. A woman near him stepped away. Naila did the thing she did, which was move a quarter step so she was between Frik and the woman, not a wall, just weather, just a shift in the room's pressure. Bashir was already on his other side, saying nothing, standing close enough that Frik had two known bodies in his peripheral and the room was smaller and the smaller a room the safer it was.

"—came down to mine our gold," the presenter went on, and his hand found the air over the glass like a faith healer's. "Think about that. A species, advanced beyond anything we can imagine, and they needed *workers*. So they made us. The book says God formed man from the dust of the ground. The *older* book—" again the *older*, like a knife sliding under a fingernail "—the older book tells you *who* did the forming. And it wasn't God."

"It is so beautiful," Bashir said, and he meant it, and it took Naila a second to understand he was not talking about the speech.

He was looking at the tablet. He'd come up on her right without her clocking it, which almost no one managed, and his head was tilted and his lips were moving, and she knew that movement. Bashir reading. Bashir's eyes did to language what hers did to number — fell into it, drowned a little, came up changed. He had once told a border guard in Trieste that he was three different nationalities in the space of forty minutes and walked out the far side of all three. He read the way other people breathed and he could not always stop, and he was reading the wedges now, the ones the doc crew had lit into oblivion, and he wasn't seeing stars. He was seeing a flood. The old flood. Six days and seven nights the wind blew, the storm and the flood overwhelmed the land. He had told her last night that it was the most frightened writing he knew, frightened in a way the king-lists and the beer-rations weren't, a man's hand shaking out the end of the world.

"They lit it like a planetarium," Naila said to him, quiet, "and you're reading the rain."

"Someone is always reading the rain," Bashir said. "That's why it's still here."

---

Here is the part nobody films, because it isn't slick, and Naila loved it more than she loved most living people.

In 1872, a man named George Smith sat in a back room of this same building, twenty-eight years old, no university, no Greek, no Latin, no money — he had left school at fourteen and apprenticed as a banknote engraver, which is a trade about putting tiny careful marks on flat surfaces so that they cannot be faked. He used to spend his lunch hours upstairs at the galleries because he could not afford to spend them anywhere that cost anything, and a man named Birch noticed the engraver who kept staring at the dead languages, and gave him fragments to sort. Smith taught himself cuneiform off broken clay on his lunch breaks. There is no slicker way to say it because there isn't a slicker version. He just did it.

And one afternoon he was working through a pile of unbaked tablet fragments that had come out of the ground at Nineveh — the library of an Assyrian king who'd been dead two and a half thousand years and had wanted, the way men with too much power sometimes want, to own every story ever written — and Smith pieced together a broken line, and then the next, and the line said that a ship had come to rest on a mountain, and that a man had sent out a dove, and the dove had found no resting place and returned.

He was, by the account a colleague left, reading the eleventh tablet of the Epic of Gilgamesh, and he came to that line, and he stood up.

The colleague's name was Budge, and the line Budge left is the one Naila kept the way some people keep a saint's relic. Smith, said Budge, *jumped up and rushed about the room in a great state of excitement, and, to the astonishment of those present, began to undress himself.*

That was the whole of it. A self-taught engraver found Noah eleven centuries early, in a hand older than the Hebrew, and was so undone by it that he started taking his clothes off in the British Museum. Not because he'd disproved the Bible. Because he'd found that the story was *older and bigger and stranger* than anyone had known, that it had been waiting in the clay the whole time, and a man with no business reading it had read it, and the proper response to that — Naila was absolutely certain of this, it was the truest thing she knew — the only proper, sane, human response to that was to be amazed past the edge of your own dignity.

The doc crew would not be putting George Smith in the segment. There was no woo in a man who got it right and wept.

---

"Run me the line again," the presenter said. "The big one. About the workers."

The producer in the expensive trainers stepped in, and Naila saw Edith go still in the new way, the working way, and Edith's hand drifted toward the camera at her hip and did not lift it. "He says they made us to *bear the toil*," the producer said. "We'll cut to the gold-mine graphics. Spaceships, the lot. But land the toil. People feel the toil."

And the thing was — the thing that made Naila's jaw set, the thing she would still be turning over in two days when she stood in front of a worse room and a worse light — the thing was that they almost had it.

*Bear the toil.* That part was real. That part was in the actual clay, older than the flood tablet even, in a poem called Atrahasis where the lesser gods went on strike — went on *strike*, downed tools, surrounded the storehouse at night because they were sick of digging the river-channels for the big gods forever — and the high gods, panicking, hit on a solution: make a new thing to carry the work. Make it out of clay. Mix the clay with the blood of a slaughtered god so the thing would have a god's spark in it and a clay's weakness, and set it to the toil, so that the gods could rest.

That was the true story, and it was the bleakest story Naila knew, and the doc crew was three feet from it and walking the other way as fast as they could go.

Because the true story is that you were made to *work*. Made of mud and a murdered god, made tired, made mortal, made to dig the canal and feed the temple and die. There is no flattery in it anywhere. And the alien version took that exact bone — made to bear the toil — and dressed it in chrome. Made you a project. Made the sky want you. Made the toil into gold-mining, which at least is a *resource*, which at least means you were worth coming light-years for. The alien story whispered: you were *designed*. You were *wanted*. Somebody important enough to have a spaceship looked at the empty earth and thought, *I'll make something here.*

It was the kindest lie in the world, and the clay refused to tell it. The clay said: mud, and a dead god, and the canal won't dig itself.

They'd swapped the bravest story humans ever told themselves for a more comfortable one and called the comfortable one the *hidden truth*. Naila didn't have a word for what that did to her chest. Bashir would have had four words for it in four languages. She just stood there counting columns and feeling it.

---

"Six lines," she said, to nobody, to Edith, to the tablet.

"What?"

"The gold." Naila kept her voice under the presenter's. "Watch the segment they're building. It's the whole thing. The gods made man to mine gold. That's the spine of it, that's the whole twelfth-planet theory in one sentence, and it's the part they'll never put on the clay because it isn't *on* any clay. The toil is on the clay. Atrahasis, the Igigi, the strike, the blood, the mud. All real, all there, you could photograph it. But the gold—" she shook her head once "—the gold is the one thing they need, and it's the one thing they had to bring with them."

Edith was looking at her now and not at the doc crew. "How do you know it's not there?"

"Because if it were there it would be the most famous line in Assyriology and they'd have it on a mug in the shop." She nodded at the gift shop's far doorway. "It's not on a mug. It's nowhere. The gods are *exhausted* in the poems. They want to *rest*. That's the want. Rest, not riches. You make a slave because you're tired, not because you've found a seam." She heard her own voice flatten the way it did when the number wasn't adding and she was the only one in the room who could see the column not balancing. "They built a planet-sized story on a load that the freight doesn't carry. The toil is freight. The gold's a stowaway."

"Say that on camera," said Edith, very dry, "and they'd cut you for being boring."

"I am boring," Naila said. "That's why I'm right."

Edith almost smiled. Almost. She'd photographed three wars and a fourth she didn't name, and the almost was as far as her face went these days, but it counted, and Naila counted it.

---

The presenter was building to the close. The producer had her two fingers up. The DP had floated the gimbal in low and slow across the glass so that on the eventual cut the tablet would seem to *turn*, to reveal itself, the pinpricks sliding under the raking light like a sky rotating, and a viewer at home with a beer would feel, in the animal back of the brain, that they were looking at the heavens.

"So the next time someone tells you the Flood is just a Bible story," the warm voice said, and Naila could already hear the swell of the music that wasn't there yet, "ask them why the older book — the *original* book — says the gods came down. Ask them what those gods were *mining*. And ask yourself—" the pause, the killer pause, the one that paid the rent "—who taught a Bronze Age people about the solar system? Who told them about a planet you can't even *see*?"

And Frik, who had held it for nine minutes, who had clicked his palate and ticked his shoulder and stood between his two known bodies and breathed, said, loud and clear and helpless into the hush the producer had so carefully built:

"*Nobody — it's a numeral —*"

The room flinched. The producer's head came round. The presenter lost his line.

Frik's hand had come up to his own mouth, too late, the way it always came too late, his eyes wide with the particular horror of a man whose body has told the truth in front of strangers again, and Bashir's hand was already flat between his shoulder blades and Naila had already moved her quarter step and Mira — Mira, who'd been reading the whole room with her back to the wall, who hadn't heard the presenter's voice but had read every centimetre of the producer's spine and the DP's hands and the way the light fell — Mira's eyes went to Frik and then to Naila and then to the door, a clean three-point question: *him, then us, then out.*

"*Three thousand six hundred,*" Frik said, lower now, the dam down, the sentence finishing itself because it had to. "*The sar. It's three-six-zero-zero. Base sixty. It's how they wrote numbers. It's not an orbit, it's a—* sorry. Sorry. It's not an orbit."

"Sir," the producer said, "we are *filming*—"

"I know," Frik said miserably. "I'm sorry. I can't always—" the shoulder ticked. "I can't always not."

---

They moved him out the way the crew had learned to move, which was not as a unit, because a unit gets stopped. Bashir drifted Frik toward the Assyrian reliefs at an angle that read as *wandering*. Mira went the other way and stood by the great winged bull where she could see the door and Edith's face both, her hands resting loose, ready to sign the all-clear or the abort. Yusuf — Naila hadn't even seen him in the room, Yusuf had a gift for being the man no one logged — peeled off toward the producer with his palms open and his voice already apologising, *so sorry, my brother, neurological thing, completely involuntary, we'll get out of your light,* the money-man's voice, the voice that unblocked things, and the producer's shoulders came down a degree because Yusuf had given her a story she could tell her boss.

Naila stayed at the rope one more second.

The presenter was getting reset. The light was getting re-aimed. The blue shadows slid back across the wedges. And the tablet sat there under all of it being exactly what it was, the most-read story on earth in its oldest surviving hand, a man's shaking record of the end of the world and the dove that came back with nothing and the dove that came back with an olive, written down by people who knew the river could kill them and had decided to write it down anyway, to say *we were here, the water came, a few of us lived.*

Frik was right and Frik was not allowed to be right out loud, which was the whole shape of the world in one room. The *sar* was 3,600. It was a numeral. It was how you wrote a big round number when you counted in sixties, the way they did, the way the clock on her phone still did, sixty seconds, sixty minutes, three hundred and sixty degrees, the dead Sumerian arithmetic still ticking under everyone's wrist. Someone had taken that numeral and called it an orbit and called the orbit a planet and called the planet a homeworld and called the homeworld a destiny, and at no point — at no single point in that whole climbing tower — had they gone back down to the bottom and checked whether the first brick was even a brick.

It wasn't. It was a number. It was the careful boring true thing, and the careful boring true thing was always the first casualty, and Naila found that she was angry in the specific way she got angry, which was quiet and patient and good with files.

There was a file. There was already a file. There would be more of a file by tomorrow.

Edith fell in beside her as they walked. She still hadn't lifted the camera. "You clocked the gold and I clocked the light," she said. "Same hole. Two ends."

"The segment was supposed to prove it," Naila said. "It just showed me where it breaks. Both places. You don't light a thing like that if the thing speaks for itself."

"No," Edith said. "You light it like that when you're selling it. The question is what." She glanced back once at the doc crew, at the producer murmuring into the headset, at the slick warm machine of it humming back to life over the rope. "Who pays for a museum shoot to tell people not to read the most-read thing on earth?"

Out past the colonnade, in the grey London afternoon, a man in good sunglasses was leaning against a bollard with his arms folded, perfectly at ease, watching the doors. He wore a clean shirt and the kind of stillness that doesn't photograph. When Edith's eye went to him on reflex — old habit, the conflict shooter's habit, find the man not looking at anything — he was already looking somewhere else, and her thumb found the camera and did not press it, and Naila understood that she'd just declined a shot, and that the declining cost her, and that she'd file *that* away too.

"He brought us here," Edith said, which was all the answer she gave about the man, and it wasn't an answer at all.

Behind them the warm voice started again from the top, earning its woo.

In front of them Frik had his breathing back and Bashir's hand off his shoulder and was already saying, low and shamed and a little wild, *it really isn't an orbit, though,* and Mira signed something at him that the crew read as *we know, you absolute disaster, we love you,* and Yusuf came out of the building grinning the grin of a man who'd just talked a producer out of calling security, and the man in the sunglasses was gone from the bollard as though he'd never had weight.

"Naila," Yusuf said. "Tonight. The file. You said you had the case all in one place."

"I have it in one place," Naila said.

The numeral. The crossing-point. The man named Dubsiga who'd had his solar system stolen and was about to get it back. She had the bottom brick, and she'd been waiting a long time for a room that wanted to see the whole tower come down honestly, with love, in the original language, which was the only honest place there was.

"Then start at the bottom," Yusuf said. "Start with the planet that isn't a planet."

"It's a planet," Naila said. "That's the good part. It's just not *that* planet."

And she went to get her file.
