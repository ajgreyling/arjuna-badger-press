# Chapter 13 — The Same Flood

The light came up grey and then gold, and Bashir was awake before any of them, which was usual, because the first hour of a day in a new place was the hour nobody talked, and the hour nobody talked was the only hour he ever truly rested. He sat on the cool of the unfinished step with a tin mug of tea that Yusuf had made too sweet, and he watched the plain take its colour back from the dark, and he ran the two stories through his head the way you'd run two threads through your fingers to feel where they were spun the same and where they parted.

He had told Naila the truth last night. He had read the flood in all of them. Hebrew with the *vav* doing its quiet work, the *and-and-and* that made the prose move like water itself. Greek with the Septuagint's careful sandals on. Syriac, his grandmother's church-tongue, the one that still wept the old words out on Good Friday. He could read the flood in his sleep. The Sumerian was the only place he had to crawl, and crawling was where the love was.

Inside, the others were beginning. He heard Mira's bare feet, which made no sound he could name but which moved the air, and then her hand on his shoulder, light, a question. He turned so she could see his mouth.

"Tea's poison," he said. "Yusuf made it."

She read his lips and her face did the thing it did, the small flat amusement that lived mostly in the eyes, and she signed something fast and sloppy that he didn't catch, and he gave her the open palm, *again, slower*, and she did it again, and it was *coward*, and she took the mug out of his hand and drank the poison herself, watching him over the rim, daring him.

Narin came out last. She had a thermos of her own — she'd brought it from London, real tea, leaves, the kind you let breathe — and a laptop under one arm and, under the other, a thing wrapped in a tea-towel that turned out to be a stack of printouts, the British Museum's own photographs, the tablets shot from above under raking light so every wedge stood up in its own little shadow.

She did not say good morning. She set the printouts down on the upturned crate they'd been using as a table and looked at the seven of them — six and herself — gathering in the cool concrete shade with their mugs, and Bashir watched her decide, again, whether to do this. She decided it fresh every morning. He respected that. A door you open carelessly is a door you've stopped guarding.

"I'm going to say something first," Narin said, "and then we read."

They waited.

"There is a stupid version of what we're about to do," she said. "The stupid version is a man on the internet with a graphic. Left side, Genesis. Right side, Gilgamesh. Big arrow. *PLAGIARISM.* Caption: *they don't want you to know.*" She let that sit. "I've spent twenty years cleaning that man's fingerprints off my field. He is, in his way, the same animal as the alien man. He needs the two texts to be *identical*, because identical is loud, and loud is what gets clicks. And identical is a lie." She tapped the printout. "They are not identical. They are *related*. Those are completely different sentences and the difference is the whole entire point. If anybody in this room says *the Bible just copied it* I will pack the truck myself. Are we clear."

"Clear," said Yusuf, quietly, and Bashir saw that it cost Yusuf to say it, because Yusuf had been, for four years, exactly the man who needed it loud, and was learning, slowly, painfully, in front of all of them, to need it true instead.

"Good." Narin pulled the first sheet to the top. "George Smith. 1872. Tell them, Bashir, you'll do it better than me."

He hadn't expected the handoff. She did that — gave you the thing you were best at and then watched to see if you'd take it greedily or hold it open. He held it open.

"George Smith was a banknote engraver," Bashir said. "Working class. London. No university. He used to spend his lunch breaks at the British Museum looking at the tablets behind the glass, the ones nobody could read yet, and he taught himself cuneiform off them. Off the *glass*. The way you'd learn a language by reading the menu in a restaurant you can't afford to eat in." He felt his own grandmother in his mouth when he said it, the autodidact ache, the man who belonged everywhere they'd let him stand. "And one day in 1872 he's working through a broken tablet from Nineveh, from Ashurbanipal's library, and he's reading along — and the line says a ship has come to rest on a mountain, and a man sends out a dove, and the dove finds no resting place and comes back."

He stopped, because the next part deserved the stop.

"And Smith — the story goes — Smith stood up. In the reading room. And he started taking his clothes off."

Frik made a sound, a short bark of a laugh that was half tic and half delight, and nobody minded it because it was exactly the right size for the thing.

"He was overcome," Bashir said. "He'd found a flood. Older than the one in his Bible. With a dove in it. He ran around the room. They thought he'd lost his mind. He hadn't. He'd just touched the oldest written wonder anybody had touched in a very long time, and the body does what the body does." He looked at Frik when he said it, on purpose, gently, and Frik looked back, and something passed between them that did not need a word. *The body does what the body does.* "He gave a lecture about it that November. The Prime Minister came. Gladstone. To hear a banknote engraver read a poem older than Moses."

"And then," Narin said, picking it up, dry as the plain, "the newspaper that funded his dig back to Nineveh wanted a headline, so the find got sold as *proof of the Bible*. Which it wasn't. And the man who actually read it spent his short life" — she didn't soften this — "trying to say the more interesting thing over the noise of the simpler one. He died at thirty-six. Dysentery. On the way home from the field. The loud version outlived him by a hundred and fifty years." She squared the printouts. "Now. The poem."

She read it to them. Not from the laptop — from the printout, with her finger moving under the wedges, translating live, and Bashir watched her do it and felt the specific envy of a man watching someone do, in a language he had to crawl in, the thing he could only fly in elsewhere. She read Utnapishtim telling Gilgamesh how the gods resolved to drown the world. How the god Ea, who could not break the oath of silence outright, whispered the warning *to a wall* — to a reed wall — so that the man on the other side of the wall would overhear it and could not later be said to have been *told*.

"That's a lawyer's god," Yusuf said, and laughed, a real one, surprised out of him. "He found the loophole."

"He found the loophole," Narin agreed. "Tear down the house, the wall says. Build a boat. Abandon wealth, seek life. Take the seed of all living things aboard." She moved her finger. "And the man builds it. Seven decks. A perfect cube, near enough — the dimensions are a riddle, very deliberate, the boat is *too* square to be a real boat, which is one of the ways you know you're inside a poem and not a logbook." She glanced up. "Hold that. The square boat. We'll come back to it."

Mira was watching Narin's hands and Narin's mouth both, and when Narin paused, Mira's own hands moved — to Bashir, fast, *the boat shape*, two flat palms making a box, a question in her eyebrows.

"Different boat," Bashir signed back, badly, and said it aloud for the room. "Mira wants the boats compared. They're different. The ark in Genesis is a long boat, a ratio, three hundred by fifty by thirty cubits — that's a *ship* shape, that floats. Utnapishtim's is a cube. Atrahasis, the older one again, the one we read last night — his might be round. A coracle. There's a tablet, found this century, that gives the recipe for a *round* ark, coiled rope and bitumen, like the boats they actually still made on these rivers into living memory." He felt the wonder of it move up his arms. "Three floods. Three different boats. That's not one blueprint photocopied three times. That's three poets, a thousand years apart, telling the *same true frightening thing* — the water came, a man was warned, the seed survived — and each one building the boat his own way."

"Which is exactly what I needed nobody to forget," Narin said, and there was approval in it, brief, real, gone. "Keep going. The water comes."

She read the water. The storm-gods harnessed. The dykes broken. The land shattered like a pot. And then the part Bashir had been waiting for, because it was the part that did the work no graphic could do — the part where the gods themselves, the very gods who had sent the flood, cowered.

*The gods were frightened by the deluge,* Narin read, slowing. *They drew back, they went up to the heaven of Anu. The gods crouched like dogs, crouched against the outer wall.*

The room was quiet.

"They made the storm," Yusuf said, "and then they were scared of it."

"They're not in control," Narin said. "That's the theology of it. The Mesopotamian gods are *inside* the weather, not above it. They're hungry. They're loud. They squabble and they regret and they cower. The goddess Belet-ili — the mother goddess, the one who made humankind — she cries out that she let it happen, she wails that her people have filled the sea like fish. The gods didn't drown the world because the world was wicked. They drowned it because" — she checked the line, because she was Narin and she would not round it off — "in the older version, the real reason, the *human noise.* People multiplied and made too much noise and the head god couldn't sleep."

Frik, who had been still, said: "*Couldn't sleep.*" Echolalia, riding it out, and then the choosing-the-end-of-it that he did only here: "That's the *reason*?"

"That's the reason," Narin said, and she did not laugh at it and she did not let them laugh at it either, and Bashir saw why. Because the next thing she said put a floor under the whole morning.

"Now hold that god — the god who drowns the world to get some sleep, the gods who cower like dogs against the wall — and hold him there, and we'll go to the other flood." She set the Gilgamesh printout aside, gently, the way you'd set down something that had earned its keep, and she opened a worn paperback with a black cover, and Bashir saw it was a Bible, an old one, the Syriac-and-English she'd carried out of London under the printouts, and he understood that she was about to read her own scripture in a room of strangers and that this was the part she decided fresh every morning.

She read Genesis. The flood. And she read it *as a believer reads it*, with the weight in the right places, and she read it *as a scholar reads it*, with her finger under the seams — and Bashir, who could read it in five tongues, sat very still, because watching someone do both of those at once, in one breath, without either one eating the other, was the rarest reading he knew, and he had been to a great many readings.

"Different reason," she said, when she came to it. "Read it. The reason here is not noise. The reason here is *moral.* The earth was filled with violence. And it grieved God in his heart that he had made man." She looked up. "Do you hear it? In the poem, the gods are annoyed. Here, God is *grieved.* The same flood — and they've put a soul in the engine. That is not a copy. That is an *argument.*"

She turned the page.

"And here," she said. "Watch the boat get a number that the other boats don't have. Genesis tells you to bring the animals in twos. And then — this is the part the loud man with the graphic always skips — it *also* says bring seven of some, the clean ones, seven pairs. Two different counts, side by side, in the same chapter." She let that hang for the scholars in the room, which was all of them now. "There are two flood accounts here, stitched together. You can see the stitch. A reader from the south, a reader from the temple, woven into one. The tradition was already arguing with *itself* before it ever got written down. That's not weakness in the text. That's the text being *old* and *honest* about being old."

"Two scribes," Naila said. It was the first thing she'd said all morning. She'd been sitting against the cool wall with her knees up and her two notebooks shut on her lap and her eyes half-closed, doing the count under her breath, and now she opened her eyes. "Two scribes and you can see the join. Like a tablet copied by two hands and the second hand's wedges sit deeper." She wasn't asking. She was placing it, snapping it into the lattice where it went, the pattern under the pattern. "It's the same as the math. The seam shows. The seam's the most honest part."

"The seam's the most honest part," Narin repeated, and looked at Naila a beat longer than she'd looked at any of them, the look of one careful reader recognising another across a table, and then she moved on before it could become a thing, because she was kind. "Now. The birds."

This was the part Bashir had wanted since the step at dawn. He leaned in.

Narin laid the two printouts side by side, the wedges and the verses, and put a finger on each.

"Utnapishtim," she said, "sends out a dove. The dove flies, finds no resting place, comes back. Then he sends a swallow. The swallow finds no resting place, comes back. Then he sends a raven. And the raven" — she paused — "does not come back. Because the raven has found the water gone down. So the man knows it's safe."

She moved her other finger.

"Noah sends out a raven first. The raven goes to and fro until the waters dry. Then he sends a dove. The dove finds no rest, comes back. He waits seven days. Sends the dove again. It comes back with an olive leaf in its beak. He waits seven more days. Sends it a third time. It does not return."

She looked up at the room.

"Same idea," she said. "Send a bird to read the world for you, because the bird can land where the man can't. *Exactly* the same idea, a thousand years apart, on two sides of the same river-world. And" — the scalpel — "*different birds, in a different order, doing different things.* Dove, swallow, raven. Raven, dove, dove, dove. One of them carries an olive leaf — that's Genesis only, that's a new thing, that's *hope on the wing*, and it's not in the poem at all. The loud man would put both lists in a box and draw an equals sign. There is no equals sign. There's a family resemblance. There's a grandmother's nose on a grandchild's face." She straightened. "If you want to feel how close two stories can be while being two stories — read the birds."

Bashir found his throat had gone tight and he didn't entirely know why, except that it was the leaf. The whole horror of the drowned world and the cowering gods and the human noise, and a poet a thousand years downstream of all of it had reached into the wreckage and given the bird a green leaf to carry back, a thing that grows, and Bashir thought that whoever did that had done it on *purpose*, had taken the old terrible flood and bent it, just slightly, toward the morning — and he could not have explained it to anyone who hadn't read both, and that was the loneliness, and that was also the gift.

"And then," Narin said, "the part that ends the morning."

She read the end of the poem. The flood subsides. The boat grounds on the mountain — *Nimush*, she said, *a real peak, you can argue about which one, somewhere up toward the Zagros, not Ararat, a different mountain entirely, hold that too* — and Utnapishtim opens a window and lets out the birds and then he comes down and he makes a sacrifice. He pours out a libation. He sets up offering vessels and burns reed and cedar and myrtle.

And the gods, she read, smelled the savour. The gods smelled the sweet savour.

*And the gods,* she read, *gathered like flies over the offering.*

She set the printout down.

"Like flies," she said.

Nobody moved.

"They've been crouched against the wall like dogs," she said, "scared of the storm they made, *starving*, because nobody's been feeding them, because all the people who fed them are drowned. And the one man left alive lights a fire and the smell of meat goes up and the gods come down on it" — her hand made the smallest gesture, a descent, a swarming — "like flies on a carcass. That's the line. A thousand years before Genesis. The gods are *hungry,* and they need us, and when they smell the smoke they come down like flies."

She let it land all the way down.

"Now hear what Genesis does with the same picture," she said, and turned the page, and read it. Noah builds an altar. He takes of every clean beast and offers burnt offerings. *And the LORD smelled a sweet savour.* The same image — the smoke goes up, the smell reaches heaven. "Same furniture," Narin said softly. "The savour. The offering. The smell rising. The single survivor at the altar. And then watch the camera move. There are no flies. There is no swarm. There is no hungry god. There is one God who smells the offering and says in his heart that he will not curse the ground again for man's sake. The picture is inherited" — she touched the wedges, touched the verses — "and the meaning is *overturned.* They took the hungry gods and they refused them. They kept the smoke and they threw away the flies."

Bashir realised he was leaning so far forward his tea had gone cold in his hand.

"That," Narin said, "is what it means to say Genesis has a genealogy. Not theft. Not a copy. A people in exile — *here*, by these rivers, in Babylon, six hundred years before Christ, sat in the captial of the empire that had burned their temple and dragged them east — and they sat in the empire's own library, with the empire's own flood story open in front of them, the gods like flies and the noise and the cube boat — and they *answered it.* Line by line. Picture by picture. They took the most powerful story their conquerors had, the story Babylon told to say *this is how the world is*, and they rebuilt it to say *no. Not flies. Not noise. Not a god who can't sleep. A God who grieves, and a covenant, and a leaf.*" She closed the black book. "They kept their soul by re-authoring the empire's own myth. That's the most extraordinary thing I know how to put in front of you. And it's a thousand times braver than aliens."

Frik said it then. He'd been holding it, Bashir could see, jaw working, the tic riding up under it, and when it came it came clean, the involuntary truth that he could not paint over and so never did:

"*They wrote back.*"

"They wrote back," Narin said, and for the first time that morning she smiled, properly, at Frik, who had said in two words the thing she'd spent twenty years and a whole dawn trying to say.

Mira had her hands over her own heart, flat, and then she signed something to the room, slow enough that even Bashir caught all of it, and he said it aloud because that was the deal, that was always the deal, nobody got talked over here: "Mira says — what did they not say." He frowned. "She means — in the answer. What did the exiles leave *out*, on purpose, that we can only see if we already know what they were answering."

The room turned to her. Narin turned to her, and Bashir watched the Assyriologist actually stop, the way she had not stopped for the loud man or the alien man or any of them, and look at the deaf woman who read the unsaid, and consider the question as a real question, the best one of the morning.

"That," Narin said slowly, "is the right question. And it's the next one." She gathered the printouts, the wedges and the verses both, squared them under the tea-towel. "Because the flood is where they *kept* the picture and bent it. There's another place — the very beginning, the creation, the waters and the deep — where they kept a single *word* and refused everything around it. Where Babylon's god makes the world by *butchering* a goddess and splitting her body — and Genesis makes it by *speaking.*" She tucked the bundle under her arm. "Combat against word. That one's not a family resemblance. That one's a fight. We'll do it tonight, in the cool, when the light's gone flat and nobody's eyes hurt."

Outside, near the Beast, Bashir could see Jakobus through the doorway, doing some slow patient thing to the water bag that hung off the truck's flank and wept its single bright drop into the dust, keeping itself cold the way it had kept itself cold across a different and farther desert. He had his back to them. He had been, Bashir was fairly sure, within earshot of the whole reading and had not come in, because the morning belonged to Narin and to the dead poets and to the people who lived here now, and he knew it, and so he stayed outside with the truck and the slow drip and let it.

Naila had her notebooks open again. She wasn't running the sar. She was making two columns, the way she did, and at the top of one she'd written, in her square small hand, *flies,* and at the top of the other she'd written nothing yet, because the other column was the silence — the thing they'd left out — and you don't fill a column like that until you know what it was answering.

"Tonight," she said, to no one, to the count, to the lattice closing its next gap. "The water that gets divided."

"Tonight," Bashir agreed, and went to make new tea, the right way, with leaves, because somebody had to, and because the morning had earned it.
