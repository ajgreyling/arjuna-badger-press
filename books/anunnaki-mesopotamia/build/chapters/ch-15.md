# Chapter 15 — The Joke in the Dead Language

The cool came late.

All day the heat had sat on the safehouse like a hand pressing a lid, and Bashir had felt the word he wanted to say going soft in his mouth, the way a thing does when you carry it too long without setting it down. He'd carried it through the morning's tea, through Yusuf's phone calls to a man in Sulaymaniyah about a man in Dohuk, through the long flat hours when nobody moved and the lizard on the wall by the window did not move either. He'd carried it past noon, when Naila had her notebook open and was checking the same column of base-sixty numerals for the fourth time, not because she doubted them but because the checking was its own kind of rest. He'd held it the way you hold a coal you mean to plant in a fire later.

Now the light was going. It came in long and yellow under the awning Jakobus had rigged from the Beast's tarp, and the dust in it turned to gold, and somewhere below the citadel a man was calling people to prayer, and the call rose and the heat broke, and Bashir set the word down.

"Ninti," he said.

Narin looked up from the low table. She had a glass of black tea going cold beside her and a photocopy of a tablet under her hand, the lines of it furred and broken where the clay had chipped three thousand years before anyone thought to photograph it. She had spent the day gating them. *Not that text. You're not ready for that text. You'd read it like Sitchin read it.* She gated by instinct now, the way you'd flinch a child's hand off a stove. But she didn't gate this. She watched him say the name and something in her face came halfway open, the same half-open Mira clocked from across the room and didn't mention.

"Go on then," Narin said.

—

He'd reached her in Aramaic, four days ago, at the museum gate. That was the thing nobody else on the crew quite understood, and he'd never tried to explain it because the explaining made it small. He'd had thirty languages by then and none of them a country. You learned to read a border guard's first word and answer in his mother tongue before he'd decided what you were, and the door that had been shut swung to a crack, and you went through the crack, and that was a life. Belong everywhere. Native nowhere. He'd said three words of her grandmother's language and watched a closed Iraqi Assyrian face decide he was not, after all, only one more foreigner come to dig her dead out of the ground for the camera.

It was the loneliest gift he owned. He could open any door and stand in no room.

But he could feel a joke that had been waiting three thousand years for someone to laugh, and tonight he was going to laugh at it with the right people, and that was a thing too.

—

"There's a poem," Bashir said. "Sumerian. *Enki and Ninhursag.* It's old — old the way the others are old, the way that stops meaning anything when you say the number. There's a paradise in it. A clean place. No sickness, the raven doesn't croak, the lion doesn't kill, the wolf doesn't snatch the lamb. A garden where nothing has gone wrong yet."

"Eden," Edith said, low. She had her camera in her lap and her hand on it, flat, the way you'd hold a dog by the collar. She did not raise it. Bashir clocked that she did not raise it and was grateful in a way he didn't have a word for in any of his languages, which was rare enough that he noticed.

"It's a paradise," Bashir said. "I won't say it's Eden. Narin will skin me." Narin's mouth moved. "But it's the same *furniture.* A garden, a god, water that makes the place live, and then — a god who can't leave the plants alone."

"Enki," Narin said. "Eats them. Eats the eight plants of the garden. He wants to *know* them, what they are, so he eats them, one after another, and Ninhursag — she's the great mother goddess, the lady of the mountain — she curses him for it. She says she won't look on him with the eye of life until he dies. And he starts to die. Eight plants, eight sicknesses. He's dying organ by organ."

"And the foxes broker a deal," Bashir said, grinning now, "because in these poems there is always, *always,* a clever animal who fixes things —"

"There's a fox," Frik said. It came out of him sideways, fast, the involuntary echo, and then a small tic of the shoulder, and then his own voice, dry: "Sorry. There's a fox."

"There's a fox," Bashir agreed, easy, no weight on it, and Frik's shoulders came down a half-inch.

"She comes back," Narin said. "Ninhursag. She relents. She sits him down — and this is the part — she takes him in and she sets a healing goddess at each sick part of him. One goddess per pain. And she asks him, each time: *brother, what hurts you?* And he tells her. *My jaw hurts. My rib hurts. My mouth hurts.* And for each one she births a deity whose name is a pun on the part."

"This," Bashir said, and he could feel it coming up his throat now, the coal he'd planted, going to flame, "is where it lives. He says — Enki says — *my rib hurts me.* My rib. The Sumerian is *ti.*" He said it carefully. "*Ti.* It means rib." He let it sit one beat. "And it means *life.* To live. To make live. *Ti* — same sound, same sign, both."

Naila's pencil had stopped. Bashir watched it stop.

"So the goddess she births to heal his rib," Bashir said, "the lady of the rib — her name is *Nin-ti.* Lady of the rib." He spread his hands. "And lady who makes live. *Nin* is lady. *Ti* is rib *and* life. The name is a joke. It's a *pun.* You can't choose between the two readings because the poet didn't choose — that's the whole point, that's why it's funny, that's why somebody three thousand years ago wrote it down and was pleased with himself. The lady of the rib is the lady of life. The same word. You hear them both at once or you don't hear it at all."

The lizard moved on the wall. Nobody else did.

"And now," Bashir said, quieter, "say it in Hebrew."

—

He turned to Narin when he said it. Because it wasn't his to land. It sat under the most famous garden in her book, and she was the one who'd grown up with that garden, and he wasn't going to be a clever foreigner who cracked her scripture open like a nut on the table for the band's benefit. He'd watched her gate them all day. He gated himself now. He held it out to her on his open hand.

She didn't take it for a moment. She looked at the photocopy under her palm and then at the dark coming on under the awning and then at him.

"You want me to say it," she said.

"It's yours," Bashir said. "I only heard it. You *carry* it."

Narin let out a breath. It wasn't quite a laugh.

"The woman," she said. "In Genesis. God puts the man to sleep and takes a *tsela'* — a rib, a side — and from it he builds the woman. And the man names her *Chavvah.* Eve." She turned her cold glass a quarter-turn on the table. "And the text *tells* you why it named her that. It glosses its own joke, in case you missed it. *Because she was the mother of all living.*" She looked up. "Mother of all *living.* From a *rib.*"

Mira, who had been reading the whole room — Bashir's hands, Narin's quarter-turned glass, Edith's flat palm on the camera — signed something to Yusuf, two slow shapes, badly, and Yusuf, who signed worse, mouthed it back to be sure, and then said it aloud so Mira could watch his lips do it: "Rib. Living."

"Yeah," Narin said, to Mira, looking at Mira when she said it, which most people forgot to do. "Rib and living. In Hebrew those are two completely different words. *Tsela'* and *chay.* They don't sound alike, they don't share a root, the pun is *dead.* It's gone. So the Hebrew has to do it the hard way — it has to take the rib and the living and *put them next to each other* and say, look, look at this, the rib became the mother of all living, here is a connection." She spread her own hands now, the way Bashir had. "It's reaching for a joke it can't make anymore. The joke's in the other language. It got left behind in the other language, on the way over, and what came across into Hebrew is the *shape* of the joke with the laugh taken out."

For a while nobody said anything. The prayer-call below had stopped. The dust had stopped being gold and started being grey.

"That's the most beautiful thing I've ever heard," Yusuf said. His voice did a thing on it. He covered the thing by reaching for the teapot. "That's — sorry. Carry on."

—

Bashir had spent a long time learning when to let a silence work and when a silence was about to curdle. This one was working. He let it. Across from him Naila had bent back over her notebook, and she wasn't writing words, she was making the sign — the one she'd put at the top of a fresh page last night and left a blank column beside, not knowing what it answered. *Ti.* She was making it again and again, small, getting the wedges in the right order, the way she'd worried at the base-sixty numerals all day. Pattern. Not magic. Just a woman who found the truth of a thing by drawing it until her hand believed it.

"Okay," Edith said. Carefully. Hand still on the camera, still flat. "Help me, because I'm the one who's going to frame this and I don't want to frame it wrong." She looked at Narin. "Tell me what I'm *not* saying. Because I can already hear how the other lot would cut this." She did a voice. Not cruel. A trailer voice, slick, the streaming-doc voice they'd all started hearing in their sleep. "*The shocking pun hidden in Genesis. The ancient code the translators didn't want you to see. What were they covering up?*" She let it go. "Right? That's the cut. That's the version that gets eleven million views and a sequel."

"That's the version that's a *lie,*" Narin said, and there was heat in it, and Bashir understood the heat. It wasn't the comparison that made her angry. It was the markup. "Nobody covered anything up. There's no code. There's no secret. The Sumerian poem is on a tablet in a museum, the Hebrew is in every house in the world, the dictionary entry for *ti* with both meanings is in a book a first-year student buys for forty pounds. It's the opposite of hidden. It's the most *read* book and one of the most studied poems on earth, and the connection isn't whispered — it's *taught,* it's in the footnotes, it's in the introductions. The scholars who worked this out a hundred and fifty years ago didn't hide it. They *published* it. They wanted you to see it." She tapped the photocopy. "The only people who need it to be a secret are the people selling you something."

"What is it for, then," Edith said. "If it's not a smoking gun. What is it for."

It was Jakobus's question. Edith said it the way you handed back a tool. None of them had decided he led; he never led; but the question had got into all of them the same way his cayenne had got into the rice, a few grains, changing everything. *Not who. Not is-it-real. What is it for.*

Narin was quiet a moment.

"For me?" she said. "Or for them?"

"Both," said Mira, watching her mouth.

—

"For them," Narin said. "For the people who made the Sumerian poem. It's *play.* That's what kills me about the alien version, more than anything. They look at this — at people who invented writing, who ran the first cities, who did astronomy with their naked eyes and got it *right,* who balanced grain accounts in base sixty and counted to numbers that would take you all night — and the aliens-people look at that and they go: *no. Couldn't be them. Must've been visitors.* As if the men who could make a pun like *Ninti* couldn't possibly have made a city."

She said it level. She'd promised herself, Bashir could tell, not to let it become a speech. She kept it level.

"And the joke proves them wrong better than any argument," she said. "Because a pun is the most *human* thing there is. It's not engineering. You don't import it from the stars. A pun is somebody being *clever for the love of it,* somebody who knows their own language so well they can make it mean two things at once and trust you to catch both. That's not a slave-race waiting for instructions. That's a poet showing off. You don't get a better proof of a civilisation than a joke it left for you, and the joke's right there, and the alien version had to *not read it* to tell its story. That's the tell. Every single time. They had to leave the clay unread."

"Say more about that," Yusuf said, and he wasn't reaching for tea now. He'd gone still in the particular way he went still when the thing being said was the thing he'd needed, once, to be the other way round. He had needed the aliens, hard, in the years he didn't talk about. The rabbit-hole had been a door out of a life he couldn't stand. Bashir watched him hold the door open now and look at the bigger room behind it and not flinch.

"They had to leave it unread to make it shine," Yusuf said, slowly, working it out as a man works out a sum he doesn't like. "If you actually read the tablet it stops being a relic and starts being a *text.* Some bloke's joke. And a joke isn't worth much on a grey market. But *PROOF OF ALIENS,* a tablet nobody's translated, a tablet that *can't* be translated, that's worth —" He stopped. He'd come at the network sideways, by accident, by following the money the way he always did, and now he'd arrived at it. "That's the markup. The mystery is the markup. The minute someone reads it and it says *the lady of the rib is the lady of life,* the price collapses. So you keep it unread. You keep the question loud and the answer buried and the crate keeps moving."

Nobody said anything. Below the citadel a generator coughed and caught.

"Right," Yusuf said, to himself.

—

Frik had been bouncing his knee for a while, a small fast bounce, and his hand had come up to his collarbone twice and gone down. The room had gone serious and the serious was loud in him. Bashir saw it building, the way you see weather. They all saw it. Nobody moved to manage him, which was the kindness, the actual one — not pretending it wasn't happening, not making him a problem to be solved, just leaving him room in the circle.

"They keep it unread," Frik said, fast, an echo of Yusuf, and then his voice tried to climb on top of itself, "keep it unread, keep it — *the sar is the orbit,*" and the wrongness of it, the over-reach he'd punctured a dozen times before, came out of him whole and helpless, and his face did a thing, the way it did, because he hadn't meant to throw the lie up, the lie had thrown itself, and the involuntary part was the worst part to live in — and then, lower, scraped out of him, the spike, the rare one, the one that only came when the truth was too big to come politely: "It's a *number.* It's just a — it's a fucking *number.*"

The word landed in the quiet. Nobody laughed. That was the rule and the rule held because it was true: the swear wasn't a gag, it was the truth coming out the only door it could find, and the truth was that the 3,600 — the *sar,* the great number the aliens had made an orbit, a doom-planet, a clock counting down to the return of the gods — was a base-sixty numeral, sixty sixties, a sign a child of Ur could read, and the colossal effort it had taken to turn a number into a spaceship was the whole crime in one syllable, and Frik had said it, involuntary, exact.

"It's just a number," Narin agreed, gently, and Frik's knee stopped, and his hand came down off his collarbone and stayed down. "You're right. That's all it ever was."

"Sorry," Frik said.

"Don't be," said Mira, who'd read the whole thing off his body before the words came and had her hand half-raised in something none of them had taught her, an apology she was offering *to* him for the room going so quiet around him.

—

It was full dark now. Someone had lit the lantern. Out past the awning, near the truck, Jakobus stood in the way he stood, shades up on his head though there was no sun, his clean hanky going over the wing mirror in slow passes, and the Beast's canvas water bag sweating its slow cold beside him, and he was not in the conversation and never came into it, but his presence sat at the edge of the circle like the desert sits at the edge of a town, and once, when Frik's voice had spiked, the slow passes of the hanky had not paused, which was its own kind of answer. *Carry on. The man's fine. The man's one of mine.* He looked at none of them. He hummed something, low, a tune none of them knew, keeping a time none of them could hear.

Bashir watched him for a moment through the dark, the man no camera could hold, and felt the old loneliness move in him, and then felt it settle, because the man out there belonged nowhere either and had made this — this circle of the written-off under a tarp on the wrong side of the world — out of exactly that not-belonging. You could make a home out of having no country. Bashir was learning that. It was taking him a long time.

"Bashir." Narin's voice brought him back. She was looking at him oddly, kindly. "Where've *you* gone now."

"Nowhere," he said. Then, because it was true and the room had earned true things tonight: "Everywhere. Same problem."

She nodded like she understood it, and maybe she did. She had two languages and one people and no safe country either. They were not so different, the two of them, the man with no nation and the woman whose nation kept being marched out of its own ground.

—

"There are more," Bashir said, because he wanted to give them one more and then let the night close. "Eden threads. Not one-to-one — Narin will skin me again — not a *blueprint.* But the furniture keeps showing up in the rooms. Adapa." He looked at Narin to take it.

"A man," she said. "Offered the bread of life and the water of life by the gods, and he refuses it — because his own god warned him it was the bread of death, a misunderstanding, a kindness that betrays him — and so he loses immortality by a *mistake.* By getting it wrong. Not by sin. By misreading the situation." Her mouth tilted. "Which, given what we've been saying about translation all night, I'll let you enjoy."

"And the plant," Bashir said. "In Gilgamesh."

"He finds it," Narin said. "The plant that makes the old man young. At the bottom of the sea, he ties stones to his feet and goes down and brings it up, the one thing in all his grief that could undo death — and he sets it down on the bank to bathe, and a serpent comes up out of the water and eats it, and sheds its skin, and slips away renewed, and the man sits down and weeps. The snake gets the immortality. The snake. In a garden, by the water." She held up a flat hand before anyone could leap. "It's *not* the Genesis serpent. Don't. It's the same furniture in a different room. A motif. A snake, a plant, a lost forever — these are the pieces the whole world built its gardens out of, everyone working with the same handful of clay."

"Same handful of clay," Naila said, from her notebook, not looking up. It was the first thing she'd said in an hour. She'd made the sign for *ti* maybe forty times by now and her hand believed it. "That's the actual finding. That's the pattern. Not *the same story.* The same *pieces.* Everyone in the room got the same Lego and built a different house, and you can tell whose house is whose by what they did with the pieces." She looked up at last, found Narin. "And the question that's left over — the real one, the one nobody's answered — isn't *which house is true.* It's *who handed Genesis the Lego.* Where the pieces came from. How they got from a tablet in a Babylonian library into the hands of the people who wrote the garden in Hebrew."

The lantern hissed. Narin had gone still, the particular stillness of a person who has been waiting all night for the others to walk themselves to the right door so she wouldn't have to drag them there.

"Yes," she said. "That."

"You know," Naila said. Not a question.

"I know where the older drafts were kept," Narin said. "And I know who got marched there, in chains, and stood in front of them, and read them." She drew the cold glass toward her and didn't drink. Outside, Jakobus's tune went on, low, marking a time none of them could hear. "There was a library," Narin said. "It belonged to the people who'd just burned your city and taken your god's house apart stone by stone and walked you a thousand miles to live in their capital. And in that library, in the language of the people who'd destroyed you, was the older version of the story of how the world began."

She looked round the circle, at the written-off, at the man no camera could hold standing out in the dark.

"Tomorrow," she said. "I'll tell you what they did with it."
