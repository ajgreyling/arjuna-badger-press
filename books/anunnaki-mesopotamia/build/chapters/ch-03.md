# Chapter 3 — The Man Who Reads the Made Thing

The thing about Jakobus, Yusuf had decided, somewhere over the dark of eastern Turkey, was that you never caught him arriving. You caught him already there.

The flight into Erbil came down through a brown haze of late afternoon, and the airport was new and chilled and wrong, all marble and cologne and a duty-free wall of watches, the kind of place that has been built recently and at speed to tell the world a story about itself. They cleared the visa-on-arrival desk in a slow Kurdish queue, Bashir doing the talking, switching from his careful Sorani to the officer's Arabic to a soft word of Aramaic that made the man look up once, surprised, and stamp the passport a half-second kinder. Mira watched the officer's hands instead of his mouth and told Bashir afterward, *He liked you,* and Bashir said *He liked the Aramaic,* and Frik said *He liked the Aramaic,* a half-beat behind, and nobody minded because nobody ever minded the echo from Frik, it was just the room saying a thing twice.

And then they came out through the sliding doors into a wall of heat that smelled of diesel and dust and somebody's cardamom, and there, leaning on the bonnet of a dust-coloured Land Cruiser that had no business being in a Kurdish airport car park, was the Beast.

Yusuf stopped.

He'd known it was shipped. He'd signed two of the carnet forms himself, in a logistics English so dry it could start fires, and he still stopped, because there is knowing a thing is coming and there is the thing already arrived and weeping water from a canvas bag lashed to its wing mirror, beading, dripping, the oldest trick in the desert — wet the canvas, let the heat steal the water, let the stealing cool what's left inside. The bag swung a little. Somebody walking past it would not have looked twice. Yusuf looked three times.

The man wasn't at the truck.

He was over by the taxi rank, crouched on his heels in the shade of a concrete pillar, and he was not doing anything, which was the part you couldn't film. He had a small tin in one hand, the lid off, and he was tipping a measure of something rust-red into a paper cup of tea a porter had given him — cayenne, Yusuf would have bet his fee on it, cayenne in the tea in this heat, a desert thing, sweat-to-cool-you-down, *makes-the-skin-do-its-job* — and he and the porter were not exactly talking. They were sitting in a quiet together. The porter said something and Jakobus answered with two words and a slow tip of the chin, and the porter laughed, and it was the laugh of a man who has just been seen by a stranger and not asked for anything.

"Spas," Jakobus said. *Thank you.* Kurdish. A courtesy handful, the right one, no more.

"He's already met the airport," Bashir said, beside Yusuf, dragging two cases. "Look. He's met it."

"He met it before we landed," Yusuf said.

—

They drove in toward the city with the light going long and amber and the Beast's air-con turned off, all the windows down, because the man wouldn't run it. *You don't learn a place from inside a fridge,* he'd said once, in the Namib, and it was the only time Yusuf had ever heard him explain anything, and even that wasn't really an explanation, it was a fact he'd put on the table for them to take or leave.

So they took the heat. Bashir up front, naming road signs out loud in three scripts. Mira behind him with her face to the window, reading the whole moving city — the men at the petrol queue, the woman arguing a price down at a fruit cart with her chin and her wrist and not one word Mira could hear, the boy who flagged them and got a flat palm, *no,* from Jakobus and grinned and flagged the next car. Frik with his hands flat on his thighs, pressing, the long-haul tic-load coming off him in the safe car the way it never came off in the airport queue, a soft *hut,* *hut,* a roll of the shoulder, and Edith beside him with her camera in her lap, lens cap on, not shooting.

She'd asked. That was the new thing about Edith, the thing Yusuf had watched grow over three jobs like a callus over a burn. At the airport she'd lifted the camera at the weeping water bag and then she'd lowered it and looked at the back of Jakobus's neck, and he hadn't turned round, and she'd put the cap back on. Nobody had said anything. It was a conversation that had happened entirely without a sentence in it, and Mira had watched the whole thing and signed, badly, to Yusuf, *She asked. He didn't answer. So no.* And that was right. That was exactly the shape of it.

"He's not going to let you get him," Yusuf told her now, low, over the engine.

"I know," Edith said. She wasn't unhappy about it. "I've shot warlords who wanted it more than he doesn't." She turned the camera over in her lap. "There's no frame. I keep finding the frame and it's empty. Like he stepped out of it on the way in."

"That's the man," Yusuf said.

—

Naila had not spoken since the airport.

She sat in the very back, the file across her knees in its grey box, both hands flat on the lid, and she was doing the thing she did, the thing that wasn't magic, that Yusuf had learned the hard way not to call magic, because the one time he'd called it that she'd gone very flat and very polite and said, *It's arithmetic. Please don't make it a séance.* She was counting the city. Not consciously, maybe. Telephone poles. The repeat of a shopfront font. The interval of the streetlights coming on as the dusk dropped. When the intervals held she eased. When the city did something irregular — a roundabout, a wall of car horns, a knot of bodies at a junction with no clear way through — Yusuf watched her hand tighten on the box and her eyes go to the doors.

"Two minutes," he said to her, because that was the deal, you gave her the time and the exit and you did not narrate her nervous system at her. "We're nearly up the hill. It opens out."

It opened out.

The road climbed and the city fell away below them in a wash of low concrete and palm and minaret and the long industrial glow of the new malls, and then ahead, lifted up out of all of it on its own mound, lamp-lit, gold against the bruise-blue east, was the Citadel.

Bashir said the number before anyone asked. He couldn't help it; it was his version of Frik's echo.

"Six thousand years," he said. "Maybe more. People have lived up there for six thousand years without stopping. Not a ruin you visit. A roof somebody slept under last night." He turned in his seat. "Oldest continuously inhabited place on earth, they argue. Jericho fights them for it. Damascus fights them for it." He grinned. "They can fight. It's old."

And Naila looked up the hill at the lamplit wall of it, at the thing that had been a town when writing was young, and her hand came off the box.

"Okay," she said quietly. "Okay. That's — okay."

It was the most Yusuf had ever heard her say about beauty.

—

The uncle's place was below the Citadel, off the bazaar, a flat-fronted house with an awning of corrugated tin and grape vine, two plastic chairs, a low table, and a samovar already going. They were not staying there — that was a hotel near the souk, all arranged, Yusuf had arranged it — but Jakobus took them here first, to the awning and the tea, before the rooms, before the work, and that was not an accident either. The uncle was Narin's; Yusuf knew that much and not much more. An old Assyrian man with a white moustache and reading glasses pushed up into his hair, who came out and clasped Jakobus's hand in both of his like a man greeting weather he'd been told was coming, and said something in Syriac that Bashir caught and didn't translate, only went still, the way Bashir went still when a language did something to him in a place too tender to repeat.

"Tea," the uncle said, to all of them, in English. "Sit. You came on the road. Sit."

So they sat in the dark under the vine with the bazaar noise coming up the lane, and the man poured tea into little waisted glasses, two-handed, the dark stewed *çay* over the sugar, and Jakobus sat among them and did the thing he did with his hands around a hot glass in the heat, which made no sense and which Yusuf had stopped trying to make sense of.

"He's not here," Edith said suddenly, very low, only to Yusuf, watching the table. "Narin. The uncle keeps glancing at the lane. He's expecting her and she's not coming tonight."

"She already said no once," Yusuf said.

"She'll say no again," Edith said. "You can see it in the way the old man's bracing for us. He knows she's going to be hard on you and he's sorry about it in advance." She turned the camera, still capped, in her hands. "Don't film the uncle. Whatever happens. He poured us tea. You don't pay a man back for tea by taking his picture."

"No," Yusuf agreed. And then, because it was the kind of thing he'd never have said three jobs ago: "Good catch."

—

Here is the part Yusuf would tell people about, after, the part that he could never quite get to land because it had no event in it.

After the tea, Jakobus took the grey box from Naila's knees — *asked* for it, palm up, and waited until she handed it, which she did, which surprised Yusuf because she handed almost nothing to anyone — and he set it on the low table and lifted the lid, and inside, on foam, in a sleeve, were the prints. Naila's working prints. Big crisp photographs of the clay, of the tablets and the seals, of the rolled-out impression of the one everyone was here for in the end, the seal they call VA 243, the one that has launched a thousand websites, the one with the man and the star and the little circles that the whole alien story hangs on like a coat on a single nail.

The streaming-doc version of this object — Yusuf had watched it on the plane, he made himself watch the competition — the streaming-doc version put it on screen with a low synth drone and a slow push-in and a voice saying *some researchers believe* over a graphic of the solar system, the little circles lit up one by one like a planetarium, *and here, four thousand years before Copernicus, is a complete diagram of the planets — including a world modern astronomers have yet to find.* And then the synth swelled and they cut to a man in a good shirt in front of a bookshelf saying you couldn't rule it out.

That was the easy version. The flattering version. The one that says: somebody knew. Somebody up there knew, and drew it for us, and we are the kind of creatures worth drawing for.

Jakobus looked at the print for a long time.

He did not say *solar system.* He did not say *aliens,* and he did not say *nonsense,* which was the other cheap thing, the sneer, the *what-idiots* that Yusuf had heard too many clever men reach for. He just looked, with his glasses on the table and his thumb not touching the surface of the photograph, and the bazaar went on past the awning, and the uncle watched him look.

"Who is he," Jakobus said.

Not *what is it.* Not *is it real.* *Who is he.* He'd found the man in it. Past the star, past the little circles, past the whole apparatus of wonder the West had bolted onto the thing — he'd gone straight to the small figure on the left, seated, one hand lifted.

"That's the part nobody films," Bashir said slowly, leaning in. "Look. He's not a chart. He's a man. He's *seated.* There's a figure standing, bringing him forward — that's the introduction posture, that's a worshipper being led in. And the seated one with the horned cap — that's the god. The little circles are around the god's shoulder, by his star, his emblem. It's not a diagram of where things are. It's a picture of a man being brought before his god." He sat back. "It's the most ordinary thing in the world. It's the single most ordinary scene in all of Mesopotamian art. A man, and his god, and the introduction. They made thousands of them."

"And the writing?" Jakobus said.

"There's writing," Bashir said. "Right there in the field. There's a name." He looked at Naila. "There's always a name. These were signatures. You rolled them in wet clay to say *this is mine, this is sealed, this is witnessed.* They're not theology. They're not astronomy. They're the Bronze Age equivalent of the franking on an envelope. And somewhere in the cuneiform on this one there is a man's name, and he is a real man, and he had a god he was being introduced to, and he sealed his letters with the two of them together because it mattered to him, the way you'd put your father's photo on your desk."

Frik had been quiet under the vine, pressing his palms. Now the over-reach came at the table — not from Frik, from the doc still playing in Yusuf's head, the *world astronomers have yet to find,* the voice swelling — and Frik flinched as though it had been said aloud in the room, and his head jerked, and out it came, fast, involuntary, true:

"It's a *receipt.*"

A beat.

"It's a receipt," he said again, quieter, his own echo, his shoulders coming down. "Sorry. It's. It's a receipt with a god on it."

And the uncle laughed. The first laugh of the night out of the old man, a short surprised bark, and he reached over and put his hand on Frik's forearm, light, and said, "Yes. A receipt. We had receipts before we had the wheel, habibi. We had receipts before we had *kings.*"

"That's the joke," Naila said. She'd come on, the way she came on when the pattern closed, flat and certain and lit up. "That's the whole — the alien version says it's a star map four thousand years ahead of its time, and it's so much *better* than that, it's so much stranger, it's a man clocking in. People had been keeping accounts in this clay for a thousand years before this seal got cut. Tokens, tallies, who owes whom how much barley, how much beer. Writing didn't start with poetry or prayer. It started with *inventory.* They invented the first writing on earth to keep track of *stuff.*" She put her finger an inch above the print, not touching. "This — this whole language, the one that the alien story says aliens had to come down and give us — we built it ourselves to count sheep and not get cheated."

She said it the way other people said *I love you.*

—

Jakobus put the print back in its sleeve. Settled it on the foam. Closed the box, and the bazaar noise came back up into the gap his attention had been holding shut, and Yusuf realised that for two full minutes nobody at the table had been thinking about anything but a man and his god in a piece of clay, and that this was the whole trick of him, the thing no camera got — he didn't tell you what to see. He stood next to the thing and asked it the right question and then got out of the light so you could look.

"It does a job," Jakobus said. To no one. To the box. "Everything that lasts does a job. You find the job, you find the man." He pushed the box an inch back toward Naila. "This does a man's job. Saying *I was here, this was mine, this was witnessed.* Four thousand years and we can still read the I-was-here." He stood, knees creaking, and pressed the always-clean hanky once to his neck and folded it away, and looked down at the six of them under the vine. "You can read the made thing. You read it better than the people who made the show about it."

"But not as well as her," Bashir said.

It wasn't a guess. It was Bashir naming the firewall before anyone could pretend it wasn't there.

"Not as well as her," Jakobus agreed, and there was no contest in it, no apology, just a fact set on the table like the man set every fact on the table — for you to take or leave. "I can tell you it does a man's job. I can't tell you the man's *name.* I can't read the franking. I can't hear the joke."

"The joke," Edith said.

"Bashir says there's always a joke," Jakobus said, and the corner of his mouth moved, the closest he came. "In a language he can feel and I can't. So." He nodded down the dark lane, where the uncle had glanced all night. "We're at the end of what we can do alone. The people who invented this clay — they're not gone. They're up the hill. They're under this vine. They poured you the tea." He looked at the old man, who looked back, steady. "And one of them reads this better than anyone alive, and she's already told you no."

—

He didn't stay for that. He never stayed for the part that wasn't his.

He took the empty glass to the samovar himself, and rinsed it, and set it upside down to drain, and clasped the uncle's hand again in both of his and said the Syriac word back to him that Bashir wouldn't translate, badly, but said, and the old man's eyes went bright and he held the hand a moment longer than the handshake needed. Then Jakobus went out under the awning to where the Beast sat ticking and cooling with its water bag weeping into the warm night, and he checked the bag with the back of two fingers the way you'd check a child's forehead, and he was, for a moment, lit orange under the bazaar lamp — and Edith had the camera up.

She'd uncapped it without anyone seeing. Old reflex. The frame was perfect: the man, the truck, the citadel gold above, the whole story of the desert man arrived in the cradle of writing, the shot that runs the credits, the shot that makes a career.

She held it.

He didn't turn around. He never turned around. But something in the set of his shoulders changed, an awareness with no face on it, and Yusuf watched Edith's finger lie on the shutter and not press it.

She lowered the camera.

"Empty again?" Yusuf said.

"No," Edith said. "Full. It's a beautiful frame." She put the cap back on. "It's just not mine to take." She looked at the uncle's lit doorway, the tea glasses draining, the lane where the woman hadn't come. "I shot this place once before. In oh-three. I shot the sacks coming out of the museum in Baghdad while the whole — while it burned. I've got a Pulitzer-shortlist of somebody else's heritage in a hard drive in London." She said it flat, the way you say the thing you've decided to stop carrying as a secret. "Some shots you take. Some shots you've already taken enough of." She nodded at Jakobus folding himself into the Beast. "He poured the old man's glass out himself. You don't take the picture of the man who washes up after himself."

—

In the Land Cruiser, down the hill, windows down, the city coming up around them again all horn and neon and the smell of grilled fat, Naila held the grey box on her knees and was quiet, and then she said, to the box, the way she said her best things to objects:

"She's going to be right about something we're wrong about. Tomorrow. She'll out-read us. I can feel the shape of it. I don't even mind." A pause. "I usually mind."

"Why don't you mind?" Bashir asked.

"Because the texts are better than the lie." Naila turned her face to the window, to the old town going up behind them into the dark, lamp by lamp. "Every single time we open them up they're better than the lie. The lie says somebody came down and gave it to us. The clay says we made it ourselves, in the mud, to count sheep, and then we used it to write the oldest poem on earth about a man who couldn't beat death and made peace with it." She breathed out. "I don't want the version where it was a gift. I want the version where it was ours. And she's the one who can give us that. So." Her hand was steady on the box now. "She can be right at me all day."

Up front, Jakobus said nothing, which was his way of agreeing.

Yusuf watched the uncle's lane shrink in the wing mirror, the awning light, the vine, the place where a woman who reads cuneiform and speaks the dying language of the men who cut it had not come tonight and would, the uncle's whole braced body had promised it, come tomorrow — to tell these foreigners no, and then, maybe, if they were lucky, and quiet, and didn't film her uncle, to tell them the name of the man kneeling in the clay.

And under everything, under the tea and the heat and the beautiful frame Edith had refused, the other thing sat in Yusuf with no answer and the start of a shape: that somebody had paid good money to put a synth drone behind a Bronze Age receipt and call it a star map, and that the loud lie travelled the world on a streaming service while the quiet truth waited under a grape vine for permission. *Who keeps choosing what we get to see.* He didn't say it out loud. There was nobody to say it to who could fix it.

The Beast went down into the new city, weeping water into the warm dark, and behind them the oldest roof on earth held its six thousand years of lamplight, and somewhere up there, or down here in the diaspora flat she worked from, a woman was deciding exactly how hard to say no.
