# Chapter 4 — The One Who Owns the Clay

She came up the lane on foot, and Edith heard her before she saw her — the wrong shoes for the gravel, hard heels, a city sound the lane wasn't built for, and under it the uncle's voice dropping from his after-prayer murmur into something fast and low and Aramaic, a warning maybe, or just *they're here, they're already here, behave*.

Edith had her camera on the strap and her hands nowhere near it. She'd promised herself that. The light at this hour was obscene — the citadel above them going from honey to bronze, the vine throwing its lace across the whitewash — and she kept her hands away from the camera the way an addict keeps her hands off a glass, knowing the want and naming it and sitting on it.

Then the woman came round the corner of the wall and Edith forgot the light entirely.

Dr. Narin Youkhanna was not tall. That was the first wrong thing about the photograph Edith had already half-built in her head — the scholar against the tablets, lit from the side, gravely beautiful, a National Geographic spread. The real woman was small and quick and dressed for a meeting she did not want to have, a grey jacket, a phone in her hand she did not put away, and her eyes went round the courtyard in one pass and counted them. Edith watched her do it. One, the loud one with the notebook clutched to her chest like a hot-water bottle — Naila, who had not slept. Two, Frik, who was already doing the small economy of motions that meant he was working hard at stillness. Three, four, five. Bashir near the wall, very deliberately not at the centre. Yusuf with his hands open and visible, the posture of a man who knew how to walk into rooms that didn't want him. Mira at the edge, watching faces, always watching faces.

And then Narin's gaze found Edith. Found the camera on the strap. Stopped.

Held.

Edith felt it land on her like weather.

"Pala," the uncle said, gentle, a hand half-raised, and there was a current of family in it, *be welcome, sit, these are guests* — and Narin answered him without looking away from Edith, two words, and the uncle subsided.

"You'll forgive my uncle," Narin said, to the courtyard, in English clean as a knife. "He has the hospitality of a saint and the security instinct of a man who thinks the worst people in the world wear bad shoes." A small, controlled smile that did not reach her eyes. "I do not share it. I think the worst people in the world wear very good shoes and carry very good cameras."

Nobody breathed for a second.

"Tea," said the uncle, and fled toward the brazier, and the spell broke into ordinary motion, and the crew did the thing Edith had watched them do at three airports and one museum basement now — they made room. Not a word about it. Naila to the inner corner with her back to the wall and an exit in her sightline, because someone she didn't have to manage had taken that decision for her years ago and it was simply how the furniture got arranged now. Frik between the wall and Yusuf, where his hands had something to face. Mira angled so the light fell on every mouth. The geometry of a found family is a thing you can see if you know to look, and Edith photographed it only with the part of her that could not be confiscated.

Narin sat. She put the phone face-down on the low table, which Edith understood was a concession the size of a country.

"So," she said. "You're the ones who want to film my ancestors as space monkeys."

"No," said Naila.

It came out flat and immediate and a little too loud, and Naila's hand tightened on the notebook, and Edith saw Yusuf shift his weight to be ready and then not need to be, because Narin had turned the full beam of her attention on Naila and Naila, for once, did not flinch from it. They looked at each other, the woman who read the system and the woman who owned the clay, and something passed between them that Edith had no name for and did not try to caption.

"No?" said Narin.

"The opposite." Naila opened the notebook. Her fingers found a page she had clearly found a hundred times. "VA 243. The Berlin tablet. The one they put behind a synth drone and called the solar system carved before telescopes." She turned the book round and pushed it across the table, and on the page in her cramped careful hand was a drawing of the seal, and around it her own arithmetic in three colours. "There are eleven dots. If it's the planets you have to invent a moon and ignore the fact that the sizes are wrong and pretend a Mesopotamian engraver who couldn't see Neptune drew Neptune. And then." Her finger stopped on the cuneiform at the top of the seal, a little box of wedges. "And then there's this. The writing. Which the documentary cropped out. Which I can't read."

Silence.

"I can read the pattern," Naila said, and her voice did something Edith hadn't heard from her — it dropped, it asked. "I can count dots. I can do the base-sixty and prove the three-thousand-six-hundred is a number and not an orbit. I brought all of it." She tapped the notebook. "But I can't read what the man who made the thing wrote on the thing. So I came to the person who can."

For a long moment Narin did not touch the notebook. She looked at it the way you look at a photograph someone has taken of your house without asking.

Then she picked it up.

Edith watched her face and so did Mira — Edith clocked Mira clocking it, the two readers of the unspoken triangulating the same closed mouth from two angles. Narin read down the page. Her thumb moved over Naila's arithmetic, the base-sixty conversion, the place where Naila had written *sar = 3600 = a numeral, a BIG one, NOT a planet's year* and underlined it twice and then, smaller, *(unless?)* and crossed the unless out.

"You crossed out the *unless*," Narin said.

"Because there isn't one," Naila said. "I checked it forty times. Sixty squared. It's how they wrote big. It's beautiful. It's *base sixty,* it's why we have sixty minutes in an hour, it's the oldest piece of arithmetic still alive in your kitchen, and they took that — they took the most elegant counting system on earth — and they sold it as a docking schedule for a planet that doesn't exist." She had run out of breath. She got more. "It made me so angry I couldn't read for a week."

Narin set the notebook down.

When she spoke again the knife had gone out of her voice and left something more tired and much older behind it.

"VA 243," she said, "is a dedication seal. A man owned it. He pressed it into the clay to say *this is mine, this contract is mine, I stand behind these words.*" She turned the notebook back to face Naila and put one fingernail, lacquered, very precise, on the box of wedges at the top. "The line your documentary cut off says he was a scribe. And under it is a name. The name of his god, or his lord, the one he served — the dub-sar, the keeper of the tablet. A man called *Dubsiga.*"

The name dropped into the courtyard like a stone into a well.

"It isn't a star map," Narin said. "It's a business card." A flicker, almost a real smile this time. "A four-thousand-year-old man wanting you to know whose servant he was. And the West looked at a dead man saying *remember me, remember who I served,* and decided he was drawing them a route to Mars."

"I knew it wasn't planets," Naila said, half to herself, fierce and quiet. "But I didn't have the *man*."

"No," said Narin. "You didn't have the man. That's the whole disease." And she looked round the courtyard again, slower this time, and Edith saw the assessment shift by some increment too small to name. "You came a long way to be told you were right about the arithmetic and wrong about everything that matters."

"That," said Yusuf, very gently, from the wall, "is the most encouraging thing anyone's said to us in two thousand miles."

The tea came. The uncle did it himself, would not let anyone help, and Edith watched Jakobus over by the Beast do the thing he did, which was to be exactly the right amount of nowhere — far enough to not be in the family's space, near enough that the uncle, carrying the tray, caught his eye and got a nod, and the nod said *your house, your kin, your call,* and the uncle's shoulders came down half an inch. Narin's eyes went to him once, the tall foreigner by the vehicle who hadn't tried to be in the photograph of this at all, and then came back, and she filed him somewhere Edith couldn't see.

It was Frik who broke first. It always was, and it was never his fault, and the crew had long ago stopped pretending the small explosions were anything but weather.

"The sar is the orbit," Frik said.

It came out of him whole and bright and wrong, in a voice that wasn't quite his, a documentary-narrator cadence, the thing he'd absorbed off some screen and that surfaced now under the pressure of a room with rules in it. His face did the thing afterward, the small collapse of *that wasn't me, that was the tic,* and his hand came up.

And Narin — Edith was watching, Edith would always be glad she was watching — Narin did not do any of the things people did. She didn't startle or smile-too-kindly or look away to spare him. She turned to Frik like he'd asked an honest question.

"No," she said. "And you know it's no, or it wouldn't have made you angry to say it."

Frik went very still. "Three thousand six hundred years," he said, in his own voice now, low. "The orbit of Nibiru. That's the line. That's the famous line."

"It's a number that means thirty-six hundred," Narin said. "It means it on a receipt for barley. It means it on a list of how many soldiers a king lied about having. It means it the way *a thousand* means a thousand to you — sometimes a count, sometimes just *a lot.* It was never a year of anything. To make it an orbit you have to take a word out of every mouth that ever used it for two thousand years and put it in the mouth of one planet that has to be invisible to work." She lifted her tea. "The tic told the truth. That *is* the famous line. The famous line is a mistranslation with a soundtrack."

Frik laughed — a real one, surprised out of him — and the laugh was permission, and the courtyard breathed.

"That's his job," Bashir said.

He'd been quiet so long Edith had half-forgotten the shape of his voice. He said it from the wall, mild, not pushing in.

"Telling the truth at the wrong moment?" Narin said.

"Telling it before anyone's decided to," Bashir said. "He says the thing the room is being careful around. We've learned to listen for it."

Narin considered him. "And you," she said. "What's yours? You've been arranging your face for ten minutes."

Edith almost flinched for him. But Bashir only inclined his head, and then he did the thing — the thing Edith had seen him do at the airport with the visa officer and once with a furious taxi driver in Erbil traffic, the small surgical kindness of meeting a person inside their own language — and he said it in Aramaic.

Edith didn't have the words. She had the music of it, and she had Narin's face, and Mira's face watching Narin's face, and that was the whole text. Whatever Bashir said was short. It was not fluent — Edith could hear even across the gap that he was reaching, that this was a borrowed tongue worn over the bones of forty others, that he was a man who belonged to no language by blood and had simply gone and *learned* the one this woman bled. He said it gently and a little wrong and entirely on purpose.

And the door that had been a finger's width open came open another inch.

Narin's whole body changed. Not dramatically. The jacket-armour stayed on. But her chin came up and her eyes brightened with what Edith, watching, identified after a second as *grief* — the specific bright grief of hearing your dying mother tongue come out of a stranger's mouth in a courtyard under a foreign occupation's ruins, badly, with love.

She answered him. Longer. Edith caught one word twice — it sounded like *lišana,* and she'd find out later it meant *tongue,* it meant *language,* it was the word at the bottom of everything they were here to do.

Then Narin turned back to the English and the room, and her voice had gone rough at one edge.

"He greets me in the language of the people who wrote the thing your documentary turned into a cartoon," she said. "It's the oldest continuously spoken language in this country. There are villages on the plain north of Mosul where they still say their prayers in it. Were. Were villages." The roughness held. "ISIS came through them in 2014. The ones who didn't die left. The language goes where the people go, and the people are in Sydney now, and Chicago, and a flat in west London where I do most of my work because it isn't safe to do it here. So." She looked at Bashir. "Thank you. For knowing it was worth learning. Most people who come for the tablets never once think the tongue is still warm."

"I think the tongue is the whole thing," Bashir said.

"It is," Narin said. "It's the only thing. Every lie you came here chasing is a translation lie. *Anunnaki* — do you know what it means? Properly?"

Naila opened her notebook. Narin held up a hand, not unkindly, *let me,* and Naila closed it.

"*Anunna,*" Narin said. "The princely ones. The princely offspring. The children of the great god, the high gods, the council of heaven. It's a *title.* It's *Your Royal Highnesses.*" She let it sit. "Over two thousand years — longer than English has existed, twice over — it drifts. By the late period it's come to mean the gods of the underworld, the judges of the dead. A word can travel that far inside one civilisation. And a man in the twentieth century picks it up at the far end of all that travel, after it's already crossed two thousand years and three meanings, and he says: *ah. Astronauts.*" Her mouth twisted. "He didn't even read it. He read *about* it, in translation, in a footnote, and built a solar system."

"Zecharia Sitchin," Yusuf said.

The name came out of him oddly. Edith looked over. Yusuf had been the easy one, the open-handed one, the one who unblocked rooms — and just now there was a thing in his face she hadn't seen, a kind of carefulness, like a man saying a name he'd once said in love.

"You've read him," Narin said. It wasn't a question. She'd heard it too.

"All of it," Yusuf said. "Years ago. The twelfth planet. The whole — " He stopped. "It got me through a bad stretch. I'm not going to pretend it didn't. The idea that there was a *reason.* That somebody made us on purpose and we mattered enough to be made." He turned his tea glass a quarter-turn on the table. "It's a kinder story than the real one. I'll defend that to anybody. It's just not true."

Narin looked at him for a long moment, and the armour came down a notch further, because she was, Edith understood, a person who had built a fortress against exactly this conversation and Yusuf had walked through the wall by being soft.

"The real one is better," Narin said quietly. "When you can bear it. But I grant you it isn't kinder. Not at the start."

"What's the real one," Naila said. Not greedy. Hungry the way Yusuf was hungry, the way they were all a little hungry, the way you get when you've come a long way for something true.

Narin shook her head once. "No," she said. "Not tonight. Not for free. You don't get the clay because you flew here and said please."

And then she turned, finally, fully, deliberately, to Edith.

Edith had known it was coming. She'd watched it not happen for forty minutes, the way you watch a wave hang.

"The camera," Narin said.

"It's off," Edith said.

"I know it's off. I can see the light isn't on. I'm not an idiot." A beat. "Do you know what's in my museum?"

"Which one," Edith said, and regretted it, and meant it.

"Good answer." Narin's voice had gone very flat and very level, the dangerous register. "Baghdad. The National Museum. April 2003. You'd have been — what. Doing your job?"

The courtyard had gone quiet in a different way now. The crew had stopped being a found family arranging furniture and become an audience for something between two women.

"I was there," Edith said.

She heard Mira go still beside her. She felt the crew not look at her, which was its own kind of looking.

"I know," Narin said. "I looked you up. The shoes thing was a joke. The cameras thing wasn't. I know exactly who you are, Edith Vos. I've seen your war." She didn't raise her voice. "There's a photograph of yours. The boy on the museum steps, the empty cases behind him through the doors, the dust. It won things. It's beautiful. It's on a wall in a building in New York where people drink wine and feel something for ninety seconds about the cradle of civilisation."

Edith said nothing. There was nothing. The photograph was real. The wine was real. The award was on a shelf she didn't dust.

"While you were making that beautiful thing," Narin said, "they were carrying the actual cradle out the back in sacks. Cylinder seals by the thousand. Tablets. The Warka Vase — they got the Warka Vase, do you understand, five thousand years old, the first picture humans ever made of a procession, of *society,* and it went out in a sack while a lens made art of the door it went out of. And the thing about your beautiful photograph," and here her voice finally cracked, just once, and re-set, "is that it taught the whole world to grieve the building and never once ask where the things *went.* It made a tragedy you could feel without doing anything. It made the looting *photogenic.* It made it a mood."

Edith found she had no hands. She had hands. They were empty and very far away.

"That's a fair charge," she said, when she could.

"I don't care if it's fair," Narin said. "I care that you're standing in my uncle's courtyard with a camera and my ancestors on the table, and I have to decide whether the next beautiful thing you make is a document or a sale."

"Then let me ask you something," Edith said, and her own voice came out steadier than she'd earned. "What's the difference. For you. What's the difference between the frame that documents and the frame that sells. Because I'll take the rule. I'd rather have your rule than my taste." She made herself say the next part. "My taste is the thing that took the museum picture. I know what my taste does. I'd like to put it down for a while and use yours."

It was the most honest thing she'd said in years and it cost her like surgery.

Narin studied her. The phone stayed face-down on the table. Up the lane a generator coughed and caught somewhere in the new city, and the citadel above them held its bronze a minute longer than seemed possible.

"The difference," Narin said slowly, "is the caption."

She picked up Naila's notebook again, the drawing of VA 243, and turned it so the seal faced Edith.

"You photograph this," she said, "and put under it *cylinder seal, Old Babylonian, dedication of a scribe, name of his lord, see catalogue, here is the museum's free page, go and read it* — that's a document. That serves the dead man. He wanted to be remembered. You remembered him correctly." Her thumb moved to the edge of the page. "You photograph the exact same object and put under it *Was This Carved by Aliens?* — and now you haven't photographed a seal, you've photographed a *price.* Because a tablet that proves spacemen is worth a fortune on the grey market and a tablet that says *Dubsiga's barley account* is worth a coffee. The lie is the markup. The lie is *why the sack is full.* You think the woo and the looting are two stories. They're the same business."

Across the courtyard, Edith heard Yusuf exhale like he'd been hit.

"Say that again," Yusuf said.

Narin looked at him.

"The lie is why the sack is full," Yusuf said. "The mystery isn't decoration. It's the *invoice.*"

"It always was," Narin said. "Make a thing too boring to steal and nobody steals it. Make it scream *secret of the gods* and it walks out the back at midnight and somebody films the door for an award." She set the notebook down a final time and looked, for the first time, weary rather than armed. "That's why I gate it. Not because you're bad people. Because the loud version of my ancestors is worth money to people I will never meet, and the quiet true version — the man saying *remember whose servant I was* — is worth nothing to them, which is exactly why it's the only version worth protecting."

Nobody said anything for a while. The brazier ticked. Mira, who had read the whole of it off mouths and shoulders, signed something small and lopsided toward Edith, and Edith caught half of it and held it, and let it land where it was meant to.

Then Narin stood, and gathered her phone, and looked round the courtyard one more time — the loud one who'd flown across the world to be told she was right and wrong; the truth-teller; the polyglot who'd learned her dying language well enough to greet her badly in it; the watcher of faces; the money man who'd needed the aliens and was letting go in real time at her uncle's table; the photographer who'd offered to put her own eye down. And the tall one by the Beast, who had gathered all of them somehow and then stood out of the picture on purpose.

"Tomorrow," Narin said. "My uncle's roof. Early, before the heat. I'll bring photographs of the real tablets — the British Museum lets you, they put it all online for free, which is the most subversive thing that institution has ever done. And I'll start at the beginning. Not the aliens. The clay. What we were actually made of, in the story they actually told." A pause. "It's worse than spacemen. It's so much braver. You won't sleep after."

"What do we have to do," Naila said.

"Be quiet," Narin said. "Don't film my uncle. And when I tell you the men I'm worried about wear very good shoes — believe me. Because someone made the loud version of my country, and someone keeps it loud, and someone makes very sure the quiet true version never trends." She was at the corner of the wall now, the city sound of her heels ready to take her back down. "I've spent twenty years watching which stories get a soundtrack and which ones disappear from the catalogue between one visit and the next. I have stopped believing it's an accident."

She let that hang in the bronze air, and did not explain it, and Edith felt the back of her neck go cold for reasons that had nothing to do with the dark coming up the lane.

"Who's keeping it loud," Yusuf said, and Edith heard the money in his voice now, the old instinct waking up, the man who could follow a sack.

Narin paused at the wall's edge, half in lamplight, half gone.

"That," she said, "is the better question than aliens. That's the one I'd film." And she went down the lane into the new city, hard heels on the wrong gravel, leaving the oldest roof on earth behind her and a courtyard full of people who had finally, all of them, started looking the right way.

Edith did not lift the camera.

It was the best shot she'd never take, and she let it go, and it felt like the first clean thing she'd done in a long time.
