# Chapter 7 — The Princely Offspring

Bashir watched her hands.

Not the page — the page he could read for himself, or read enough of, the dun column with its struck-match wedges, the single word set apart at the top like a name on a door. He watched Narin's hands hold it, because hands told him things before mouths did, and her hands were doing the thing he did at borders: holding still on purpose, the stillness costing something.

The lane behind her uncle's house ran narrow and white between two walls, and the awning over the low bench cut the heat into shade and not-shade in a hard line, and the not-shade landed on the printout and made the glyphs lift. Yusuf had pulled a plastic stool from somewhere. Naila stood where she could see the whole lane and both ends of it, which Bashir had learned not to mention. Mira had her back to the wall and her eyes on Narin's mouth. Edith's camera sat on the bench beside her with the lens cap on, which was its own kind of sentence. Frik bounced once on his heels and went still.

"The princely offspring," Narin said again. "Say it the Sumerian way and you'll forget the planet exists. *A-nun-na.* The seed of An. An is the sky — the sky-as-a-person, the oldest of the old — and the *nun* is the prince, the noble, the high-born. So: the high-born seed of the sky. The aristocracy of the gods." She tipped the page so the light ran down it. "Their children. Their grandchildren. The ones who got the good seats."

"Not a crew," Naila said. It wasn't a question. She'd been turning something over since the tea. "A *family.*"

"A dynasty." Narin let that sit. "And here's the part nobody selling you a spaceship has ever once stayed in the library long enough to find. The word doesn't *hold still.*"

Bashir felt it land in his chest before his head caught up — the small electric thing that happened when someone was about to do the only trick he truly loved, which was to take a word and walk it through time and show you it had legs.

"Go on," he said. Quietly. Just enough to half-open the door.

She glanced at him. Something in her shoulders gave a quarter-inch.

"In the oldest texts," she said, "third millennium, the Anunnaki are *everything.* They are the great gods, the deciders. There's a phrase — they fix the *me*, the destinies, the divine order of things. The flood is coming or it isn't because the Anunnaki sit in council and decide. They are the supreme court of the cosmos. The board." She turned the sheet a few degrees. "You want a word for power, that's the word. They don't dig gold. They sit. They rule. People down here in the mud are terrified of them in the good way — the way you're terrified of weather and harvest and the river."

"And later?" Yusuf had leaned forward on the stool. Bashir clocked it — Yusuf always leaned in at exactly the moment a story started doing something he hadn't paid for.

"Later." Narin's mouth did something complicated. "Centuries pass. Empires fall and other empires write over them. Akkadian comes up under Sumerian like a second language stitched into the first. And there's another bunch of gods running around the texts by now — the *Igigi.*"

"The Igigi," Bashir repeated. The word had a gear in it. *Igigu.* The plural sat in his mouth like something tired.

"The junior gods. The working gods. And in some of the texts the line between Anunnaki and Igigi gets drawn one way, and in others the other way, because — and write this down, Edith, because this is the thing —" she didn't look up to check whether Edith was writing; she just trusted it the way you trust a wall is behind you — "because it was *never one fixed system.* It was a theology. A living one. Two thousand years of priests in twenty cities arguing about who outranked whom. The names migrate. The jobs migrate. In the late texts — first millennium, the period when half the famous tablets in the British Museum were actually copied — the Anunnaki have *slid all the way down.* They're not the high court of heaven anymore."

She stopped.

Bashir watched her decide whether to give it to them. The gating again — the firewall she ran on access, on her own ancestors' word, the one Yusuf had bumped into twice and learned to wait out.

"Where do they end up?" Mira signed it and said it, voice flat and careful the way she shaped the words she couldn't hear, and the question had no patience in it, only weight.

Narin answered her face-on, which Bashir noticed, because almost nobody did.

"They become," Narin said, "the *judges of the dead.* The Anunnaki of the underworld. The grey ones who sit in the dark and weigh you when you go down. By the late period that's mostly what the word means. Not the lords of destiny up in the bright. The bailiffs of the pit." She let the page drop a little. "Two thousand years. From the board of directors of the universe to the parole panel in hell. The same word. The whole way down."

Nobody said anything for a second.

Then Naila said, "That's a *drift.*" And there was hunger in it, and Bashir knew that hunger — it was the same one he got, only she got it in shapes and he got it in sounds. "That's a — the word's not a thing, it's a *trajectory.* You'd plot it. Early, middle, late, and the meaning's a curve, it's *sliding,* and if you—" she stopped herself, the way she did, the brakes coming on visibly, and Bashir watched her choose to finish anyway, smaller — "if you only had one point on the curve you could call it anything you wanted."

"Yes," Narin said. Just that. And she looked at Naila for a beat longer than the room expected, the look of one specialist recognising another's instrument.

Frik, who'd been holding it, said it.

"One point — *one point on the curve—*" the echo running first, the way it always did, and then the blurt arriving under its own power, fast and clean and not askable-back: "*they froze it.*"

The lane went quiet around the words.

"They froze it," Frik said again, quieter, his hand opening and closing. "Whole — the whole word *moving,* two thousand years of it moving, and they just — *click.* Snapshot. Crew of twelve, gold mine, planet. Done." He blinked. "That's the cheat. That's the whole—"

"That's the whole cheat," Narin agreed, and she said it to Frik directly, no flinch, no wince, no managing him, just answering the truest thing in the lane like it was the truest thing in the lane. "You take a word that drifts through twenty centuries and you nail one frame of it to a corkboard and you sell tickets. You take a theology — a *changing,* arguing, living theology — and you call it a roster. A roster doesn't change. That's the appeal. People don't want a god whose meaning is wet shale under your feet. They want a name tag."

Bashir felt the seam open. Not loudly. Just the first half-inch.

Because this was the move, this was the actual move the book they didn't know they were in kept making: the alien version was the *easy* version, and the easy version fell apart the second you read the clay, and it fell apart not because someone shouted *fake* at it but because the real thing was *more.* The real word had been alive. The fringe had embalmed it.

"Show me how the planet dies in *this* one," Yusuf said. "VA 243 we did. The number, the Nibiru thing, fine. But this—" he gestured at the dun column — "if I'm the network, I'm telling a guy in a hangar this says *the Anunnaki came from the sky to mine gold.* What does it actually say? Make it die in the words. That's the only place I believe it."

Narin almost smiled. Bashir saw it cross and go.

"You learn fast for an honest man," she said.

"I'm not honest. I'm reformed. There's a discount."

She did smile then, briefly, and turned the page so the top word stood clear, and put one fingertip — not touching, hovering — over the wedges of *nun.*

"Here's the planet dying," she said. "*Nun* is the prince. You can *read* it. It's a sign for nobility, for the high-born, and it's all over the language — it's in titles, it's in the names of cities, *Eridu* has it, the temple of Enki at Eridu is the *E-abzu,* the house of the deep, and the deep, the *abzu,* the sweet water under the earth — that's where the wisdom lives, down, not up. The high-born gods of the *sky-and-earth* couple. An above, Ki below. Their children straddle both. They are not from *somewhere else.* They are from *here* and *up there* at once, because the whole cosmos is family, and family is how a Sumerian explained the weather."

"So when Sitchin reads *they came down,*" Bashir said, feeling the translation crack along its real fault line, "the *down*—"

"The *down* is theology, not a landing." Narin's eyes were bright now, the gating relaxed a notch further, the scholar coming up through the gatekeeper. "Gods come down to their temples. They *inhabit* the statue, the house, the city. The divine descends into the made thing — the cult image, the ziggurat that's literally built as a stairway so the god has somewhere to put his foot. *Come down* is the most ordinary verb in the religion. It's what the god does on a feast day. You translate it as a spaceship and you've — you've heard a man say *grace* and reported that he summoned food from orbit."

Naila made a sound — not a laugh, a kind of exhale through the nose, the closest she came in a crowd. "That's good," she said. "That's a clean — that's *clean.*"

"It's clean because it's *true,*" Narin said, and for the first time there was an edge under it, the old wound flexing, "and the true reading is *better,* and that's the part that makes me—" she stopped. Recalibrated. Bashir watched her put the heat down somewhere safe and pick the words up cooler. "These were my ancestors. Not in a poster way. The Nineveh plain is forty kilometres from where you're sitting. My family is from villages where they were still naming kids out of these names a hundred years ago. And a Russian-American businessman who couldn't read a line of Sumerian decided my great-great-grandparents' great-great-grandparents were too stupid to invent the wheel, the city, the *first writing on earth,* and that sky-people must have done it for them. And then a streaming company made it look beautiful." She breathed out. "So when you ask me what they were *for.* That's the tense I keep wanting to break."

The lane held it. Even Frik held it.

It was Mira who moved into the quiet — Mira, who read the thing the mouth wouldn't say, and who had been watching Narin's face the whole time and not the page at all.

She signed something small and only half for the room — to herself, working it out — and then she said it aloud, slow: "You're not angry that they got it wrong."

Narin looked at her.

"You're angry," Mira said, "that they got it wrong in a way that made your people *furniture.* In their own story."

Bashir watched Narin not answer for a second, which was its own answer, and watched her decide Mira had earned the truth and hand it over with a single nod, no words wasted, the way you pass something heavy to the one person who reached for the right handle.

"Yes," Narin said.

And that — Bashir thought, and didn't say — that was the whole inversion. Not *clever foreigners crack Sumer.* The cracking had been done by foreigners once already and they'd cracked it open and poured aliens in. The repair was hers. They were here to watch her do it, and hold the light, and write it down, and the win was not theirs to take.

Edith had not picked up the camera.

He noticed because he was watching for it now. She'd had three moments any photographer would kill for — Narin's hand over the wedge, Naila's exhale, the nod to Mira — and she'd sat there with the cap on and her hands in her lap, watching like she'd taught her hands to wait. At the nod to Mira her fingers had twitched toward the strap and stopped.

"You can shoot the page," Narin said, without turning, like she'd felt it. "Not us. The page."

"I wasn't going to shoot you," Edith said.

"I know." A beat. "That's new. Shoot the page."

Edith lifted the camera, took the cap off, and framed the dun column flat and square in the lane light — the document, not the woo, the clay and not the headline — and the shutter went once, soft. She put it down again.

Yusuf was still on the planet. Bashir recognised that too: the man circling back to the thing he most wanted to be true, picking at it, not ready to let it go clean.

"But the *Igigi,*" Yusuf said. "You said it like it mattered. Junior gods. Working gods. That's the part — if I'm selling, that's where I plant the gold mine, isn't it? *Working* gods. Labour. There's labour in it somewhere, that's real, that's actually in the clay, that's why it *grabs.*"

"There's labour in it," Narin said carefully. "That part's not invented. That's the thing I want you to understand about the good lies — they're not made of nothing. They're made of something *true* with the lights changed." She set the dun page down on the bench, flat, and put her two hands on her knees. "The Igigi. The junior gods. And in one of the oldest stories we have — older than Genesis by a long way, older than most of the Bible by a thousand years and more—"

She stopped, and Bashir saw her clock the word *Bible* land in the lane, saw her register that it had changed the air, and saw her not flinch from it and not lunge at it either. Just set it down on the bench beside the page, gently, like a thing she'd handle properly later.

"—in one of the oldest stories, the junior gods are made to *dig.*"

"There it is," Yusuf breathed.

"Not for gold." Narin's voice came down like a level. "For the *rivers.* For irrigation. The Igigi are made to dig the Tigris and the Euphrates, to haul the basket of earth, to break their backs on the canals — which is the most Mesopotamian image in the world, by the way, because that's what *living here was.* You dig the canal or you die. You haul the basket or there's no barley. The whole civilisation is a fight with two rivers and a desert. So when they imagined the gods working, they imagined them doing *the work everyone in the room had blisters from.* Digging mud. Carrying mud. Year on year on year."

"For how long?" Naila asked. Of course she did.

"Forty years, in one version. The text says it like a complaint. *They have borne the toil night and day.* Forty years of carrying the basket." Narin's hands turned over. "And then—"

She let it hang.

"And then they put down the basket," she said. "The Igigi. The junior gods. They set fire to their tools in the night and they march on the house of the chief god and they surround it and they refuse to dig one more cubit of canal. A *strike.* Four thousand years ago. The oldest labour action in literature is a *god* throwing his shovel in the fire."

Frik laughed — a sharp bark, half tic, half genuine, his hand going to his mouth and not making it. "*Sorry* — sorry, that's — the gods *unionised,*" and the echo caught it and ran it once more under his breath, *unionised, unionised,* and then he was grinning helplessly at the clay.

"They unionised," Narin agreed, and let him have it, and the lane laughed with him and not at him, which Bashir had come to understand was the entire architecture of this crew, the one load-bearing wall — that the laugh went *with.*

"So the management's got a problem," Yusuf said, leaning all the way in now, money-brain fully lit. "Workforce down tools. Production stops. Canals silt up. No barley. What's the fix? You can't make the gods go back, they'll just strike again. You need—"

"You need a *new* worker," Narin said. "Cheaper. Replaceable. One that won't burn its tools, or one that you don't mind if it does."

She picked the dun page back up. The light ran down the word at the top of it one more time — *the princely offspring,* the high-born seed of the sky, the ones who sat in council and weighed the world and would, two thousand years on, slide all the way down into the grey courts of the dead.

"And what they decide to make," she said, and her voice changed, went lower, went almost reverent, the scholar and the heir and the believer all standing in the same body in the same heat, "what the council of the great gods decides to make so that the junior gods can rest — what they reach for, to make this new worker out of—"

She didn't finish it.

She looked at them — Naila at the lane's mouth, Yusuf on the stool with his whole hunger showing, Mira against the wall reading every face including hers, Edith with her hands folded over a camera she hadn't raised, Frik bright and undefended, Bashir holding the door half-open with his whole body.

Out past the awning, in the white part of the lane, the Beast sat ticking in the heat with its water bag weeping a dark line down the dust, and a shape moved around it slow and unhurried, checking the shade, doing the thing he did that nobody could ever quite photograph; and somewhere in the citadel above them the call to something drifted thin across the rooftops, and the two rivers that had made all of this kept running south the way they'd run for six thousand years toward the cradle none of them had a permit to enter yet.

"You'll want this in the right tongue," Narin said. "Not mine paraphrasing it. The actual words. Because the next part is where they stole the most, and built the most, and where it stops being about Iraq and starts being about a book half the world prays out of — and I'm not handing *that* across on a paraphrase."

She stood. She gathered the dun page against her chest the way she had before, the gatekeeper coming back up over the scholar, but gentler now, the gate left on the latch.

"Tomorrow," she said. "My uncle's got the Atrahasis. The good edition. And we read what they made the worker out of, in the language it was written in, line by line." She looked at Bashir. "You read Sumerian?"

"Some," he said. "Enough to embarrass myself in front of you."

"Good." Almost a smile. "I'll need someone who can hear when I'm rushing it. There's a pun in the next part." She paused at the door. "There's a pun three thousand years old that the whole West missed because it can't survive the trip into Hebrew. You'll like it. It's the best joke in the Bible and nobody who quotes that verse has ever once laughed."

And she went inside, into the cool, among her uncle's shelves, and left the seven of them in the cut light of the lane with the word still hanging there unfinished — *what they reach for, to make this new worker out of* — and the answer waiting one day off, down in the older clay, in the tongue it was written in.

Bashir looked at the dust where the man and the truck had been.

The clay didn't say *spaceship.* It never had. It said *we were tired, and we made something to be tired in our place, and we made it out of—*

He didn't finish it either.

He'd wait for her to say it right.
