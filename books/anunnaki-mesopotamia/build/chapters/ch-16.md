# Chapter 16 — The Enemy's Library

Morning in Erbil came in flat and white, the heat already a held breath by the time Mira came out under the awning. The others were arranging themselves on cushions and crates in the half-shade. She took the place she always took now, back to the wall, angle on every face. Naila sat to her left where she could see the hands; that had become a quiet agreement between them, never spoken, the best kind.

Narin came last, with a tray. Tea in the small waisted glasses, and beside it a slab of bread torn from a sheet the size of a tablecloth. She set the tray down and lowered herself onto a cushion and did not look at any of them for a moment, looking instead at the steam coming off the glass nearest her hand.

Through the gap in the awning Mira could see the truck, and the long shape of Jakobus beside it, doing the thing he did with the water bag — the canvas one that wept itself cool, sweating in the heat the way nothing here did, a desert trick on a desert he hadn't been born to. He wasn't watching them. He never watched them in a way you could catch. He was reading the lane.

"Last night," Narin said, and her hands found the glass and turned it, "Naila asked the right question. She said: who handed Genesis the Lego." A small movement at the mouth that wasn't quite a smile. "I have hated that metaphor all night and I can't replace it."

Naila signed something at Mira, fast and badly — *I'm sorry* and *good* tangled into one shape. Mira shook her head: *no, it's good.* Across the circle Bashir caught the exchange and translated it for himself silently, his lips moving, the way he couldn't help.

"So I'll answer it," Narin said. "And the answer is a city. And a march. And a library."

---

She started with the march, because that was where the people started.

She told it plainly. In the year the West likes to round to 586 before its own era, the armies of Babylon came up the Levantine coast and did to Jerusalem what Babylon did to cities that didn't pay. Mira watched the words on Narin's mouth and the words under them. *Came up.* *Did.* Narin was not performing grief. She was holding it at a distance you could only manage with a thing you'd lived around your whole life, the way you carry a body you have washed yourself.

The temple, Narin said, was taken apart. Not burned only — *taken apart.* The bronze pillars cut up for the metal. The vessels carried off as inventory. And the people — not all of them; the ones who mattered, the ones with words in their heads — the priests, the scribes, the skilled, the literate, the dangerous-because-they-remembered — were chained and walked east.

"A thousand kilometres," Narin said. "Give or take. To the heart of the empire. To the capital of the people who had just unmade your god's house with their hands."

Yusuf had gone very still. Mira knew the stillness. It was the stillness of a man recognising a road from the wrong side of it.

"And here is the thing nobody draws in the children's Bible," Narin said. "They didn't go to a camp. They weren't dumped in a desert to die. Babylon was *cosmopolitan.* The cleverest city on earth, then. They settled the exiles by a canal — there's a man, Ezekiel, who tells you the name, the Kebar, the *nār Kabari,* it's in the cuneiform too, it's a real waterway — and the skilled among them found their level. And a scribe is a scribe. A scribe with letters in two languages is a scribe anywhere."

She let that sit.

"They could go into the city," she said. "And the city had a library."

---

This was where Bashir came forward, because libraries were his. He didn't take it from her — Mira watched the handover, the small tilt of Narin's head that *gave* it to him, *go on, you know this part* — and he took it gently, the way you take a sleeping child off someone's lap.

"You have to understand what a Babylonian library was," Bashir said. "Not a building of scrolls that rot. Clay. Baked clay. Tens of thousands of tablets, shelved, *catalogued* — they had catalogues, they had the equivalent of a card index, they had a tablet that told you which tablets they had. A scribe's whole training was copying. You copied to learn the signs, you copied to keep the texts alive, you copied because that is what a library is — a machine for not forgetting."

"And the Hebrew scribes could read it," Naila said. Not a question. She had a stylus going on her knee, the cut reed, marking the clay tablet she'd been allowed, the soft grey one. Mira couldn't see what she was writing. It didn't matter. The writing was how Naila held still.

"Akkadian was the diplomatic language of the whole ancient Near East for centuries," Bashir said. "It was the French of its day, the English of its day. A trained Judean scribe, a man whose job was words — there is no reason on earth he couldn't read the tablets. Every reason he would *want* to. You are a person of the book in a city that invented the book. You are a writer in the largest library that has ever existed. Of course you went in."

He spread his hands.

"And there, in the language of the people who marched you here in chains, was the older draft of your own beginning."

Mira watched Narin while Bashir said it. Narin's face did a thing then — not a flinch, the opposite of a flinch, a settling, like a stone finding the bottom of a well.

---

"Tell them what was on the shelf," Narin said.

Bashir told them.

He told them about the flood — not the way the streaming-doc would tell it, with a dramatic chord and a dark water effect, but the way the clay told it. A council of gods. A man warned. A boat built, sealed with bitumen, loaded with kin and craft and beasts. The rain. The waters. The boat grounding on a mountain. The birds sent out to test for land. The sacrifice when the man comes ashore, and the gods — Bashir's voice did something here, dropped — the gods crowding to the smell of the offering "like flies," because they'd flooded the world and forgotten they liked to be fed.

"A thousand years older than Genesis," Bashir said. "At least. Sitting on a shelf the exiles could walk up to."

"So they copied it," Edith said. She had her camera in her lap. The lens cap was on. Mira had clocked that the moment she sat down — the cap on, the strap looped twice round Edith's wrist so the thing couldn't lift itself to her eye by old habit. Edith was keeping her own hands honest.

"That," Narin said, "is exactly the wrong word. And it's the word people reach for, and it's why this whole conversation goes to ruin in the comments section." Her mouth quirked. She'd clearly read the comments section. "*Copied* is what the man who needs scripture to be a fraud says. *Copied* is the looting word. You took it, it was always theirs, you're a thief." She turned the glass a half-turn. "And it's wrong. Naila, tell them why it's wrong. You found it yourself, you don't need me."

Naila looked up from the clay. The whole circle waited, and didn't crowd, because they'd learned not to crowd.

"Because the houses are different," she said. "Same Lego." The faintest of grins. "Sorry. Same pieces, different houses. The flood story isn't *copied,* because if you copied it you'd get the *same one,* and you don't. The mountains are different — theirs is a mountain called Nimush, the Hebrew one is the mountains of Ararat, that's not a transcription error, that's a different mountain. The birds go out in a different order. The boat's a different shape. The number of days is different. The reason for the flood is *completely* different, and that's the whole — that's the whole tell."

"Say the reason," Narin said.

"In the old one," Naila said, "the gods flood the world because people are too *loud.* They're breeding, they're noisy, the gods can't sleep. It's a noise complaint. It's the upstairs neighbours calling the council on the human race." A few breaths around the circle, the laugh that isn't quite a laugh. "And in Genesis the reason is *moral.* The world is full of violence, of *hamas,* and that's why. Somebody took the same flood and changed *why God does it.* You don't change the why by accident. You change the why because you have a different idea of what God is *for.*"

She looked down at her clay again, finished.

"That's not copying," she said, to the reed. "That's arguing."

---

Frik had been quiet the whole morning. He sat a little apart, as he always did when he was working hard at holding, his shoulder giving the soft repeated rise it gave. Now his head ticked once, twice, and the word came out of him sideways, not aimed at anyone:

"*Better.*"

Then, the involuntary completion, riding the same breath, surprising him as much as them: "*The arguing's the good bit.*"

Narin looked at him. Something passed over her that Mira read clearly and that the others probably missed, because they were watching Frik to see if he was all right, which was the kind thing and the wrong thing. The thing that passed over Narin was *relief.* Frik had said the load-bearing sentence and he'd had no choice about it, which meant it was true the way only the things a person can't stop are true.

"Yes," Narin said quietly. "The arguing is the good bit. Hold that."

---

She poured more tea. The morning had thickened; you could hear the bazaar starting up below the citadel, the metal-on-metal, a generator, a long way off a call.

"Now I'm going to do the thing I trust the fewest people on earth to do," Narin said. "I'm going to take you to the second shelf. And I want you to watch how careful I am, because there's a version of this conversation that turns my faith into a museum case, and I won't have it, not in my own family's house. So watch my hands."

She put both hands flat on her knees, like a witness swearing in.

"The tower," she said. "Babel."

Mira felt the room change shape. Even Yusuf leaned in.

"Genesis eleven," Narin said. "The whole world had one language. They came to a plain in the land of Shinar — and Shinar *is* Sumer, that's not in dispute, that's just the Hebrew name for the place — and they said, come, let us build a city and a tower whose top is in the heavens, and let us make a name for ourselves, lest we be scattered. And God comes down to see it, and confounds the languages, and scatters them. And the place is called Babel, *because there the LORD confused* — *balal* — *the languages.*" She let the breath out. "And every Sunday-school child in the world is taught that the tower fell down. It doesn't. Read it. The tower doesn't fall. God just stops the building by breaking the words."

She looked at Bashir.

"Tell them what the writer was looking at."

Bashir's whole body had come awake. This was his country, the country of words.

"He was looking at a ziggurat," Bashir said. "A real one. Babylon had a great temple-tower, a stepped mountain of brick. It had a name — Etemenanki. It means, roughly, *the house of the foundation of heaven and earth.* House. Of the foundation. Of heaven and earth. A tower whose top is meant to reach the heavens. It is *literally named* the thing the Genesis tower is *for.*" He laughed, short. "And the name of the city, Babylon — in the Babylonians' own language it's *Bāb-ilim, bāb-ili,* the gate of the gods. A holy name. A proud name. *The gate where heaven touches earth.*"

"And the Hebrew writer," Narin said, "takes *the gate of the gods* — that proud, sacred name — and says no. He hears *bāb-ili* and he answers with *balal.* Confusion. Babble." Her mouth was a thin line and there was a fierce light behind it. "He puns. He takes the conquering city's holiest word for itself and turns it into the sound a baby makes. The gate of the gods is just *noise.* That's not a man who got the story wrong. That's a man who got the story exactly right and *answered it.*"

Bashir picked it up like a relay baton. "And it's in the building too. Genesis: *let us make bricks, and burn them thoroughly, and they had brick for stone, and bitumen for mortar.*" He looked round at them. "Stop. Listen to that. Burnt brick. Bitumen for mortar. That is not how you build in Canaan. In the Levant you build with *stone.* You quarry it, you stack it, you mortar it with lime. Mud-brick maybe, sun-dried, but you don't bake your bricks in kilns and you don't seal a tower with tar — *because you don't have the tar.*" He brought a hand down soft on his own knee. "Baked brick and bitumen mortar is the *Mesopotamian* method. It's how you build in the alluvial plain where there's no stone and the river gives you mud and the ground sweats asphalt. The Genesis writer doesn't just *know about* Babylon. He knows how Babylon's bricklayers worked. He's describing a building technique that doesn't belong in his own country."

"Because," Naila said, very quietly, the reed gone still on the clay, "he was *there.*"

The awning ticked in the heat. Outside, Jakobus's tune started up, low, the same handful of notes he used when nothing was wrong. Mira couldn't hear it, but she saw the others' shoulders settle to it the way they always did, and she watched his free hand keep the time against his thigh, and she understood it was a metronome for a clock none of them could hear and most of them had stopped noticing they obeyed.

---

"This," Narin said, "is the part where the comments section divides into two armies, and both of them are wrong."

She held up one finger.

"Army one says: *see, it's all stolen, the Bible is just Babylonian myth with the names changed, your faith is a forgery.*" Her voice didn't rise. "And to that I say: you have understood nothing. A forger copies to fool you. These writers re-wrote to *argue with you.* They kept the flood and changed why God sends it. They kept the tower and turned the gate of the gods into babble. They sat in the largest library on earth, in chains, in the language of their conqueror, and they read the conqueror's account of how the world began — and they wrote it back the way they believed it actually was, and every single change is a *theological claim.* That is the opposite of theft. That is the most defiant act of authorship I know of in the ancient world. They were a conquered people and they kept their soul by re-writing the empire's own story until it said what they needed it to say. You don't lose your faith over that. You should be on your knees about that."

Mira watched the faithful crew member's face — she watched Narin's, because Narin was the faithful one in this room, the living heir to both the people who got marched and the language they got marched in, and Narin's face was doing the thing the mouth would never say aloud. Not pride. Pride was too small. It was the face of a woman defending a grave from people who'd come to either rob it or photograph it, and finding, to her surprise, that the people in front of her had come to neither.

Edith had not touched the camera. Mira clocked that too. Edith's hands were folded over the lens cap like she was keeping it warm.

Narin held up a second finger.

"Army two," she said, "is the sneerers' twin. The believer who's so frightened of the library that he pretends the library isn't there. *No, no, there's no connection, it's a coincidence, the floods are just both floods.*" She shook her head. "No. The connection is real. It's mainstream. It's been the consensus among people who actually read the tablets for a hundred and fifty years. Hiding from it doesn't protect the faith. It just hands the whole conversation to army one." She turned the empty glass. "The genealogy is real. Genesis has a family tree, and the older branches of it are sitting in a Babylonian library a thousand years before the Hebrew was written. That is not the threat army two thinks it is. It's the inheritance."

---

It was Yusuf who broke. Not loudly. He'd been leaning further and further forward, elbows on knees, and now he scrubbed both hands down his face and made a sound that was almost a laugh and entirely not.

"You know what I sold," he said. To his own hands. "Not directly. You understand, never directly, that's the whole — there's always a layer. But I moved the paper for crates I knew, I *knew,* came up out of the south. And the ones with the gods on, the ones some idiot stamped *Anunnaki* on in the catalogue copy — those moved at a premium. Because *aliens.* Because somebody'd seen a video. The school tablets, the ration lists, the ones that actually *say* something — 'so-and-so, your servant, received this much barley' — those were filler. Bulk. You priced them by the kilo." He looked up, and his eyes were wet and angry. "I had it exactly backwards. The whole world has it exactly backwards. The *aliens* are the cheap part. The aliens are the markup and the smokescreen and the thing that makes sure nobody who matters reads what the crate actually contains. The *real* one — the one where a man in chains rewrites the gate of the gods into babble because he won't let the empire keep his God — that's the priceless one. And I'd have sold it for filler."

Nobody filled the silence. They'd learned that too. Mira watched Frik's shoulder rise and fall, and Frik held, this time. He held, and that holding was its own kind of speech.

Narin looked at Yusuf for a long moment. When she spoke it was without softness and that was the kindness of it.

"Yes," she said. "You had it backwards. So did the men who put my country's beginning in sacks." She didn't look at Edith when she said *sacks,* and the not-looking was louder than a look. "The grift needs the question loud and the answer cheap. *Were they aliens* keeps the lights on. *What did a conquered scribe do with the enemy's library* — that one nobody can put a price on, because the moment you understand it, you stop wanting to *own* the tablet and start wanting to *read* it. And a thing that's read can't be sold to one buyer in a dark room." Her jaw set. "That's why somebody, somewhere, would much rather you watched the alien documentary."

She said *somebody, somewhere* the way you set a thing down on a table and step back from it. Mira watched it land. Naila's reed had stopped. Bashir's lips had stopped moving. The shape of the sentence had a shape they all recognised now — the loud stories pushed up into the light, the quiet ones gone soft and missing — and none of them touched it. You didn't touch it. You let it sit on the table and you went on.

---

"There's one more," Narin said, after a while, when the tea was cold. "And then I'll have given you the whole shelf and you can decide what it's worth to you."

"The law," Bashir said, because he'd seen it coming.

"The law." Narin almost smiled. "Hammurabi. Everyone's heard of him. Eye for eye, tooth for tooth. There's a great black stone with his code carved on it — it's in the Louvre, it got carried off as loot in *antiquity,* the looting is older than any of us — and it says, if a man puts out another man's eye, they shall put out his eye. The talion. The world's most famous brutal little rule." She tipped her head. "And it's older than Moses. By centuries. And it's in Exodus, almost word for word. *Eye for eye, tooth for tooth, hand for hand, foot for foot.*"

Bashir leaned in. "But read what Hammurabi *actually* says, all of it. The talion in Babylon is for *aristocrats.* If a noble blinds a noble, eye for eye. If a noble blinds a commoner, he pays *silver.* If a noble's house collapses and kills the owner's son" — Bashir's eyes went bright with the awfulness of it — "they execute *the builder's son.* The son. For the father's bad work. Vicarious punishment. The price of justice depends on whose eye it was and how rich you are."

"And Exodus," Narin said, "takes the same rule — and it's careful, this is a real scholarly argument, whether it's borrowed direct or whether they're both drinking from the same legal world of the ancient Near East, I'll say *shared legal world* and a careful person should — Exodus takes that rule and *universalises* it. One law for the rich man and the poor man. And it forbids the thing Hammurabi does — you do not put a son to death for his father, *a person dies for his own sin.* It edits the empire's law toward equality and away from punishing the innocent. Same furniture. Re-arranged to argue."

"*Better,*" said Frik, again, helplessly, and this time it was almost a benediction.

---

Mira had been watching the truck on and off the whole morning, the way the light moved on the long shape beside it. Now Jakobus came toward the awning, unhurried, the always-clean hanky going over the back of his neck, and he didn't come *in.* He stopped at the edge of the shade, where the gold light gave way, and he looked at the tray and the cold glasses and the cut reed in Naila's hand and the slab of bread no one had finished, and he said something — Mira read it off his mouth, the courtesy handful he'd taught himself, *good, this is good* in the local words, badly, with respect — and then he said the other thing, the thing he said, that wasn't a question because he never asked it as a question.

She read it off his lips and gave it to the room in sign so Narin would have it whole, hands slow and plain:

*What's it for.*

Not *who built it.* Not *is it true.* The made thing in his hand was a story now, not a tablet, and the question came down on it the same way it came down on everything — flat, patient, refusing every reading but the working one.

Jakobus didn't wait for the answer. He never did. He touched two fingers to the brim of nothing and went back to the lane, and the tune went with him.

The crew turned, all of them, to Narin.

Because that was the question, wasn't it. Not whose Lego, not which house, not even who handed it over in chains by the Kebar canal. They'd done all that. They knew, now, *what was on the shelf* and *what the exiles did with it* and that the doing wasn't theft and wasn't surrender but the third thing, the unnamed brave thing — survival by authorship, keeping your soul by re-writing the empire until it said your God instead of its own.

But *what is it for.* What is a story *for,* to the people who carry it home from the place that conquered them. Why do you bother to re-write the flood when you're a slave by a foreign canal and your temple is rubble and your God's house is inventory in someone else's storeroom. What does that get you. What does it *do.*

Mira watched Narin's face, and saw that this was the one Narin couldn't read off the clay — that no decipherer, no scholar, no man with a working eye for a made thing could read this answer off the object, because it didn't live in the object. It lived in the people who still said the words. And there was only one person in the awning who could give it.

Narin set down the cold glass. She looked at her uncle's house, and the lane, and the man no camera could hold standing in the white light beyond the shade.

"Tomorrow," she said, and stopped, and corrected herself, because there was no more tomorrow to hide behind. She looked at them.

"No," she said. "Now."
