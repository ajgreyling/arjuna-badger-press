# Chapter 8 — Made of Clay

The good edition of the Atrahasis turned out to be three books, two photocopied folders, and a paper-clipped sheaf that smelled of someone else's cigarettes, all of it spread across the uncle's table under the awning while the morning still had some cool left in it.

Bashir had not slept. He'd lain on the borrowed cot in the back room and run the line in his head — *we were tired, and we made something to be tired in our place* — except in his head it kept trying to assemble itself in Sumerian and failing, because he didn't have the lines, only the rumour of them, and a man who's spent his life inside other people's words knows the difference between a sentence he can carry and one he's only heard described. He'd lain there reaching for it and missing, the way you reach in a dark kitchen for a glass you put down somewhere else.

Now Narin set a real glass in front of him. Tea, not water. The uncle's, poured from the dented pot, two sugars dropped without asking because the uncle did not ask, he simply decided you were the kind of guest who took two and was usually right.

"Drink," Narin said. "Then we work."

The uncle had gone in out of the heat. Jakobus was somewhere past the gate with the Beast and a problem involving a radiator hose and a length of rubber that had, the last anyone heard, come off a tractor; you could hear the occasional clink of a spanner and, once, a low run of whistling that climbed a scale and then abandoned it. Edith sat at the table's far end with her camera on the bench beside her, lens-cap on, hands folded, asking nothing. Frik had his back to the wall and his knee going. Mira sat where she could see all their faces at once, which was a thing Bashir had stopped noticing she did until he'd noticed it, and now he couldn't stop seeing it. Yusuf had taken the chair closest to the books. He had a notebook open. He had not written anything in it.

"Atrahasis," Narin said. She laid her hand flat on the topmost folder. "Old Babylonian. The copy we lean on dates to the reign of Ammi-saduqa — that's a name, not a date, but call it eighteenth century before Christ. Three tablets. The scribe signs them. His name is Ku-Aya. A junior scribe. He tells you he's junior. He apologises, basically, in the colophon."

"The scribe apologises," Frik said.

"He says he's a junior apprentice and he writes the date and then he asks the gods to bless the man who doesn't erase his tablet." A small dryness moved across her face. "Three thousand seven hundred years and a junior scribe is still asking you not to delete his file."

Yusuf made a sound that wasn't quite a laugh.

"This is older than the Bible," Narin said, and she said it plainly, the way you'd say a river is older than a bridge — not a provocation, a fact about water and stone. "Older than the oldest layer of it. I want that sitting in the room before we start. Not to take anything from anybody. Just so nobody's surprised later about which way the borrowing ran." Her eyes touched Yusuf, then moved on, kind and quick. "Now. The opening. Bashir. Read me the first line you can read."

He pulled the photocopy toward him. The transliteration ran in its little italic forest, the Sumerograms shouting in capitals through the Akkadian, and his eye found the place she'd marked with a pencil tick.

"*Inūma ilū awīlum*," he read.

He stopped. He read it again, slower, and the stopping was not for the others.

"When the gods," he said, "were man."

The awning ticked in the heat. Out by the gate the spanner went still.

"That's the first line," Narin said. "*Inūma ilū awīlum.* When the gods were man. When the gods were the man. It's deliberately strange. It's meant to snag. The gods *were* the human — meaning, before there was a human, the gods did the human's work. They were the labour. There was no us yet, so the gods themselves had to dig."

"Dig what," Yusuf said.

"Canals." She turned a page in the folder, and there it was, the lines marching down. "This is the part everyone skips on the way to the flood. The gods are sorted into two ranks. The Anunnaki up top — the high ones, the princely ones, the bosses." She let the word sit; they'd earned it the day before, and she didn't relitigate it. "And under them, the Igigi. The working gods. The labour gods. And the Igigi carry the load. They dig the Tigris. They dig the Euphrates. By hand. For — the text gives you a span, and the span is brutal."

Bashir found it. The number stood up off the page at him.

"Forty years," he said, before he'd meant to translate it out loud. Then he checked himself, because numbers in cuneiform were exactly the trap they'd spent two days falling into. "It says — that's how I'd read it. Long stretches. Forty. Then ten. The text piles up time on them."

"Read it as 'too long,'" Narin said. "Read it as 'longer than a god should be made to dig.' That's the register. The point isn't the arithmetic." She didn't look at Naila when she said it, but Naila, who'd come up quietly to the table's edge with a heel-thump of socked feet, looked at the number and then at Narin and gave one short nod, granting it, *yes, that one's not for me, that one's a feeling dressed as a figure*, and the small permission of it passed between them without a word.

"And then," Narin said, "the Igigi have had enough."

She read it herself now, in the Akkadian, low and even, not performing it, and Bashir watched the room change shape. Mira had gone very still and was reading Narin's mouth and her throat and the small muscles beside her eyes, gathering the part of the meaning that doesn't ride on sound. Frik's knee had stopped. Even Edith's folded hands had loosened.

"They down tools," Narin said, coming back into English. "That's the only way to say it. They set fire to their digging tools. At night. They surround the house of Enlil — the chief god, the boss of bosses — they march on his door in the dark with torches, and they will not dig another canal." She tapped the page. "It's the oldest labour action in the written record. The first wildcat strike is *gods*. Striking against *god*."

"They strike," Frik said. Soft. Delighted, almost, and ashamed of being delighted, which was its own kind of true. "The workers strike."

"The workers strike," Narin said, and let him have it.

Yusuf had stopped pretending to hold the pen. "So what do the bosses do."

"They panic." Narin almost smiled. "They genuinely panic. Enlil cries. There's a whole scene of the high gods milling around at the door in the dark not knowing what to do, sending messengers, asking *who started this, who's the ringleader* — and the Igigi answer, beautifully, that *all of us* started it, every one of us, we declared war together. No ringleader. You can't behead a no." She turned the page. "And the mother. Now we get the mother."

Bashir felt the chapter of it turn under his hand. The forest of italics rearranged itself around a name.

"There's a goddess," Narin said. "Names shift on her across the versions — Nintu, Mami, Bēlet-ilī, Lady of the gods. She's the womb-goddess. The birth-goddess. The midwife of heaven. And one of the gods — the clever one, Enki, Ea, the water-and-wisdom one — stands up in the council and proposes the solution. The Igigi won't dig. Fine. *Make a substitute.* Make a thing that will carry the load instead. He says it to her directly." Her finger came down on the line and stayed there. "He says: *you are the womb-goddess, the one who can create. Create a* lullû. *Create the human. Let him bear the yoke.*"

She looked up.

"*Lullû awīlum.* The savage human. The primitive man. The clay-thing. *Let him bear the yoke that was the gods' yoke. Let him bear the load.* And then there's the line. Bashir."

He'd already found it. He'd found it before she said his name and he'd been sitting on it because saying it felt like a step you couldn't take back.

"*I have imposed your toil on man*," he read.

He let it land in his own chest first. It went in clean.

"*I have imposed your toil on man,*" Narin said after him, in English, and then in the Akkadian, so the room had it both ways, so it had the dressed version and the naked one. "The gods are freed. The Igigi never have to dig again. That's what we're for. That's the *job description.* In the oldest version of the human story this civilisation wrote down, the reason we exist is that the gods got tired of working, and they made a tool, and the tool was us."

Nobody said anything.

Out at the gate a hose-clamp went *tk-tk-tk* against the radiator, three small turns, and stopped.

"Now," Narin said. "What do they make us *out* of."

She didn't rush it. She let them want it. The gatekeeper and the teacher had become, in two days, hard to tell apart; the gate was open but she still chose the order you went through.

"Two ingredients," she said. "First. Clay. Earth. The mud of the abzu, the deep waters under the ground." She paused. "Hold the clay. We come back to the clay. Second—"

She turned to the other folder, the Enuma Elish, the *good* edition with the better photographs, and slid out a glossy plate, a museum photograph of a tablet, the wedges crowding the broken edge.

"—because the recipe gets two writers, and they don't agree on the cooking, only the ingredients. Atrahasis says: they slaughter a god. A god named We-ila — a god who *had* something, the texts argue about what, intelligence, breath, a heartbeat, a *planning-spirit* — and they kill him in the assembly, and they mix his flesh and his blood into the clay. And the womb-goddess spits on the clay. And the great gods spit on it. And it's worked together — *and a ghost comes into being from the god's flesh.* The text says that. A spirit, an *eṭemmu*, a ghost, comes into the clay from the slain god and it stays. It's the part of us that doesn't go quiet. The drumbeat that goes on beating after the body's mixed. They give us a leftover piece of a dead god so the clay will get up and walk."

Bashir's hands were flat on the table. He found he'd flattened them to stop them moving.

"And Enuma Elish," Narin said, sliding the plate an inch toward the middle of the table so they could all see the photograph, "the Babylonian one, the great one, says it slightly differently and worse. There the slain god is Kingu — the rebel general, the consort of the chaos-mother Tiamat, the one who carried the rebellion's authority. The victorious god, Marduk, has won the war in heaven. And the council needs a labour force, same as ever — *to set the gods free, to release them from service.* And the question goes around: who's to blame for the war? Who do we kill for this? And Kingu's given up. They arrest him. They cut his arteries. And from *his* blood — the rebel's, the traitor's, the guilty party's — Ea fashions humankind."

She set her hand beside the photograph but did not touch it.

"So in the version half of you might've grown up adjacent to, in the great state liturgy of Babylon," she said, "the substance of the human being is the blood of a condemned rebel, mixed into mud, *so that the gods can take a day off.* We are made of the guilty. We are made of the loser. We are made to clock in."

It was Yusuf who broke.

He didn't make a scene of it. That was almost the worst part, Bashir thought afterward — that Yusuf, who could fill a room when he wanted to, who'd talked their way past three checkpoints and a man with a clipboard who'd hated all of them on sight, just sat there with his closed notebook and his idle pen and went somewhere behind his own eyes, and his jaw worked once, and he said, to the photograph, not to any of them:

"That's it?"

"Yusuf," Narin said.

"No — I'm asking. That's the real one. That's the original. The thing under the thing." His voice stayed level and that was how Bashir knew it wasn't level at all. "We dug it up, we deciphered it, we did all the work, and *that's* what it says. We were made because somebody upstairs didn't want to dig their own ditch."

"Yes," Narin said. She didn't soften it. She'd watched a lot of people meet this text and she knew the only insult was a lie. "That's what it says."

Yusuf turned the pen over in his fingers. Once. Twice.

And Bashir watched the door close on him, and understood — fully, with his whole chest, for the first time since a man with sunglasses and a clean hanky had found him in a Marseille hostel and asked him in four languages whether he'd ever wondered about a certain seal-impression — understood what the man had actually gathered them around. Not a mystery. A grief, with a mystery painted over it.

Because Bashir had read the other version too. He'd read it in English, years back, in a paperback with a foil cover, on a long train with nowhere to be. *They came for gold.* The sky-people, the long-lived ones, the home planet with the failing atmosphere; they came down to the marsh-lands to mine, and the work was hard, and the working ones rebelled — *they* down their tools, in that telling, same as the Igigi — and so the chief scientist and the birth-goddess took the clay of this planet and the *essence* of a sky-person and they made a worker. A primitive worker. Made in their image, with a piece of them inside it, to carry the load.

The skeleton was the same. He could see it now, laid bare on the table beside the real one: rebellion-of-the-workers, decision-to-make-a-substitute, clay-of-the-earth plus a-divine-contribution, made-to-bear-the-load. Bone for bone. Joint for joint. Whoever had written the foil paperback had not invented a thing. He'd kept the whole skeleton and changed the meat. He'd swapped the gods for astronauts and the canals for gold and the ghost-in-the-clay for *a splice of DNA*, and—

And he'd done one more thing, and that was the thing that had Yusuf turning his pen over, and Bashir said it before he could stop himself, the way Narin had read the line about the toil before she meant to.

"He made it kinder."

They all looked at him. Mira's eyes came to his mouth.

"The — the other version. The famous one." He kept his eyes on the photograph because it was easier than faces. "It says the same skeleton. But it tells it kinder. In that one we're *designed.* We're a *project.* Someone clever stood over the clay and *wanted* a thing and made it on purpose and put a piece of themselves in it because they needed it to be smart enough to use a *pickaxe.* You're — in that story you matter. You're the solution. You're wanted." He swallowed. "In *this* one—" he put two fingers on the museum plate, near the broken edge, not quite touching the glossy wedges "—in this one we're a way the bosses got out of digging. We're made of mud and a corpse so the management can knock off early. There's no part of it that needs us to be clever. There's no part of it that *wants* us. It just needs the ditch dug by something that isn't a god."

He stopped.

"That's the trade," he said. "That's the whole trade. The alien story keeps every bone and gives you back the one thing this story takes away. It tells you you were *meant.*"

The awning ticked. Somewhere a generator two streets over coughed and caught.

Naila had been standing at the corner of the table with her arms wrapped around herself, swaying a little, the table-edge under one hand where she could feel the grain of it. She'd been quiet through all of it, doing her own arithmetic, and now she said, to the wood, in the flat clear way she said the true things:

"It's the same shape. Drawn around a nicer middle." She tapped twice on the table, lining something up nobody else could see. "If you want the shape to be true you have to take the middle that comes with it. They cut the middle out and glued in a better one and kept the shape, and people felt the shape was *real* — because it is, it's three thousand seven hundred years real — and they thought the feeling of the shape was a feeling about the middle." Another tap. "It isn't. The shape's old. The middle's new. The new middle is the part that makes you feel chosen." She looked up, found Yusuf, held it for the half-second she could. "It's a comfort wearing a fact's coat."

Yusuf put the pen down. Flat. Deliberate. Like a man laying down a tool.

"I read all of it," he said. Quietly. To Naila, because she'd given him the room to. "All twelve books. I read them on a phone, in a Land Cruiser, between two consignments I'm not proud of, while a man across the dashboard counted money that was the wrong colour. And the whole time I'm reading—" He stopped. Started again. "You want to know the worst of it? It wasn't even about the aliens for me. It was that it made the world have a *reason.* It made all of it — the consignments, the money, the man, *me* — it made it a chapter in something. Like there was a plan and I'd just lost the page. And I needed there to be a plan so badly that I never once asked the page what it actually said." He looked at the photograph. At the wedges. "And this is what it actually said. We were made to dig. And then to die. There's a flood coming in tablet three to thin us out because we get too loud. We breed and we make noise and the boss can't sleep, so he drowns us. That's the *plan*, Naila. That's what was on the page I was so desperate to find."

"Yes," said Narin, again, and this time she did soften it, but not the fact — only the silence around it. She reached and turned the Atrahasis folder so the photograph faced her, and she touched the broken edge of the worker-line, the *I have imposed your toil on man*, with a fingertip, the way you'd touch a scar that wasn't yours.

"But listen to me, because I'm the one who has to live next door to this text," she said. "It is the bleaker story. It's mud and a dead man's blood and forty years of digging and a flood for the crime of being loud. That's all true. And it is also the *braver* one. Because these people—" her hand opened over the books, the photocopies, the cigarette-smelling sheaf, her uncle's whole crowded shelf "—these people sat in the heat between two rivers four thousand years ago and looked their actual situation in the face. The canals really did have to be dug. By hand. By people. Forever. The river really did flood and really did drown the loud and the quiet alike with no regard for anybody's plan. And instead of telling themselves a story where the sky loved them and built them on purpose, they told the truth: *we are clay and a borrowed spark, and the work is endless, and the water does not care.* And then—"

She stopped. The teacher caught herself one syllable before a sermon, and the gate came down to exactly the height she wanted it.

"And then they kept going anyway," she said. "They wrote it down. They signed it. Ku-Aya the junior scribe wrote *please don't erase me* at the bottom of the bleakest story a person ever told about themselves, and three thousand seven hundred years later we did not erase him. He's right here. We're saying his name." She looked around the table. "That's not nothing. That's the opposite of nothing. They didn't need to be chosen to be worth the clay. That's braver than any spaceship."

It was very quiet for a moment.

Then Frik, who had not blurted once in twenty minutes, who'd held it the way you hold a breath, let it out, and it came out true the way it always came out true, aimed at the foil paperback that wasn't even on the table:

"He gave you the gold to look at so you'd never read the *ditch.*"

And the awful elegant rightness of it sat there, because that was the seam, exactly — the gold was the bright thing, the gold pulled the eye, *they came for gold* was a treasure-hunt and a treasure-hunt is a kind of hope; and behind the gold the original word stood plain and unhopeful: *toil.* Imposed. On man.

Out at the gate the spanner clinked down onto stone. There was the sound of a hand testing a fresh clamp, the squeak of rubber seating home, and then the low whistle again, climbing its scale, and this time finishing it — a small, complete, unhurried tune, the sound of a man who had fixed a thing and was content to let the fixing be the whole of it, and asked nothing of the table about what it had found.

Narin let it breathe. Then she pulled the third folder over — thinner, older photocopies, a different hand of marginalia in it, in a script Bashir didn't know.

"Tomorrow we'll do the pun," she said. "The Lady of the Rib. I promised you the best joke in the Bible and I keep my promises." A flicker. "But you're not ready for the joke until you believe the rest. Until you believe it isn't a fairy tale somebody made up at a campfire. Until you believe people sat in a real city and read this off a real tablet and *checked each other's work.*"

She set her hand on the thin folder.

"Because that's the part nobody tells you," she said. "Everyone wants to argue about what the clay *says.* Almost nobody asks the question that should come first." She looked at them, the gatekeeper gone all the way down now, the scholar offering it up. "How do we even know we can *read* it? A dead language nobody'd spoken in two thousand years, in a script with six hundred signs, dug out of a hill in pieces? How does anyone *prove* they cracked that — and didn't just make up a story about a story?"

She turned the folder so its faded first page faced the room.

"Tomorrow," Narin said, "I'm going to tell you about the time four men sealed their answers in four envelopes."

Yusuf picked his pen back up.

This time, he wrote.
