# Chapter 20 — Inheritance

The tea came in glasses too hot to hold, which was the point. You waited. You let the steam do its work on the back of your hand and you waited, and while you waited the man who'd poured it watched the street the way everyone in Baghdad watched the street — not afraid, just literate, reading the traffic for grammar Mira couldn't parse.

They were on a balcony above Mutanabbi Street, the booksellers' street, where the Friday market had spilled paper across the pavement until the road itself looked typeset. Below them a boy was selling Qur'ans and pirated calculus textbooks off the same blanket. A man in a wheelchair sold dictionaries. Somebody had laid out a run of water-stained Agatha Christies in English, spines up, like fish on ice.

"You know she wrote here," Omar said. He had his feet on the railing and a glass balanced on his knee in a way that made Lens, two chairs down, physically unable to relax. "Christie. *Murder in Mesopotamia.* Her husband dug at Ur. She washed the pottery. Mallowan. She used to clean three-thousand-year-old ivory with her face cream."

"That's apocryphal," Mira said.

"It's *true* apocryphal. There's a difference." He tipped his head back. "Everybody who comes here writes the place down and takes it home. That's the whole — that's the entire racket, isn't it. You come, you look, you leave with a book."

Below, the boy folded a Qur'an in newspaper for a buyer, handling it with two hands, the way you'd lift something asleep.

---

Here is the part that is true, and that nobody disputes, and that you can check tonight against a library catalogue without leaving your chair.

The stories travelled. Not metaphorically — physically, on baked clay and in human memory, down trade roads and exile roads, from a flooded southern marsh into a hill kingdom that would one day be marched, in chains, back down those same roads into Babylon. The Flood narrative of *Gilgamesh* Tablet XI sits in the British Museum, K.3375, and it is older than the Flood narrative in Genesis by something on the order of a thousand years. This is not a fringe reading. It is in the standard commentaries. Hermann Gunkel wrote it down before the First World War. It is taught, in seminaries, to people who go on to preach the text with their whole hearts and lose nothing in the trade.

A man builds a boat. He seals it with pitch. He sends out birds to test the water — a dove, a swallow, a raven. The waters fall. He makes a sacrifice on a mountain, and the gods, or God, smell the savour and are moved.

That is two stories. It is also, in the bones, one story, twice.

The official account of how this happened is sober and well-evidenced and beautiful: a small people, defeated, deported, sat for two generations in the libraries of the empire that had broken them, and found there the older drafts of their own beginnings — and did not throw them away. They *answered* them. They took the council of squabbling gods and made it one God. They took the flood sent because humanity was *noisy* and made it a flood sent because humanity was *cruel*. They argued with the source. You can hear the argument if you read the two side by side, and the argument is the inheritance.

So. That is the official story, and it is correct, and the crew had spent five chapters loving it.

---

"I keep getting stuck on the verb," Mira said.

Omar waited. He'd learned to wait; it had taken him eleven months and Naila standing on his foot twice.

"Inherit. We say a culture *inherits* a story like it's a watch in a drawer. Like it just — sits there, and then it's yours, and you wind it." She turned her glass a quarter-turn on the rail, lining the seam up with the railing's edge without deciding to. "But you can't inherit a watch you have to rebuild from someone else's blueprint in a language you only half speak while the previous owner is standing in the room telling you you've got it wrong."

"That's most inheritances," Omar said. "You should meet my uncles."

"It's not ownership. That's the — that's the thing I can't make come out clean." She frowned at the street. "Sitchin owned it. That's what people loved. He gave you a thing you could *hold*. The gods came down, they mined the gold, they made us out of apes to swing the picks, and here's the seal that proves it, here's the planet, here's the orbit — it's *yours*, you possess it now, you know the secret. Ownership feels like understanding. It's the cheapest version of understanding there is and it feels exactly like the real one."

*"Twelve planets,"* came Hecklefish's voice from the laptop propped in the chair between them, screen turned to the shade. *"He counted twelve planets in a four-thousand-year-old picture the size of a postage stamp and one of them was a man named Dubsiga and the genre's been running on it for fifty years. I'd be embarrassed. I'm a fish. I have no skull. I'd still be embarrassed."*

"He's not wrong," Mira said, "and he should still shut up."

*"The seal's a dedication. 'To Dubsiga, from his servant, may he be well.' It's a get-well card. They turned a get-well card into a solar system."* The fish paused. *"It's almost beautiful. He wanted it so bad."*

That last part Mira let stand, because it was the only honest thing the heckle had ever said.

---

The hole, the real one, the one the debunk leaves open because it isn't *its* hole to close:

Everyone agrees the alien reading is dead. Sumerologists agree, Assyriologists agree, the dictionary agrees — *nibiru* is "crossing-place," it's Jupiter at its station, you can see it from a roof in Nasiriyah on a clear night without a spaceship in sight; *Anunnaki* is "the princely offspring," gods on thrones, not pilots in cockpits; the three-thousand-six-hundred is a base-sixty number, the same arithmetic that still cuts your hour into minutes. Killed in the clay. The crew had watched it die. It was a clean death.

But here is what the clean death does not tell you. It does not tell you *why the stories moved at all.*

Because they didn't have to. A defeated people, dragged to the centre of the world that defeated them, could have refused the library. Could have sat in Babylon and held their own small story shut like a fist and called the rest blasphemy. Plenty of conquered peoples did exactly that and the stories died with them in a single generation, unrecorded, and we will never know what was lost because losing it was the whole event.

These ones didn't refuse. They *read the enemy's books.* They took the noise-flood and the boat and the birds and the savour-of-the-sacrifice and they carried it home and built it into the holiest thing they had, and they did it so well that three thousand years later a man in a wheelchair on Mutanabbi Street could sell that exact story, the flood and the boat and the dove, folded in newspaper, to a buyer who handled it like something asleep.

The transmission is the hole. Not *whether* — *why.* Nobody in any of the standard commentaries can tell you why a beaten people inherits its conqueror's draft instead of burning it. That is not a settled question. That is not even a scholar's question, really. It's the question under all of it, and the field is honest enough to leave it open.

The crew had spent the whole journey thinking the prize was the texts.

The prize was the verb.

---

Huda found them an hour later, climbing the balcony stairs with a folder under her arm and dust on her good shoes. She refused the tea, then took it, then held it without drinking the way the local always did — the tea was an excuse, not a beverage.

"My student," she said, without preamble, because preamble was for people with time, "is teaching her own students this week. First-years. Sumerian sign-list. *Ah*, *engur*, *abzu*." She set the folder on the table. "I sat at the back. I have not sat at the back of a room in twenty years. It was unbearable. She got the determinative for *deity* wrong twice and I did not correct her and I aged a decade."

"She got it wrong, or the textbook got it wrong?" Omar asked.

"The textbook is German and forty years old and assumes you already know what it is teaching you." Huda's mouth did the thing that was almost a smile. "She corrected the textbook. In front of the class. Then she got it wrong again. Both, in the same hour. That is what teaching is."

Mira watched the folder, not Huda — she always watched the made thing first, it was where her eye went, it was how she was built. Photocopies. A first-year's handout, the signs printed large, the wedges drawn fat and patient so a sixteen-year-old's hand could find them.

"Can I ask something rude," Mira said.

"You will regardless."

"Does it bother you that we're here. Not — Archer. Us. People who'll leave. People who'll write it down and take it home." She made herself look up. "We came for the same thing the streaming crews come for. The story. We're — better behaved. But we're the same shape. We come, we look, we leave with a book."

Huda set the tea down. Down on the street the booksellers were starting to pack, folding the blankets up over the spines, the road going back to being a road.

"You think the question is whether you own it," she said.

"I think I just realised that's the wrong question and I don't know the right one yet."

"No." Huda picked up the first-year's handout, the fat patient wedges, and turned it so Mira could see. "Nobody owns this. *I* don't own this. I have given my whole life to it and it is not mine. It was not the scribes' — they copied what was already old. It was not Babylon's; Babylon found it in the south and carried it north. The Hebrews did not own it; they argued with it, and you do not argue with what you own, you argue with what was given to you that you did not ask for." She set the handout down between them, very lightly, the way the man below had folded the Qur'an. "Ownership is the alien story. *Mine, the secret, I possess it now.* That is Sitchin's whole gift and it is why people loved him and I will not laugh at them for it because it is a real hunger and he fed it with sawdust."

*"Sawdust,"* the fish said, with deep satisfaction. *"Write that down."*

"What's the right question, then," Mira said.

Huda looked at her for a long moment, and Mira braced for the answer, because it was Huda's to give — it had always been Huda's to give, the whole way, that was the only law that had never bent — and Mira had finally learned to sit still inside the gap and not reach for it.

Huda gave it.

"Not *who owns it.* *Who is in the room when it is passed.*" She tapped the handout. "My student is in the room. Her students are in the room. You four are in the room, today, on this balcony, with the wrong tea, asking rude questions. That is all anyone has ever been. The scribe was a witness. The exile was a witness. The man who wrote Genesis was a witness to something a thousand years older that he did not own and could not have owned and would not have wanted to own — he wanted to *answer* it. To be in the room and say *no, not noise — cruelty. Not many gods — one. Here is what I would change.*" She drank her tea, finally, all of it, fast. "You inherit a story the way you inherit a language. Not by owning a word. By being trusted to be in the room when it is said, and to say it again, a little wrong, to someone younger, who will correct you, and get it wrong themselves."

Omar had gone very still, which for Omar was a kind of shout.

"We're not owners," he said.

"You were never going to be owners. You're not equipped." Huda almost smiled again. "You're witnesses. It is a smaller job than the streaming people think they are doing. It is the only job there has ever been."

---

The booksellers were gone by the time they came down, the road bare and grey and ordinary, the way the floor of Room 55 was ordinary under the glass, the way a roof in Nasiriyah was ordinary under Jupiter. Mira stood on the empty pavement where the spines had been laid out like fish on ice and felt the shape of the thing settle without closing.

They had walked in through the alien door. *Were the gods aliens.* The grandest, gaudiest door the genre had ever built, and they'd loved the people who built it, and they'd watched the answer die honest in the clay: no. Killed clean. The crossing-place is Jupiter. The seal is a get-well card. The princely offspring sit on thrones.

And they'd come out the far side into the bigger room, and the bigger room was this: an empty street where the oldest story we can read had just been sold, folded in newspaper, by a man who did not own it to a buyer who did not own it, in a city that had been carrying it longer than anyone had been writing it down. A first-year, this week, getting the sign for *god* wrong, twice, in front of a teacher who let her.

The why was still open. Why does a beaten people read its conqueror's books. Why does anyone carry the older draft home instead of burning it. Huda had not closed it. Nobody had closed it. It sat there on the empty pavement, the size of the whole road, and it was not theirs to close, and Mira found she could stand inside it now without flinching, which was the only thing the journey had actually taught her.

"What were they *for*," Omar said quietly. "The Anunnaki. That was the whole question, the start. What were they for."

Lens lowered the camera. He'd been shooting the empty street, the blankets gone, and now he let it hang.

"They were for being in the room," he said, "when it got passed."

Up the stairs behind them, a glass clinked. Huda, going back to her students. The road, going back to being a road. And somewhere south, the river that all of it had come up from, running the way it had run before anyone could read, carrying nothing it owned, downstream, toward the sea.
