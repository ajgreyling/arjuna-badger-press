# Chapter 10 — What She Won't Say

The bazaar came at Mira through her feet first.

Erbil's old market wrapped the base of the citadel like a thing that had grown there, and Mira walked it the way she walked everywhere — through the soles of her boots, through her hips, through the rail of warm air that pressed off a baker's oven and broke against her cheek. She felt the generator before she saw it: a low industrial shudder coming up the cobbles, the kind of drone that lived under hearing aids and made them useless, so she'd switched them off an hour ago and let the world go to its truer self. Vibration. Light. Faces.

The faces were the loud part.

Two men by a copper stall, arguing about a price with their whole bodies, hands open, shoulders rolling, neither of them angry — she could tell from the eyes, which stayed soft even as the hands shouted. A grandmother in black, sorting dried limes, her mouth moving in the patient nothing-shapes of a song. A boy running a tray of tea glasses overhead, threading the crowd, his attention a wire pulled taut from the tray to the gap to the next gap. Mira read it all without trying. She'd never had a choice. When the ears go, the eyes do the work the ears refuse, and after thirty-some years the eyes don't stop, can't be told to stand down, will sit in a meeting and tell you who's lying before the lie finishes its first syllable.

Narin walked two steps ahead, and Narin was the only person in the bazaar Mira couldn't fully read.

That was the interesting thing. That was the thing that had kept Mira up the night before, after the session under the awning, after she'd reached out one bad clumsy hand and shown Narin that she'd seen the edge of it — the thing under the lecture, the thing the lecture was a wall against. Narin had built a beautiful wall. Mira admired walls. She'd built a few. But she'd spent a lifetime watching the seams where a wall meets the face that's holding it up, and Narin's seam was *there*, just under the left eye, in the small muscle that tightened every time the talk got near the third folder. The one with the grandmother's handwriting in the margins.

Narin turned and said something. Mira caught *uncle*, caught *tea*, caught the lift of her brows that meant a question, and nodded, and they climbed.

---

The uncle's place was up a stair off the bazaar, under an awning of corrugated tin and threaded vine, on a flat roof that looked over the whole churning market toward the walls of the oldest continuously inhabited place on the planet. Mira knew that fact the way you know a thing from a placard. Standing in it was different. The citadel sat up there in the late gold like a loaf of risen earth, and somewhere under it, layer on layer on layer, were the bones of every Erbil that had ever been, going down past memory into the dark where the first writing had been pressed into the first clay by people who never imagined a woman would stand here who couldn't hear a word of their language and would understand them anyway.

The uncle was small and broad and had hands like the limes downstairs, brown and creased and capable. He saw Mira's hearing aids — she watched him see them, watched the half-second recalibration that most people botched and he didn't — and he didn't shout, didn't slow his lips into a mush, didn't grab her elbow. He simply turned his body square to her, full face, good light behind her so his own face was lit, and welcomed her with his whole front. Some people knew. You couldn't predict who. A man who'd spent eighty years on a roof above a market had learned to be read, and so he made himself readable, and Mira loved him instantly and without complication.

He poured tea. There was no negotiating it. The glass came small and waisted and scalding, amber going to copper, a sugar cube parked on the saucer, and he set it in front of her and watched until she lifted it, and only then did his shoulders come down. *You cannot refuse this,* his whole posture said, and Mira had no intention of refusing anything on this roof.

The rest of the crew came up the stair in ones and twos. Bashir, who folded onto a cushion and started talking to the uncle before he'd finished sitting, the two of them lobbing words back and forth — Arabic, then something older, the uncle's eyebrows going up in delight at whatever Bashir had reached for. Naila, who took the corner seat with the wall at her back and the stair in her sightline, and to whom the uncle handed tea without making it a thing, because Bashir must have said a quiet word at some point about exits. Frik, whose head ticked twice toward the citadel and who said, very clearly, "*Big,*" and nothing else, and the uncle laughed, a real laugh, and Frik's whole face opened. Edith last, with the camera at her hip, not raised, the strap doubled around her wrist.

And outside the awning, down the stair and across the little square where the Beast was parked under a plane tree, Jakobus. Mira could see him through the gap in the vine: a shape at the rear of the vehicle, doing something to a canvas bag that hung off the back and wept a slow dark line of water down the dusty paint. Cooling the water by letting it cool itself, some desert magic. He didn't come up. He never came up to these. He stood by the made thing he understood and let the people who understood the other things do their understanding. The uncle, though — the uncle had gone to the edge of the roof when the Beast first pulled in, and called something down, and Jakobus had answered him, three words, and the uncle had come back to his stove looking like a man who'd been handed a small good gift. Mira had no idea what was said. She only knew that Jakobus, who could not be read, who stood at the wrong angle to every light, had said something to an old Kurdish man on a roof that made the old man glad.

Then Narin sat, and the tea went around, and the third folder came out of her bag and lay on the low table, closed.

---

Edith lifted the camera.

She did it slowly, the way she'd been doing everything slowly since Baghdad came up — and it had come up, the museum, the sacks, the year that was in Edith's camera roll and was the reason Narin had looked at the lens for the first three days like it was a weapon, because for Narin it had been. Mira watched the lift. She watched Narin's eyes go to it. She watched Edith *stop*, the camera at half-height, and turn it toward Narin instead of toward the table, and ask — actually ask, not with a raised brow and a hopeful angle but with her free hand making the small flat circle the crew used for *okay?*, then a point at the folder, then a question-shrug.

Narin looked at the folder. Looked at the camera. Said something. Mira caught the shape: *the folder, no. My uncle, no. Me, you can.*

Edith lowered the camera all the way down. Set it in her lap. Put her open hand over the lens like a lid.

Something in Narin's seam eased. Not the deep one. A shallow one, the one that had been about the lens. There were two walls, Mira had understood that under the awning — one against the camera, which Edith was learning to disarm, and one further in, against the folder, which no one could disarm because Narin was holding it up herself with her own face.

The uncle refilled glasses no one had emptied.

---

Bashir began to translate the session as it ran, but Mira didn't need the words and the words weren't where it lived anyway, so she let his hands do their job at the edge of her sight and gave her eyes to Narin.

The folder opened. The grandmother's Syriac was there, looping up the margins of a typed Sumerian transcription, and beneath each line in a third hand the English. Narin's finger moved along a line. Her mouth made a word Mira had seen her make before, two days ago, under the awning, when the whole conversation had bent around a single broken bone of a joke. *Ninti.* The Lady of the Rib. Bashir's hands flowered when he came to it — Mira saw him reach for the air the way he did when a thing only lived in one language, his fingers opening on *rib*, then opening again on *life*, the same sign twice because Sumerian had hung two meanings on one peg and the joke was the peg. *Ti* is rib. *Ti* is life. So Ninti, the goddess made to heal the water-god's aching side, is both Lady of the Rib and Lady Who Makes Live, and the pun is three thousand years old and it is still funny, and it is hiding under a woman built from a sleeping man's rib in a book that came a thousand years later, the joke worn smooth by the crossing, the peg pulled out and only the rib left.

Mira watched Frik take that in. Watched his hands go still on his knees the way they had at the very end of the last session — the stillness that wasn't a tic at all, that was the opposite of a tic, a man so caught by a true thing that the noise in him went quiet. Naila had gone still the same way. They'd all gone still. A joke that survived three thousand years and one whole change of God.

But Mira wasn't watching the joke.

She was watching Narin, because the joke was the easy part, the part Narin had promised under the awning — *tomorrow, the Lady of the Rib* — and the easy part was over now, and Narin's finger had stopped moving on the page. It rested at the foot of the column. Below the joke. Where the typed Sumerian ran out and there was only the grandmother's Syriac and then the English under it, and Narin's mouth was not making the English.

Bashir's hands paused, waiting for her.

Naila leaned in toward the page, and her voice came — Mira couldn't hear it, but she saw it land on the others, saw Bashir's hands pick it up: *the same flood, the same clay, the same council of gods deciding to make a worker out of mud and a dead god's blood.* The pattern. Naila's gift. The same shape across cultures, the older draft showing through the newer one like a wall showing through plaster. Naila said it cleanly, kindly, the way she said the true things, with no idea — Mira could see she had no idea — what it was doing to the woman across the table.

Because Narin had not moved.

---

Here is what being talked-over feels like, from the inside.

It is not loud. That's the thing people get wrong, the hearing people, who think the harm is volume. It's the opposite of volume. You are in the room. You have a thing to say, and the thing is true and you can see that it's true and you have seen it before anyone, you saw it three sentences ago and you have been carrying it, waiting for the gap — and the gap doesn't come, because the gap is sound and you don't make sound the way they do, and the talk runs over the place where your turn would be like water running over a stone. You are not contradicted. You are not even disagreed with. You are simply not there in the part of the room where the deciding happens, and you watch them decide without the thing you carried, and the thing was the thing they needed, and you watch them go without it.

Mira had lived in that room her whole life. She knew it from the floor up. And so she knew, watching Narin's finger rest at the foot of the column, watching Narin not make the English, that Narin was in a version of the same room. Not Mira's room. Narin could hear. Narin could speak; Narin spoke five languages and out-read every person on this roof on the clay and the meaning, and it was *her* meaning, her language, her dead and her grief and her God. But she had walked up to a thing she carried, the thing under all the other things, and she had reached the gap where her turn was — and her own mouth had run over it like water over a stone. She was talking-over herself. She had a wall and the wall was made of her own voice, the beautiful confident teacher's voice, and the voice was running so the silence couldn't, because in the silence was the thing she would not say.

Naila and Bashir kept working the pattern between them, gentle, brilliant, building the bridge from the clay to the page, the older draft to the newer, and Mira understood with her whole body that they were building it *toward* Narin and that Narin could not cross it, and that no one but Mira could see she couldn't.

So Mira reached across the low table and put two fingers, very lightly, on the back of Narin's resting hand.

The talk stopped. Bashir's hands froze mid-sign. The whole roof felt it — the way a room feels it, the way the cobbles had felt the generator. Narin looked up.

Mira signed.

She signed badly. The crew's handmade dialect, half-invented at a thousand campfires, was a pidgin even by the generous standards of pidgins, and Mira's own native sign was a different country anyway, and Narin knew no sign at all. So Mira did the only true thing, which was to make the signs slowly enough that the *shape* of them carried even when the words didn't. She touched her own chest. She touched the folder. She made the sign the crew used for *holy*, which was wrong, was somebody's guess from years ago, two hands lifting like a roof — and Narin, who couldn't read the sign, read the roof, read the lift, read Mira's face under it, which was not asking Narin to say the thing.

That was the whole message. Mira had it down to nothing. *I see that you won't say it. You don't have to say it. I am not going to make you say it, the way they're about to make you say it without knowing they're making you.*

Bashir started to translate the sign and Mira shook her head at him, once, and he stopped, and got it, and his hands came down, and the roof understood that for once the words were not the point and the man who lived inside all the world's words was the one who saw it first.

---

Narin's seam went. The deep one. The one under the left eye that the whole lecture had been built to hold.

She didn't cry. It wasn't that. Mira would have hated it if it had been that — the easy release, the witnessed weep, the camera-shaped grief. It was smaller and worse and truer. The muscle let go, and for one second Narin's face was not a teacher's face and not a gatekeeper's face and not the face of the woman who had told the West *I will not hand them to you*. It was the face of a person who had found out, somewhere back along the line of her training, in a library or over her grandmother's shoulder, that the book her people had carried into exile and out of it, the book they'd kept their soul inside while empire after empire tried to grind them into scenery — that this book had a genealogy. That the Flood in it had an older draft. That the clay-and-blood, the council, the worker made of mud, were *there before*, in Babylon, in the very tongue of the people who carried her ancestors off in chains.

And that this was not a wound to the book.

That was the thing under the thing. Mira read it whole, in the second the seam gave, before Narin's voice came back to cover it. The terror in the wall was that the genealogy would be a verdict — that a hundred grinning men with green-lit logos would say *see, it's all just stolen myth, the Bible's a fraud, the woo and the shrug are the same coin and we'll spend it both ways* — and that her grandmother's loops in the margin would become a punchline in someone's streaming doc. And under *that* terror, the deeper thing, the thing she had walked up to and would not say:

That she had read the older draft, and the newer one, and she knew what the newer one had *done* with the older furniture. The Babylonian gods make the man out of mud to do the gods' toil, to break their backs digging the rivers so the gods can rest — the man is a slave, made because somebody had to dig and the gods were sick of digging. And the book her people carried took that same furniture — the clay, the breath, the council, the *let us make* — and turned it inside out. Made the man in the image. Not for toil. Not slave-clay. The same mud, and a god who didn't fight a monster to build the world but *spoke* it, and a man not bred to dig but made to be like the one who made him. The newer draft is an argument *against* the older one, written in the older one's own words, by a conquered people sitting in the conqueror's city refusing the conqueror's story of what a human being is for.

Narin knew this. It was the most beautiful thing she knew. And she could not say it on a roof to six foreigners with a camera, because to say it she would have to lay the genealogy on the table first, naked, the sources, the dates, the older clay — and trust that none of them would pick it up and run to the market and sell it as *the Bible is a Babylonian knockoff* before she got to the part where it stops being a theft and starts being the bravest re-authoring a people ever performed under occupation. She would have to hand them the explosive to get to the song. And the West had already shown her, once, in 2003, in sacks, what it did with the explosives.

Mira knew all of it because Narin's face said all of it in the second the seam gave, and faces don't lie, not at that depth, not for that fraction of time.

---

Mira didn't say any of it.

That was the whole gift, and she knew it, and she gave it. She had read the thing the mouth wouldn't say, and the reading was complete, and the completion did not require speech. A book about everything that ever got written down — the clay, the sealed envelopes, the four scholars who proved the language was real, the grandmother's loops in the margin, the joke that crossed three thousand years — a whole book of the written, and it was finished here, on a roof, by the one who read what stayed unsaid, and who understood that the kindest thing she could do with what she'd read was to keep it unsaid too.

She just held Narin's gaze. And nodded. Once. *I have it. It's safe. You don't have to.*

Narin's hand turned over under Mira's two fingers, slowly, until it was palm up, and her fingers closed around Mira's, brief and hard, a grip and a release, the most articulate sentence spoken on that roof and the only one with no language in it at all.

Then Narin's voice came back — Mira saw it return, saw the teacher reassemble — but it came back changed, lower, and she did not pick the folder up. She left it open on the table, the grandmother's loops face-up in the gold light, and she said something short to all of them, and Bashir's hands made it:

*Tomorrow. The whole of it. Not today.*

And Naila, who missed faces the way Mira missed sound, looked up from the pattern she'd been building and read, finally, from Mira's stillness rather than from any face, that the bridge had been built toward something that wasn't ready, and let it lie. She closed her own folder. She put her tea to her lips. The crowd-shudder of the bazaar came up through the roof, and Mira watched Naila's jaw, and reached over without a word and tapped the table twice in front of her — *exit, the stair, two steps* — and Naila's shoulder came down a centimetre, and that was a whole conversation too.

The uncle, who had read the entire wordless thing from his stove, came and refilled the glasses one more time, and set the pot down with finality, and said something to the roof in general that made Bashir laugh out loud and translate it as: *Drink, argue tomorrow, the dead have waited five thousand years, they will wait one more night.*

---

Down in the square, under the plane tree, the Beast's water bag had stopped weeping. The light was nearly gone off the citadel. Jakobus was at the front of the vehicle now, hood up, a torch in his teeth, and Edith — who had not raised her camera once on the roof, who had kept her hand over the lens like a lid through the whole of it — leaned at the parapet and watched him work and did not photograph that either.

He shut the hood. Wiped his hands on a hanky that came out of his pocket impossibly clean and went back impossibly clean. Looked up at the roof, at the line of them along the parapet with their copper glasses, and lifted two fingers off the brim of nothing in a small salute, and said three words up the dark — Mira couldn't hear them, couldn't read his mouth at the angle, would never know them — and the uncle, beside her, raised his glass down into the square in answer.

Then Jakobus crouched by the rear wheel with the torch and unfolded a map across his knee, and traced a line on it with one finger, north and west, toward the edge of the gold country where the safe north thinned out into the disputed ground, and held the finger there, on a place, in the dark, and did not look up again.
