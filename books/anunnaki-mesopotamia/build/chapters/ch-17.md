# Chapter 17 — What Is It For

The kettle had gone cold. Yusuf noticed it the way you notice a thing your hands have been pretending to do — turning the little glass in its saucer, the tea inside it the colour of old brick, untouched. He'd poured it twenty minutes ago. Or an hour. The morning had stopped behaving like a thing with edges.

Narin had said *now,* and then she hadn't said anything else for a while, and nobody pushed her, because you don't push the person standing at the top of a stair they've been climbing their whole life. She'd sat back down. Mira had moved the sugar bowl out of the sun so it wouldn't sweat. Bashir had put his pen down. Even Frik had gone quiet, which for Frik was its own kind of effort, the small machine of him idling, ticking — a click of the tongue, a roll of one shoulder, contained.

Out beyond the awning's edge the white light stood on the lane like a poured thing, and in it, near the Beast, Jakobus was doing something with the water bag. The canvas one. He'd hung it off the wing mirror that morning and it had been weeping all day, that slow desert sweat the man swore by — *wet canvas drinks the wind, makes its own cold* — and you could see, even from here, the dark patch on the dust beneath it where it had been spending itself drop by drop to keep the water inside cool enough to be a kindness. He had his sleeves rolled. He wasn't looking in at them. He never looked in when the talk turned to the clay. It wasn't his to look at, and he knew it, and Yusuf had watched him *practise* not-looking the way other men practise not-flinching.

"He should hear this," Yusuf said. He hadn't planned to.

Narin's eyes came up.

"It's his question," Yusuf said. "*What is it for.* He's the one who keeps asking it." He felt the heat of his own face. "I just mean — it feels wrong, him out there."

Mira's hands moved. Bashir, without looking, voiced it under his breath, the habit they'd all fallen into so she was never the only one who'd missed a thing: *He stays out for a reason.*

"He stays out so the doors aren't his," Narin said. She said it gently. "If I want him, I go to him. That's the deal. He told me on the first night. He said —" she turned the cold glass once "— he said, *I open the road, you say where it goes.* So." She set the glass down. "This one doesn't go to him. He already knows that. Look at him."

Yusuf looked.

The man wasn't waiting to be invited. That was the thing the surveillance vans never managed to capture, the thing the network's clean-handed people would put in a file under *no clear read* — that Jakobus standing apart wasn't a man excluded. It was a man on guard at a door he'd decided, on purpose, not to walk through. He had no business with what a story meant to the people who said it. He'd told them as much, days ago, squatting in front of VA 243's photograph with that cayenne tin balanced on his knee: *I can tell you what a made thing is for. I can't tell you what it means to pray to.*

"All right," Yusuf said, and sat back, and let the tea go cold for good.

---

Narin began the way she'd begun everything — sideways, through somebody else's hands.

"In 1872," she said, "a man named George Smith was in the basement of the British Museum, where some of you have been," and she nodded at the memory of the cold room, the steel shelving, the cradle of grey foam, "going through the tablets that came out of Nineveh. Ashurbanipal's library. Smith taught himself the script. He'd been a banknote engraver — he had the eye for tiny marks. And he's reading along, fragment by fragment, and he hits a line about a ship grounding on a mountain. A man sends out a dove. The dove comes back. He sends a raven. The raven doesn't."

Bashir's head had tipped. Yusuf watched the language-part of him lean toward it like a plant toward a window.

"And the story goes," Narin said, "that George Smith stood up in that basement and started taking his clothes off."

Frik laughed — a single bright bark of it, surprised out of him.

"It's probably exaggerated," Narin said, allowing herself the smallest smile. "He got excited. He ran around the room. He was the first person in two and a half thousand years to read that flood, in the language it was written in, and to understand what he was holding. He gave a lecture. The Prime Minister came. People wept. They thought — some of them — that he'd *proved* the Bible. Found the flood in the dirt."

She let that sit.

"He hadn't found the flood in the Bible," she said. "He'd found one that was older."

The awning held very still. Out on the lane the water bag wept.

"A thousand years older, at least," Narin said. "The version Smith read is woven into Gilgamesh, and the flood that's *inside* Gilgamesh is older than that again — Atrahasis, the one we've been living in all week. A man warned by his god. A boat sealed with pitch. The waters. The birds. The grounding on a mountain. The sacrifice when it's over, and the gods gathering over the smoke like —" she paused, and her mouth did something complicated "— Smith translated it himself: *the gods gathered like flies over the offering.* Because they were starving. Because the flood had killed the people who fed them and they'd nearly starved themselves out of pure rage."

"That's not in Genesis," Naila said. It was the first she'd spoken in an hour. She'd been writing — not words, Yusuf saw, but the *shape* of something, columns, a ledger drawn in pencil on the back of a permit form. "The flies. The starving. None of that's in Genesis."

"No."

"Because Genesis took it out."

"Because Genesis," Narin said, "*argued* with it."

---

This was the place Yusuf had been afraid of all week without naming it.

He could name it now. Sitting under a borrowed awning in the Erbil heat, with the citadel up on its mound behind them holding seven thousand years of people who'd never once needed a spaceship to be remarkable — he could finally name what had been hollow in him since the night the rabbit-hole had opened under his ordinary life and let him fall, gratefully, out of it.

He'd needed the aliens to be real.

Not the way a child needs a story. The way a drowning man needs the thing his hand closes on to be a rope and not a snake. His life before this — the logistics desk, the manifests, the crates with the wrong paperwork that he'd learned not to look inside, the slow knowledge that he'd been a clean pair of hands on a dirty river — that life had been a corridor with no doors. And then the videos, the diagrams, Nibiru's long ellipse, the gold mines, the gods who came down and made us in a lab, and oh, the *relief* of it. Because if that were true, then nothing was small. Then the universe had bothered. Then he, Yusuf, who had spent his good years carrying other men's loot down to the sea, was not an accident in a corridor but a designed thing, wanted, on purpose, by someone who'd crossed the dark to make him.

That was the whole engine of it. He understood that now, watching the water bag weep. The alien story flattered you. It said: *you were wanted.*

And the real one didn't.

"Atrahasis," Narin said, "doesn't start with a flood. It starts with a strike."

She'd turned to a tablet line in her notebook, the transliteration in her own neat hand, and Bashir's lips were already moving over it before she read it.

"The gods used to do the labour themselves," she said. "The lesser gods — the Igigi. Digging the rivers. Hauling the baskets of earth. The Tigris and the Euphrates, dug by hand, by gods, for three thousand six hundred years —" she looked at Naila "— a *sar,* yes, a number, a big round base-sixty number, not an orbit of anything —"

"*The sar is the orbit—*" Frik said, and then his whole face folded with the effort of catching it, and he got the rest out himself, fast, dragging it back by the collar, "*—is a numeral. It's a numeral. Sixty squared. It's a numeral.*" He pressed two fingers hard to his sternum. "Sorry. It's a numeral."

"It's a numeral," Naila agreed, mild, and tapped her pencil twice on her column of sixties, and that was the whole of it — no fuss, no one looking at him, the thing said and corrected and let go, and Frik breathed out.

"For three thousand six hundred years they hauled the baskets," Narin went on, as if nothing had snagged, because to her nothing had. "And then they'd had enough. They burned their tools. They surrounded the house of the chief god in the middle of the night with torches. A strike. A mutiny in heaven. And the gods up in the assembly are *frightened* — that's the astonishing thing, the gods are scared of the work stopping — and they hold a council, and someone says: we'll make a replacement. A thing to carry the baskets so we don't have to."

She turned the page.

"They kill a god to do it. A god who has — the text says — *planning,* intelligence, *ṭēmu.* They kill him, and they mix his blood and his mind into clay, and the womb-goddess pinches off fourteen pieces, and they're left to ripen, and out come people. Made of dead-god's-blood and river-mud. And the very first thing the new people are *for* —"

"To carry the baskets," Yusuf said. His voice came out rougher than he meant.

"To carry the baskets," Narin said. "To bear the gods' toil. So the gods could rest." She looked at him, and there was no cruelty in it at all, none, only a terrible level kindness, the kindness of a person handing you the true weight of a thing because the false one was breaking your back worse. "That's what we were made for, in the oldest story this part of the world ever told about itself. Not to be loved. Not to be wanted. To *work,* so that someone higher up didn't have to."

The lane was quiet. The bag wept.

"And it gets worse," Narin said softly, "or better, depending. Because the people multiply, and they're loud, and the noise of all that human labour and life keeps the chief god awake. So he sends plagues. Then a drought. Then the famine — and there are lines, broken lines, that are almost too much to read, about mothers, about what hunger does in a city. And when none of it quiets us, he sends the flood. Not because we'd sinned. There's no sin in it. We were just — *too many.* Too loud. An infestation in the made world. He floods us the way you'd fumigate a house."

She closed the notebook.

"That," she said, "is the story Sitchin found. The clay. The made workers. The dead god's blood. The rebellion of the ones who were tired of digging. He found the real, ancient, gorgeous, *brutal* thing — and he kept the whole skeleton of it. He just couldn't bear what it was *for.* So he swapped the gods for astronauts, and the baskets of earth for gold mines, and the clay for a splice in a lab. He took the bleakest, bravest answer a working people ever gave to the question *why does my back hurt and why do I serve and why will I die* — and he turned it into a flattering bedtime story about being special."

---

Yusuf had to look away from her. He looked at the citadel.

He thought about the manifests. He thought about a crate he'd signed off on in 2004 with a customs stamp that wasn't real and a Sumerian word inside it he hadn't known then and knew now. He thought about the corridor with no doors and how the alien story had felt like a door and how it had really been a mirror, all along, just a mirror, angled up at the stars so he wouldn't have to see his own hands in it.

"It's smaller," he said. He hadn't meant to speak. "The real one. It makes us — smaller."

Narin didn't rush to comfort him.

It was Bashir who spoke, and Bashir spoke to the clay, the way he always did when the human thing was too near to say to a face.

"There's a pun," Bashir said.

Everyone turned.

"In another piece of it — the Enki and Ninhursag one, the garden, Enki eats the plants he shouldn't, and he gets sick, eight parts of him, eight organs, and the goddess heals each one, and she makes a little goddess for each cure. And for the rib —" his hands had come up, holding the air "— the Sumerian word for rib is *ti.* And the word for *to make live* is also *ti.* So the goddess of the rib is *Nin-ti.* Lady of the Rib. *And* Lady-Who-Makes-Live. The same word. You can't tell them apart. It's a joke, it's a real joke, in Sumerian, four thousand years ago, a healer's joke about how the bone and the life are the same sound —"

He stopped. His eyes were very bright.

"And it's only in Sumerian," he said. "You translate it into Hebrew and the pun dies, the bone and the life come apart, you can't keep them in one word anymore. But it leaves a *fossil.* The woman made from the man's rib. The woman who's the mother of all living. The rib, and the making-alive, in the same person — that's a Sumerian pun that fell into Hebrew and couldn't bring its own joke with it, so the joke turned into a story instead." He laughed, helpless with it. "Eve. *Hawwah.* Mother of all living. Made from a rib. It's a four-thousand-year-old pun you can still feel under the floorboards if you know both tongues. *That's* a decode. That's what a real one feels like. Not a star chart. A joke that outlived the language it was funny in."

Frik made a sound — not a tic this time, Yusuf thought, just a sound a person makes — and put his hand over his mouth.

Mira had been watching all their faces, turning, the way she did, reading the room the way the rest of them read the page. Now her hands started, and this time it was Narin, oddly — Narin who'd been picking up the badly-learned signs all week — who began to voice it, and then stopped, because the sign was one she didn't have. Mira slowed it. Spelled it. Yusuf caught the shapes but not the meaning.

Bashir caught it. "She says," he said carefully, watching Mira's face to be corrected, "*so the man who needed it to be aliens* —" Mira nodded, kept going "— *was reaching for the thing the story already had.* The wonder. He just reached past it. Because the real one —"

Mira finished it herself, slowly, so they'd all be inside the same beat: a sign Yusuf did know, that they'd all learned the first day, the open hand turned to the heart and out again — *give* — and then she touched her own chest, and then she pointed, gently, deliberately, at Yusuf.

*The real one asks more of you.*

Nobody had told her to look at him. She'd just read it off his face, the way she read everything, the thing his mouth wouldn't say — that the let-go was happening right now, under the awning, in the cold tea and the weeping bag, and that it hurt exactly as much as losing a religion, because in a way it was one.

Yusuf put the back of his wrist to his eyes and made a noise that was meant to be a laugh.

"It's a better story," he said, into his wrist. "Isn't it. It's the better one. I just —" He breathed. "I just liked being wanted."

"I know," Narin said. And then, very quietly, because she was the only one of them who'd earned the right to: "Everybody does. That's the whole engine of it. That's why it sells."

---

She let him have a minute. They all did. Edith — who hadn't lifted the camera in two days, who had it in the bag at her feet with the lens cap on, on purpose, a discipline she wore now like a healed bone — reached over and put her hand flat on the table near Yusuf's, not on it, near it, and left it there.

Then Naila said, into the quiet, in her flat clear voice that never decorated anything: "But that's not the answer to his question."

Heads turned.

"Whose," Bashir said.

"Jakobus's. *What is it for.*" Naila tapped the ledger she'd been drawing, the columns of sixties. "We just answered what the *Babylonian* one was for. We were made to carry baskets so the gods could sleep. Fine. That's clear. That's in the clay, you can read it off the object. But Narin —" she turned, the whole of her attention swinging round like a beam "— you said *now.* When he asked. You said it was the one you couldn't read off the clay. So that wasn't the question." She frowned, working it. "The question is what the *re-written* one is for. The one the exiles made. Why bother. You're a slave by a canal, your temple's rubble, your God's furniture is somebody's inventory — Yusuf knows about inventory —" a small dry beat that startled a wet laugh out of him "— and you take the empire's bleakest story about being mud-slaves, and you don't just keep it and you don't just throw it away, you *re-write* it, line by line, you change why the flood comes and you change what we're made of and you change what we're *for.* That's a colossal amount of work. To do, in chains. For nothing you can sell. *What is it for.*"

She set the pencil down.

"That one's not on the object," she said. "I checked. I read the whole shape of it three times. It's not in the clay and it's not in the Hebrew either, not as a sentence. It's only in the people who still say it." She looked at Narin, and her voice didn't soften, because Naila's voice didn't, but something in her face opened a half-inch, which from Naila was an embrace. "So it's yours. You're the only one here who carries it home from the place that conquered them."

Mira was already watching Narin's hands, Narin's mouth, Narin's eyes — reading her before she spoke, the way you read weather coming over a ridge.

And Yusuf, who had just put down the heaviest thing he'd ever carried, found he was leaning in for the next one.

Narin was quiet a long moment. When she spoke it wasn't to any of them, not at first. She was looking out at the lane, at the white light, at the man in it who would never ask her this and would never be able to answer it, who could squat in front of any made thing on the earth and tell you what it was *for* — a basket, a boat, a battery-jar with the wrong wiring, a king kneeling before his god in a little carved seal — and who'd stopped, deliberately, at the one door he couldn't open, and stood his guard there in the heat so it could stay hers.

"The Babylonian flood," Narin said slowly, "comes because we're noise. Because we're too many and too loud and the god can't sleep. We're vermin, and he fumigates."

She turned back to them.

"And the exiles, sitting in that library, *reading that* — in the city that had just done to them exactly what the god did to those people, drowned them, scattered them, smoked them out of their own house — they took the same flood, and they made one change that changes everything." She wasn't lecturing. Her voice had gone very low and even. "In their version, the flood doesn't come because we're loud. It comes because we're *cruel.* Because the earth was *filled with violence.* They put a *reason* in it. A moral reason. They said: the universe is not a landlord with a noise complaint. It's a court. And then — this is the part —" and here, just here, her hand pressed flat to the table, the same gesture Mira had made, *give* "— they wrote in a promise. The bow in the cloud. *Never again.* The god who flooded the world *binds himself.* Puts down his weapon. Hangs it in the sky where everyone can see it, and swears off. The Babylonian gods never swear off anything. They just regret it over the smoke, like flies."

She breathed.

"That's what it's for," she said. "That's what a conquered slave by a foreign canal gets out of re-writing the empire's own myth in the empire's own language. You take their story that says *you are mud, made to serve, and your suffering is just noise* — and you re-author it until it says *you are made in the image, not the labour; your suffering is seen, not ignored; and even the one who could drown you has bound himself never to again.* You don't win your freedom that way. You don't get your temple back. You get to keep your *soul.* You get to walk into exile still able to tell your children that the world has a floor under it. That's worth more than the temple. The temple they can take. *That* they can't carry off in a crate."

Edith's hand had closed, very gently, over Yusuf's.

"It's not proof of anything," Narin said, level, the scalpel never far. "I'm not telling you the bow is real or the flood happened or any of it. I'm a scholar; I can tell you when the texts were written and how and from what. I can't tell you what's true the way you mean true. That's not the clay's job and it's not mine." She looked at each of them. "But you asked what it's *for.* And what a story is for, to the people who carry it home from the place that broke them — that I can tell you. Because my people did exactly this, in exactly that city, and we're still here, saying the words, after the empire that conquered us turned to dust and got sold off in pieces." A breath. "Twice. Sold off twice."

The 2003 of it hung there, unspoken and entire, and nobody touched it.

Out on the lane, the man near the truck reached up and unhooked the water bag from the wing mirror, weighed it in his hand, found it cool, and brought it to his mouth. Drank. Hung it back to keep weeping. He did not look in at them. He didn't need to. He'd asked the only question he had to ask, days ago, and then he'd gone and stood where it could be answered by the right person, and waited, and now it had been, and he couldn't have read a word of it off the clay if he'd stared at it for a hundred years.

Yusuf wiped his face properly this time, with the heel of his hand, and laughed, and it came out clean.

"So the better door," he said. "The human one."

"Bigger room," Naila said, not looking up from her ledger.

Bashir was already turning his pen over, already reaching for the next line of clay, hungry — and through the awning's edge, in the lane, a car they didn't recognise slowed at the top of the rise, idled, and did not come down.

Edith saw it first. Her hand left Yusuf's and went, by old reflex, toward the bag at her feet — and then stopped, and stayed empty, open, ready to ask before it took anything at all.
