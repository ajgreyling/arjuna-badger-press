# Chapter 22 — Real / Debated / Invented

The reading room of the British Library smells of paper and old coffee. Mira has a desk by the window, the kind they let you keep for a week if you fill in the right form, and on it she has built a small city of books and printouts and one battered legal pad. The pad is the point. Everything in this chapter came off that pad, eventually, so we may as well do you the courtesy of showing you the pad.

She has drawn three columns. She drew them on the first day in Baghdad and she has redrawn them, in airports and basements and the back of Jakobus's van, perhaps forty times since. The headings never change.

**REAL. DEBATED. INVENTED.**

It is the most honest thing in the book, and the least dramatic, which is why it lives at the back where you go only if you cared enough to keep turning. Welcome. You cared. Sit down.

---

Here is the column marked **REAL**, and we are going to be boring about it on purpose, because the whole machine only works if you can check it.

The Flood Tablet exists. It is **K.3375**, the eleventh tablet of the standard Babylonian *Gilgamesh*, and you can stand in front of it in Room 55 of the British Museum, where George Smith read it in 1872 and got so excited he reportedly began undressing in the gallery. The flood, the ark, the door sealed with pitch, the birds sent out, the bird that does not return, the sacrifice the gods crowd around "like flies" — it is all there, in Akkadian, on baked clay, and it is older than the Hebrew of Genesis by the better part of a thousand years. That is not fringe. That is the placard. You can photograph the placard.

The *Atrahasis* epic is real, and in it humankind is fashioned from clay mixed with the blood of a slain god, made to carry the gods' labour so the gods can rest — *to bear the toil*, the verb does the work — and then made mortal because they got too loud. That is **British Museum tablets** copied by a scribe named Ku-Aya, who signed and dated his work, which means a specific man's specific hand is the reason you can read it. Real.

The *Enuma Elish* is real, and Tiamat is real, and the Hebrew word *tehom* — "the deep" over which the spirit moves in Genesis 1:2 — is a genuine cognate of *tiamat*, the same old Semitic root for the salt-water deep. Real, and not a copy; the dossier is very firm on the difference, and so is Huda, who out-firmed us all. A shared root is not a photocopy. Two languages remembering the same ocean.

The Babylonian Exile is real. In 587 BCE the Babylonians took Jerusalem apart and walked a defeated hill-tribe's scribal and priestly class down to the very city whose libraries held the older drafts. That deportation, that proximity, that catastrophe-that-became-a-workshop — real, and dated, and in 2 Kings if you want it in the scripture's own voice.

And the demolition of the alien story is real, which we put in the **REAL** column with love and not with a sneer, because being right is allowed to feel good as long as it doesn't make you cruel. *Nibiru* means "crossing-point" and the crossing-point it most often names is Jupiter, which you can see tonight if the sky is clear and you are willing to wait for it; it is never, in any tablet, the home planet of the Anunnaki. *Anunnaki* means, roughly, "the princely offspring" — the seed of the prince, the high god's children. The 3,600 is *šar*, an ordinary base-sixty numeral, the same arithmetic that gave us sixty minutes and a circle of three hundred and sixty degrees, misread as an orbit by a man who wanted an orbit. And the famous "solar-system seal," **VA 243** in Berlin, is a dedication. There is a man's name on it. The man is called Dubsiga. The "sun in the centre" is a star symbol of a kind that appears on hundreds of seals, and the count of the dots does not match the count of the planets, which is the sort of thing you only notice if you bother to count, and Mira bothered to count.

"You always count," Hecklefish says, from the column he is not supposed to be in. "It's your whole thing. Three columns and a fish. Put me in REAL."

We will get to you.

---

The middle column is the hard one. **DEBATED** is where honesty lives, because anyone can be certain in the first column and anyone can be playful in the third, but the middle column is where you have to say *we don't fully know* and mean it without flinching toward woo.

The *fact* of dialogue between the Hebrew primeval history and older Mesopotamian literature is not debated. The *mechanism* is. How, exactly, did the flood story travel? Through the exile, most scholars agree — but how much earlier, through what trade, through which scribal schools, in whose mouth across how many borrowings before anyone wrote a word of it down? That is genuinely open. Reasonable, credentialed people disagree, in journals, with footnotes, and not one of them needs a spaceship to disagree.

Whether the *Atrahasis* "noise" — the clamour of humankind that wakes the irritated god — is the same theological nerve as the corruption that brings Genesis's flood, or only a family resemblance, is debated. The dossier marks it with the little open-circle symbol Mira uses for *this is real scholarship arguing, leave the door open.* She drew a lot of open circles in the middle column. She is proud of that. So are we.

Whether the Eden serpent owes anything to older Mesopotamian motifs, or is its own Hebrew thing — debated. Whether Babel is a satire of a specific ziggurat or of the whole imperial impulse — debated. Naila spent two days in the middle column over a single line break in a Sumerian dispute poem, and at the end of it said, with the flat satisfaction of a woman who has just refused to overstate her case, "I think it's *probably* this. But I won't write *is*." That sentence is the entire ethic of the middle column. *Probably this. I won't write is.*

There is a subtler thing in **DEBATED**, and Huda put it there herself, which is why it gets to stay.

We came to Iraq following the *transmission*. We wanted the genealogy of Genesis, the clean line from clay to scripture, the chain you could hold up and rattle. And Huda Abbas sat us down in her office in Nasiriyah, under the dead air-conditioner, with the tea going cold, and said the thing that goes in the middle column and stays there.

"You want a chain," she said. "A then B then C. The tidy descent. I understand. It is a good story and most of it is even true." She turned a sherd over in her fingers the way Jakobus turns a thing he's reading. "But people did not pass these stories like a relay baton. They lived next to each other for centuries. They traded, intermarried, were conquered and conquered back, told the stories badly in second languages, forgot the names and kept the shape. *Transmission* is your clean word for a thing that was never clean." She set the sherd down. "Write that it happened. You may not write *exactly how*. Nobody may. The man on your screen who says he knows exactly how the aliens did it — he and the man who says he knows exactly how the exile worked are closer cousins than either would like."

Mira wrote it in the middle column. She wrote it in Huda's words, not her own, which is the only reason it earned its place.

---

The third column is short and you already know what's in it.

**INVENTED.** Us. The chase. Mira's hyperlexia is a real wiring and Mira is not a real person; we built her to *be* the way a certain kind of mind reads a room nobody let it stop watching. Omar moves the way ADHD moves and Omar is invented; we gave him the questions because somebody has to be allowed to ask the rude one out loud. Naila reads cuneiform with her fingers, which real epigraphers have genuinely learned to do — the wedges have depth, you can feel a damaged sign your eyes would skip — and Naila herself is a fiction stitched from that real skill and a late diagnosis and a fierce loyalty we wanted in the room. LENS is invented, and the cost LENS sees in every frame is not.

The funding-foundation race is invented in its particulars and bone-true in its premise: there are people, right now, with real money, who want the *objects* and the *narrative* more than they want the truth, and the looting of Iraqi heritage during and after 2003 is, regrettably, the realest thing in this book. Dr. Simon Archer is invented. The belief that history is a possession of the powerful is not, and Archer's worst hour — when he stood in the museum and understood that the genius he'd been packaging belonged to the woman correcting his Akkadian — we invented that too, but only because we have watched real people have it.

Huda Abbas is invented. We want to be very careful here, at the back, where you came to check. Dr. Huda Abbas is not a real Assyriologist. But Iraqi Assyriologists are real, and they are extraordinary, and they have spent careers reclaiming their ancestors' genius from a Western imagination that turned the inventors of the city and the written word into the extras in a film about spacemen. We made Huda so that the book would have someone to out-read the crew on the thing that matters — because in real life that someone exists, in real offices, in Nasiriyah and Baghdad and Mosul, and we owed it to them to put their authority where it belongs: above ours. If Huda reads as wiser than the visiting band, that is not a flourish. That is the correction. The clever outsiders did not crack the natives' mystery. The native was never confused. She was waiting for the camera to point the right way.

Jakobus is invented, and Jakobus is the road, and the things Jakobus reads — the press of a stylus, the way a forged tablet bakes wrong, the weight that tells him a cylinder seal is old — those readings are real craft, and he hands every meaning to someone else, because the man who reads the made thing is not the man who gets to say what it's *for*.

---

So. The tools are on the desk now. You can pull every item in the first column yourself. The tablets have numbers; we gave you the numbers. The placards are public. The journals are in any university library and a great many of them are free online, and we put the better ones in the sources that follow this chapter, which is the part nobody reads and the part that makes it not a campfire story but a thing you can break if it deserves breaking.

That is the offer. Go and break it. We mean that as the highest compliment a book of this kind can pay you. *Check us.*

Mira finishes the pad. She holds it up to the window light to see if the three columns balance, which is a thing she does, weighing the honest middle against the certain edges, and she says the thing she said to Huda's voicemail the night the museum let the case stay where it was.

"The maybe's in the middle column."

It is. It always was. Not in the first column, where the aliens never were — they died in the clay, cleanly, with the dots that don't match counted out one by one. Not in the third column, where we admit we made you a band of misfits to walk you in. The maybe lives in the middle, in the open circles, in *how* a drowned story crossed a border in a defeated man's memory and surfaced a thousand years later in the most-read book on Earth. Nobody knows the exact how. The people who tell you they know the exact how are selling you something, and the something has a planet's name on it, and the planet is Jupiter, and you can see it tonight.

What were the Anunnaki *for*? They were for carrying the work the gods would not. The princely offspring, made of clay and a dead god's blood, made to labour and then to die. A conquered people read that, and recognised themselves in it, and carried it home, and made it theirs — which is the only thing anyone has ever done with a story worth keeping.

"And the fish?" Hecklefish says. "What column does the fish go in?"

You go in all three, obviously. You're real enough to heckle. You're debated by everyone who insists a fish can't talk. And you were invented to make sure nobody in here ever got to preach.

He considers this.

"Best column," he says. "The maybe one."

Yours now. We checked our work. Go check it back.

The pad goes in the bag. The light goes off the desk. The numbers are at the back.
