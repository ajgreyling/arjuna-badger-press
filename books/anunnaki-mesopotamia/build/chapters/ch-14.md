# Chapter 14 — By Word, Not Combat

The light went flat the way Narin had promised it would.

Naila felt it before she saw it — the heat coming off its high white edge and slumping into something the skin could stand, the concrete giving its hoarded sun back to the room a degree at a time. Outside, the call to prayer wound up from somewhere across the rooftops, threaded the unfinished window-holes, and the diesel generator two doors down kept its low animal idle going underneath, and the two sounds did not agree and did not need to. She liked the hour. It had no glare in it. Glare was a kind of noise.

They'd dragged the plastic chairs into a loose ring around the camp lantern. Yusuf had found a second lantern somewhere — Yusuf found things — and set it on the floor, so the light came up from below and made everybody's faces strange and tall-shadowed, like figures pressed into a seal and rolled out across a tablet.

Narin had her laptop open on her knees, the British Museum photographs queued, but she hadn't turned it toward them yet. She was waiting. She had a teacher's instinct for the held breath.

"Tonight," she said, "I'm going to break a rule I made you keep this morning."

Bashir, cross-legged on a folded blanket, looked up. "You said *identical equals lie.*"

"I did. And I meant it." She set the laptop aside on the floor, screen down, so the only thing on offer for the moment was her own face. "This one isn't a resemblance. This one's a fight. And I want you to see the fight before I let you anywhere near the things people get wrong about it."

Naila uncapped her pen. New page. She drew the line down the middle without thinking about it, the way her hand drew lines down the middle of things, and at the top of the left column she wrote *combat.* At the top of the right column she wrote nothing.

The right column was for what the fight was *for.* You don't fill that one early.

---

"Enuma Elish," Bashir said, and the words came out of him the way they always did when a language opened its own door — easier than English, smoother, his mouth glad of the work. "*When on high.* That's the title, which isn't a title, it's just the first two words, because that's how they named things. *Enūma eliš lā nabû šamāmū* — when the heavens above were not yet named."

"Not yet *named,*" Narin repeated, and let it hang. "Hold that. We'll need it later."

Naila wrote *named* in the right column, faint, in pencil, because she wasn't sure yet what it was answering.

"It's Babylon's creation poem," Narin went on. "Seven tablets. Recited at the New Year festival, every spring, out loud, in the Esagila — Marduk's temple, in the city of Babylon. Imagine that. Not a book on a shelf. A thing the king himself processes through the streets to hear, while the river comes up and the fields drink. It is *liturgy.* It is the city telling itself why the city gets to exist."

"And why does the city get to exist?" Yusuf asked. He had his elbows on his knees. He'd gone quiet and forward in the lantern light, the way he did when a story had a shape he hadn't expected, and Naila clocked it — Yusuf leaning in was its own data point, Yusuf who'd come into all this needing the bigness, the spacefarers, the secret kings. He leaned into the dust-version now the same way. She didn't think he'd noticed yet that he was doing it.

"Because Marduk won a war," Narin said.

She picked the laptop back up. Turned it. The first plate came up — not the poem itself, a relief, a thing of muscle and wing, a god striding with the lightning gripped in his fist like a sheaf of reeds, and a great coiling horror underfoot, all jaw and serpent-loop.

"At the start there's only water," Narin said. "Two waters. Apsu — the fresh water, the sweet water, the deep that wells up under the ground. And Tiamat — the salt water, the sea. They mingle. From the mingling come the gods, generation on generation, and the young gods are *loud.* They dance, they make noise, they disturb the old quiet of the deep. Apsu wants to kill them all so he can sleep. The young gods kill him first. And Tiamat — the sea, the mother — rises to avenge her husband."

The relief held the room. Frik had stopped tapping his knee, which for Frik was a kind of held breath of its own.

"She makes an army." Narin's voice didn't perform it; she let the thing be enormous on its own. "Eleven monsters. Serpent-dragons, the scorpion-man, the bull-man, the great lion, demons with venom for blood. She puts a consort over them, a god named Kingu, and she gives Kingu the Tablet of Destinies — fastens it to his chest — and that's the rule of the world made portable. And the young gods are terrified. The high gods, the wise ones, they go out one by one to face her and they come back white. Nobody can stand against the sea."

"And Marduk says he'll do it," Bashir said quietly, because he knew the shape of it.

"Marduk says he'll do it. For a price." Narin's mouth tightened, almost a smile, almost not. "He's young. He's the storm-god, the city-god of Babylon, which a few centuries before this was nobody's idea of a capital. And he says: *I'll fight your monster. But if I win, I'm in charge. Forever. My word fixes fate. You bow.* And they're so frightened they agree. They throw a feast, they get drunk on the strong beer, they test his power by telling him to destroy a constellation and recreate it with a word — and he does — and they hand him everything."

Naila wrote, in the left column, *power = won, not given.* Then she underlined *won* twice, because that was the whole hinge and she could already feel where the right column was going to swing against it.

"So he goes out to the sea," Narin said. "And this is the part. This is the engine of the whole thing." She didn't raise her voice. She lowered it. "He takes the four winds. He makes a net to catch her. He fills his body with flame. He rides the storm-chariot, and he rolls out the lightning, and when Tiamat opens her mouth to swallow him — he drives the evil wind in so she *can't close it.* Her belly distends. And he shoots the arrow in through the open mouth and splits her heart."

The generator coughed, two doors down, and steadied.

"Then he stands on her corpse," Narin said, "and he splits her. Like a fish split for drying. He takes the top half and raises it up and makes the sky, and he sets a bar across it and posts a guard, and he tells the guard: *do not let her waters out.* He takes the bottom half and makes the earth. Her eyes become the sources of the two rivers — the Tigris, the Euphrates. Her breasts become the mountains. He bends her tail up into the sky to make the Milky Way. The whole world, the dome over your head and the ground under your feet, is the carved-open carcass of a murdered goddess, held apart by force, because if the force ever lets go the waters come back together and drown everything."

Nobody spoke. Edith, on the far chair, had her camera in her lap, both hands folded over the body of it, not lifting it, and Naila saw her decide not to lift it, which was a thing Edith was learning to do in front of them like a man learning to set down a drink.

"Creation," Narin said, "is what's left over after a fight. The sky is a wound that won't reopen because somebody's standing on it."

---

"Now." She put the laptop down again. Her voice changed register — not less reverent, *more,* the way a hand gets more careful near the thing it loves. "I'm going to read you something. Bashir, you'll know it before I'm three words in."

"I already do," Bashir said, and there was a thickness in it, because he heard the trap she was springing and it was a beautiful one.

"*In the beginning,*" Narin said, "*God created the heavens and the earth. And the earth was without form, and void; and darkness was upon the face of the deep. And the Spirit of God moved upon the face of the waters. And God said, Let there be light.*"

She let *Let there be* sit in the lantern-light.

"There's the deep," she said. "There's the dark water at the start, no shape to it, waiting. Same furniture. Same problem the Babylonians were chewing on — what do you do about the formless water that was there before everything. And on the second day —" she found it from memory, no screen — "*And God said, Let there be a firmament in the midst of the waters, and let it divide the waters from the waters. And God made the firmament, and divided the waters which were under the firmament from the waters which were above the firmament.*"

She looked up.

"The dome," she said. "Holding back the waters above from the waters below. The exact same cosmic plumbing. The waters of chaos, divided, held apart, a barrier set between them. It is, I promise you, the *same room.* Anyone who tells you the Hebrews didn't know this poem, didn't know this whole way of seeing the sky — they're not being careful."

Yusuf was nodding, slow, and Naila could see the old door in his face, the one he'd come in through — the *aha, it's all connected, it was all one secret thing.* He was a half-second from saying it.

"So they copied it," Yusuf said. Not triumphant. Almost mournful. "It's just Babylon with the serial numbers filed off."

And Narin smiled, and it was the warmest Naila had seen her, because Yusuf had walked exactly into the spot she'd dug.

"No," she said. "Look at *how* the firmament gets made."

---

She stood up. She couldn't help it; she was a teacher and the thing wanted standing for. The lantern threw her shadow up the unfinished wall, huge and rough on the grey block.

"Marduk *butchers a god,*" she said. "He shoots the arrow into Tiamat's open mouth and splits her body and shoves the halves apart with his hands and the dome is her carcass. Combat. Murder. Dismemberment. The sky is held up by an act of war and it stays up because a soldier's posted to keep it up."

She turned, found the page in the air.

"Genesis. *And God said, Let there be a firmament.* And there was. He *talks.* There's no monster. There's no sea-mother. There's no rival to fight, because there's no second power in the room to fight *with.* The deep isn't a goddess. It's just water. It's just dark water with no will of its own, waiting to be told what to do — and it gets *told,* it doesn't get *fought,* and it obeys, because the one doing the telling is the only one there. The waters get divided by a *sentence.*"

Naila's pen had already moved. In the left column: *split by force.* In the right, where she'd been waiting all night to write something true: *divided by word.*

There it was. The lattice clicked its gap shut.

"That's not filing off the serial numbers," Narin said, and now her voice had grief in it as well as fire, and the grief was the part Mira was watching — Naila could see Mira's eyes on Narin's hands, on the set of her jaw, reading the thing Narin wasn't saying yet. "That's taking the conqueror's own poem, the one his soldiers recited over your burning city, and bending every joint of it. They kept the furniture. The deep, the dome, the waters above and below — they kept all of it, because that's the furniture *everyone* in that world had, that's just what the sky was *made of* if you were a human being between the two rivers. But they changed the *verb.* Combat became speech. Theomachy became a single voice in the dark. War became a word."

She sat back down. The shadow came down off the wall.

"And the people," she said, quieter. "Tell them, Bashir. What are people for, in Marduk's version."

---

Bashir had been waiting too. He'd half-said this before, days ago, but he said it whole now, and Naila watched the language carry him.

"After Marduk wins," he said, "the gods are tired. They've been doing the digging — the canals, the irrigation, the whole back-breaking apparatus of keeping Mesopotamia alive, because Mesopotamia does not feed you unless you *break your back* moving water around. And Marduk says, I'll make a thing to take that off you. And they take Kingu — Tiamat's general, the one who held the Tablet of Destinies, the loser, the traitor — and they cut his throat. And from his blood they make people."

"To do the work," Yusuf said. Very low.

"To do the work," Bashir said. "So the gods don't have to dig. *Lullû* — a savage, a labourer. Made from the blood of a defeated rebel god, mixed with — in the older poem, Atrahasis, it's clay — mixed with clay, so that the rebel's blood carries the spark and the clay carries the body. You are dirt and a dead traitor's blood and your job is the irrigation roster. That's what a human being is. That's the *good* news in the poem — congratulations, the gods don't have to dig anymore, because now there's *you.*"

He stopped. Frik had gone very still again and then he wasn't still — his shoulder jerked, his head went sideways and back, and the words came out of him before he could weigh them, clean and flat the way his true things came:

"*Slaves.* Made to be slaves."

He blinked. Nobody laughed. Nobody ever laughed; the crew had unlearned that early. The blurt sat in the lantern-light, exactly right, the thing the poem said and would not soften.

"Slaves," Narin agreed, gently, like she was thanking him. "Made of clay to bear the gods' toil. That's the Mesopotamian answer to *why are we here.* You're here because somebody with more power than you got tired of digging."

She let it lie a moment.

"Now Genesis," she said. "*And God said, Let us make man in our image, after our likeness.* In the *image.* Not as a labour-saving device. Not from a traitor's blood to spare the gods a sore back. In the image — the word they used for the *statue of the king* you'd set up in a province so everyone knew who ruled there. Every single human being, the king's standing-stone. Put down here to *represent.* Same dust — God forms him from the dust of the ground, the clay's still there, they kept the clay — but the clay gets the breath of the only god there is, and gets told to *rule,* not to dig."

She spread her hands.

"That's the argument. That's the whole, furious, brilliant argument a defeated people made with their captor's own toys. *You said we were made from a dead god's blood to spare the masters their labour. We say we were made in the image of the only Master, to stand for him over the whole earth.* You can't make that argument unless you know Babylon's version cold. You have to *love* the enemy's poem to fight it that precisely."

Mira lifted her hands and signed something, slow and careful, and when nobody caught it she said it, in her own flat clear voice that the crew had stopped hearing as flat months ago: "They didn't run from the big story. They climbed inside it and turned it around."

"Yes," Narin said, and looked straight at her, full at her, the way people so rarely did. "Exactly that."

---

And then she did the thing Naila had been waiting for, the thing that was Narin's whole gift, the reason the win in this room was hers and would never be theirs to take: she turned the scalpel on her own side.

"And now," she said, "I'm going to disappoint the people who want this to be neater than it is. Because there's a version of what I just told you that's *too* clean, and the internet loves it, and it's wrong, and if you go out and repeat the wrong version you'll deserve every Assyriologist who corrects you."

She held up one finger.

"You'll hear that *tehom* — the Hebrew word for the deep, 'darkness on the face of the deep' — is just Tiamat with the vowels changed. That Genesis literally *borrowed the goddess's name.* It's all over the place. It sounds amazing. It's the kind of thing that makes you feel like you've cracked something." A breath. "It's not true. *Tehom* and *Tiamat* both come down from a common Semitic root — *t-h-m* — that just means the deep, the abyss, the primeval ocean. They're cousins. They share a great-grandmother. *Tehom* is not a loan of the *name* Tiamat; if the Hebrews had borrowed her name directly the word would have a different shape and it doesn't, it behaves like an ordinary inherited noun, no goddess attached. It's a cognate, not a theft. And the difference *matters,* because the whole point I just made you — that they stripped the personhood off the deep, that they turned a goddess into plain water — *only works* if *tehom* is a word for water and not a smuggled-in deity. The reverent reading and the careful reading are the same reading. The cheap version breaks both."

Second finger.

"And you'll hear: *seven tablets, seven days.* The poem's in seven tablets, creation's in seven days, ooh." She made the *ooh* gently, no cruelty in it. "It's a coincidence people fall in love with. The seventh tablet of Enuma Elish isn't a day of rest — it's fifty hymns of praise piled on Marduk, his fifty names, the council exalting him. It's nothing like a Sabbath. The poem isn't even organised by days. You can want the parallel as hard as you like; it isn't there. Hold the parts that are real *because* they're real, and let the pretty fake ones go, or you hand the whole case to the first person who knows the texts."

Naila wrote, in small letters in the margin between her two columns: *cousins, not theft. 7≠7.* She felt the same plain satisfaction she got from a balanced sum. The over-reach died the same way the aliens died — in the actual words, with the actual root, *t-h-m,* a thing you could go and check.

"So what's left," Yusuf said. He'd stopped leaning forward. He was sitting up now, and there was a kind of vertigo in his face, the man who'd come for the secret-spacefarers finding the floor of the real thing was further down and held more weight. "Strip the cheap stuff out. What's left."

---

Narin closed the laptop. The screen-glow went, and there was only the two lanterns, one high, one low.

"What's left is bulletproof and it's the only thing worth saying," she said. "The Hebrew scriptures — the creation, the flood, Eden, the tower, the law-codes — are in deliberate conversation with older Mesopotamian literature. Older by a thousand years and more. And the channel's not a mystery. It's the Exile. Six hundred years before your calendar starts, Babylon takes Jerusalem apart and marches the educated class — the priests, the scribes, the people who *make texts* — to Babylon and keeps them there for two generations. They live in the city of Marduk. They hear Enuma Elish recited at the New Year while the river comes up. They go home, eventually, and they write — and what they write is shot through, on every page, with the poems of the people who conquered them, *answered.* Not copied. Answered. That's not a fringe theory. That's the first week of any honest seminary. It has a genealogy, and the genealogy runs straight through the worst thing that ever happened to them."

She stopped. Let it be as big and as quiet as it was.

"That's the real thing," she said. "And it's harder to live with than either lie. It's harder than *the Bible fell out of the sky with no fingerprints on it.* And it's a thousand times harder than *aliens did it and it's all just myth anyway, who cares.* Because it asks you to hold two things at once: that this is a made thing, made by human beings at a specific desk in a specific catastrophe — *and* that what they made out of their captivity was a vision of the dignity of every human being that the people who wrote the alien comics couldn't dream up on their best day. Made of clay, yes. But made to *stand,* not to dig."

Outside, the call to prayer had ended; the generator alone now, and far off a dog, and once, the soft particular sound of Jakobus moving the Beast's water bag a hand's width along the rail so it could drip on dry ground instead of mud. He hadn't come in. The room was Narin's and he'd left it hers. Naila found she'd stopped resenting how the man arranged himself around the edges of rooms; he arranged himself the way she arranged the margins of a page, leaving the centre for the count.

She looked down at her two columns. *Combat* and *word.* *Force* and *speech.* *Made to dig* and *made to stand.* The left column full, the right column full now too, and across the bottom of the right one, the pencil thing she'd written hours ago, when Bashir first said it — *named* — *when the heavens were not yet named.*

Marduk *split* the world.

But before any of it — before sky, before earth — the very first verb in the Babylonian poem was the world being *named.* And Genesis ran the same verb the whole way through: God said, and called the light Day, and called the dark Night, *named* and *named* and *named.* Two civilisations, both reaching for the same intuition that to make a thing real you first had to *say* it.

The number sat clean. She liked it. She didn't say it; it wasn't hers to say.

Bashir had gone quiet on his blanket, and Naila recognised the quiet — the polyglot's quiet, the one that meant a language was holding a thing he couldn't put in English without breaking it. He had his lips moving, soundless, working at a word.

"Bashir," Narin said. She'd seen it too. "Where've you gone."

He looked up, and there was a grin starting in him that had nothing of mockery and everything of love.

"There's a better one," he said. "There's a *joke.* Older than all of this. In a poem about a paradise-garden and a god who eats the wrong plants, there's a pun — and it's hiding under one of the most famous verses in your Bible, Narin, and it only works in *one* dead language, and the moment you translate it into Hebrew it dies, and nobody who reads it in English has ever heard it laugh." He shook his head, slow, the way you do at something that's lasted three thousand years and is still, somehow, funny. "The rib. The woman from the rib. Wait till you hear what *rib* used to mean."

Narin's eyebrows went up.

"Tomorrow," she said. "In the cool. When the light's gone flat."

"Tomorrow," Bashir agreed.

And Naila turned the page, and at the top of the fresh one, square and small, she wrote a single Sumerian sign she didn't yet understand, and left the column beside it empty, the way you do when you don't yet know what it's answering.
