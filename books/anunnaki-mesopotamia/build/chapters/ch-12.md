# Chapter 12 — The Seam

The safehouse was a half-finished concrete shell on the edge of a town Yusuf had stopped trying to name, two floors up a stairwell with no rail, and the room they'd taken had a window with no glass and a view of a sodium light and, under the sodium light, a goat. Yusuf had been watching the goat for an hour. The goat had been watching him back. Of the two of them, the goat seemed more at peace.

Below, the Beast sat in the dark with its hood down and its water bag rehung, drinking nothing, weeping nothing, finally. Jakobus was somewhere out by it, a shape that resolved into a man only when the sodium light caught the rim of his glasses and then unresolved again, the way he did, going quiet and grey at the edge of any frame. Edith had her camera in her lap with the cap on. She'd had the cap on for two days. Yusuf had clocked it without saying anything. They all had.

Naila sat on the floor with her back to the good wall — she always found the good wall, the one with two exits in her sightline — and she had Narin's grandmother's notebook open beside her own, not touching it, just open, two pages of two different hands. She'd been doing the thing she did, the soft thing under her breath, numbers, the base-sixty going round and round. *Sixty, six hundred, three thousand six hundred.* The sar. She'd killed the sar a week and a country ago — *it's a numeral, it's a big round Mesopotamian ten, it's not an orbit* — but she still ran it sometimes, the way you press a bruise.

"I keep coming back to the gold," Yusuf said. To the room. To the goat.

Nobody answered for a moment. Bashir was lying flat on the bare floor with his eyes closed, which was how he listened. Mira sat by the window where the light was, because the light was how she heard, and she was watching Yusuf's mouth.

"The gold," Yusuf said again, slower, for Mira, turning so she had his face. "Sitchin's whole thing. The Anunnaki come down, they need gold — for their atmosphere, for their dying planet, whatever — and they're too grand to mine it themselves, so they make us. Workers. Out of an ape, with a bit of their own genes spliced in. Made-to-dig." He laughed, once, with no air behind it. "That's the version I bought. That's the version that got me through about four years of nightshifts at a freight desk."

"It's a good story," Bashir said, eyes still shut.

"It's a *great* story." Yusuf heard his own voice go up and brought it down. "It's got everything. We're special. We're engineered. We matter so much the gods crossed the dark to build us. You ever notice that? The whole thing's a love letter to *us.* We're not nothing. We're a *project.*"

Naila stopped the number under her breath. She looked up.

"That's the cheat," she said.

Yusuf waited. With Naila you waited; the thing came when it was built.

"That's the whole cheat in one sentence." She had the two notebooks open and she put a finger on neither of them, hovering, the way she held a pattern before she'd say it. "Sitchin's story makes us *want it.* It flatters. Designed. Wanted. Important enough to build a star-bridge for." She tipped her head at the dark, at the plain out there under the goat-light, the old floors under the new grass. "But that's not what the clay *says.* I read it again on the truck. Atrahasis. Tablet one." She found it in her own book and turned it to him though he couldn't read a word of it, because that wasn't the point; the point was she was holding the real thing. "The little gods — the Igigi — they're the ones digging. Forty years they dig the Tigris and the Euphrates out by hand, by *order,* and they crack. They burn their tools. They march on the big house at night with torches. A *strike.*" She almost smiled. "Forty centuries before anyone had a word for it. And so the great gods — to break the strike — kill one of their own, a god named We-ila, and they mix his blood and his *spirit* into clay, and the womb-goddess pinches off fourteen lumps, and that's us. That's what we're for." She closed the book. "Toil. We're a strike-breaker. We're made of clay and a murdered god so the gods don't have to dig their own canals."

The room held still. The sodium light buzzed.

"That's *worse,*" Yusuf said. It came out small.

"Yes," Naila said. Not unkindly. "It's much worse."

"I mean—" He scrubbed a hand down his face. "I mean it's *bleaker.* Nobody crossed the dark for us. We weren't a project. We were a *labour solution.* Clay and blood, get to work, you'll die, the gods will sleep better." He surprised himself with the shape of it in his mouth. "That's not a love letter. That's a—"

"A foreman's memo," Bashir said, from the floor, and opened one eye, and there was no cruelty in it.

"A foreman's memo," Yusuf agreed, and something in his chest did a thing he didn't have a clean name for, half a laugh and half the other.

Mira had been reading Yusuf's face the whole way down, and now she signed something to him — slow, because his sign was bad and she was patient — and when he didn't catch it she spelled the last word into the air with her hand, *G-R-I-E-V-I-N-G,* and pointed at him, eyebrows up. A question, not a verdict.

Yusuf looked at the goat.

"Yeah," he said. To Mira, to her face, so she had it. "Yeah. I think I am."

---

It would have been easier, Naila thought, watching him, if the real one were boring. That was the lie the genre lived on — that the choice was *aliens* or *nothing,* magic or homework, the wonder or the dust. As if when you took the spaceship away you were left holding a school-text about beer rations and a hangover. Bashir kept saying it on the truck, the loneliest true thing she knew: *every alien decode is a bad translation.* And the corollary, which was the whole reason she could stand any of this — that a *good* translation didn't leave you with less. It left you with a four-thousand-year-old labour dispute. It left you with a strike.

She did the arithmetic of it almost without meaning to, the way other people hummed. Atrahasis: older than Genesis by — she ran it — over a thousand years on the conservative copies, more on the oldest. Enuma Elish, the Babylon one, where the warrior-god Marduk splits the salt-sea goddess Tiamat like a fish for drying and builds the sky from one half and the ground from the other and then makes mankind from the blood of a *second* murdered god, the rebel Kingu — that one a little later, but the bones of it old, old, the bones of it everywhere. The clay-and-blood. The slain god. The toil. It wasn't one text. It was a *tradition.* You could lay the copies out across centuries and watch the same skeleton get re-dressed for each new empire, and the skeleton was always the same:

*The gods were tired. So they made us to be tired in their place.*

That was the thing the West had taken. Not a tablet. Not even a story, exactly. The *authorship.*

"It's not stealing," she said, out loud, picking up the thread Narin had dropped in the truck two nights ago and left hanging in the dark. "Yusuf, you said it. Narin said it. *Stealing's not big enough.*"

"Then what is it." Bashir sat up. He'd been waiting on this; she could see he had the same itch she did, the one where a word was *almost* there and your whole skin wanted it.

Naila put her two notebooks side by side, the dead language and her grandmother's-equivalent, her own hand.

"There's a man," she said. "Swiss. Von Däniken. He's the one who really lit the fuse, before Sitchin, the sixties — *Chariots of the Gods.* And his actual argument, when you strip the airport bookshop off it, is one sentence. He looks at the Nazca lines, the pyramids, the Sumerian king lists, the canals — all of it, every wonder built by brown and Black and ancient hands — and he says: *they couldn't have done this.*"

The room had gone very quiet now, a different quiet.

"That's it," Naila said. "That's the engine. *These people were too primitive.* The lines are too straight, the stones too big, the maths too good — for *them.* So someone came down and did it. Someone from outside. Someone—" she didn't say it, let it sit, "—from *up.*"

"Whiter," Bashir said softly. Stateless, switched-tongues-at-the-border Bashir, who had crossed more lines than the rest of them put together. "Up is whiter. Up is always whiter."

"It's the oldest move there is," Naila said. "It's the colonial move with the serial numbers filed off. You arrive, you see the city, the city is too good, so you decide the people in it didn't build it — a lost race did, a vanished superior, the *real* architects, who happen to look more like the man holding the notebook. And then the people who actually quarried the stone and cut the canals and *invented writing* —" her voice didn't rise; it went flatter, which was worse, which was the thing she did when it was real, "— they become the squatters. The tenants. The ones who found the keys to a house they could never have made."

She looked at the window, at the plain.

"That's what was done here. Sumer invents the *written word.* The first poem. The first law. The first joke we can still read. And we looked at it and decided they were too primitive to have done it, so we gave it to the sky." She closed both books. "It's not theft. Theft's a thing you can give back. This took the *making* away from the makers. It un-authored a civilisation."

Frik had been quiet in the corner the whole time — Frik went quiet when it mattered, that was the secret nobody knew about Frik, that the blurts came when it *didn't* — and now his head jerked once and his shoulder rose and he said, fast, helpless, in the voice that wasn't choosing it:

"They wrote it *down.*"

It hung there.

"They wrote it down," Frik said again, slower, and this time it was him, riding the back of the tic, claiming it. "That's the — that's the thing nobody — you can't *un-write* it. It's in the clay. It's their *hand* in the clay. You can lie about it but you can't—" his neck pulled, his eyes squeezed, "—you can't get their *hand* off it."

"No," Naila said. "You can't."

And Mira, watching all their faces from the window, in the light, saw Yusuf's do the thing again, the half-and-half, and she signed nothing this time, just held his eye and put her flat hand to her own chest and pressed it there. *I see it.* Yusuf nodded. The goat, outside, ate something off the ground and did not care.

---

Narin came up the stairwell with no rail like she'd been born to it, ducked the lintel, and stood in the doorway with two glasses of tea she'd got from God-knew-where in a half-built town at midnight, the way she always could, the local always could, and she handed one to Naila and one — after a beat that was a decision — to Edith, whose camera-cap had been on for two days.

Edith took the tea like it weighed something.

Narin had heard the last of it from the stairs; you could tell. She didn't perform having heard it. She just leaned in the doorway and let the steam off her glass go up into the sodium light.

"You're close," she said. To all of them, in English, her London English with the Nineveh under it. "You read the pattern. The same flood, the same clay, the same council of gods, the same strike. You read it across the cultures and you saw the skeleton and you named what the aliens did to it." She drank. "That's good. That's real work. Most people who come to my office with the word *Anunnaki* in their mouth can't do half of it."

"But," Bashir said. Quietly. The way you say *but* to someone who's earned it.

"But you're reading it like a crime," Narin said. "Sitchin took it. Von Däniken un-authored it. The looters down there pack it into grain sacks. The doc people up in their offices sell it back to the world with a spaceship glued on. All theft. All loss. The clay goes out the door and the hand goes off it." She set her glass on the windowsill, careful, near Mira so Mira had her mouth. "And that's all true. I've spent twenty years being angry about exactly that. I watched my own museum go out the door in sacks in 2003." She didn't look at Edith when she said it; she very precisely didn't, which was its own kind of looking. "So believe me, I know the inventory of what's been lost."

She picked the glass back up.

"But you've stopped one country too early."

Naila's head came up.

"You all keep saying it went *out,*" Narin said. "Out the door. Into the market. Into the lie. And it did. But that's not the only direction this story ever travelled." She turned the glass in her hand. "You're sitting in the room thinking about where the tablets *go.* I'm asking where the *story* went. Not the looted object. The thing Frik says you can't get the hand off. The flood. The clay. The murdered god. The seven days of toil before the gods rest." A small pause on that last; Naila clocked it, didn't know yet why. "Where did *that* go, after Babylon?"

"It—" Yusuf started, and stopped, because he didn't actually know, and the not-knowing was a clean, cold, exciting thing in him, the first clean thing in days.

"You know what happened to Babylon," Narin said. "Six hundred years before anyone's Christ. The empire comes — and it takes a people, a small people, a people with a god and a temple and not much else, and it marches them off into exile. Out of their hills. Into the great brick city. Into the libraries. Into the *liturgy.* Into the place where every educated man and woman knew Enuma Elish the way you all know — what, song lyrics. By heart. Recited at the new year. *Marduk splits the sea-goddess, builds the sky from her ribs, makes the slaves from a dead god's blood.*" She let it sit. "And the exiles sat in that city for two generations. Hearing it. Knowing it. *Made of clay to bear the gods' toil.*"

The sodium light buzzed. The goat had lain down.

"And then," Narin said, "some of them wrote something."

Naila felt it land before she had the words — felt the pattern *click,* the way it did, two pages of two hands sliding into register. She put her finger down at last, on the dead language and on her grandmother's hand, both, at once.

"Oh," she said. Just that.

"You said it un-authored a civilisation," Narin said to her, gentle, the local out-reading them all and choosing to do it kindly. "I want to show you a civilisation that got *re-authored.* By the people the empire tried to delete. With the empire's own furniture." She finished her tea. "There's a flood story you all know. Older than you think — *no.* Not older. The *Mesopotamian* one is older, by a thousand years and more, that part you've got right, don't let anyone tell you the other way round. But there's a *second* flood, written down by a conquered people with the first one ringing in their ears, and it is *not the same,* and the places it's not the same are the whole point." A flicker of the scholar's fierce joy, the thing the West had aliened out of her ancestors and she was about to hand back. "Different mountain. Different birds. Different boat. Different god — and the *difference of the god* is the argument. They're not copying. They're *answering.*"

"Genesis," Bashir said. The word came out of him soft and enormous.

"Genesis," Narin said. And then, before anyone could fill the room with the wrong thing, before Yusuf's face could fall the wrong way or Edith's hand could go to a camera that would steal it, she put up one finger and held them there. "And I want to be very careful with you now, because the cheap version of where I'm taking you is just as much a theft as Sitchin's. The man who says *aha, the Bible's plagiarism, it's all just stolen myth* — he's doing the exact same move von Däniken did. He's un-authoring it. He's getting the hand off the clay." She looked, for a second, almost tired. "Both of those men think they're the clever one in the room. They're the same man."

"Then what is it," Naila said. The question of the whole book, in three words, in her flat clear voice.

Narin smiled, and it was the smile of someone who had waited a long time for the right people to ask.

"That," she said, "is the question." She stood off the doorframe. "It's not a copy and it's not a coincidence. It's a *conversation.* A conquered people sat in the heart of the empire that ate them, and they took the empire's biggest, oldest, most sacred story — *the gods made you out of mud to be their slaves* — and they wrote it back, line for line, furniture and all, and they changed the verb. Not *made to toil.* Made in the—" she stopped herself, deliberate, a teacher refusing to give the answer the night before the lesson. "No. You'll read it. In the morning. In the light. I'm not handing it to you in a concrete box at midnight like it's contraband. It deserves better than the way the *other* tablets travel."

She looked at the window, at the dark plain, the old floors under the new grass, the same long look she'd given the night the Beast carried them out.

"You wanted to know what was bigger than *stealing,*" she said. "Here it is. Somebody downstairs is packing the flood into a grain sack to sell with a spaceship glued on. And three thousand years ago, somebody who'd lost everything took the same flood and used it to keep their soul. Same story. Two directions." She turned for the stairwell. "One of those is the saddest thing I know. The other one is why I do this."

At the lintel she paused.

"Mira," she said, and faced her full-on, mouth clear in the light, and made the sign — clumsy, learned-for-this, the way they all signed for each other, badly and on purpose — for *thank you.* Because Mira had been watching her face the whole time, reading the thing under the lecture, the grief and the joy braided so tight you couldn't pull one out without the other, and Narin had felt herself being read, and had let it happen.

Mira signed it back. Cleaner. *Thank you.*

Then Narin ducked the lintel and went down the stairwell with no rail, and her footsteps faded, and outside, far down, you could just hear — for a moment, no longer — three notes of something hummed low and steady by a grey shape near the Beast, a metronome for a man no light could hold, and then that faded too.

---

Yusuf sat with the goat-light on him and the cold tea in his hands and the foreman's memo in his chest, and waited for the grief to be the only thing in there.

It wasn't.

That was the part he hadn't seen coming, on all those nightshifts, with the spaceship and the splice and the *we're a project.* He'd thought the rabbit-hole was the door out of an ordinary life, and he'd thought that letting go of the aliens meant the door swung shut and you were back at the freight desk with nothing but dust. He'd grieved it on the truck. He was grieving it now, for real, the way Mira had named.

But there was a draught coming under the other door. He could feel it. *Same story, two directions.* A conquered people who'd taken the empire's own myth and turned it into a way to survive the empire. He didn't have it yet — Narin had pulled her hand back, *in the morning, in the light,* and she was right to, he'd have grabbed at it and broken it — but he could feel the size of the room on the far side. It was bigger than the spaceship. The spaceship had only ever flattered him. This thing didn't flatter anybody. It had been written by people in chains and it was *braver* than anything he'd believed for four years on the nightshift.

Naila had her two notebooks closed in her lap, and her eyes shut, and she was running it under her breath again, but it wasn't the sar this time. It was something new, a count he couldn't follow, the rhythm of a thing that hadn't been read yet and was about to be.

"Bashir," she said, eyes still shut.

"Mm."

"The flood story. The Genesis one. You've read it in—"

"All of them," Bashir said, simply. The polyglot's loneliness and the polyglot's gift, one sentence. "Hebrew. Greek. Syriac. Aramaic. The Sumerian's the only place I have to crawl."

"Tomorrow," Naila said. "In the light. The two of them. Side by side. The mountain and the birds and the boat. Where they're the same and where they—"

"Answer," Bashir said.

"Answer," she agreed.

Frik, in the corner, jerked once and said, very quietly, to no one, riding the tic and then choosing the last of it himself the way he'd learned to in this room and nowhere else on earth:

"Same flood."

Nobody corrected him, because he wasn't wrong, and nobody finished it for him, because he didn't need them to.

Outside, the sodium light buzzed, and the goat slept, and the plain stretched off to a horizon they couldn't see, full of old floors and old names cut into walls so the gods would remember — and three thousand years of a story that had gone two directions out of this same mud, one toward a grain sack, one toward the morning.

In the morning, in the light, they would read the other flood.
