# Chapter 5 — Conveniently Loud

Yusuf knew the room before he knew the city.

He'd never been to Erbil and the city wasn't the room. The room was on his phone, behind a glass face smeared with the courtyard's lamp-oil and the grease of three days of bad falafel, and it was the room he'd worked out of for two years of his life he didn't talk about. Same furniture, even. There was always a long table you could project onto, a coffee machine that cost more than a forklift driver's month, and a slide deck with the word *narrative* in it.

He'd waited until the others were asleep. He sat on the low wall at the edge of the uncle's roof with a glass of tea going cold against his knee, the new-city lights laid out below like a circuit board someone had soldered over the old ground, and he typed the name of Narin's streaming-doc company into a search bar with two thumbs that had stopped being steady about an hour ago.

*Apsû Media.*

Of course. Apsû. The freshwater deep under the world in the old stories, the abyss the gods came out of. Some MBA had paid a consultant for that. They always named themselves after the thing they were strip-mining. He'd worked for a logistics firm once called *Restitution*.

He pulled the corporate site. Slick. Of course it was slick — that was the whole engine. A landing video that opened on a slow drone shot over a desert at dawn, a voice with the gravel sanded off it, *some questions are older than history,* and a montage: a cuneiform tablet rotating in a black void, lit like a watch in a watch advert, the wedges catching highlight as it turned. A man's hands — gloved, white cotton — laying it on velvet. A title card. *THE FIRST CONTACT — coming this autumn.*

Then the part that made the old part of him go very quiet and very awake at the same time.

A press page. A grants page. A *partnerships* page with logos. And buried in the footer, in the legal grey that nobody reads because nobody is supposed to, a line about *cultural-asset facilitation services* and a sister entity registered three jurisdictions away.

He knew that line. He had *written* lines like that line.

He sat back. Below him a generator coughed somewhere and steadied. The cold tea had a skin on it. He didn't drink it.

It was Naila who found him, which surprised him, because Naila didn't generally come looking. She came up the roof stair without announcing herself and stood at the lip of the light with her notebook held against her sternum the way she held it when she'd been thinking too hard to sleep.

"You're doing money," she said. Not a question. She'd clocked his posture from across a courtyard once and named the precise figure he was anxious about within fifty pounds; she read him the way she read a ledger that had been balanced too neatly.

"I'm doing money," Yusuf said.

She came and sat, not close, an arm's length down the wall, which from Naila was an embrace. She set the notebook on her knees. On the open page she'd drawn the VA 243 seal from memory, the figures, the eleven dots, and beside it a column of base-sixty arithmetic, and beside *that*, underlined twice, she'd written *NOT AN ORBIT* in block capitals with the satisfaction of a woman who had personally executed a lie.

"Narin said the men wear good shoes," she said. "I keep trying to fit it to the tablet. It doesn't fit the tablet. The tablet's solved. Dubsiga's the owner, the dots are stars but the arrangement is decorative, the whole thing's a dedication. There's no mystery in the object. So the worry can't be the object." She turned her face to him, that level grey look. "So what's the worry."

"The worry's not what it *says*," Yusuf said. "The worry's where it *goes.*"

She waited. She was very good at waiting; it was one of the few social moves she trusted, and on him, tonight, it worked like a key.

"Okay." He set the phone down between them, screen up, the rotating tablet still turning in its expensive void. "I'm going to tell you how a thing like this actually runs, because I was inside one. Not this one. One like it. You ready?"

"I prefer the real one to the polite one," she said.

"Right. Cradle of writing, yeah? Best clay in the world's been coming out of this ground for four thousand years. Most of it boring. *Gloriously* boring. Three sheep delivered to the temple of Inanna, receipt acknowledged, sealed by Lu-Ningirsu. A school exercise where some kid copied out a proverb forty times and got the third line wrong. Beer rations. So much beer." He could hear something coming alive in his own voice, the part of him that had loved this before he'd learned what he was loving it *for.* "A tablet like that — a real one, properly out of the ground — on the legal market it's worth, what. A few hundred quid? If it's clean. Most aren't clean. Most can't *be* clean, because the only way they get here from there is a chain of hands that all need deniability, and deniability has a price, and the price eats the few hundred quid."

"So a boring true tablet isn't worth moving," Naila said slowly. He watched her start to assemble it.

"A boring true tablet is a *liability,*" Yusuf said. "It's heavy, it's traceable, it's illegal, and it's worth less than the bribe you paid the man at the second border. The math is terrible. I sat in rooms and watched men look at genuine cradle-of-civilisation school texts and go *ugh, paperwork,* like it was a parking ticket." He picked the phone back up, turned it so the tablet caught the light again. "Now. Same tablet. Same beer receipt. Except this time, the man with the good shoes says — out loud, on a podcast, in a doc with a drone shot — *this object may rewrite human history.* This object has *symbols modern science can't explain.* This object proves we were *visited.*"

"It doesn't," Naila said. "It says Lu-Ningirsu got his sheep."

"It says Lu-Ningirsu got his sheep," Yusuf agreed. "But nobody who'll pay for it can read it. And here's the thing you have to actually sit in, because it's ugly: *nobody who could read it will ever be in the room.* You've made the object worth a hundred times more, and you've made it a hundred times *safer* to move, with the exact same sentence. Because now it's not a smuggled artefact. Now it's *evidence.* Now it's a *relic.* Now a rich man in a city I won't name buys it for a number that makes the border bribes look like the tea money, and he buys it *because* of the story, and the story is the thing that means he will never, ever hand it to a woman like Narin who'd take one look and say *Dubsiga, your servant,* and price-crash his miracle back down to beer."

Naila had gone very still in the way she went still when a pattern locked. She picked up her pen. She didn't write anything. She just held it over the page like she needed something in her hand to think with.

"The story is the markup," she said.

"The story is the markup." He'd never heard it put that clean. "You said it better than the slide deck did."

"And the truth—" She stopped. He could see her run it both directions, the way she ran everything both directions before she'd let herself trust it. "The truth is what makes it *cheap* again. The truth is the thing that empties the value out of the crate."

"The truth," Yusuf said, "is what turns the relic back into a parking ticket. So the truth is the enemy. Not because they want the aliens to be real—" and this was the part he'd been turning over on the wall for an hour, the part that had made his hands stop being steady, "—they don't care if the aliens are real. I never met a single one who *believed* it. That's the part nobody gets. They're not cranks. They're not even liars, technically; they never *say* it's true, they say *could it be? Some experts believe,* they keep it a *question.* They need the question loud. A loud question is a market. A loud question keeps the catalogue full of mysteries and empty of beer receipts. And a loud question keeps people like us—" he tipped his head down the roof toward the dark rooms where the others slept "—looking at the wrong tablet."

The generator below them cut out. For a second there was only the new city's hum and, further off and lower, the older sound, the one Yusuf had been half-hearing all night without naming it — water somewhere, a drip, the Beast's canvas bag weeping against the truck's flank in the lane, Jakobus's desert habit of keeping water cool by letting it cry through the cloth. A sound that belonged two thousand miles south and had come up the road anyway, the way the man had, the way the whole crew had.

"There's a worse part," Yusuf said. "You want the worse part?"

"Always," Naila said.

"The loud version's only half the cover." He swiped the doc page closed and the rotating tablet died. The roof got darker. "The other half is *you.* Or — people like you and me before we got here. The skeptic. The smart one in the comments who watches the drone shot and goes, *oh come on, it's all just myth, it's a fairy tale, who cares.* You know what that man is, to a trafficker? He's the *best customer in the world.* Because he's just told everyone it doesn't matter. He's just announced to the room that the clay is worthless mumbo-jumbo not worth protecting. The believer makes the price go up and the skeptic makes the *guarding* go down, and between the two of them they walk a four-thousand-year-old object out the back of a country that's already been bled white, and the only person in the entire transaction who actually *loses* — who loses her ancestors, her language, her dead — is the woman who can read it. Who isn't invited. Who they keep *very* sure isn't invited."

He hadn't meant to say it with that much in it. His voice had thickened on the last of it and he heard the thickening and didn't like it, because the thickening was the part that was about him, and this wasn't supposed to be about him.

Naila was looking at the dark where the city was.

"You sound like you miss it," she said. Flat. Not cruel. Just true, the way she was true, with no padding around it.

He breathed out. The tea was undrinkable. He drank it anyway, cold and skinned and bitter.

"I miss who I got to be when I believed the cover," he said. "Not the second one. The first one. The believer." He turned the empty glass in his hands. "You want to know why I came on this trip? Everyone thinks it's because I read the manifests, I follow the money, I'm useful. And I am. But I came because some part of me still — God, this is embarrassing. Some part of me still *wanted it to be true.* The big version. The visitors. The twelfth planet. I read Sitchin at nineteen on a night shift in a warehouse outside Mosul, moving boxes I wasn't supposed to ask about, and that book — it was a *door,* Naila. It was a door out of the warehouse. It said: you, specifically you, are not an accident of mud and bad luck and a war you didn't start. You were *made.* On purpose. *Wanted.* By something out among the stars that bent down and chose. That's — that's not nothing. To a kid in a warehouse, that's not nothing at all."

Naila wrote something now. One line. He didn't look.

"And then I got good at moving the boxes," he said, "and one day I understood that the men paying me to move them needed me to believe the door so I wouldn't ask what was actually in the crate. And the door I needed to walk through and the lie they needed me asleep behind were the *same door.* That's the part I can't—" He stopped. "They built my way out of the warehouse out of the same lumber they used to rob the warehouse. And I helped."

The water wept against the canvas in the lane. Somewhere a dog. Somewhere, faint, music — Jakobus, awake, almost certainly, the radio in the Beast turned low to something with a steady spine to it, the metronome he kept the dark off with.

"Narin's going to think we're them," Naila said.

"Narin already thinks we're them," Yusuf said. "She'd be an idiot not to, and she's not an idiot, she's the smartest person I've met since I stopped being one of them. She's seen this exact crew before. Western strangers, a camera, a notebook, a fixer who knows too much about logistics—" he laughed, no humour in it "—a *man no one can photograph* — and a beautiful theory about her ancestors. She's been here. The version of us she's met before walked off with the contents of her museum in 2003. In sacks. While the building burned. Edith's got pictures of it. *Edith was there.* You think Narin doesn't know that? You think she didn't clock the camera the first second?"

"Edith didn't take the museum's pictures," Naila said. "Edith took pictures *of* the museum being taken."

"Same lens, far as Narin's concerned, until proven otherwise. The witness and the thief use the same machine. That's Edith's whole problem. That's why she didn't lift the camera tonight." He set the glass down on the wall. "And it's why she was right not to."

Naila was quiet. He could feel her doing the thing, fitting it against the others, the great clicking ledger behind her eyes balancing the trip the way she'd balanced his anxiety to fifty quid. When she spoke again it was slower, and there was an edge in it he didn't expect.

"Apsû Media has a partnerships page," she said.

"You saw that."

"I saw it over your shoulder ten minutes ago." Of course she had. "Logos. One of them I know. A foundation that funds digitisation projects. *Open access. Heritage for everyone.* They put museum collections online. Tablets. Photographs. Catalogue numbers." She turned the notebook toward him in the dark, though he couldn't read it. "I use their database. Everyone does. It's how I checked Dubsiga while Narin was still on the wall — I pulled the seal's record, the publication history, the line that says it's a personal dedication. It's a good database. It's *free.*"

"So?"

"So Narin said something." Naila's voice had gone careful, the way it went when she was holding a pattern she didn't want to be true. "On the wall. She said she's watched stories disappear *from the catalogue between one visit and the next.* The quiet true ones. The ones that say *beer rations.* She said she's stopped believing it's an accident."

The roof was very dark now. Below them the new city pulsed its circuit-board pulse, and under it the old ground held its four thousand years of receipts and proverbs and the names of men who'd wanted to be remembered enough to press them into wet clay before they died, and Yusuf felt the floor of the thing he'd thought he understood tilt very slightly, just enough.

"You're saying," he said slowly, "it's not just that they make the loud ones loud."

"I'm saying I can't prove that anyone makes the quiet ones quiet." Naila said it fast, the disclaimer of a woman who had been burned for pattern-matching past the evidence and would not do it again. "I'm an arithmetic person. I count what's there. I don't count what's missing, because *missing* isn't a number, missing is a story, and stories are where the lying lives." She held the notebook tighter. "But Narin counts it. Narin's spent twenty years watching the catalogue. She says the entries thin out. She says the disproving ones, the ones that price-crash a miracle, those are the ones that go *under review* and don't come back. And she's not an arithmetic person either. She's a *language* person. She reads what got written *and* she reads what got—"

She stopped.

"What got un-written," Yusuf said.

"I don't have a word for it," Naila admitted, and from her that was nearly a confession of helplessness, this woman who had a base-sixty column for everything. "There isn't a number for a tablet that used to be in the catalogue and isn't. There's just a gap. And a gap could be a clerk. A gap could be a server migration. A gap could be nothing." She breathed. "Or a gap could be a person with very good shoes who understands that you don't have to *burn* the truth anymore. You don't need sacks and a fire. You just need to make sure the truth is never the version with the soundtrack, and you need to be patient, and you need to control which door the world walks through."

Yusuf looked at the dead black phone screen with its smear of lamp-oil and his own face faintly ghosted in it.

He thought about the rooms he'd worked in. He thought about how nobody in them had ever once said anything you could put in a court. How they'd never needed to. How the whole genius of it was that no single hand ever did a single thing you could point at — the believer believed for free, the skeptic shrugged for free, the database had a clerk who was probably just overworked, the doc company kept everything a *question,* and somewhere a long way back from all of it, where there were no fingerprints because there were no fingers, the value moved and the clay moved and the only loud thing in the whole arrangement was the one thing that wasn't true.

"I want it to be a clerk," he said.

"I know," Naila said.

"I want it to be a server migration and an overworked clerk and Narin being twenty years tired and seeing a shape in noise, because the other thing is too big and too quiet and I've watched what too-quiet looks like up close, and I would rather it be a clerk."

"I know," Naila said again. And then, after a moment, because she was Naila and she would give him the true thing over the kind thing every time and that was the kindest thing about her: "But you don't think it's a clerk."

Below, in the lane, the canvas water bag wept its slow tears against the Beast's flank, and the low radio kept its steady spine, and out past the parapet the new city's lights ran their bright loud current over the dark of the old, where the receipts slept.

"No," Yusuf said. "I think someone made the loud version of her country. And I think someone keeps it loud. And I think the thing they're keeping loud—" he heard the shape of it as he said it, the awful economy of it, the markup and the cover folded into one perfect sentence "—is a question they don't even *want* answered, because the question's the asset. The answer crashes the price. The answer's a parking ticket. The answer's *Dubsiga, your servant.*"

He thought about the boy on the night shift outside Mosul who'd needed the door so badly. He thought about how the people who'd robbed him had needed the same boy to keep loving the door. And he thought, with a kind of vertigo, that the door had never once been locked from the inside — that it had been held open *for* him, all those years, by men in good shoes who wanted exactly one thing from a kid who needed the stars to have made him on purpose, which was for him to keep his eyes up so they could empty the room.

Naila closed her notebook. She stood. She didn't put a hand on his shoulder, because she didn't do that, but she stood near him a moment longer than her arithmetic required, which was its own grammar.

"Tomorrow," she said, "Narin reads us the real one. The clay version. The one that out-wonders the fiction." She looked down at the lane, at the truck, at the slow-weeping water and the man no light ever quite held. "We should hear it before anyone makes it quiet."

"Yeah," Yusuf said.

She went down the stair. Yusuf stayed on the wall a while longer with the cold dregs and the dead phone and the city laid out below him like a board someone else had wired, and he thought: *if it wasn't aliens, then who, and why did a boy in a warehouse need it so badly that the men robbing him could build his only exit out of it.*

He didn't have the answer. That wasn't his to give. The language wasn't his and the meaning wasn't his and the grief, God knew, was not his — it belonged to the woman who'd gone down the lane in the wrong heels on the wrong gravel, who'd left the oldest roof on earth behind her and told them all, finally, to look the right way.

But the money was his. The grift was his. He'd lived in its rooms and he knew its furniture and he knew, now, sitting on a roof over a city he'd never see again, exactly what shape the hand had to be that could turn a beer receipt into a relic and a relic into a parking ticket with the same loud, patient, fingerprintless sentence.

He just didn't know whose hand it was.

He poured the cold tea over the parapet, into the dark, where it fell without a sound he could hear over the city, and went down to sleep among the others, badly, the way he slept now that he'd stopped believing the door.
