# Foreword

### *by Kelvin F. Girin* †

I have spent the better part of forty years in a room full of clay, and I want to begin by telling you the
single most astonishing thing I know, because almost nobody will let me say it plainly. It is not that the
gods came down from a planet. They didn't. It is this: *the oldest people we can read wrote the first draft
of your Bible, and they wrote it more than a thousand years before there was anyone we would call a Hebrew to
read it.*

That sentence is not fringe. It is not a provocation. It is what the tablets say, and I have held the tablets.
A man named George Smith — a banknote engraver's apprentice who taught himself to read the dead script in his
lunch hours — sat down in the British Museum in 1872 with a broken piece of seventh-century clay, and read a
flood: a man warned by a god, a boat built to a measure, a mountain, a dove sent out and returning, a raven
sent out and gone, a sacrifice, and the gods gathering over the smoke of it *like flies.* He was so undone by
what he was reading that, by the account of the man who knew him, he leapt up and began to take off his
clothes. I have always loved him for that. He had found Noah, sleeping in the clay, eleven centuries older
than Genesis — and he had the decency to be *amazed.*

Now. Here is where almost everyone, on both sides, runs for the exits, and I want to ask you not to. On the
one side stands the believer in the visitors — the gold-mining astronauts, the twelfth planet, the slave-race
bred from apes — and he holds the *wonder* and drops the *rigour*, and reaches past the staggering true thing
for a cheap one from the sky. And I understand the reach, I do; I will be tender with it in a moment. But it
is wrong, and it is wrong in a way you can check in an afternoon. The word he builds his cathedral on,
*Anunnaki*, does not mean "those who from heaven came." It means *the princely offspring* — the high-born
gods, the children of the sky and the earth — and across two thousand years of clay it drifts, quietly, from
the great deciders of fate to the grey judges of the dead, and it is never, not once, hitched to a planet. The
planet he wants, *Nibiru*, is a word that means *crossing* — a ferryman's crossing, a star's crossing of the
meridian — and when it is a planet at all it is Jupiter, and you can walk outside tonight and see it. The
"orbit of three thousand six hundred years" is a *number*, the Sumerian word for thirty-six hundred, the same
round figure their scribes used for the reigns of kings before the Flood. It is arithmetic mistaken for
astronomy. The whole sky-ship comes apart in your hands the moment you read the actual language — which is the
one thing its prophet never did.

On the other side stands the debunker, and he holds the *rigour* and drops the *wonder*, and having dismissed
the aliens he thinks he is finished, and walks away — and in walking away he steps straight past the real
thing, which is far stranger and cuts far closer. Because the gods those people made were not aliens; they
were an *answer* — the first written answer to the oldest questions a working, dying body asks: *why does the
labour hurt, why must we serve, why do we die?* And the answer they wrote down, in the cold dignity of clay,
is that the gods grew tired of their own toil and made us out of river mud and the blood of a god they had
killed, to carry the work for them, and built our dying in from the first day. That is not a comfortable
story. It does not flatter us the way the astronauts do — *designed, wanted, important enough to engineer.* It
is bleaker and it is braver, and it is *ours,* and it is the thing the sky-ship was invented to avoid looking
at.

And then — this is the part that still stops my breath in the gallery at closing time — a small, beaten people
were marched in chains to Babylon, the very city whose libraries held the older draft of the whole human
beginning, and instead of being swallowed they did the most extraordinary thing in the ancient record: they
*took it.* They took the conqueror's flood and his garden and his tower and his murdered-dragon sea, and they
bent every beam of it toward one God and a strange new insistence that the human being is not the gods'
slave-labour but the gods' *image* — and they carried it home and built it into the book half the planet now
lives by. That is not plagiarism, and any fool who tells you it is has understood nothing. It is *survival by
authorship.* It is a people who lost their temple and kept their soul by re-writing the empire's own story
until it served their God instead of Babylon's.

The author of this book is that rarest thing, the one who will stand in the gallery and hold both hands open
at once: *this is exactly as old as the careful men say, and it overturns the order we thought our story ran
in, and I will not flee it into aliens and I will not flee it into a shrug.* He gives the diggers and the
decipherers their full and honest due — he reveres the patient men who proved, in 1857, that they could read
the script at all, by sealing their four translations in four envelopes and finding they *agreed* — and he is
right to, because that blind sealed test is the exact opposite of the prophet's method, which is to choose the
answer first and bend the glyphs until they fit. But he is tender, too, where tenderness is owed: to the
lonely autodidact who needed the gods to be astronauts, and to the faithful who hold the old book as holy, and
he never once mistakes *a text has a history* for *a text is a lie.* Those are not the same sentence. He holds
them apart like glass.

There is a quiet man in here, too, far from his own country, who reads a made thing the way I have spent my
life trying to read the clay — who looks at the most famous "alien" seal in the world and sees, plainly, an
ordinary man kneeling before his god, and asks the only question that has ever opened anything: not *who made
it,* not *is it real,* but *what is it for.* I have watched clever people spend whole careers on the first two
and never think to turn the third, and the third is the key to the whole locked room.

You may close this book certain the old order is overthrown. You may close it sure we know far less than we
pretend, and that someone, somewhere, has been quietly deciding for a long time which of these stories the
rest of us are allowed to hear. If the author has done his work — and he has — you will close it as something
better and harder than either: a person willing to stand in front of a slab of river mud with the beginning of
the world cut into it, and to feel the whole vertigo of where it came from and where it went, and to say *I
don't know,* and to find in those three words not an ending but the oldest feeling we have — the one that very
likely made the gods in the first place.

Go and stand in front of the clay. There is a gallery in Bloomsbury, and there is a cradle in the south of
Iraq, and the people who made the first writing on Earth have living grandchildren who can still read it. Go.
The answer is allowed to stay a maybe. That it is allowed to is the whole gift of this book — and, I think,
the whole of what I have loved about the clay for forty years.

*— Kelvin F. Girin*
