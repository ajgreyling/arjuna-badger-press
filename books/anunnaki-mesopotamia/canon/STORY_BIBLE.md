Premise:  
A neurodiverse, outsider crew travels to Iraq and the British Museum to chase the founding myth of the ancient-astronaut genre: the Anunnaki — the “princely offspring” of Sumer. They follow the trail from the aliens-in-the-clay story, through the real cuneiform texts, to the moment a conquered people found themselves in their conquerors’ story — and carried it home as Genesis. The crew’s chase is a race to read the real residue before a well-funded, plausible-deniability outfit can scoop and erase it.  
Theme (never stated aloud):  
The real wonder isn’t that humans were made by aliens, but that stories themselves can be inherited, subverted, and survive as acts of genius — and that the greatest book on Earth is built partly from the bricks of a conquered world.  
The Why Files register:  
Rabbit-hole curiosity; official story played dead straight, skeptics and hoaxers both given respect; find the one genuine hole; wink; let the reader decide. The fish heckles. Never sneer at scripture, never sneer at the dreamers. Humanity law inviolable.  
Intended reader:  
Curious, skeptical, and wonder-hungry adults and teens who grew up on “aliens built it” shows, but want the rigor and the awe together; those who love a real rabbit-hole and want to see the wonder handed back to its real makers.  
POV:  
The ND crew own the telling (close-third), always in their filter; the local out-reads them on the thing that matters; Jakobus is road, never lead.  
Content bounds:  
Scripture is handled with dignity; aliens never confirmed or sneered at; no omnipotent cabal; no “outsiders crack the natives’ code” — the local always owns the final answer.  
Creative driver:  
The inversion: the genre’s founding myth is a decoy, and the real story is both stranger and more human — and the act of reading it is the act of giving credit back to the written-off.
