# Source Ledger — *The Sky Still Runs the House*

> Research ledger, not publishable prose. Consulted 2026-08-30. The manuscript paraphrases; it does
> not copy. Sources are grouped by the load-bearing joint they support. Where a source is a museum
> catalogue or public-facing explainer, it supports the object or narrow fact named, not a larger
> historiographic conclusion.

## Mesopotamia: omen series, diaries, zodiac, horoscopes

1. Francesca Rochberg, *The Heavenly Writing: Divination, Horoscopy, and Astronomy in Mesopotamian
   Culture* (Cambridge University Press, 2004), especially “Personal Celestial Divination: The
   Babylonian Horoscopes.” Supports the long cuneiform history of celestial divination, the
   “heavenly writing” frame, and the late turn to personal horoscopy.
   <https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511617409.007>
2. A. Leo Oppenheim, *Ancient Mesopotamia: Portrait of a Dead Civilization*, revised ed., “The Arts
   of the Diviner.” Supports the scope and organisation of *Enūma Anu Enlil* and the late dating of
   surviving personal horoscopes.
   <https://isac.uchicago.edu/sites/default/files/uploads/shared/docs/ancient_mesopotamia.pdf>
3. John M. Steele, ed., *The Circulation of Astronomical Knowledge in the Ancient World*, and
   Mathieu Ossendrijver, *Babylonian Mathematical Astronomy: Procedure Texts*. Background for
   periodic methods, tables, and the difference between observation, calculation, and omen reading.
4. Institute for the Study of Ancient Cultures, *Periodicities and Period Relations in Babylonian
   Celestial Sciences*. Supports the systematic attention to lunar cycles and the relationship
   between omen and predictive astronomical corpora.
   <https://isac.uchicago.edu/sites/default/files/uploads/shared/docs/saoc68.pdf>
5. Metropolitan Museum of Art, “Astronomical Calendar with Drawings Showing Signs of the Zodiac,”
   in *The World between Empires*. Supports Babylonian origins of the zodiac and horoscopes, the
   earlier king/state focus of celestial omens, and the use of long observation series for modern
   chronological work.
   <https://resources.metmuseum.org/resources/metpublications/pdf/The_World_between_Empires_Art_and_Identity_in_the_Ancient_Middle_East.pdf>
6. British Museum, clay cuneiform tablet containing a Babylonian horoscope, collection image
   398422001. Object anchor only.
   <https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/image/398422001>
7. International Astronomical Union, Astronomy FAQ. Supports the modern distinction between equal
   zodiac signs and unequal official constellation regions. The manuscript does **not** rely on the
   FAQ’s simplified Ophiuchus origin story.
   <https://www.iau.org/IAU/IAU/Astronomy-FAQs/FAQs.aspx>

## Egypt and the Hellenistic synthesis

8. Metropolitan Museum of Art, “Astronomical Ceiling,” after the tomb of Senenmut. Supports Egyptian
   decans, planet/star lists, and hour/month schemes as visible architectural order.
   <https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/544566>
9. Metropolitan Museum of Art, “Telling Time in Ancient Egypt.” Supports Sopdet/Sirius, Nile-season
   association, and the drift of the 365-day civil calendar.
   <https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/telling-time-in-ancient-egypt>
10. Ptolemy, *Tetrabiblos*, Book I, via Perseus. Primary-source anchor for Ptolemy’s explicit two-part
    division: calculating configurations of celestial bodies, then judging terrestrial changes
    attributed to them.
    <https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A2008.01.0636>
11. Alexander Jones, *A Portable Cosmos: Revealing the Antikythera Mechanism, Scientific Wonder of
    the Ancient World* (Oxford University Press, 2017). Background for the mechanical and calendrical
    culture of Hellenistic astronomy; the manuscript does not claim the mechanism was a horoscope
    machine.

## Planetary week

12. Ilaria Bultrighini, “The seven-day week in the Roman Empire and the Near East,” UCL. Supports
    the parallel biblical and planetary traditions, first-century-BCE emergence in practical use,
    and late-antique standardisation.
    <https://www.ucl.ac.uk/arts-humanities/hebrew-jewish/hjs-research/research-projects-hjs/calendars-late-antiquity-and-middle-ages-standardization-and-fixation/seven-day-week-roman-empire-and-near-east>
13. Ilaria Bultrighini, “Theôn hemerai: Astrology, the Planetary Week, and the Cult of the Seven
    Planets in the Graeco-Roman World.” Supports the planet sequence and planetary-hour account.
    <https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/10115451/1/Bultrighini_Chapter%2010_LATEST%20copy%202.pdf>

## India

14. David Pingree, *Jyotiḥśāstra: Astral and Mathematical Literature* and *The Yavanajātaka of
    Sphujidhvaja*. Background for the synthesis of older Indian calendrical/lunar-mansion practice
    with Hellenistic horoscopy. Dates and transmission claims are kept qualified because parts of
    Pingree’s reconstruction remain debated.
15. Metropolitan Museum of Art, “Pisces (Mina), from a Mandi Astrology Series.” Supports later
    Indian use and visual remaking of zodiacal material associated with the *Yavanajātaka*.
    <https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/75235>
16. Michio Yano, “The first equation table for Mercury in Indian astronomy,” and Kim Plofker,
    *Mathematics in India*. Background for Indian mathematical astronomy as an active local
    tradition, not merely received Greek material.

## China

17. David W. Pankenier, *Astrology and Cosmology in Early China: Conforming Earth to Heaven*
    (Cambridge University Press, 2013). Supports the long connection among astral omenology, art,
    architecture, calendrical science, myth, and political/military decision-making, while noting
    that modern astronomy/astrology categories do not map neatly onto early China.
    <https://www.si.edu/object/astrology-and-cosmology-early-china-conforming-earth-heaven-david-w-pankenier-lehigh-university%3Asiris_sil_1084222>
18. Christopher Cullen, *Astronomy and Mathematics in Ancient China: The Zhou Bi Suan Jing*.
    Supports the distinction between portent reading and calendrical methods, and the state’s need
    for accurate ritual time.
    <https://assets.cambridge.org/97805210/35378/excerpt/9780521035378_excerpt.pdf>
19. Qiao Yang, “Becoming An Astronomer in Late Medieval China,” *Journal of Chinese History* 9
    (2025). Supports the institutional structure, training, and restricted interpretive authority
    of the imperial astronomical bureau.
    <https://doi.org/10.1017/jch.2025.11>
20. Chao Xie, “Bureaucracy from the Bottom Up: Diviners in Qing Local Governance,” *Journal of
    Chinese History* (2025). Supports the later administrative work of local yin-yang officers;
    used as a long-tail institutional comparison, not evidence for ancient conditions.
    <https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-chinese-history/article/bureaucracy-from-the-bottom-up-diviners-in-qing-local-governance/72DAF6E627E595DD186021E2CA821296>

## Maya and Mesoamerican time

21. National Museum of the American Indian, “Living Maya Time: The Calendar System.” Supports the
    Haab, Tzolk’in/Chol Q’ij, Calendar Round, Long Count, and the explicit continuity of living Maya
    calendar keeping.
    <https://maya.nmai.si.edu/calendar/calendar-system>
22. Susan Milbrath, “Maya Astronomical Observations and the Agricultural Cycle in the Postclassic
    Madrid Codex,” *Ancient Mesoamerica* 28 (2017). Supports the integration of Venus/eclipses,
    260-day almanacs, and agriculture.
    <https://doi.org/10.1017/S0956536117000189>
23. Elizabeth M. Brumfiel, “Technologies of Time: Calendrics and Commoners in Postclassic Mexico,”
    *Ancient Mesoamerica* 22 (2011). Supports the 260-day divinatory calendar as more than an elite
    institution.
    <https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/ancient-mesoamerica/article/abs/technologies-of-time-calendrics-and-commoners-in-postclassic-mexico/A30410C4C83ECDC097436499630F5215>
24. Harvey M. Bricker and Victoria Reifler Bricker, *Astronomy in the Maya Codices* (American
    Philosophical Society, 2011). Background for Venus, eclipses, seasons, and codex structure.
    <https://www.si.edu/object/astronomy-maya-codices-harvey-m-bricker-and-victoria-r-bricker%3Asiris_sil_1063950>

## Islamic worlds, medicine, court, and print

25. Stefano Carboni, *Following the Stars: Images of the Zodiac in Islamic Art* (Metropolitan Museum
    of Art, 1997/2013 digital edition). Supports translation, further mathematical development, wide
    social range, court horoscopes, and astral imagery in Islamic art.
    <https://resources.metmuseum.org/resources/metpublications/pdf/Following_the_Stars_Images_of_the_Zodiac_in_Islamic_Art.pdf>
26. National Library of Medicine, “Zodiac Man,” from Johannes de Ketham, *Fasciculo de Medicina*
    (1493). Supports the body/macrocosm diagram and medical timing claim as a historical practice,
    not as medicine.
    <https://www.nlm.nih.gov/hmd/topics/horse/sm-101146662_deKetham_zodiac.html>
27. Lauren Kassell, “Casebooks in Early Modern England: Medicine, Astrology, and Written Records,”
    *Bulletin of the History of Medicine* 88 (2014). Supports the serial written form of astrological
    consultations, university medical curricula, and observation as an epistemic genre.
    <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4335571/>
28. Bernard Capp, *Astrology and the Popular Press: English Almanacs 1500–1800* (Faber, 1979).
    Background for almanacs as mass print, mixtures of calendar, practical information, and
    prognostication.

## Separation, testing, and psychology

29. Sheila J. Rabin, “Pico on Magic and Astrology,” in *Pico della Mirandola: New Essays*. Supports
    astrology’s place in mathematics, philosophy, and medicine curricula and Ptolemy’s two linked
    celestial sciences.
    <https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511619274.008>
30. “Reassessing the Marginalization of Astrology in the Early Modern World,” *Historical Journal*
    (2023). Supports the gradual, uneven separation driven by humanist
    history, institutional change, natural philosophy, and professional boundary-making.
    <https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/historical-journal/article/reassessing-the-marginalization-of-astrology-in-the-early-modern-world/873EA8DCA2DFA2D3FF5F86E7F4CBEB52>
31. Shawn Carlson, “A double-blind test of astrology,” *Nature* 318 (1985), 419–425. Supports the
    result that participating astrologers did not match natal charts to personality inventories
    above chance under the study’s protocol. The manuscript notes the existence of later criticism
    and does not make one experiment carry the entire modern verdict.
    <https://doi.org/10.1038/318419a0>
32. Bertram R. Forer, “The Fallacy of Personal Validation: A Classroom Demonstration of
    Gullibility,” *Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology* 44 (1949), 118–123. Supports the
    identical-profile classroom experiment and the narrower claim that general descriptions can
    feel personally diagnostic.
    <https://doi.org/10.1037/h0059240>
33. Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Science and Pseudo-Science.” Supports the demarcation
    discussion, especially Kuhn’s contrast between astronomical failures that generate research
    puzzles and astrological failures that historically did not revise the core tradition in the
    same way.
    <https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pseudo-science/>
34. NASA Space Place, “What Are Constellations?” Supports the modern scientific distinction and the
    narrow statement that astrological personality/future claims are not based on scientific
    evidence.
    <https://spaceplace.nasa.gov/constellations/en/>
35. NASA/JPL, “Constellations of the Zodiac.” Supports precession and the distinction between equal
    30-degree signs and unequal constellations.
    <https://nightsky.jpl.nasa.gov/documents/850/SolSysStarMaps2.pdf>
36. B. R. D. K. R. van Rooij et al., “Science versus the stars: a double-blind test of the validity
    of the NEO Five-Factor Inventory and computer-generated astrological natal charts,” *Journal of
    General Psychology* 135 (2008). Supports the persistence of Barnum-style acceptance in both
    ostensibly psychological and astrological feedback, and the need not to treat “psychological” as
    an automatic validity stamp.
    <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18649494/>

## Claim-strength audit

- **Secure enough for main prose:** state orientation of early Mesopotamian omens; late Babylonian
  personal horoscopes; twelve equal 30-degree signs; two-source history of the seven-day week;
  Ptolemy’s two-part celestial project; independent Chinese and Mesoamerican systems; astrological
  medicine and almanac print as historical practices; gradual separation; lack of demonstrated
  controlled predictive validity.
- **Use with a qualifier:** exact place where the planetary week was devised; detailed dating and
  translation history of the *Yavanajātaka*; any single route from Babylonian division practices to
  a specific modern coordinate convention; the extent to which a particular court’s observations
  were motivated by divination rather than calendar, ritual, navigation, or prestige.
- **Do not claim:** astrology invented astronomy, the 360-degree circle, the calendar, algorithms,
  psychology, medicine, or the state; the telescope alone ended astrology; all ancient sky systems
  descend from Babylon; cultural survival proves celestial causation; empirical failure makes
  practitioners stupid or their rituals personally meaningless.
