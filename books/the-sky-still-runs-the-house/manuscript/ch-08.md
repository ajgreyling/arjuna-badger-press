# Chapter 8 — India Translates and Remakes

When the zodiac entered India, it did not arrive in an empty sky.

Indian traditions had long joined celestial observation to ritual time. *Jyotisha* — the broad astral
discipline whose name is often translated as the science of lights — included calendrical reckoning
needed to place sacrifices and festivals correctly. Lunar mansions, or *nakshatras*, organised the
Moon’s path against stars. Months, lunar days, intercalation, seasons, and ritual obligations had
their own local histories before a Greek-speaking astrologer ever explained a rising sign.

From the early centuries of the common era, Hellenistic horoscopic material was translated and
adapted in South Asia. Texts associated with *yavanas* — a term connected first with Greeks and later
used more broadly for western foreigners — became part of the record. The *Yavanajātaka*, in the
form known through later transmission, presents zodiacal and natal techniques in Sanskrit. Its exact
dating and textual history have been argued over, which is a good reason to leave them argued over.
The secure larger point is that horoscopy crossed into an existing learned culture and was remade
there.

The imported signs acquired Sanskrit names and local lives. Planetary deities, zodiacal signs,
houses, aspects, and timing methods were organised alongside older lunar and calendrical structures.
Indian authors did not merely preserve a Greek manual until Europe wanted it back. They compared,
extended, criticised, and integrated. By the sixth century, Varāhamihira could draw openly on Greek
astral learning while writing within an Indian scholarly world whose mathematics, calendars,
religion, court culture, and languages changed what the material did.

Translation is structural engineering. A word must find a beam in the receiving language. A rule
must fit local units. A foreign deity’s planet may already have a local divine identity. A zodiac
designed around one seasonal and cultural landscape must operate elsewhere, eventually including
latitudes under a different sky. What survives the crossing is not whatever sounds most poetic. It
is what can be attached.

The result is why “astrology came from Babylon” can be both broadly true about one lineage and
useless as a description of *jyotisha*. Western horoscopy carries Babylonian and Hellenistic ancestry.
Indian astrology absorbed important parts of that lineage. But Indian astral practice is not a
Babylonian relic in Sanskrit clothing. Its older calendrical foundations, later innovations,
religious contexts, regional schools, and continuous social history are not decorative additions to
an original western machine.

This chapter also cannot end in antiquity, because *jyotisha* is a living tradition. People consult
charts for marriage, naming, timing, vocation, ritual, and uncertainty now. Families may treat an
astrological judgement as serious social evidence. Practitioners range from learned specialists to
television performers and software services. A practice can be historically sophisticated,
personally meaningful, socially powerful, commercially exploited, and empirically unsupported in
its predictive claims at the same time. History does not force those facts to queue politely.

Calling it a living tradition imposes an ethical duty. A living practice is not available only as a
colourful exhibit in a western history of science. Criticism must address claims and consequences,
not stage an entire culture as the irrational opposite of modernity. Respect, however, cannot mean
granting medical, marital, or political authority to a method merely because it has endured. Long
survival shows adaptability and embedded value. It does not measure predictive accuracy.

India makes the book’s structural thesis visible because the borrowed and indigenous pieces can be
seen operating together. The equal twelve-sign zodiac is a direct transmitted form. Lunar mansions
belong to a wider and older family of Asian astral timekeeping with contested early relationships.
Mathematical astronomy develops through local work and multiple exchanges. The social authority of
a horoscope belongs to institutions such as family, caste, temple, market, and law, not only to the
chart’s geometry.

One nineteenth-century painting from the Punjab Hills depicts Pisces as two magnificent golden fish
moving through a river full of ordinary life — boats, ablutions, temples, people carrying water. The
zodiacal pair is recognisable across cultures. The river is not Babylonian, Greek, or generic. It is
the receiving world made visible.

The sign crossed a border. The border crossed back through the sign.
