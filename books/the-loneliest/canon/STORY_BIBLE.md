# STORY BIBLE — *The Loneliest People in the World* (working title)

> **A standalone literary novella. Its own world. No connection to The African Gold Trilogy or
> History Before Time — no Jakobus, no gold, no Order, no ancient-aliens, no South Africa setting.**
> The cleanest possible firewall.

## THE FIREWALL (binding — read first)
This book is **inspired-by, never based-on.** It carries one thing and one thing only: an *emotional
truth* about two lonely young people. **Every fact, name, place, era, agency, and reason is invented
and dissolved.** There is no real recruitment, no real handler, no real public figure, no real
operation, no real country anywhere in this book. Anyone reading it cold reads a quiet novel about
loneliness and a choice. **Nothing in it is a record of anything.** This guard is absolute and
overrides every other instinct: if a detail risks pointing at a real person, place, or event, it is
changed until it points only at the fiction.

## What the book IS (the only truths carried — as spirit, not fact)
- A **gifted, lonely teenage boy** whose one real talent is *reading people* — not charm, not
  strength, not confidence: a quiet, almost involuntary ability to feel what a room is feeling, born
  of a childhood spent forecasting a volatile house. He does not yet know it is a gift, or a weapon.
- He is **recruited, young, by older men** who recognise the talent before he does, and given a task
  dressed up as an opportunity: get close to **the daughter of a powerful, feared man**, and bring
  back what she lets slip. The boy barely understands what the information is *for*. It is not
  villainous or dramatic — it is **dull, bureaucratic, about money and resources** — which is part of
  the quiet horror: a child's heart spent on something so grey.
- **The girl is the loneliest person he has ever met.** Her father is so feared that she has *never
  lost a game in her life* — every chess match, every hand of cards, conceded to her by people too
  frightened to win. The few friends allowed near her arrive having been *told what they may and may
  not do.* She has never once been met by a person who saw *her* and not her father's shadow.
- **The boy recognises her.** He went in to use her and instead he *sees himself* — the other child
  who believed they were the only one who couldn't work the world. And the moral compass that was
  somehow already true in him at that age **overrides his task.** He feeds his handlers just enough
  to believe they have it all, and holds back the parts that would harm her. **He protects her world
  instead of wrecking it.** He never fully understands how the information could be used and chooses,
  out of recognition and mercy, not to risk her on it.
- It **ends without resolution, which is the point.** He is ordered never to write or contact her
  again; if he ever passes her by chance he is to look through her like a stranger. The apparatus
  that sent him **changes, falls, vanishes** — the men disappear, never heard from again. He never
  sees her again. *Neither ever knows they may have saved the other, simply by being, for one short
  season, truly seen.* The deepest ache: two people met once, recognised each other, and were never
  permitted to know what it meant.

## The transposition (how the real is dissolved into fiction)
- **Setting:** an **invented small country** (or a deliberately unnamed one), in a deliberately
  blurred late-twentieth-century register — borders that don't map to a real map, a regime that
  isn't a real regime. NOT South Africa. NOT any nameable bloc. The texture is *cold corridors,
  forms in triplicate, a summer programme for promising youths, a lake, a grand frightening house* —
  archetypal, not documentary.
- **The boy:** NOT the author, NOT named Andries, NOT Jakobus. Give him his own name and his own
  small life (an invented town, an invented family). The carried interior: the weather-reading, the
  dyspraxic softness, the certainty that he is alone in a world he can't operate. **Nothing
  biographical that could identify the real.**
- **The men who recruit him:** archetypal, unnamed or invented. **No real mentor figure. No named
  trainer. No "how to eliminate obstacles" instruction rendered operationally** — at most, the boy's
  dawning, frightened *sense* that the people around him are not gentle and that the kindness asked
  of him is a tool. Kept impressionistic, a child's-eye fog, never a manual.
- **The girl:** invented entirely. Her father is "a powerful man," "a feared man" — never a real
  oligarch, never a real industry tied to a real place. The information is "to do with money, and
  land, and things being dug out of the ground far away" — kept vague, grey, and fictional.
- **The reason / the intel:** dull and fictional on purpose. The book is not a thriller about the
  secret. The secret is almost beside the point. The book is about the two children.

## Register & shape
- **Quiet, literary, interior.** Closer to the tenderness of the Cape book than to any thriller.
  Close third on the boy. Spare. The dread is ambient, never plotted; the heart is the recognition.
- **Let the story find its length** (author's call). Likely a **novella (~25–40k)**, but uncaged.
  Word count is a guide, never a floor.
- Machine-tells banned (inherits the de-LLM taboos: no "almost [emotion]" over budget, no
  load-bearing "Not X. Y." reframe, no "something [verb]" placeholder, no weasels, "the way" capped).
- **Theme as effect only, never spoken:** that to truly *see* another person — especially one the
  world only sees through fear — is the rarest mercy, and that two people can save each other and
  never be allowed to know it. Never stated by a character.
