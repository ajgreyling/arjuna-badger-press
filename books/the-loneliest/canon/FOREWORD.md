# Foreword

### *by Megan Hargere* †

The territory I worked in all my life had no maps, because the cartographers refused to admit it existed. It is the country where the secret world and the moral world overlap — where a man is sent to do a small dishonest thing to a particular person and discovers, too late, that the person has become real to him, and that his trade has no procedure for what to do when the target acquires a soul.

This book is set in that country. It is the most exact account of it I have read in many years, and it was written, I am almost annoyed to report, by someone far too young to have earned such knowledge honestly.

What the author has understood — and what the spy novel, my own beloved disreputable form, almost always gets wrong — is that the betrayal that destroys a person is rarely the large one. It is the small one. It is the moment a watcher, sent to use someone, *sees* them instead; and is seen in return; and understands, in the seeing, that he has spent his life being looked at *by* people and has never once, until now, been looked *into.* That recognition is more dangerous than any secret, because you cannot hand it back, and you cannot act on it, and you cannot un-know it, and it ruins you for the ordinary half-light in which most people are content to live.

His boy — for the loneliest person in this book is very young, and that is the knife of it — is sent to get close to a girl in order to reach her father, and goes in as a tradesman of the affections, and comes out having met the only other person on earth who is as alone as he is. The author handles this with a restraint that I, who prized restraint above nearly every virtue, can only call masterly. He never raises his voice. He never tells you what to feel. He simply arranges the facts so that you arrive, on your own, at the unbearable conclusion a single beat before the characters do — which is the entire art, and the cruellest gift a writer can give a reader, and he gives it again and again.

There is a moral seriousness here that has nothing to do with sermonising. The book does not tell you that using people is wrong. It does something far worse: it makes you *feel* the precise weight of a single human being treated as a means, and then it makes you complicit, because you wanted the boy to succeed, and succeeding meant the betrayal, and now you must sit with having wanted it. I spent my career trying to put a reader in exactly that chair. This author seats you in it without your noticing he has pulled it out.

I should say, because it is the truest praise I have, that the book is *sad* in the right way — not the cheap sadness that wallows, but the dry, clean, permanent sadness of a thing that could not have gone otherwise. Two people were seen, once, completely, and were not permitted to know what it meant, and the not-knowing is the whole of the tragedy, and the author has the rare courage not to rescue them from it. There is no consolation here that has not been earned, and very little even then, which is exactly as it should be, because consolation is the one lie this kind of story must never tell.

Read it quietly, and alone, which is the only honest way to read a book about loneliness. And when it is over, sit for a moment before you turn on the lights. You will understand, I think, why a man who spent his life among liars came, at the end, to trust most the writers who could make a small private heartbreak feel like the fall of a kingdom.

This one can. Begin.

*— Megan Hargere*
