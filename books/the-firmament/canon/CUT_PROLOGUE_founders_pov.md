## The Last True Daylight (original founders' prologue)

They worked until the light failed, because the light was the thing they were losing.

All that final afternoon the sky had the wrong colour — a green in it, high up, like a bruise coming
through skin — and the birds had stopped, and the far hills wore a shimmer that was not heat. The
people who understood the machines did not look at the hills. They looked at their hands, and at the
dials, and at each other, and they finished the work.

It was good work. That mattered to them, at the end, more than they could have said. Every seal was set
by someone who had checked it twice; every line of the record was written in a clear hand by someone
who believed a clear hand was a kind of love. They had built a floor broad enough to grow a world on,
and a roof strong enough to hold the sky out, and a cold white ring at the edge of it where the seeds of
everything green were laid down to sleep. They had built a light that would rise and cross and set on a
track, so that their children's children would have days. They had thought of the days. They had thought
of everything they could think of, which was not everything, but was a great deal, and was done in love.

And in a small room off the deep passages, hardened past the hardening of everything else, they had left
a way to reach the others — the far quiet stations that had gone into the ground the same year they had,
each under its own borrowed name, all of them agreed on this one thing: that whatever came, the pieces
would keep a wire between them and could find one another again when it was over. They set the log open
on the desk, the call-signs listed in a clear hand, and they did not know it would be the first thing
their descendants learned to forget.

A woman stood at the last hatch with her daughter's hand in hers and looked out at the ruined green of
the sky, at the last of the real afternoon, and she did not tell the child what it was. She had decided
this already, with the others, in a long argument that had left no one happy: that the children would be
given the world as it *was for them*, and not as it had been for anyone else. A flat broad floor. A roof
above. A ring of ice at the rim, past which was nothing, because past which there would truly be nothing
they could ever reach. It was not a lie. It was the shape of the place they were making. She held to
that. She would hold to it as the hatch came down.

"Is it time?" the child asked.

"Nearly," the woman said.

She let the daughter look a moment longer at the failing afternoon — the last unmade light any of their
line would see for twelve generations — and then she drew her back, gently, and the seal came down with
a sound like a held breath let go, and the outside was gone.

For a moment there was only the dark and the hum.

Then, overhead, along its track, the made sun lit — pale at first, and then warming, and then climbing —
and the child laughed, because it was beautiful, and it was hers, and it was the first morning of the
world.

Behind them, in the record, in a clear and loving hand, the first line was already written. It said that
in the beginning there was the firmament, and the light upon its track, and the ice at the ends of the
earth.

It was all true.

It was only the size of it they got wrong, and the getting-wrong was not theirs. That would come later,
from hands that meant less well, and were less brave, and were afraid of a book they could not close.
