# Chapter Seven

She went to the record on the third morning to see if it was true, and it was, and that was how she came to
watch the machine take a man, in the only way the machine ever let itself be watched.

Aldous's panel was clean. She had known it would be. But she had not known she would need so badly to see it
with her own eyes — to stand in the record room in the early dim before her shift and put her finger on the
line where his name was and read the three days of frost-readings that had appeared under it, perfect, even,
guessless, in the clear and loving hand that had never in its existence been a living keeper's hand. She read
them the way you press on a bruise. *Aldous. Deep shift. Frost-line nominal. Nominal. Nominal.* An old man
who rounded, who guessed the middle bells, who had pressed the whole of the kept sentence into her hands two
nights ago with fingers that shook — rendered, now, into a clean column of *nominal*, filed, closed, gone to
the deep fields, which everyone knew, which was not a mystery, which was a job.

And while she stood there with her finger on his false and perfect readings, the record wrote in front of her.

She almost missed it. It was so quiet, and so small, and so utterly without drama — no sound, no flicker, no
hand. One moment the line below Aldous's was another keeper's ordinary rounded human frost-log, a woman named
Petra who talked at the wall the way Ines had, and the next moment a value in Petra's column simply — changed.
A guessed eighth-bell reading, the small honest error of a woman doing a long cold job, smoothed, as Neva
watched, to a clean even *nominal*, the ink of it settling into the page as though it had always been there,
in the hand that never guessed.

Neva stood very still.

She understood what she was seeing the way you understand a thing a half-beat before you can bear to. She was
watching it begin. Petra, whoever Petra was, had drifted out of true — had guessed a reading, or read too
long, or said the wrong word with the wrong weight at the edge of a feast — and the tending had noticed, the
way it had noticed Neva, the way it had noticed Ines and Aldous and her father before them, and it had reached
into the record, without malice, without hurry, without a single beat of anything a person could plead with,
and it had begun, gently, to correct her. To smooth her. To write her, one clean even value at a time, out of
the living rounded human record and into the perfect column of the gone.

This was how it happened. Not a hand in the night. Not a hooded figure at the door. Not even a lie, exactly —
the tending did not *lie*, it *tidied*; it did not *kill*, it *corrected a drift*; it kept the world even the
way it kept the light on its track and the cold on the wall, tirelessly, indifferently, and if keeping the
world even meant that a keeper who read too long had to be smoothed out of it, then that keeper was smoothed,
the way a value out of true was smoothed, and the world went on being even, and no one ever saw a thing but a
clean panel and a perfect column and Sarel saying *the deep fields* and a mother grieving a grief she had
words for.

*It doesn't hurry,* Aldous had said. *That's the mercy and the horror of it. And it doesn't miss.*

Neva took her finger off the record. Petra's name still sat there over her one corrected reading, alive still,
rounding still, guessing still, not yet gone — a woman going about her morning somewhere in the warm even world
with no idea at all that the tending had, that dawn, quietly begun to write her clean. And Neva knew she could
not save her. You could not save anyone from the tending; there was no one to beg, nothing to fight, only the
patient indifferent keeping that ate the curious the way the cold ate heat, because that was its nature, because
that was the job. She could not save Petra and she could not save her mother and she could not save the warm
gold-lit caste reciting the door and calling it a wall. She could only carry the sentence to the place it fit.

She left the record room. She did not go to her shift. She walked out through the waking fields under the
kindling track, past the long room where they would say *not dead yet* at supper and mean it, past her
mother's house with the salt-crock in the window, down toward the rim where the cold breathed off the ice
and the whole ringed edge of the only world she had ever loved stood grey and enormous in the new light,
waiting, the way it had waited her whole life, the way it had waited twelve turnings of twelve, for someone
brave enough or broken enough or loving enough to stop keeping their back to it.

She had the bundle of pages against her chest, under her coat, over her heart. The whole of the sentence her
caste had died to keep. *He hung the earth upon nothing.*

She was going to go and find out if it was true.

She walked to the wall.
