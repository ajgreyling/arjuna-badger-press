# Chapter Eight

She did not go to the wall that first day, in the end, because there was one thing she had to do first, and
it was harder than the wall.

She had to say goodbye to her mother without saying goodbye.

She went home in the middle of the shift, which she had never done, and her mother was at the board with
the dough, of course, hands busy, and she looked up and saw Neva's face and something crossed her own — her
mother was not a foolish woman, whatever Neva had let herself think these last days — and she said, "What's
happened," not as a question, in the flat voice of a woman who has been braced her whole life for the thing
that finally comes.

"Nothing's happened," Neva said. The first of the lies. She was getting good at them. It frightened her how
good.

"You've got your father's face on," her mother said. "He got that face. Right at the end, he'd come in from
the wall with that exact face and tell me nothing had happened." She turned back to the dough, and worked
it, and her hands were not quite steady, and she did not look up again, because if she did not look up then
the thing did not have to be looked at, and that was how her mother had survived everything: sideways, hands
busy, the truth allowed into the room only if no one made it stand in the light.

Neva understood, watching her, that her mother knew. Not the pages, not the verse, not the round Earth or
the machine or the back of the wall — but the shape of it. That her daughter had her father's face on. That
her daughter had begun to read too long. That there was a kind of going that the deep fields were only the
polite word for, and that it had come for the husband and was coming now for the girl, and that there was
nothing at all a mother could do but work the dough and not look up and let her have, for as long as it
lasted, the mercy of a house where nothing had happened.

So Neva did the cruelest kind thing she would ever do. She sat down at the board, and she floured her hands,
and she helped with the bread, the way she had a thousand times, and she let her mother have the afternoon —
one ordinary gold afternoon in the even light, a mother and a daughter and the dough, nothing happening,
nothing happening at all — because she could not give her the truth without giving her a grief that had no
words and no bottom, and she could give her this instead: a clean last day she would not know was the last,
and afterward a clean grief, a *deep-fields* grief, a grief the caste had words for and hands for and a
place to put. Aldous had been right. It was a gift. It was the last gift she had, and it was made entirely
of a lie, and it was the truest thing she had ever done for the woman who bore her.

"Stay for supper," her mother said, when the bread was in.

"I can't," Neva said. "The deep shift." The last lie. It even had the deep in it, and neither of them said
so, and both of them heard it.

Her mother nodded at the dough. And then — she did look up, once, at the very end, when Neva was at the door,
and her eyes were dry and enormous and full of everything she was not going to say, and she said only, "You
read like your father, girl," which was the nearest a woman like her mother would ever come to *I know, and
I love you, and go* — and Neva said, "I know, Ma," and went out into the even light before either of them
could make the truth stand up in the room, and the door closed, and that was the last of her mother, and it
was clean, and it was a lie, and she would carry both of those things through the wall and out the other
side and for the whole of the rest of her life.

---

She did not go straight to the low store. She went first, alone, to the room she had slept in since she was
small, because a keeper packs for the cold with her hands and packs for the leaving with something else, and
she did not yet know what that something else was.

There was not much to choose from. A narrow bed, made, because she had made it that morning not knowing it
was the last morning she would make it. A shelf her father had built her, uneven at one end the way his hands
had gone uneven near the end, holding the small worn things a life collects and does not think to call
precious until it is choosing among them: a smooth stone from the fields she had carried in a pocket at seven
for no reason she could now remember; a broken pencil torch, hers before it was Ines's, kept because throwing
a thing away felt like a kind of lie about how much you had loved using it; her mother's spare hairpin, taken
once as a child and never returned, and never missed enough to be asked for.

She stood in the middle of the small room with the whole of her life arranged on one shelf and understood,
finally, without argument, what her mother had understood at the board with the dough: that leaving was not
a decision a person made once, cleanly, and then carried out. It was a hundred small robberies of yourself,
committed against your own life, one shelf at a time, and every one of them felt like this — like standing
still in a familiar room while something in your chest quietly tore along a seam you hadn't known was there.

She took nothing from the shelf. That was the terror, when it came, and it came all at once, cresting over
the calm she had built so carefully in the kitchen: not the wall, not the cold, not even the dying, but this
— that whatever she took, she would be lying to it. A stone did not know it was being taken to the end of the
world. A pencil torch would go dark the way pencil torches did, indifferent to what it had witnessed. Nothing
on the shelf could carry the room with it, only stand in for the room, poorly, forever, in some pocket at the
edge of the ice, a small dead reminder of a bed she had not slept a last night in because she had not known it
was the last. She would rather carry the room whole, in her chest, unfinished, than carry a piece of it dead
in her hand.

So she left the shelf as it was. She straightened the blanket she had already straightened. She stood in the
doorway of the room a moment with her hand on the frame at the height her father used to touch it, going out,
without noticing he did it, every morning of her childhood — and she noticed it now, doing it herself, and
understood that some part of a person goes on keeping a door's threshold long after the reason for it is
buried in the deep fields — and then she went out, and did not look back at the room, because the room, unlike
the shelf, unlike the pencil torch, unlike anything a hand could carry, she was allowed to keep exactly as it
was, forever, by the one sure means available to a person leaving a place for good: never looking at it again.

---

She went down to the low store under the fields where the keepers kept the cold-weather gear, and she took
what she would need, and no more, because the pages were the weight that mattered and she would carry them
over her heart. Heavy coat. The frost-lamp. Her father's old wall-iron, the short bright bar for reading the
deep seams of the ice, which she had kept when he went and which no one had ever asked her to give back. She
did not know what was past the wall. Aldous had not known either; no one living knew; that was the whole of
what *past the wall* meant. She packed for cold because the wall was cold, and she packed light because a
person walking into the end of the world does not need much, and she was surprised, kneeling in the low
store, by how steady her hands had become. The shaking was gone. She had shaken over the verse in her
kitchen; she did not shake now. She had gone past the shaking into the still cold place, and she recognized
it, and she was not afraid of it anymore, because she understood at last that it was not despair.

It was her father's place. The place he had lived in, at the end, reading too long, loving the world too
much to leave it and too much to lie to himself about it. She had thought, her whole life, that the still
cold place was where you went to give up. She knelt in the low store with his wall-iron in her hand and
understood that it was the opposite. It was where you went when you had finally stopped being able to look
away — when the looking-away had cost more than the truth would — and from the outside it looked like a
person going cold and quiet and lost, and from the inside it was the clearest and warmest she had ever been,
because for the first time in her life she was not spending any part of herself keeping her back to the wall.

She put the bundle of pages against her chest, under the coat, over her heart. The whole of the sentence.
*He hung the earth upon nothing.* She was going to carry it to the place it fit. She did not know if there
was such a place. She was going to go and find out, because a person who has read half a sentence cannot
live without the rest, and because someone had to, and because everyone she had ever loved was sealed inside
a warm gold lie reciting the door and calling it a wall, and she could not save a single one of them, but
she could do this: she could go through, and read the rest of the sentence out loud on the other side, and
find out whether her caste had died to keep a truth or a madness.

She thought it was a truth. She had thought so since the floor would not stop being a floor and the verse
would not stop being true, both at once, impossible, in her kitchen over the spilled salt.

She was going to go and prove her whole dead line right.

She shouldered the coat, and took up the frost-lamp, and went out of the low store into the last of the
even light, and turned, for the first time in her life on purpose, toward the ice.
