# Chapter Eleven

The rim is long.

That is the first thing the search teaches her. The wall of her world was always one thing, a single grey
fact at the edge of everything, the way a horizon is one thing; and now it is a *distance* — cliff after
breathing cliff of it going away into the dark on either side of her little lamp, and if the founders left
a door, it is one seam in all of that, somewhere, unlit, unmarked, and she has one night to put her hand
on it.

She searches the way her father taught her to read: without wanting. *Wanting bends the hand,* he used to
say, on the working side, schooling his daughter in frost while the light went long — *a keeper who wants
a reading gets the reading she wants. Rule out the wall. A palm-width at a time.* So she rules out the
wall. She walks close in against the ice with her palm an inch off the face of it, reading the cold going
by, palm-width after palm-width, and the cold reads even, even, even — deep and old and grained, the
sleeping cold of the seedbank, mile on mile of *no*.

Twice the wall lies to her.

The first time it is a joint — straight-edged, made, her heart up in her throat for the whole quarter-hour
it takes to read the height of it — and it is a working joint, a place the founders left for the ice to
breathe against itself, the cold flowing over it unbroken, no edge behind the edge. A seam that joins and
does not open. She makes herself say it aloud, like a reading — "joins; doesn't open" — and enters it in
the ledger she is keeping now in her head, the last ledger of her keeping life, and walks on.

The second time it is warmth: a stretch where the grain goes shallow and quick and almost alive, and under
it the labouring note of the big machinery rises close behind the ice — some working heart of the world,
running, tended by nothing living, nothing made for hands. She gives it a wide berth without deciding to,
the way you step around a sleeping thing, and for a long while afterward her palm keeps the memory of it,
a warmth felt through a shut oven door.

The cold takes her hands early and keeps them. She reads bare-handed because there is no other way to
read, and warms the reading hand in her armpit while the other carries the lamp, turn and turn about, her
father's economy; and she trims the lamp low to make the oil last the night, and lower, until the wall is
a pale breathing presence more felt than seen and the little light is only for the reading.

Somewhere in the dead middle of the night, the doubt arrives. It does not shout. It reads even, like the
wall: *there is no door. The founders sealed the leaving the way the tending seals a page. You will walk
the ring of the world palm-width by palm-width and come around at last to your own boot-prints in the
frost — the circle of the earth, closed, no ends to it and no way out of it either.* She stands still with
it a while, her hand in her armpit, her breath smoking in the dark. Then she answers it the only way a
keeper can answer anything: she rules out another palm-width. She had not known, before tonight, that this
is what courage looks like from the inside. It looks like going on reading.

She finds it near what would be the fourth bell of the deep shift, though there are no bells here, only the
cold and the lamp and the labouring dark: a seam.

It is not hidden. That is the thing she will remember. It is not disguised, not sealed against discovery, not
guarded — it is simply *at the ends of the earth*, where no one comes, where no one has come in twelve
generations because the whole world is built and taught so that no one would ever want to. A place needs no
lock when no one can imagine walking to it. The seam runs up the ice in a line too straight to be ice, and
when she brings the lamp close and reads the cold along it with her palm the cold says, plainly, in the grain
of it: *here. A joint. A made edge. Two things meeting that were meant to open.*

She works it with her father's iron. It takes a long time, or no time; she loses the shape of time in the
cold and the labouring hum. She scrapes frost from the seam and the seam becomes a groove and the groove
becomes an edge and behind the edge, when the lamp finds it, is not more ice.

It is metal. A hatch. Riveted, hinged, made — a made door in the made edge of the made world, exactly where
the verse said the ends of the earth would be, and exactly not what the caste has meant by those words for
twelve generations. There is a wheel on it, frost-locked, a keeper's wheel, a thing hands were meant to
turn.

Neva kneels in front of the door at the end of the world and understands that she is about to do the one
thing her whole life, her whole caste, her whole world has been built to make unthinkable — and that she can
think it, easily, now, because she has read the verse, and once you have read the verse the unthinkable is
just the next thing you do.

She sets the lamp down. She takes the wheel in both hands. It is so cold it burns.

She turns it.

---

It does not want to turn. Twelve generations of frost have sealed it; her hands scream with the cold of it;
she braces her boots against the riveted floor of the world and puts her whole body into it, her father's
daughter, the still cold place gone through her like iron, and she turns the wheel that no hand has turned
since the last true daylight, and it gives — a crack, a shriek of ice, a shudder that goes up the whole
breathing cliff of the wall — and it turns, and turns, and stops.

For a moment nothing.

Then the door breathes.

A seam of it opens along the edge, thin as a knife, and through the seam comes — air. Air she has never
breathed. Air that is not scrubbed, not cycled, not even; air that is *cold* in a way the wall's cold is
not, a moving living cold, and it carries a smell into the sealed world that has no name in the sealed world
because the sealed world has never held it — a green smell, a wet smell, a vast and rotting and living smell,
the smell of *outside*, of weather, of a world that grows and dies and grows, and it pours through the knife-
edge of the opening door into twelve generations of even scrubbed air and Neva breathes it in and it is like
being struck.

She pulls the door.

It swings. It is heavy as the whole world and it swings, groaning, shedding frost, and beyond it there is —

darkness. Only darkness. A short dark space, a made space, a between-place, and she understands even in the
reeling shock of it what this is: not the outside, not yet, but the *seal itself*, the space between the
inside of the world and the far side of the wall, an airlock, a threshold, a between. The founders built a
door with a room behind the door. Of course they did. They thought of everything they could think of. They
built even the leaving carefully.

The lamp finds the care of them everywhere in the little space: a rail for a hand to hold, a row of hooks
for whatever hung there once, long since dust. And in the side of the between, low in the metal, another
door — not hers. Sealed, wheel-less, made to stay shut, marked in symbols she cannot read; and the marks
say one thing plainly, in no letters she knows: the founders built more into the flank of their world than
the leaving. She does not try it. She has one night and one direction. She notes it the way she notes a
value — there; unread; kept — and turns to her own way out.

She steps into the between, and the pages are against her heart, and the frost-lamp is in her cold-burned
hand, and behind her is the only world there has ever been — the warm gold caste, the even light, the
Lighting, her mother's dough, *and it was good* — the whole of it, warm and lit and sealed and reciting the
door and calling it a wall, and none of them will ever know she stood here, in the seam of it, with the
outside pouring cold and green through a door twelve generations swore was the end of creation.

Ahead of her is another door. The far side. The true one.

She looks back, once, at the world.

Then she does not look back again.

She crosses the between, and she takes the far wheel in her ruined hands, and she does not hesitate, because
her father hesitated his whole life for love of them and it cost him everything and she will not, she *will
not*, she has come to read the rest of the sentence in the place it fits and the place is on the far side of
this last door — and she turns the wheel, and it gives easier than the first, as if the outside has been
waiting, as if the outside always opens more easily than the in, and the door swings, and the last of the
made dark falls away, and

light.

Not the track. Not the even pale climbing light of the made sun on its rail. *Light* — flooding, sourceless,
everywhere, cold and white and impossibly bright, more light than she has seen in the whole of her life at
once, light with no track and no schedule and no verse to call it up, light that simply *is*, pouring through
the open door onto her upturned freezing face —

and Neva, keeper of the flat world, daughter of a man who read too long, carrier of the whole kept sentence,
steps out of the ends of the earth into the light of the round world, and the door of the between swings
shut behind her, and she is outside, and there is no roof.

There is no roof.

She looks up, and up, and there is no roof at all.
