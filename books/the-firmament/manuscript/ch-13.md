# Chapter Thirteen

She walked until the slope gave onto a height, and from the height she saw the ends of the earth, and there
were none.

She had come up through the wet green all that first day — she understood *day* differently now; the light
did not cross a track, it climbed and stood and slid, and the rain passed and came back and passed, and the
roofless up went from grey to a torn shifting blue and back — and near what her body still called the setting
bells she came out of the trees onto a bare high place of rock and wind, and stopped, because the world fell
away in front of her, and kept falling away, farther than the fields, farther than the whole of the sealed
world, farther than she had known *far* could go.

There was no wall. That was the first thing, and it took her a while to understand that it *was* a thing,
the absence of it. All her life the world had ended in the cold ring, the ice, the rim, the ends of the
earth — the world was a thing with an edge, and you kept your back to the edge, and past the edge was nothing.
And here she stood on the high rock and looked out and the world did not end. It went on. Green and grey and
silver where water lay in it, ridge behind ridge behind ridge, paler and bluer as it went, until the farthest
of it was not land anymore but a kind of haze, a softness, where the ground met the sky — and the ground and
the sky did not meet at a wall. They met at a line. A long, faint, curving line.

*Curving.*

Neva stood very still on the height in the wind.

She had said *the circle of the earth* her whole life. Everyone had. It was in the book; it was the rim, the
edge, the flat round plate of the world with the ice about it — *the circle of the earth*, the caste said,
meaning the disc, meaning the edge you kept your back to. And Ouma Renske had said it at the Lighting looking
*up*, turning a coin to a side she wasn't allowed to show, and Neva had not understood, and she understood now,
on the height, in the wind, looking at the far faint curving line where the round world bent away from her in
both directions at once.

The circle of the earth. Not the edge of a plate. The *curve* of a ball. The same three words — the very same
words, in the very same book, the words her mother had said and her father had said and the eldest had read up
into the close dark firmament every Lighting of her life — the same words meant *this*: not a flat disc with a
rim, but a great round turning world curving away past all seeing, so vast that its edge was not an ending but
a *horizon*, a line that moved when you moved, that you could walk toward forever and never reach, because it
was not the end of the world; it was only as far as the round of the world let you see at once.

She began to laugh. She did not decide to. It came up out of her on the height in the wind, cracked and wild
and wet, the laugh of a person watching a thing she was told her whole life come true in front of her in a way
so much bigger and stranger than the telling that there is nothing to do but laugh — *ons lag*, she thought,
though she did not have the phrase yet, that would come later, from other mouths — she laughed at the circle
of the earth, at Ouma Renske's coin, at the whole warm gold caste reciting the true words in the sealed dark
and never once, in twelve generations, getting to stand on a height in the wind and see what the words had
been *for.*

Because they had not been wrong. That was the thing that put her on her knees again, laughing and weeping both,
on the high rock. The caste had not been wrong. *The circle of the earth* was true — had always been true —
was true right now, curving away in front of her in the failing light. They had said a true thing in the dark
for twelve generations. They had only never been let out to see it come true. The words were not a lie the
redactors had told. The words were a *promise* the redactors had tried to keep them from ever collecting —
cut where they got too big, taught small, folded under the salt — and here she was, the one who carried the
folded pages out to the height, collecting the promise on behalf of all of them, on behalf of her mother
kneading dough under a made sun, on behalf of her father who read too long, on behalf of Aldous whose last
readings were perfect, on behalf of every keeper who ever said *the circle of the earth* meaning a rim and
was speaking, without knowing it, of *this.*

"You were right," she said, into the wind, to all of them, to her dead. "You were right. Ma. You were right.
It's a circle. It's — it's so much bigger than a circle, it's — you were *right.*"

The wind took it. The world curved away. The far line held its long faint bend against the torn blue, and did
not end, and did not end, and did not end.

And Neva knelt on the height with the pages against her heart and understood that she had not walked out of
her faith. She had walked out *into* it — out past the small warm sealed edge of it into the roofless
brimming enormous round of what it had been trying, in its cut and folded and salt-hidden way, to tell them
all along — and that everything the wall and the machine and the frightened redactors had taken from the
caste, every seam-word cut, every keeper smoothed to *nominal*, every daughter who walked into the ice, had
been taken to keep them from exactly this: from standing on a height in the wind and finding out that the
book was true, and the world was round, and the ends of the earth went on forever, and it was good.

It was good. She said it aloud, on the height, the oldest answer, the caste's answer, into the wind and the
curving world.

"And it was good," said Neva, alone, at the ends of the earth that had no end.

And it was.
