# Chapter Fourteen

On the third day she came to the edge of the land, and there she read the last of the sentence, and it was
true.

She had walked east — she had a word for it now; the climbing light came from one way and she had chosen to
walk toward it, which was its own small astonishment, that you could *choose* a direction in a world with no
rim to keep your back to. She had eaten what the pages of her caste's oldest lore and her father's bushcraft
between them let her eat; she had drunk the falling water and the running water; she had slept two nights
under no roof and not fallen into the sky, and had begun, the second night, lying in the wet grass looking up,
to be able to *bear* the up — even to love it — the deep unroofed dark of it pricked all over with lights she
had no name for, thousands of them, cold and high and endlessly far, a firmament that did not come down close
and dark but went *up*, forever, brimming with distant fire. She had wept at that too. She was done being
ashamed of the weeping. It was the only honest size of response to a world this true.

And on the third day the land ran out.

Not at a wall. At a cliff — a real one, a torn edge of rock where the green height simply stopped and the
ground fell away, and beyond it and below it was a vastness of moving grey water going out to the curved far
line, more water than she had known was possible, the sea, though she would not have the word for a while yet;
and she came out to the very lip of it, to the last of the solid ground, and stood at the edge of the round
world with the wind coming up off the water and the pages against her heart, and she took them out.

She had not read them since her kitchen. Aldous had told her not to. *Here it'll only break your heart,* he'd
said, *because here every word of it hangs on nothing you can see. You read it out there. You read it where the
words come true.*

She unwrapped the oiled cloth in the wind, carefully, her cold-scarred hands steady, and she found the page —
the first page, the one from under the salt, soft at its creases from all the hands that had carried it — and
she read it aloud, at the edge of the world, into the wind and the water and the roofless brimming sky, the
whole of the sentence her caste had died to keep, in her own voice, in the place it fit:

*In the beginning was the firmament, and the light upon its track, and the ice at the ends of the earth —*

and she did not stop there, where the caste stopped, where the book stopped, where her whole life the words
had closed and everyone answered *and it was good.* She went on, into the cut part, into the kept part, the
part they had folded small and hidden under the salt:

*— and he stretched out the firmament over the deep like a curtain, and set the waters above it and the waters
below.*

The rain had touched her face on the first day. *The waters above.* She had felt it before she read it. She
read on.

*And he set the circle of the earth turning, and the ends of it went on beyond all seeing.*

She had stood on the height and watched the far line curve and not end. *The circle of the earth. The ends of
it beyond all seeing.* She had walked toward the horizon for three days and never once reached it. She read on,
and her voice was shaking now, not with cold, and she came to the last of it, the deepest cut, the verse the
redactors had not been able to teach small, the verse they had to take out whole because there was no way to
make it fit a floor you could stand on — the keystone, the one that had put her on the ground in her kitchen
over the spilled salt, the one that could not be true and could not be a lie:

*And he hung the earth,* she read, at the edge of the world, in the wind, *upon nothing.*

And she looked down.

She was standing at the lip of the cliff. She had been standing on the solid holding ground her whole life,
the flat broad floor that came up to meet the firmament, the floor she had stamped her foot against in her
kitchen — *the floor is right here, it holds, it stands on the ground* — and she looked down now past the torn
edge of the rock, down the long fall of the cliff to the moving grey water, and out past the water to the
curved line where the round world bent away, and up from the line into the roofless enormous sky, and she
understood, all at once, in her body, the way she had understood the rain, that the ground she stood on did
not stand on anything.

It was a ball. A great round turning ball of rock and water and green, hung in the roofless dark she had slept
under, pricked all over with far cold fire — hung there, turning, with nothing above it and nothing below it
and nothing beside it, nothing holding it, nothing under the rivets, nothing under the plate, nothing at the
bottom of the fall — the whole round world and everything on it, the sea and the cliffs and the green heights
and the sealed warm gold caste still reciting the door in their lit room far behind her, all of it, all of
them, all of her, hung upon *nothing at all.*

*He hung the earth upon nothing.*

And it held.

That was the thing. That was the thing that brought her down to her knees at the very edge of the world with
the page pressed to her chest and her face turned up into the falling sky. It was true — the floor hung on
nothing, the whole world hung on nothing, there was no solid holding ground, there had never been, the verse
was true and the redactors had cut it because it was too true to bear — and *it held.* The nothing held. The
world hung upon nothing and did not fall, had not fallen in all the turning ages, would not fall, held there
by the nothing the way she was held, the way the ground had held her the first morning when she was afraid of
falling up — *whatever held the ground held her too* — and it was nothing, it was nothing at all, and it was
enough, it had always been enough, it had held the whole round brimming world and every soul that ever lived
upon it, upon nothing, and it was good.

"You were right," Neva said, again, for the last time, to Aldous, to her father, to her mother, to the whole
of her dead and kept and folded and salt-hidden line, at the edge of the round world under the roofless sky.
"You kept it. You were right. It hangs upon nothing." Her voice broke and she let it. "And it holds. Oh — it
*holds.*"

The wind came up off the endless water. The earth turned under her, upon nothing, and carried her with it,
and did not let her fall.

She knelt at the ends of the earth that had no end, and held the sentence her people had died to carry to the
place it fit, and wept, and was not afraid; and behind her, twelve generations sealed in a lit warm room recited
the true words into the dark and did not know, would never know, that one of them had carried the rest of the
sentence out to the edge of the world and read it aloud and found that every word of it was true.

She had proved her whole dead line right.

The earth hung upon nothing.

And it was good.
