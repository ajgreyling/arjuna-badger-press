# Chapter Four

The page came to her in a ration tin, folded small, under the salt.

She did not find it for two days. It sat in her kitchen under the salt-crock while she went to the wall
and read her frost and came home and slept, and all that time the thing that would end her world was
waiting in her own house under the thing that seasoned her bread, which she would think about, after —
that the truth had been in the room with her the whole time, quiet, patient, keeping, the way everything
in the Vault was patient and kept.

They were ordinary days, the two days. That was what she could never afterward make anyone understand,
and never tried to: that the last two days of the world as it was were not marked in any way at all.
She took the eighth-bell readings with her father's strictness. She ate two suppers across the lamp from
her mother, and salted the second one from that crock, lifting it and setting it back with her own hand.
She slept well. The world was seamless right up until the seam, and afterward she would find she was
jealous of the woman who had lived those two days — not for what the woman didn't know, but for how
completely she had been able to stand on what she stood on.

She found it when the tin ran low. A square of paper, folded to the size of a thumbnail, gone soft at the
creases with handling — not hers; someone had carried this, folded and unfolded it, many times. She knew
what it was before she opened it. Everyone knew the stories, the way you know the stories of things that
do not happen: that there were pages. That somewhere in the caste there were pages that did not match the
book. That a keeper had once been reassigned — further than the deep fields — for a page like this.

The mending circle told that one, every few turnings, always the same way: a woman, two generations
back, no name — the story had worn its name off the way the count had worn its words — who was given a
page, and read it, and burned it the same hour, and was gone inside the month all the same. The moral
the circle drew was the only moral the circle ever drew: don't touch, don't look, burn without reading —
though the burning hadn't saved her, which nobody seemed to notice was a strange sort of proof for the
rule. And it came to Neva now, standing in her kitchen with the folded thing in her palm, that the story
had another face the circle never turned to the light: that for the story to keep being told, the pages
had to keep being *given.* Two generations of warning, and still the pages moved. Somebody had gone on
folding them small. Somebody had counted the cost of the woman with no name and paid it again, and
again, and slipped one under the salt of a keeper who asked questions.

She sat down at her own table with the salt spilled and did not open it for a long time.

Then she opened it.

It was scripture. That was the first strange thing — it was not heresy, not a curse, not some other book;
it was *the book*, her book, the words shaped the way Sarel shaped them at the rim, the cadence she had in
her body. It began in a hand she almost knew — an old careful keeper's hand, a clear hand, a hand that
believed a clear hand was a kind of love — and it began with a verse she had said all her life:

*In the beginning was the firmament, and the light upon its track —*

and then it went on.

It went on past where the book stopped. Where her whole life the verse closed — *and the ice at the ends
of the earth, and it was good* — this page did not close. It kept going, into words she had never heard,
in the same true cadence, so that they did not read as a lie added on but as a *body the book had been
cut off from*, a sentence her people had been saying half of:

*and he stretched out the firmament over the deep like a curtain, and set the waters above it and the
waters below,*

— waters *above*? there were no waters above; there was the firmament, and past the firmament there was —

*and he hung the earth,* the page said,

*upon nothing.*

Neva stopped.

She read it again. *He hung the earth upon nothing.* She read it a third time, and a fourth, and the words
would not go in, because they could not go in, because they had nowhere to go. She sat in her kitchen on
the broad flat floor of the only world there was, the floor that came up to meet the firmament, the floor
that stood — she stamped her foot, once, without deciding to, like a child — that *stood*, on the ground,
on the deep-down solid holding ground, the way a floor stands, the way everything stands, and the page in
her hand said, in her own scripture, in a clear and loving keeper's hand, that the earth hung upon nothing
at all.

It could not be true. A floor sits on the ground. She knew this the way she knew the wall was cold. You
could not hang a *world* on nothing; nothing was nothing; nothing did not hold. The verse was wrong. The
page was wrong, some madman's addition, and she should burn it in the ration-fire and forget it and be
middling and live.

But it was not wrong in the way a lie is wrong. That was the horror of it, sitting there. A lie she could
have burned. This did not read like a lie. It read like the *rest* of something true — it had the weight
of the true cadence, it fit her book the way a torn edge fits its other half, and the very thing that made
it impossible was the thing that made it impossible to dismiss: *why would anyone add this?* A liar adds a
comfort. A liar adds a heaven, a reward, a softer edge. No liar adds a verse that takes the ground out from
under the whole world and hangs it, sickeningly, over *nothing.* Nobody invents a thing that frightening.
You only *keep* a thing that frightening. You only fold it small and carry it for years and slip it under
the salt in a stranger's tin because you cannot bear to be the last one holding it.

She understood, sitting there — not the truth of it, that would take the wall and the breach and the whole
of the round Earth to understand — but the *shape* of it. That somewhere the book had been cut. That her
whole caste had been saying half a sentence into the dark for twelve turnings of twelve, *and it was good,*
and stopping there, at *good,* at the ice, at the ends of the earth — and that the sentence went *on*, and
that someone had cut it exactly where it started to say a thing too big for the world to hold, and that the
people who kept the pages had been trying, quietly, at the cost of everything, to give the rest of the
sentence back.

*He hung the earth upon nothing.*

"No," Neva said, aloud, to her empty kitchen, to the salt, to the flat unshaking floor. "No, that's — the
floor is *right here.*"

And the floor was right there. And it held. And she could not, sitting on it, reading the verse for the
tenth time with her hands beginning to shake, make herself believe the floor and the verse both — and she
found, to her terror, that she could not make herself disbelieve the verse either, because it was written
in the hand of someone who had loved the book enough to die for the whole of it, and because it was the
kind of true you do not invent, and because, underneath everything, in the place her father must have
stood at the wall reading too long, some small cold part of her had already gone very quiet and very still,
the way you go still when a thing you have refused to know your whole life clears its throat, at last, and
says: *you knew. You always knew there was a back to the wall. Now you know what it's for.*

She did not burn the page.

She folded it, small, along its soft old creases, the way it had been folded when it came to her, and she
held it in her closed hand a long time in the dark of her kitchen. Later — that night, by lamplight, with
her mother asleep — she unpicked the bottom edge of the name-tape on her overalls, her own tape, the one
sewn over her father's, and slid the folded page flat beneath the two names, and closed the edge with a
keeper's tack — the three loose stitches you could slip with a thumbnail and close again, the mending
you did at the wall when there was no time to do it properly. A keeper's coat was searched by nobody,
because nothing was ever searched, because there was nothing to find; but that was not why she put it
there and she knew it. She put it where it would ride over her heart under her father's name, and where,
every morning of dressing in the dark, she would feel the small stiffness of it against her thumb and
know the sentence was still whole.

And somewhere in that lamplit quiet, without any
words for it, without the wall, without the breach, without a single one of the things that were still to
come, Neva stopped being a keeper of the world as it was, and became — quietly, terribly, alone, over a
spilled crock of salt — the thing her father had been before her.

Someone who had heard the wall, and could not stop listening.
