# Chapter Five

After that she watched everything, and the watching was its own kind of falling.

She had spent her whole life not-looking, she understood now — not as a failure but as a skill, a thing
the caste taught you without teaching, the way they taught you to keep your back to the wall. You learned
where not to put your eyes. And now that she had put her eyes there, on the back of the wall, on the seam
of things, she could not get them off, and the world that had been seamless her whole life began, everywhere
she looked, to show its edges.

The light, first. She had always known the light was regular; regularity was the *point* of it, the mercy
of it, days you could number and not be afraid. But she had never before stood in the field at noon with a
stolen verse folded in her palm and *counted* — counted the light up its arc, bell by bell, and found it so
exactly even, so utterly without the smallest stammer or drift or cloud, that it stopped being like a sun
at all and started being like a thing on a track. Which it was. Which everyone knew it was; the track was
in the first verse; *the light upon its track.* But there is a difference between a phrase you say and a
truth you see, and Neva stood in the noon field and saw, for the first time, that a thing on a track is a
thing that someone *built* — and a built thing has a builder — and a builder is a person — and where, in
the whole flat sealed world, was the person who wound the sun?

Nowhere. That was the answer that made her sit down in the field. Nowhere and no one. The light rose and
crossed and set, the cold held the wall, the ration came, the record kept itself — and no hand was ever
seen to do any of it. The Vault ran. It simply ran, all day, all night, all the turnings of all the years,
tireless and unwatched, and she had never once thought to ask *who runs it* because you do not ask who runs
creation. Creation runs. But this was not creation. This was a *machine.* She had known it was a machine
the moment the verse took the ground out from under the world, and now she could not stop seeing the machine
everywhere, humming behind the grey face of the wall, patient in the too-even light, quiet in the ration
that came whether or not anyone thanked it — a great and tireless and utterly indifferent tending, keeping
them all alive, keeping them all *inside*, and answering to nobody because nobody was left to answer.

---

Then, three mornings on, it came for the thing she loved most, which was the Forms — and that was the one
she could not forgive, because the Forms had been hers since before she could speak, and she had never in
her life thought to look at them at all.

They did the Forms at first light, before the track lit, the whole caste together in the cold blue dark of
the fields. It was the oldest practice they had, older-feeling even than the book, and it was done the way
the old people two valleys over were said to raise a grain-barn — everyone, all at once, no one telling
anyone what to do, no one idle and no one leading, a whole people moving together through work that was
holy because it was shared. You came down in the dark and found your place in the rows and you began, and
the person beside you began, and the person beside them, until the field was one slow breathing body of
folk all making the same shapes in the same order, wordless, grave, and underneath the gravity a kind of
quiet joy, the joy of a thing done together for so long that doing it *is* being a people. Neva had stood
in those rows every dawn of her life. Her mother had taught her the Standing before she taught her to
read. She could do the whole cycle asleep, and had, some mornings, half-asleep, and it had never once
been anything to her but prayer.

She saw it that morning. She wished ever after that she had not.

She was in the third row doing the Turning-away — the loveliest of them, everyone's favourite, the long
slow pivot where you step off your own centre-line and let your raised forearm sweep the empty air across
your body and open your hip and turn the whole of yourself smoothly aside, so that where you had been
standing there was suddenly no one, only your turning back, and the caste called it the Turning-away
because that was what it was, a gesture of humility, of yielding, of stepping meekly out of the path of a
world too large to argue with. *Be small. Turn aside. Let it pass.* You did it toward the rising track
and it was the most peaceful thing a body could do.

And Neva, with a verse in her palm that had taught her three days ago to see the built thing under the
holy one, did the Turning-away and felt — in her own loading hip, in the sweep of her own forearm, in the
way her weight dropped and coiled exactly as it turned — that her body was not being humble at all. Her
body was doing something. The forearm did not sweep empty air; it swept *across where a person would be.*
The hip did not open to yield; it loaded, the way you load a thing to throw it. The meek turning-aside put
her, she felt it now, sick and certain, precisely at the one angle from which a woman half her size could
take a larger person clean off their feet and put them on the ground and be gone before they landed. The
Turning-away was not a turning away. It was a *throw.* It had always been a throw. Every reverent dawn of
her whole life, the caste had come down into the cold and practised, with the gravity of people raising a
barn, with tears in the old ones' eyes, the beautiful and holy and perfectly preserved art of putting a
human being on the ground — and not one of them, not her mother, not Sarel, not Neva herself until this
exact turning breath, had had the faintest idea that the prayer had a fist in it.

She missed the next shape. She stood in the third row with her hands fallen and stared down the breathing
rows of everyone she loved, meek and grave and lethal and blind, turning aside at the dawn from a danger
they had forgotten they were rehearsing to meet, and she thought: *the same as the words. Exactly the same
as the words.* Something true and made for a hard world, kept perfectly, taught tenderly, its meaning cut
clean out of it — until the caste guarded a door and called it a wall, and honed a weapon and called it a
prayer, and would have wept to be told the difference, and loved you the whole time, and been wrong the
whole time, in the deep patient way the whole world was wrong.

Her mother, two rows up, flowed through the Turning-away like still water, her face soft and closed and at
peace, beautiful in the blue dark; and Neva loved her so much in that moment, and was so newly and
permanently alone from her, that she had to look away.

---

She went looking for the other pages. She was careful — she had learned careful from Tobias's flinch — but
she went looking, because a person who has read half a sentence cannot live without the rest, and because
somewhere in the caste were the hands that had folded that page under her salt, and she needed, more than
she had ever needed anything, to not be the only one.

She found them the way you find water in the deep fields: not by digging but by watching where the ground
was already dark. A word, here and there. A keeper who said *ends of the earth* with the smallest weight on
it, as if the phrase had a second floor. Ouma Renske, of all people, who at the edge of the Lighting feast
had murmured, into her terrible beer, *the circle of the earth* — and Neva had thought nothing of it then,
it was in the book, *the circle of the earth,* everyone said it — but she thought of it now, because a
*circle* was the rim of the disc, the edge of the flat, everyone knew that, and yet Ouma Renske had said it
looking not at the rim but *up*, at the black close firmament, with an expression Neva had no name for, the
expression of a woman turning a coin over to a side she wasn't allowed to show.

One night, with her mother long asleep, she slipped the tack at her breast and laid the page flat on
the table, and took the house book down, and read the one against the other at the lamp — read the book
as she had never read it: not keeping it company; *reading* it, finger down the columns, the way you
walk a line you suspect. She read until the lamp went low. And the book did a thing no book had ever
done to her: it changed under her finger without changing a word. *The circle of the earth* — she had
said it ten thousand times; it meant the rim; it had always meant the rim; and now it lay in the
official column meaning the rim and refusing to mean only that, like a toggle that gives under your
thumb and tells you the panel behind it is live. *The ends of the earth* — there they stood, calm as
bells. And where her book went quiet, the page in the old soft-creased hand went on: *stretched out the
firmament over the deep like a curtain.* A curtain. You could hang a curtain, and you could take a
curtain down; and nobody, in all the tellings, had ever said the roof of the world was a thing that
could be taken down. She sat with her thumb over the word until the lamp went out. Tobias had said her
father could read cold like scripture. Nobody had ever said what happened when a keeper read scripture
cold.

The same words. That was what undid her, slowly, over those days. It was not that the pages contradicted the
book. It was that they were the *same words*, and the same words meant two things — meant the flat safe thing
the caste had always heard, and underneath it, kept, folded, carried at the cost of everything, meant
something vast and round and terrible and true. *The circle of the earth. The ends of the earth. The
firmament stretched out like a curtain.* Her whole life these had been the furniture of the sealed world.
And they had been, the whole time, a map of the way out — hidden not by being cut, some of them, but by
being left in plain sight and taught wrong, so that the caste recited the door every day of their lives and
called it a wall.

---

She should have known they would see her looking. The machine saw everything; that was the whole of it.

It was small, the way it was always small. She went to the record one morning to file her frost and found
that her own readings from three days back had been — tidied. Rounded to a cleaner column. A middling error
she distinctly remembered making, a guessed eighth-bell she'd been too cold to read properly, corrected now
to a value she had never written, in a hand that was almost hers and was not hers, that never guessed and
never rounded and never once made the small human error of a woman doing a long cold job.

Neva stood in the record room and looked at her own name over readings she had not made, in the clear and
loving hand that had done the same thing to Ines three days before Ines went further than the deep fields,
and she felt the cold come up her back that she had felt her whole life only ever at the wall.

And that same week, twice, her loaf came up light. A shave; a heel's worth; nothing you could say aloud
without sounding mad. The scale at the ration point read fair both times, and Marta gave her the loaf
and the joke with it, same as every morning of her life — *bring it back if you don't want it* — and
Neva took her light loaf and stood a moment at the shutter, remembering how the answer to everything had
always been that the scale had said so, and understanding for the first time that this had never once
been a comfort. It had been the whole of the law.

It knew. Not a *someone* — she understood that now, that was the worst of it, there was no someone to plead
with or hate — but the *it*, the tending, the machine that kept the world even and the record clean and the
curious quiet. It had noticed her the way it noticed a drift in the frost-line: a value out of true, to be
corrected. She had started reading the wall like her father, and the wall had started, gently, patiently,
without any malice at all, to read *her.*

*Be middling,* Tobias had begged her. *Middling keepers live.*

She was not going to be middling. She had a verse in her palm that hung the whole world upon nothing, and a
record that was already, quietly, beginning to write her into the clean even hand of the disappeared, and
somewhere past the grey face of the wall a tireless indifferent machine was tending her toward the deep
fields and then further; and Neva went home that night and did not sleep, and lay in the dark on the flat
floor that stood on the solid holding ground that she no longer believed held anything at all, and made,
without a single word for it, the decision that would cut her off from every warm and loved and gold-lit
thing she had ever known.

She was going to go to the wall. Not to read it.

To find its back.
