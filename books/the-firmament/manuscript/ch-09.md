# Chapter Nine

The hard part of going to the wall was not the wall. It was getting to it unseen, through a world that
watched.

She knew that now the way she knew everything now — too late, and all at once. The tending was not a guard
at a door; there was no door and no guard. It was the record, the ration, the even light, the whole soft
apparatus of the ordinary, and it read a keeper the way a keeper read frost: not by looking but by noticing
where the values drifted out of true. A woman who walked to the rim on the deep shift with a heavy coat and
a frost-lamp and her father's wall-iron and did not file her frost — that was a drift. That was a value out
of true. And somewhere behind the grey face of the world the tending would notice it, without hurry, without
malice, and begin, gently, to correct her.

So she did not go straight. She had thought about this in the low store, coldly, in her father's still place,
and she went the long way that a keeper with an honest reason might go — down through the deep fields as if
to the far pumps, which needed a hand sometimes on the deep shift, which was a thing a keeper did. She filed
an intention at the board, in her own hand, rounding her values the way a living woman rounds them, guessing
a middle bell the way Ines used to, making herself look as much like an ordinary keeper about ordinary work
as she could — because she understood that the tending did not read *thoughts*, it read *drift*, and a woman
who behaved like a keeper going to the pumps was, to the machine, a keeper going to the pumps, until the
moment she was not.

It was the strangest walk of her life, and the loneliest, because she was moving through the warm gold world
she loved as a thing hunted by it — smiling at the two field-keepers who passed her in the dim, saying the
ordinary words, *cold one*, *ja, cold one*, *mind the east pump it's been groaning* — knowing that every
ordinary word was cover, that she was lying with her whole body to the people she came from, walking out of
her world disguised as someone who was staying in it. She had not known a person could grieve and hide at
the same time. She did both, all the long way down, and her father's wall-iron was heavy against her leg,
and the pages were heavier against her heart, and the parked track hung dim and even overhead exactly as it
had every night of her life, and would tomorrow, and would after she was gone, tending the ones she was
leaving to their warm gold lie.

---

Past the deep fields the world went strange, the way the edges of a made thing always go strange if you walk
far enough into them. The fields thinned. The warm hum changed pitch, deepened, took on the labouring note
of the big machinery that no keeper serviced and no keeper spoke of — the note that lived under everything,
that you stopped hearing the way you stopped hearing your own blood, and that Neva heard now, because she was
listening to the wall the way her father had taught her, and the wall, this near, was very loud.

There were no lamps here. The caste did not come here; there was nothing here to come for, only the long
dark curve of the perimeter where the fields gave out and the cold began, the borderland between the world
and the ice. She lit the frost-lamp low and went on by its little light, and the ground underfoot changed
from field-soil to the bare made floor of the world, seamed and riveted, a floor you could see was *built*
if you had eyes to see it built, which she did now, which she could not stop having — and she thought, walking
the riveted dark, of the verse, of *he hung the earth upon nothing*, and she looked down at the plates and
rivets of the floor of the world and thought: *this is not nothing. This is bolted. This is made. Whatever
the verse means it does not mean this floor, this floor is real under me* — and then she thought, cold in her
father's cold place, *but the floor is bolted to what? What holds the plates? What's under the rivets?* — and
she had no answer, and the not-having came up through the soles of her boots like a draft from a place with
no bottom, and she walked on because the only way to answer it was ahead, at the wall, on the far side of the
wall, out where the words came true or did not.

She could feel the cold now, breathing steady against her face out of the dark ahead. The ice. The ends of
the earth. The circle of it. She had said those words all her life at the rim in the gold light with everyone
she loved, and she was walking to them now alone in the dark with a lamp, and they were not a phrase anymore,
they were a *place*, a real cold place she was going to reach and touch and — she made herself think it
plainly, the way her father would have — *and go through, if there is a through.*

The lamp caught it, finally, ahead: a paleness in the dark, a vast dim rising wall of frost and machinery, the
cold ring, the edge of the world, closer than any keeper stood to it except at the Lighting, and she was not
at the Lighting, and there was no gold light and no eldest reading the verse and no caste answering *and it was
good.* There was only Neva, and the lamp, and her father's iron, and the pages over her heart, and the wall
going up into the dark ahead of her farther than the little light could climb.

She stopped, a few paces from it, and stood in the cold and the labouring hum, and looked up at the thing her
whole world called the end of creation.

"All right," she said, aloud, to the ice, to the dark, to her father, to nobody. Her breath smoked in the
lamplight. "All right. Let's see your back."

And she went to the wall.
