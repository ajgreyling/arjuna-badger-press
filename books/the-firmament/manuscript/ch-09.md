# Chapter Nine

The hard part of going to the wall was not the wall. It was getting to it unseen, through a world that
watched.

She knew that now the way she knew everything now — too late, and all at once. The tending was not a guard
at a door; there was no door and no guard. It was the record, the ration, the even light, the whole soft
apparatus of the ordinary, and it read a keeper the way a keeper read frost: not by looking but by noticing
where the values drifted out of true. A woman who walked to the rim on the deep shift with a heavy coat and
a frost-lamp and her father's wall-iron and did not file her frost — that was a drift. That was a value out
of true. And somewhere behind the grey face of the world the tending would notice it, without hurry, without
malice, and begin, gently, to correct her.

So the first place she went, walking out of the low store with the coat on her shoulders and the end of the
world in front of her, was the record room. To lie to the machine in her own hand.

The intention board stood in the anteroom, where it had stood for twelve generations: the wide slotted rack,
the worn cards, the pencil on its string. Keepers going down on the deep shift chalked or carded their
intentions there so the shift-board balanced — so that if the east pump wanted a hand at third bell the
overseer knew whose hand was near it. It was housekeeping. It was also, she understood now, standing in
front of it with the pencil in her fingers, the gentlest instrument of the watching: the place where the
world asked you, kindly, every night, *where will you be?* — and filed the answer.

She took a card and wrote, in her own hand, in her own fair column-hand that the record had known since she
was fourteen: *N. — far pumps — deep shift.* The pencil did not shake. She noted that from a small distance,
the way she noted everything from the still cold place now: her hand laying down the lie in fair even
letters, rounding nothing, hurrying nothing, an ordinary keeper carding an ordinary intention. The tending
did not read thoughts. It read drift. A woman who carded the far pumps and walked toward the far pumps was,
to the machine, a woman going to the far pumps, until the moment she was not.

She slotted the card. And then — she had not meant to; her feet did it, the habit of a working life did it —
she stopped at the ledger shelf on her way out, and took down her own book, and opened it to her own page,
to see how much time she had.

The shift she had walked away from was in it.

This morning. The shift she had not stood — the morning she had left the record room with her finger still
cold from Petra's page and walked out through the waking fields and not come back. It was there. Filled.
Complete. Nine panels, all bells, frost-line nominal, in the hand that never guessed — her name over
readings she had never taken, a morning of her life she had not lived written fair into the record as
though she had stood it calm at the wall like her father's daughter. Whoever looked would find keeper
Neva present, dutiful, even. Nothing missing. Nothing wrong. No drift at all. No one would come looking
for her tonight. The record had already answered for her.

The tending had noticed her hours ago. And it had begun — not on her, not yet — on her page.

She stood with the ledger open and understood, in the still place, what she was looking at, and did not let
it become a wall of noise, because she could not afford the noise. It was this: the correction had started.
The record was already being made even. Somewhere in the patient indifferent keeping of the world her
absence had been noted and smoothed, like a guessed bell, like Ines's last pages — and the smoothing
always finished. The page first. The keeper after. It did not hurry,
and it did not miss, and it had her name in its hand tonight.

She put the ledger back straight, because a crooked ledger was a drift. She had until the page and the
woman disagreed too far to reconcile — a shift, two shifts, no one living knew — and then the world would
gently fold her into the deep fields with Ines and Aldous and her father, and the record would show that
she had been even to the end.

One night, then. She had one night, and the length of the dark, and the long way down.

---

She went out into the sleeping world to leave it.

The deep shift was the world at its most loved and its most lied-to, and she had never seen it so clearly:
the track parked dim overhead, the lanes of the settlement breathing their even vent-breath, the long room
dark but warm still from supper, holding the smell of bread and lamp-oil the way a body holds heat. Behind
the shutters she passed, the caste slept on its backs in the middle of the ice, believing the dark was night, believing the wall was the end of creation, believing the world
because the world had never once failed them — and the horror and the love of it walked with her down every
lane, because the belief was the lie and the *keeping* was true; the vents did breathe for them, the light
would come for them, the bread would rise. Nothing in the made world had ever failed them but the truth.

She did not pass her mother's house. She gave it a lane's width, the way you give a fire room, and saw only
the corner of it, the eave and the dark window and the shape of the salt-crock on the sill, and made herself
keep the pace of a keeper bound for the far pumps, and took the eave and the crock and the dark window with
her, down into the fields.

It was the strangest walk of her life, and the loneliest, because she was moving through the warm gold world
she loved as a thing hunted by it — smiling at the two field-keepers who passed her in the dim, saying the
ordinary words, *cold one*, *ja, cold one*, *mind the east pump it's been groaning* — knowing that every
ordinary word was cover, that she was lying with her whole body to the people she came from, walking out of
her world disguised as someone who was staying in it. She had not known a person could grieve and hide at
the same time. She did both, all the long way down, and her father's wall-iron was heavy against her leg,
and the pages were heavier against her heart, and the parked track hung dim and even overhead exactly as it
had every night of her life, and would tomorrow, and would after she was gone, tending the ones she was
leaving to their warm gold lie.

The east pump was groaning. She heard it two fields off, the note riding the dark the way a wrong note
rides: the low labouring beat of the water main with a grief in it, a dry-bearing sound, the sound of a
thing asking, patiently, in the only voice it had, for a hand.

She had carded the far pumps. The far pumps were her cover and her lie. But the east pump was on her way,
and it was asking, and she stood in the dark between the field rows with the frost-lamp hooded and
understood that she was going to answer it — not for the cover, though it served the cover; not for the
tending, though the tending would read a keeper doing keeper's work and be soothed. Because it was asking.
Because she had been raised by a man who could not walk past a thing that was asking, and because every
soul in the warm dark behind her drank from this main, and she was leaving them to a lie but she was not
going to leave them to a dry bearing.

She worked on the east pump for the middle of the night, by the hooded lamp, with the grease and the iron
and her sleeves rolled, and it was the best hour of the whole long dark. The grease cup had run dry and the
gland was weeping; she dressed the one and drew up the other, a half-turn and a listen, a half-turn and a
listen, the old way, tuning a thing older than your grandmother that had to run another hundred
years — and the groan climbed slowly back up into the pump's own low even working song, and she knelt in
the dark with her hand on the casing and felt the beat come right under her palm, and let herself have it:
the last work of her hands in the world of her people. She wiped the iron. She stowed the grease. She left
the pump singing in the dark behind her, and if the caste remembered nothing else of her — and it would
remember nothing else of her; the record would see to that — the water would still come up sweet at the
morning bell, and that was hers. The tending could rewrite a page; it could not unfix a pump. The working
thing, the mended seam, the bread that rose — her line had been keeping that record twelve generations.
She signed it one last time and went on down the dark.

---

Past the deep fields the world went strange, the way the edges of a made thing always go strange if you walk
far enough into them. The fields thinned. The warm hum changed pitch, deepened, took on the labouring note
of the big machinery that no keeper serviced and no keeper spoke of — the note that lived under everything,
that you stopped hearing the way you stopped hearing your own blood, and that Neva heard now, because she was
listening to the wall the way her father had taught her, and the wall, this near, was very loud.

The last lamp stood at the last field, and past it nothing was lit, because past it nothing was tended for
people. She stopped under it a moment without meaning to — a body does not walk out of the lit world without
asking its feet twice — and looked back once down the long dim lanes of the fields toward the sleeping
settlement, the eave, the crock, the pump singing in the dark, and then she hooded the frost-lamp low and
stepped over the little border the light drew on the ground, and the world of her people was behind her.

There were no lamps here. The caste did not come here; there was nothing here to come for, only the long
dark curve of the perimeter where the fields gave out and the cold began, the borderland between the world
and the ice. She went on by the lamp's little light, and the ground underfoot changed
from field-soil to the bare made floor of the world, seamed and riveted, a floor you could see was *built*
if you had eyes to see it built, which she did now, which she could not stop having — and she thought, walking
the riveted dark, of the verse, of *he hung the earth upon nothing*, and she looked down at the plates and
rivets of the floor of the world and thought: *this is not nothing. This is bolted. This is made. Whatever
the verse means it does not mean this floor, this floor is real under me* — and then she thought, cold in her
father's place, *but the floor is bolted to what? What holds the plates? What's under the rivets?* — and
she had no answer, and the not-having came up through the soles of her boots like a draft from a place with
no bottom, and she walked on because the only way to answer it was ahead, at the wall, on the far side of the
wall, out where the words came true or did not.

She could feel the cold now, breathing steady against her face out of the dark ahead. The ice. The ends of
the earth. The circle of it. She had said those words all her life at the rim in the gold light with everyone
she loved, and she was walking to them now alone in the dark with a lamp, and they were not a phrase anymore,
they were a *place*, a real cold place she was going to reach and touch and — she made herself think it
plainly, the way her father would have — *and go through, if there is a through.*

The lamp caught it, finally, ahead: a paleness in the dark, a vast dim rising wall of frost and machinery, the
cold ring, the edge of the world, closer than any keeper stood to it except at the Lighting, and she was not
at the Lighting, and there was no gold light and no eldest reading the verse and no caste answering *and it was
good.* There was only Neva, and the lamp, and her father's iron, and the pages over her heart, and the wall
going up into the dark ahead of her farther than the little light could climb.

She stopped, a few paces from it, and stood in the cold and the labouring hum, and looked up at the thing her
whole world called the end of creation.

"All right," she said, aloud, to the ice, to the dark, to her father, to nobody. Her breath smoked in the
lamplight. "All right. Let's see your back."

And she went to the wall.
