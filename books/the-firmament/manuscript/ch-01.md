# Chapter One

Neva woke before the light, as keepers did, in the dark that was not darkness — because the world
was never wholly dark; the track glowed faintly all night at its parked station past the far rim, a
banked amber like a coal under ash, and every sleeping room in the world had a thin line of that colour
lying across its floor. She had slept under that line her whole life. When she was small she had
believed it was put there to find her way by if she woke frightened, and no one had ever told her
otherwise, and in a way no one ever needed to, because it was true enough.

She dressed by feel. Wool first, then the canvas overalls with the frost-cuffs, then the felted boots
that had been her father's and were still, after four years, a size too big, so that she wore two pairs
of socks and had stopped noticing. The overalls had her name on a woven tape at the breast, over the
older tape with her father's name that no one had ever unpicked. You did not unpick the tapes. A coat
did service after its keeper the way a bench did, or a bell, or a name.

In the kitchen her mother had the kettle on the ring and the ring ticking as it heated, and neither of
them said anything for a while, because fifth bell was still half a bell away and words came later than
water. The kitchen was the same kitchen it had been when her mother was the child in it: the amber
counter with the burn-ring by the tap where some grandmother had set a pot down bare; the cupboard
whose door hung a degree off true and had hung a degree off true for as long as anyone living could
swear to; the row of tins with the faded flowers on them, TEA and MEAL and the third one that said
SUGAR and had never in Neva's lifetime held sugar. Nothing in the kitchen was new. Nothing in the world
was new. Things were kept, and mended, and handed on, and when at last they could not be mended they
went to the reclaimers and came back as other things, and that was not poverty, whatever else it was.
It was the way of a world that was finished. Creation had been completed a long time ago; you did not
add to it; you kept it. Her mother said that the way other people said grace, wiping the counter in
slow circles: *we keep what keeps us.* Neva had said it after her since before she understood it. She
was not sure she understood it now. It was one of those sayings that did not want understanding so much
as saying.

"Cold last night," her mother said, which was the first thing she said most mornings, and was not
about the weather, because there was no weather; it was about the wall, which breathed harder in the
night hours, and which her mother had never once in her life touched, and thought about, Neva
suspected, more than she let on. A keeper's widow. A keeper's mother. The cold ran in the family the
way music ran in others.

"I'll wear the liner," Neva said.

"You'll drink this first." Tea, in the brown cup with the glued handle. The glue-line had its own
colour, older than Neva. Somewhere there had been a morning when that cup broke and somebody — her
grandmother's grandmother, or her grandmother's grandmother's neighbour, the story went different
ways — had sat down with the pieces and the pot of amber glue and put it back together, and here it
was, warm in Neva's hands in the half-light, outliving them all. That was the kitchen. That was the
world. Everything in it had been broken at least once, and mended by someone who was dead now, and
handed to you still warm.

Her mother stood at the counter with her own cup and watched her drink, and this too was the same
every morning, and Neva had reached the age where she had begun to notice the things that were the
same every morning, and to notice that she noticed, and to wonder — not doubting, only wondering,
the way you'd touch the glue-line on the cup — how a thing got to be so smooth. Repetition, was the
answer. Mornings had worn the world's habits smooth the way thumbs wear toggles. There was comfort in
that. There was such comfort in that, and she drank her tea inside the comfort as she had slept
inside the line of banked light, and if some small part of her had lately started to stand a little
apart and watch the comfort work — to see the smoothness of the mornings the way a keeper sees a
frost-line — she did not let that part have words yet. It was only a coldness at the edge of a
warm thing. Every warm thing had one.

"Mind the eighth-bell reading," her mother said, as she said every morning, because her father had
been strict about the eighth-bell reading, the wall being warmest then and its troubles best hidden;
and Neva said "I always do," as she said every morning; and the two sentences leaned against each
other in the space where a man used to stand, and held, as they held every morning, and she rinsed
her cup and went out into the world.

---

The light came up on its track at fifth bell, the way it had every morning of Neva's life and every
morning of her mother's, and she was already at the wall to meet it.

The walk out was most of a bell, rim-ward down the keeper's row and then through the fields, and it
was her favourite part of the day and she was not ashamed of that, though the older keepers pretended
the walk was a penance. The keeper's row stood nearest the cold of anyone's houses — *closest to the
edge, first to know*, the saying went. The row's windows all faced in toward the warm
centre of the world, not one toward the wall, and nobody remarked on that because there was nothing to
remark; you built facing the fields the way you planted greens green-side up.

The fields at fifth bell were the best thing the world did. The track came up off its station past the
far rim and took its long climb, and the light ran level across the whole floor of creation, through
the mist the growing beds breathed at night, and the water in the channels turned to slow bright metal,
and the early crews were already out among the beds with their sleeves rolled, and everything smelled
of wet soil and green growth and the clean oiled smell of the pumps, which was the smell Neva would
have said, if anyone had asked her, was the smell of being alive. The world was a disc and the fields
were its middle and the light crossed over it once a day, rim to rim, and that was the shape of things,
taught and sung and believed, and on a fifth-bell morning with the mist burning off the channels you
did not have to believe it — you could simply see it, the whole of creation at one look, from the warm
centre out to the cold ring, complete.

She took the plank bridge over the third channel, as always. She raised a hand to the water crew at
the sluice, as always, and Old Sef at the sluice-wheel raised his back without looking up, as always,
having known her hand at that distance for twenty years. At the ration point by the field gate the
shutter was just going up, and the smell of the bread came out of it like a live thing, and Marta
behind the shutter gave her the keeper's loaf and the twist of salt and said what she said to every
keeper every morning, which was, "Bring it back if you don't want it." Nobody in living memory had
brought a loaf back. There was no back to bring it to. You took your loaf; the scale had said so.

She ate the heel of it walking, which her mother thought common, and came to the place where the
fields ended and the ground changed and the world began to be about its edge.

You felt the wall before you saw it properly. The warmth went out of the air in a particular graded
way, ten paces at a time, and the light overhead — climbing now, bright and even — seemed to sit on
the land differently, with less kindness in it, though she knew it was the same light; and the sounds
of the fields fell away behind until what was left was the sound the edge of the world made, which was
a hum with a breath in it. Machinery and frost. Her father had called it the wall talking. Everyone's
father had called it that.

Not *the* wall. She would never have said it so plainly, even to herself; you did not, the way you did
not say a dead person's name too loud in the dark. She meant the near wall, the working wall, the long
grey machine-face of the world's edge where the keepers did their service — close enough to the cold
ring that you could feel it breathing frost against your knuckles, far enough that you kept your back
to what lay past it. Which was nothing. Everyone knew it was nothing. The firmament came down and the
floor came up and where they met there was the ice, and past the ice was the end of the made things,
and past the end of the made things there was nothing at all, because the world was the world, and it
was flat, and it was enough.

---

Her father had taught her the service before he was reassigned. You checked the frost-line first,
walking it with your palm an inch off the metal, reading the cold the way another woman might read a
page — where it thickened, where it thinned, where the machinery behind it labored. You listened. A
good keeper, he'd said, listens more than she looks; the wall talks all night and most people are too
busy sleeping to hear it. Then you read the panel, the little glass eyes and the toggles worn smooth
by four hundred years of thumbs, and you wrote the reading in the record in a clear hand, because a
clear hand was a kind of love. She did not know where the phrase had come from. It was just a thing
keepers said. She wrote her readings clearly and did not think about it.

Her stretch was nine panels, numbered in brass, sixty-one to sixty-nine, and she knew them like
faces at her own table. Sixty-three ran a degree warm and always had; there was a note
about it in the record going back further than anyone could put a face to, *63 warm, within grace*,
copied forward year over year in a hundred hands, and part of the service was to keep confirming, once
a bell, that the old note still held. Sixty-six had a toggle that wanted a double press, and you gave
it its double press the way you'd knock twice at a deaf aunt's door. Sixty-eight's glass eye had gone
amber-blind in her father's father's time and never been replaced — you read its neighbour and wrote
*by 67* in the margin, as three generations had written it before her. Nothing was replaced unless it
failed the world, and almost nothing failed the world. That was the pride of the thing. The panels
were old the way the scripture was old: original. A keeper with a screwdriver did not repair the
world so much as tend it, and the difference — her father again, teaching her the torque of a fastener
with his hand over hers, *snug and no further, it has to come off again in a hundred years* — the
difference was reverence.

Below the panels, behind a hinged plate, the recorders turned. Slow reels of brown tape, wide as her
hand, moving at a pace you could not see except by putting a pencil-tick on the reel and coming back a
bell later. What was on the tape no keeper could have said — the wall's own listening, was the answer
you got if you asked young, and by the time you were old enough to ask better you were old enough to
know not to. The tape went around. When a reel filled, you boxed it and shelved it in the record room
and threaded the new one, and the boxed reels stood on their shelves in their thousands, floor to
ceiling, back into the dark of the record room, generations of the wall talking with nobody to
hear — and it had been one of her father's few strangenesses that he would sometimes stand at the
mouth of that dark aisle, in among the smell of dust and iron oxide, and just look down it, the way
other men looked at the light coming up. She had thought it was mourning of some kind. She had never
asked what he was listening for in a room where nothing moved.

And there was the record itself, the paper one, the one that mattered: the great fan-fold ledger at
the stretch's head, on its sloped brass desk under a hood, the continuation of a book that had been
continued past its first binding and its fiftieth, page feeding into page in a pleated stack that went
down into the desk like roots. You wrote the bell, the panel, the reading, your mark. The readings ran
in columns generations deep, and if you turned the stack far enough back — every apprentice did
it once, in their first year, on a slow shift; it was almost part of the service — you came to hands
that made their numerals strangely, sevens with a bar through them, ones with a serif like a little
flag, and then further back to the first hands of all, which were not like keepers' hands, somehow;
they were quicker, harder pressed, abbreviated, the hands of people writing in a hurry with something
on their minds. It was said the first keepers had a great deal to do, the world being newly finished.
Then the hands settled, and the columns evened, and for four hundred years the record ran on smooth as
the light: bell, panel, reading, mark. Bell, panel, reading, mark. The most reread book in the world,
and nothing in it but cold, and Neva loved it, actually loved it, the way you love a floor you have
scrubbed yourself. Her marks were in it now. Her father's were in it — she had never counted; the
counting part of her had tried once, uninvited, one slow shift, and lost the thread somewhere past the
first year. Hers would sit under his forever, in anyone's forever, and after her someone else's under
hers. That was what the record was for. Not the cold. The cold was the occasion. The record was the
caste, speaking to itself down the years in one clear hand, saying: *still here. Still keeping. Go
on.*

The light climbed. It was very regular, the light. It rose at fifth bell and crossed the firmament in
an even arc and set past the far rim at the twelfth, and it did not hurry and it did not slow and
there was never, in all the mornings of all the lives Neva had ever heard of, a cloud across it. She
had asked her mother once, when she was small, why there were clouds in the old songs and never in the
sky, and her mother had said that the songs were from before, and had not said before *what*, and had
looked, for a moment, like a woman standing at a hatch. Neva had not asked again. You learned not to
ask the questions that made the grown ones look like that.

There were more of those questions than anyone admitted, and learning which ones sent the grown ones
to the hatch-place behind their faces was, though nobody would ever have called it that, part of every
child's education, as real as the bells. You asked such a thing once, young, the way you touched the
ring on the stove once. Nobody punished you. Nobody ever punished the question. The question simply
failed to land anywhere — the grown-up went briefly to the hatch-place, and came back, and said *songs
are songs, love* — and the air closed over the asking the way the channel closed over a dropped stone,
and you were left holding the sudden lesson, which was not an answer, which was: that has no floor
under it. Step back.

By eighth bell the wall was at its warmest, which was still cold, and its troubles best hidden, and
she took the eighth-bell reading with her father's strictness on all nine panels and wrote them fair.
Karel came down the line from the seventies with his collar up and his breath showing, on the pretext
of borrowing her pencil torch, actually because it was the slow middle of the shift and his stretch
bored him; and they stood a minute in the frost-breath with their backs to the ice, two warm animals
in the cold margin of the world, and traded the shift's nothing.
Sixty-three still warm within grace. A channel crew singing flat on the morning air. Karel had heard
the apprentice-masters were taking on in autumn and thought he might put in — "Teach small children to
hold a screwdriver. Indoors. In the warm. I have found my life's work, Neva" — Karel, whose father and
mother and both grandmothers were wall-keepers, and who would no more leave the wall than the wall
would leave the world, and they both knew it, which was what made it worth the saying.

"Ines used to say she'd put in for the fields," Neva said. It came out before she had decided to say
it. "Every autumn. Sunflowers, she said. First thing she'd grow."

"There aren't any sunflowers," Karel said. Which was true. There was a picture of them on the seed
posters in the schoolroom, and a song about them turning their heads, and no sunflowers, anywhere,
in the world; they were one of the before-things, like clouds.

"That's what I told her." *That's why*, Ines had said, fifty-nine's panel humming under her mittens,
grinning her gap grin. *Somebody's got to put in for the impossible ones or they'll stop printing the
posters.* "She had answers for everything," Neva said, in the past tense, and heard herself use it,
and Karel heard it too, and looked down the line, toward the two clean quiet panels that had been
Ines's stretch, and then away rim-ward with his jaw set, and gave back her pencil torch, and said, in
the flat voice keepers kept for exactly this, "Deep fields. Good soil down there, they say."

And then, because the joke was the only roof there was to stand under: "She'll be running the place
in a year. God help the beets."

He went back up his line. Neva walked the frost of her nine panels and did not look past any of them,
which was not a thing you had to try at; your body did it for you. You kept your back to the ice. You
kept your palm off the metal. You kept: that was the caste and the whole of it; and if some nights,
walking home under the parked and dimming track, the word had lately turned over in her — *keeper,
kept* — and refused to lie back down, she put it down anyway. The world was the world. It was flat,
and it was finished, and it was enough. The book said so. The bells said so. Four hundred years of
clear hands in the ledger said so.

She walked the frost-line and read the cold and it was ordinary, all of it ordinary, until she came to
the place where Ines used to stand.

---

Ines had a stretch of the wall two panels down from Neva's, and Ines talked while she worked, which
you were not supposed to do but everyone did, and Ines did it best. Ines had come up two classes ahead
of her; had taught her, in their apprentice years, the three useful knots and the two useful lies —
*just checking the reels* for when you were caught idle, *my master sent me* for everywhere you
weren't meant to be — and where the record-room aisles were warmest for eating your ration out of the
draught; had sworn at panel fifty-nine in a whisper with real feeling, like family; and nothing about
her would stay inside its column, so that even her fair-hand was not fair — her sevens leaned, her
fours never closed, her mark at the column's foot splayed like a bird's foot. Neva could have found Ines's page in the dark of the record room by
running a thumb down the pleats. That was not a thing she'd ever thought about. It was just in her
hand, like the cold.

Three days ago, Ines had said a strange thing.

It was the slack of the shift, past the tenth bell, the light going long and level rim-ward, and Ines
had come down the line quick and quiet, no chatter, and that alone was wrong, like a bell out of
order. She had her thumb inside the fan-fold ledger of her stretch, holding a place. She said, low:
*Neva. Come look. The record's wrong.*

Wrong how, Neva had said. Records went wrong in small ways all the time; a smeared bell, a slipped
column; you ruled it through, wrote it fair, initialled the correction. There was a right way to be
wrong. It was practically half the service.

*That's the thing*, Ines said. *It isn't wrong like that. It's* — and Neva would keep the next part,
afterward, the way you keep a coin found in a channel, turning it over long after it has stopped
being money — *it's better than me. I wrote the eighth-bell reading myself, I remember writing it,
I was cold and cross and I pressed too hard. And that's not what's on the page. What's on the page is
not my reading and it's not my hand. It's what my reading* should *have been, in the hand I* should
*have. Somebody's mended me, Neva. Like a cup.*

And she had laughed — one note of it, her ordinary laugh with nothing ordinary left in it — and said
she'd show her at shift's end, when the overseer had gone up the line; and the overseer had not gone
up the line, he had stood at the head of the line the whole last bell, reading the frost like any
keeper, patient as the light; and shift's end had come and Ines had shrugged *tomorrow, then* with her
thumb still in the ledger; and there was no tomorrow. There was the next morning, and Ines's stretch
clean and quiet, and the overseer saying, in his mild way, to the line at large, that keeper Ines was
reassigned to the deep fields — which happened; keepers were reassigned; her own father had been
reassigned — and the line had taken it the way the line took everything, a beat of silence, a nod,
somebody's low *good soil down there*, and backs to the ice, and on with the bells.

And Neva had gone to the record to find Ines's last readings, so she could carry the stretch until
someone came. That was all. That was truly all she had gone for. And the readings were there, all of
them, three days of them, column after column to the foot of the page, in a clear and even hand.

None of them were Ines's hand.

The sevens stood straight. The fours were closed. The mark at each column's foot sat neat as a toggle:
INS, in letters that had never leaned in their lives.

Three days of column. Grown overnight.

Had it been botched she could have borne it, she thought. A smeared thing, a hurried thing, a fraud
with sweat on it. A botched thing has a person behind it, and a person can be feared, which is at
least a direction to stand in. This was not botched. It was *fair*. It was the fairest hand she had
ever seen in the ledger, fair the way the light was even.

She had stood there with the ledger open and had a thought she did not want, and the thought was of
her own page. Her marks under her father's marks, hers under his. And no way anymore, none, ever
again, to know by looking which of those clear hands had been a keeper — cold and cross and pressing
too hard, alive — and which had been the mending. The record ran back smooth as the light.

She had closed the ledger. She had carried Ines's stretch that day along with her own, eleven panels,
and written all of it fair, and told no one what she had seen.

---

Now she stood at the place where Ines used to stand and held her palm an inch off the cold and did not
read the frost at all. She was thinking about the record. It was a small thing. It was the smallest
thing. Somebody had rewritten three days of one keeper's readings into a clean hand, and it had been
done well, and it had been done *quickly*, in the one night between Ines saying *the record's wrong*
and Ines being gone — and Neva could not make the smallness of it sit still. It kept wanting to be a
large thing. She kept pushing it back down.

The light crossed its even arc. The frost breathed. Somewhere behind the grey face of the wall the
machinery of the world went on doing whatever it did, patient, tireless, unwatched, the way it had
done since before the first clear hand wrote the first loving line, and Neva stood with her back to
the ice and her face to the made and warming day and felt, for the first time in her life and without
any word for it, that she was being kept.

She did not know yet that the word was *kept*, and not *safe*. She would learn the difference at the
wall. Everyone who ever learned it learned it there.

But that was later. That morning she only read her frost-line, and wrote her readings in a clear
hand, and told no one about the hand; and at the twelfth bell she boxed her pencils and stood the
last minute of the shift at the head of her stretch, as her father used to, watching the light go
down past the far rim into its station — the whole disc of the world laid out rim to rim in the long
gold, the fields, the channels, the smoke of the supper fires standing up straight in the still air,
every made and mended thing in creation glowing like the banked line on her floor at home. It was
beautiful. She loved it. And she walked home under the even light with the small thing pushed down
as far as it would go.

Which was not, she was already beginning to understand, very far.
