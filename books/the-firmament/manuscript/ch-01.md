# Chapter One

The light came up on its track a little after fifth bell, the way it had every morning of Neva's life
and every morning of her mother's, and she was already at the wall to meet it.

Not *the* wall. She would never have said it so plainly, even to herself; you did not, the way you did
not say a dead person's name too loud in the dark. She meant the near wall, the working wall, the long
grey machine-face of the world's edge where the keepers did their service — close enough to the cold
ring that you could feel it breathing frost against your knuckles, far enough that you kept your back to
what lay past it. Which was nothing. Everyone knew it was nothing. The firmament came down and the floor
came up and where they met there was the ice, and past the ice was the end of the made things, and past
the end of the made things there was nothing at all, because the world was the world, and it was enough,
and it was flat, and a flat world was a mercy: it meant there was a floor under all of them and a roof
over all of them and no direction a person could fall.

That was the teaching, and Neva believed it, because it was true. You had only to stand at the wall to
see that it was true. The floor ran level and unbroken to the near rim; the firmament arced over it,
seamless and grey-blue and close; the light crossed between them on its even path. There was no other
shape the world could be. A child who was told the world was a ball would have laughed — a ball, with
people on the underside of it, hanging by their feet over the nothing? You could see it was flat. Sight
was not a thing you argued with.

Her father had taught her the service before he was reassigned. You checked the frost-line first, walking
it with your palm an inch off the metal, reading the cold the way another woman might read a page —
where it thickened, where it thinned, where the machinery behind it labored. You listened. A good keeper,
he'd said, listens more than she looks; the wall talks all night and most people are too busy sleeping to
hear it. Then you read the panel, the little glass eyes and the toggles worn smooth by four hundred
years of thumbs, and you wrote the reading in the record in a clear hand, because a clear hand was a kind
of love. She did not know where the phrase had come from. It was just a thing keepers said. She wrote her
readings clearly and did not think about it.

The light climbed. It was very regular, the light. It rose at fifth bell and crossed the firmament in an
even arc and set past the far rim at the twelfth, and it did not hurry and it did not slow. Neva had
heard travelling keepers say that at the far edge of the disc the light rose a breath later and set a
breath sooner, and nearer the middle a breath the other way, and that a keeper who kept the record long
enough could tell you her own place in the world by nothing but the bell the light lit at — as if the
whole even arc of it were a thing scheduled, laid down once and run off ever since, the same on the
thousandth morning as the first. She thought that was beautiful. She thought it was the most beautiful
thing about the world, that it could be *trusted* like that; that the light was faithful; that whoever
had set it going in the beginning had loved his children enough to make them a day that would never once,
in four hundred years, fail to come.

*The sun runs his course,* said the book she had been raised on — the good grey authorised book, the one
in every hall and every hand, the one the ration-clerks read from at the turning of the seasons — *and
his going forth is from the end of the heaven, and his circuit unto the ends of it.* Neva had known the
line before she could read it. Standing at the wall you could *watch* it be true: the light went forth
from the one end of the heaven and made its circuit to the other, faithfully, on its track, and no woman
who had eyes could doubt a word of it. That was the thing about the book that she loved and had never
questioned — it described the world *exactly.* You did not need faith to believe the sun ran a course.
You needed only to stand at fifth bell and look up and see it go.

It never occurred to her — how could it — that a thing described *that* exactly, a sun you could set your
bells by, a course that never varied by a breath in four hundred years, is not the kind of thing you
*describe.* It is the kind of thing you *build.*

There was never a cloud across it. She had asked her mother once, when she was small, why there were
clouds in the old songs and never in the sky, and her mother had said that the songs were from before,
and had not said before *what*, and had looked, for a moment, like a woman standing at a hatch. Neva had
not asked again. You learned not to ask the questions that made the grown ones look like that.

She walked the frost-line and read the cold, and it was ordinary, all of it ordinary, and if she slowed
at the one place — she always slowed at the one place — it was a private thing and she told no one. Two
panels down from her own stretch, low, near where the ice met the metal, the grey face of the wall was
not quite seamless. There was a line in it. A join. It ran up out of the frost for the height of a tall
man and stopped, and if you laid your palm flat against it in the cold you could feel the faint even
breath of *warmth* leaking from the seam — warmth, at the wall, where all the cold in the world was made.
The other keepers, the ones who noticed it at all, said it was where two courses of the firmament had
been fitted together in the making, and that the warmth was the breath of the world coming through the
joint, the world breathing, the way the book said the world was a living thing that God kept alive by
keeping it warm. Neva liked that. She would put her palm to the warm seam some mornings and feel the
world breathe, and it steadied her. She did not think — because there was no thought in her whole world
that could have arrived at it — that a living thing does not have *right angles*, and that the seam ran
dead straight, and turned once, square, and ran on, the way a thing does that a person has *built*.

The light crossed its even arc. The frost breathed. And Neva walked on to the place where Ines used to
stand.

Ines had a stretch of the wall two panels down from the warm seam, and Ines talked while she worked,
which you were not supposed to do but everyone did, and three days ago Ines had said a strange thing. She
had said, *Neva, come look, the record's wrong.* She had said, *I wrote the eighth-bell reading myself
and it's not my reading and it's not my hand.* She had been going to show her. And then it had been the
next morning, and Ines's panel was clean and quiet, and the overseer had said Ines was reassigned to the
deep fields, which happened, keepers were reassigned, her own father had been reassigned; and Neva had
gone to the record to find Ines's last readings so she could carry the stretch until someone came, and
the readings were there, all of them, in a clear and even hand.

None of them were Ines's hand.

Neva stood at the place where Ines used to stand and held her palm an inch off the cold and did not read
the frost at all. She was thinking about the record. It was a small thing. It was the smallest thing.
Somebody had rewritten three days of one keeper's readings into a clean hand, and it had been done well,
and it had been done *quickly*, in the one night between Ines saying *the record's wrong* and Ines being
gone — and Neva could not make the smallness of it sit still. It kept wanting to be a large thing. She
kept pushing it back down.

The light crossed its even arc, faithful, scheduled, the same as the thousandth morning and the first.
Somewhere behind the grey face of the wall the machinery of the world went on doing whatever it did,
patient, tireless, unwatched, the way it had done since before the first clear hand wrote the first
loving line. And Neva stood with her back to the ice and her face to the warming day, one stretch of wall
away from a seam that breathed warm and turned in a dead-straight square, and felt, for the first time in
her life and without any word for it, that she was being kept.

She did not know yet that the word was *kept*, and not *safe*. She would learn the difference at the
wall. Everyone who ever learned it learned it there.

But that was later. That morning she only read her frost-line, and wrote her readings in a clear hand,
and told no one about Ines, and went home under the even light with the small thing pushed down as far as
it would go — which was not, she was already beginning to understand, very far.
