# Chapter One

The light came up on its track a little after fifth bell, the way it had every morning of Neva's life
and every morning of her mother's, and she was already at the wall to meet it.

Not *the* wall. She would never have said it so plainly, even to herself; you did not, the way you did
not say a dead person's name too loud in the dark. She meant the near wall, the working wall, the long
grey machine-face of the world's edge where the keepers did their service — close enough to the cold
ring that you could feel it breathing frost against your knuckles, far enough that you kept your back to
what lay past it. Which was nothing. Everyone knew it was nothing. The firmament came down and the floor
came up and where they met there was the ice, and past the ice was the end of the made things, and past
the end of the made things there was nothing at all, because the world was the world, and it was flat,
and it was enough.

Her father had taught her the service before he was reassigned. You checked the frost-line first, walking
it with your palm an inch off the metal, reading the cold the way another woman might read a page —
where it thickened, where it thinned, where the machinery behind it labored. You listened. A good keeper,
he'd said, listens more than she looks; the wall talks all night and most people are too busy sleeping to
hear it. Then you read the panel, the little glass eyes and the toggles worn smooth by four hundred
years of thumbs, and you wrote the reading in the record in a clear hand, because a clear hand was a kind
of love. She did not know where the phrase had come from. It was just a thing keepers said. She wrote her
readings clearly and did not think about it.

The light climbed. It was very regular, the light. It rose at fifth bell and crossed the firmament in an
even arc and set past the far rim at the twelfth, and it did not hurry and it did not slow and there was
never, in all the mornings of all the lives Neva had ever heard of, a cloud across it. She had asked her
mother once, when she was small, why there were clouds in the old songs and never in the sky, and her
mother had said that the songs were from before, and had not said before *what*, and had looked, for a
moment, like a woman standing at a hatch. Neva had not asked again. You learned not to ask the questions
that made the grown ones look like that.

She walked the frost-line and read the cold and it was ordinary, all of it ordinary, until she came to
the place where Ines used to stand.

Ines had a stretch of the wall two panels down from Neva's, and Ines talked while she worked, which you
were not supposed to do but everyone did, and three days ago Ines had said a strange thing. She had said,
*Neva, come look, the record's wrong.* She had said, *I wrote the eighth-bell reading myself and it's not
my reading and it's not my hand.* She had been going to show her. And then it had been the next morning,
and Ines's panel was clean and quiet, and the overseer had said Ines was reassigned to the deep fields,
which happened, keepers were reassigned, her own father had been reassigned; and Neva had gone to the
record to find Ines's last readings so she could carry the stretch until someone came, and the readings
were there, all of them, in a clear and even hand.

None of them were Ines's hand.

Neva stood at the place where Ines used to stand and held her palm an inch off the cold and did not read
the frost at all. She was thinking about the record. It was a small thing. It was the smallest thing.
Somebody had rewritten three days of one keeper's readings into a clean hand, and it had been done well,
and it had been done *quickly*, in the one night between Ines saying *the record's wrong* and Ines being
gone — and Neva could not make the smallness of it sit still. It kept wanting to be a large thing. She
kept pushing it back down.

The light crossed its even arc. The frost breathed. Somewhere behind the grey face of the wall the
machinery of the world went on doing whatever it did, patient, tireless, unwatched, the way it had done
since before the first clear hand wrote the first loving line, and Neva stood with her back to the ice
and her face to the made and warming day and felt, for the first time in her life and without any word
for it, that she was being kept.

She did not know yet that the word was *kept*, and not *safe*. She would learn the difference at the
wall. Everyone who ever learned it learned it there.

But that was later. That morning she only read her frost-line, and wrote her readings in a clear hand,
and told no one about Ines, and went home under the even light with the small thing pushed down as far as
it would go — which was not, she was already beginning to understand, very far.
