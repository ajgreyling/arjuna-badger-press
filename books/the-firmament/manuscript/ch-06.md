# Chapter Six

The keeper who had folded the page under her salt was named Aldous, and he found her before she found him,
because he had been watching her the way you watch a fuse.

He came to her at the wall, on the deep shift, when the track was parked and the frost breathed hardest and
there was no one on the long grey line but the two of them and the cold. An old man, older than Tobias,
stooped, with a keeper's ruined hands and a face gone to soft folds, and eyes — she saw it the moment he
turned them on her — eyes that were not afraid. That was the thing she would remember about Aldous. In a
world where every old keeper's eyes went careful and wet and frightened the moment you asked the real
question, Aldous looked at her across the frost with eyes that had made their peace with the answer a long
time ago, and were only sorry, terribly sorry, that she was going to have to make hers too.

"You didn't burn it," he said.

She knew him then. She didn't know how; she knew.

"No," she said.

"Most burn it." He came and stood beside her at the wall, his back to it, the way you stood, and for a
moment they were just two keepers at the cold, an old one and a young one, the most ordinary thing in the
world. "I gave one to your father," Aldous said. "Long ago. He didn't burn his either."

The frost breathed. Neva did not trust her voice.

"He read too long," she managed, finally. Tobias's words. Her mother's words. The words the whole caste
used to fold the blanket over the fire.

"He read exactly long enough," Aldous said, and there was iron in it, sudden and clean, the first hard thing
she'd heard anyone say about her father in nine turnings. "Don't let them tell it the other way. Your father
wasn't a man who read too long. Your father was a man who read the *truth* and loved this world too much to
leave it — and so he stayed, and he said nothing, and he taught his girl to read cold, and he let the wall
tend him toward the fields rather than take the rest of you with him through it." Aldous looked at her.
"That was his choice. It was a good man's choice and it cost him everything and I'll not have you think it
was weakness. But it was his. Yours is still yours."

"Mine," Neva said.

"You've read the verse." It was not a question. "You know the floor hangs on nothing. You know the light's
on a track and no hand winds it. You know the record's already writing you clean." He said it gently, but he
said all of it, every seam she'd found alone in the dark, laid out plain by an old man at the wall as if he'd
been walking three steps behind her the whole time. Which, she understood, he had. "So you know what's coming.
It's already started. It started the day you counted the light. It doesn't hurry — that's the mercy and the
horror of it, it never hurries — but it doesn't stop, and it doesn't miss, and one morning your panel will be
clean and quiet and Sarel will say you've gone to the deep fields, and your mother will believe it, because
your mother *needs* to believe it, and there'll be three turnings of your frost-readings in the record in a
hand that never guesses."

"Then I go now," Neva said. Her voice shook and did not break. "Before it — before the record finishes me.
I go to the wall and I find the back of it."

And Aldous, who had made his peace with the answer a long time ago, closed his old eyes for a moment in the
cold, and when he opened them they were wet at last — not with fear, she saw; with something else, something
that had waited a very long time — and he said, "Yes. That's the one. That's the choice your father couldn't
make because he had you. You've no one it'd take with you?"

"My mother," Neva said, and the word was a stone.

"Your mother believes the record," Aldous said, not unkindly. "She'll grieve you gone to the fields and it
will be a clean grief, a grief she has words for. That's not nothing, girl. That's a gift you can give her
— a grief she can carry. The other kind —" He didn't finish. He didn't need to. The other kind was a
daughter who walked into the ice on purpose, and there were no words in the caste for that, no clean grief,
nothing to carry.

They stood a while longer at the wall. The cold breathed. And then Aldous reached into his keeper's coat,
slow, an old man's slow, and took out a bundle wrapped in oiled cloth, heavier than a page, and pressed it
into her hands the way the first page had been pressed into a ration tin.

"The rest of it," he said. "All the pages we've kept. The whole of the sentence, best we've pieced it. You
take it through. You don't read it here — here it'll only break your heart, the way it broke your father's,
because here every word of it hangs on nothing you can see. You read it *out there.*" His ruined hands closed
over hers, over the bundle. "You read it where the words come true, Neva. That's the whole of why we keep it.
Not to be right in here. To be *read* out there. Somebody has to carry the sentence to the place it fits."

Neva held the bundle of everything her caste had died to keep, in the cold, at the wall, with the parked
track dim overhead and the machine tending patiently behind the grey face at her back, and she did not weep,
because she had gone past weeping into the still cold place her father must have lived in at the end.

"I'll carry it," she said.

"I know," said Aldous. "I've known since you didn't burn the page." And he patted her hands, once, the old
blessing, Sarel's blessing, her father's blessing, the caste's one gesture for *go, and be more than we were
brave enough to be* — and then he turned and walked back down the long grey line into the dim, an old man
going to file his frost, and he did not look back, and she never saw him again.

The record would say, three days later, that Aldous had gone to the deep fields.

His last readings were perfect.
