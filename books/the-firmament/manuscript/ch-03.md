# Chapter Three

On the day of the Lighting, which came once in the turning of the record and was the oldest and best of
the keepers' feasts, Neva woke before her mother and lay in the dark and waited for the world to begin.

This was the one morning the track did not simply light. On the Lighting it was *called* up — the eldest
keeper standing at the rim beneath the dark firmament, reading the first verse of the book aloud into the
black, and the whole of the caste answering, and only then, only when the words were said, did the pale
edge of the made sun kindle at the eastern rim and climb. It was not that anyone believed the track would
fail to light if the words went unsaid. It was that the words and the light were one thing, had always
been one thing, and to let the light come up unwelcomed would have been like letting your own child be born
in an empty room. You met the day. You said the day was good. And then the day was good.

Neva loved the Lighting the way she loved few things — wholly, without the stone. She dressed in the dark
and woke her mother and they went down together through the sleeping fields to the rim, where the others
were already gathering, hundreds of them, the whole keeping-caste and half the Vault besides, their lamps
making a low warm constellation along the base of the wall. The cold breathed off the ice at their backs.
Above them the firmament was black and close. And old Sarel, who was eldest now that her father was gone,
lifted his hands in the dark and began.

*In the beginning was the firmament,* Sarel said, *and the light upon its track, and the ice at the ends
of the earth.*

And the caste answered: *and it was good.*

*And the maker set the light to rise and to cross and to set,* Sarel said, *that we should have days, and
number them, and not be afraid.*

*And it was good,* they answered, and Neva answered with them, her mother's shoulder warm against hers, and
she meant it with her whole body, the way you can only mean a thing you have said every year of your life
in the dark with the people you come from — she meant *and it was good* the way you mean your own name.

And the light came up. It always came up on the last word; the eldest timed it by long practice to the
breath, so that *good* and the first pale kindling at the rim were the same event, and every year it took
the whole crowd the same way, a sound going through them that was not quite a cheer and not quite a sob,
the sound a people makes when the world proves faithful one more time. The made sun climbed. It touched the
ice wall and the ice went to gold and rose, cliff on cliff of it, the whole ringed edge of the world burning
soft and holy in the new light, and Neva stood in the middle of everyone she loved and wept a little, easily,
the way you're allowed to weep at the Lighting, and thought: *this. Whatever else. This is true.*

She would think of it after, out on the round Earth, under a sky with no roof — she would think of the
Lighting and the gold ice and *and it was good*, and she would understand a thing that had no name yet on
this morning: that she had not been wrong to weep. That the day *was* good. That the light *was* faithful.
That everything the caste had said into the dark had been true — every word of it — and that the only thing
the book had not told them, the only thing, was how much bigger *the ends of the earth* would turn out to
be.

But that was after. This morning she only wept at the beauty of her small true world, and did not know it
was small, and was happy.

---

They feasted the rest of the day, and it was the good kind of day where the work stops and the caste is
just itself. There was bread and the once-a-turning honey and Ouma Renske's beer that could strip the
frost off a panel, and the young ones ran mad through the fields, and old Tobias, who had been careful with
her since the record room and would not quite meet her eye, unbent enough by afternoon to fall asleep in a
chair with his mouth open while the children drew on his face, which was tradition. And in the long warm
gold of it Neva let herself forget the stone. It was possible, on the Lighting, to believe that Ines had
simply gone to the deep fields, and that the record had simply been tidied by some careful hand, and that
the wall was simply the wall, and that *nothing at all, past the ice* was simply *true*, the way it had been
true her whole life, and good.

It was Sarel, the eldest, who found her in the low of the evening, when the track was setting and the feast
was going soft and sleepy. He sat down beside her without asking, the way the old are allowed, and for a
while he said nothing, and watched the gold ice go to rose and then to grey as the light went down the
western rim.

"Your father read this feast for eleven turnings," Sarel said at last. "Best voice we ever had at the rim.
When he said *and it was good* you'd swear the maker heard it personally." He was quiet. "I've read it nine
turnings now and I still say it a little the way he did. Stole it off him. He'd have laughed."

"He would," Neva said. Her throat was tight.

"He loved this world," Sarel said. "That's the thing about your father the young ones won't know now. He
read too long at the wall and he heard whatever it is the good ones hear, and it didn't make him love the
world *less.*" The old man turned and looked at her, and his eyes were not frightened the way Tobias's had
been; they were only very old, and very sad, and very kind. "It made him love it *more,*" Sarel said. "You
remember that, if you ever — " He stopped. He seemed to decide something, and to decide against it, and to
settle for less. "You remember your father loved this world right up until the end of it," he said. "Both
ends of it. You hear me, girl?"

*Both ends of it.* Neva sat very still.

"I hear you," she said.

Sarel patted her knee, once, an old man's blessing, and got up with the small grunt of his age — and
then he paused, standing over her in the last of the light, and she felt him weighing something, the way
a keeper weighs a reading he is not sure he should write down. When he spoke again his voice had gone
very low, and very careful, and not frightened the way Tobias's had been, but deliberate, like a man
setting a thing down gently in a place where it might be found later.

"There were keepers before us," he said, "who thought a clear hand was too fine a thing to spend on half
a truth. You'd not have known them. They're long past the deep fields now, every one." He looked at the
firmament coming black overhead. "But a thing that's written in a clear hand doesn't die when the hand
does. It gets folded up small and it waits. It's a patient thing, a page. More patient than the people
who carry it." He brought his old eyes down to her, and held her there. "If ever the world puts one in
your hand, girl — you read the *whole* of it before you decide it's mad. You hear me? The whole of it.
Your father would've told you the same. He read the whole of everything. That's what it cost him."

And that was all. He would not say more; she could see he had already said more than he had meant to, and
was a little afraid of it, and covered the fear by patting her knee a second time and telling her to eat
before Ouma Renske's beer took the roof off the young ones. He went crooked and unhurried back toward the
lamps.

Neva sat alone at the rim a long time with the firmament black and close above her and the ground solid
and unquestionable beneath her, turning the old man's words over. *Read the whole of it.* The whole of
what? The book was the book; there was no more of it; the verse closed at *and it was good* the way it
always had. She did not understand him. She would not understand him for two more days — not until a
folded page waited for her under the salt-crock in her own kitchen, patient, keeping, the way everything
in the Vault was patient and kept — and then she would understand him all at once, and completely, and
wish to God she didn't.

But that was after. This evening she only sat with the stone come back, changed by the day — because she
had spent the whole of the Lighting learning again how much she loved this world, and a person does not
walk away easily from a world she loves.

That, she would understand much later, was the cruelest kindness of it. They had not kept the caste sealed
with fear. Fear you can fight. They had kept them with *love* — with gold ice and warm bread and *and it
was good* said in the dark by the people you come from — and there is no fighting that at all. You can only,
if you are your father's daughter, and if the wall has begun to talk to you too, love the world enough to
need the truth of it.
