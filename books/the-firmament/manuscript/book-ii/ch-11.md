# Chapter Eleven
### *Book II · Two thrones · close-third, Marika*

The packet sat on the long table for nine days, unopened, and Marika fed it.

Not food. Attention — the currency her table actually ran on, the one nobody named. Every soul who
sat down to eat sat down in sight of it: a flat sealed thing with Neva's name on it in that even
clerk's hand, squared to the table's edge because Elsabé could not help herself, and it changed the
room the way a stranger at the fire changes a room. Conversations bent around it. Steve had tried
three jokes at it and retired all three, which Marika marked, because Steve retiring a joke was the
werf's most reliable barometer of real weather. And Neva — Neva ate opposite it every day, level,
unhurried, as though the not-opening were a task she had been assigned and meant to complete
honourably; and only Marika, who watched the whole table the way Hettie watched breathing, saw what
it cost, and said nothing, and topped her bowl.

The child was well. That was the other thing at the table, the bright thing, and it had a shadow.
The small girl was back at her lessons with opinions intact, the fever-page of the ledger closed,
the werf's gratitude real as bread — and the arithmetic under the gratitude, which nobody said and
everybody carried, went: *the Laer's medicine did that. The counting man's shelf. We owe the voice.*
A debt of grain is a weight; a debt of a child is a lever. Marika had known that since before the
chest was opened, and she had watched Dawid know it, and she had watched him get quieter since the
crossroads in the particular way of a man doing sums he does not like.

Into that room, on the ninth night, the wire brought Reuel's next move.

---

It arrived as everything from the Laer arrived: reasonable, generous, and aimed.

PROPOSAL, the strip said. YOUR HARVEST IS SHORT-HANDED. YOUR TABLE OFFERED LABOUR IN TRADE. THE LAER
ACCEPTS THE PRINCIPLE AND REVERSES IT. WE SEND THREE PROVEN HANDS FOR YOUR HARVEST. WAGE: NIL.
REPAYMENT IN YOUR OWN COIN, BROTHER. THE ACCOUNT BALANCES.

"He's quoting you," Elsabé said, flat, into the silence. "Your doctrine, your words, your coin,
turned around and offered back with interest. It is beautifully done and my answer is no."

"Your answer," said Steyn, dangerously pleasant, from the far end. He had come in from the lands
with the dust still on him; the grain was standing heavy — the weather had held through the fever, barely — and every day of it
was a day nearer the turning. "I must have missed the vote that made it yours. We are three weeks from losing
grain we cannot afford to a harvest we cannot lift, and the only offer of hands on this whole dead
earth is on that strip, and the table's answer is *no* because the hands come from a man Elsabé
doesn't like the sound of?"

"The hands come from a man who counts," Elsabé said.

"The hands come with *arms attached*, and the grain doesn't care." Steyn was up now. Three
pass-overs and a season of cordons were up with him; Marika could see every one of them standing
behind his chair. "You drove to his crossroads. You banked his medicine. The child is alive because
the table said ja to that — but boots in our lands, suddenly the table says nee? Where's the line,
Dawid? Because from where I stand it moves every time, and it always moves *after* I've obeyed it."

And Dawid — Dawid, who had been silent down the length of the argument with the strip in front of
him, doing the sums — Dawid said: "We accept the hands."

The table went still. Marika felt the room tilt toward the head of it, as rooms did at Dawid's flat
announcements; and she heard, under her own breastbone, quite clearly, the sound of a
line being crossed — not the Laer's line; theirs; the one that ran down the middle of this family's
whole way of deciding things — and she put her cloth down on the board, and the sound of a damp
cloth on wood stopped the room the way no shout would have.

"No," said Marika. "We don't."

"Marika —"

"You can rota the lands, Dawid. You can rota the watch and the wall and the diesel and me, and I
will stand my hours like any soul on this werf." She was not loud. The big laugh was nowhere in it;
this was the other register, the one the werf heard perhaps once a year and obeyed faster than any
order. "But who sits at this table, who sleeps under this roof, who walks in these doors — that is
not a rota question, and it has never once been decided in this house by one voice, and it will not
start being decided that way tonight, on the say-so of a strip of paper from a man who counts
children. The head of the table is yours. The *table* is mine. And the table says: this house
decides who enters it the way this house has decided everything since the light failed — together,
out loud, and slowly."

The two thrones looked at each other down the length of the long table, in front of everyone, for
the first time in two years of never quite needing to; and it was Dawid — Marika would give him
this, always, in every telling — it was Dawid who inclined his head.

"The table stands adjourned," he said, "until it agrees," which was retreat and dignity in one
motion; and he took the strip and went out to walk his line in the dark, the way he did when the
sums would not come right; and Steyn put his chair back so hard it cracked against the wall, and
went out the other door, toward the barn, with his three pass-overs and his standing grain and his
unspent fury off to find something that would hold still long enough to be furious at.

What it found was Ronel.

---

She had come in at dusk from four days on the lines, dropped meat at the kitchen, and been headed
for the barn and her own company, as always; and Marika, carrying scraps out in the yard dark,
heard the collision start by the barn wall — Steyn's voice with all the table's splinters in it, *of
course you'd side with nobody, siding with nobody is the whole of what you are*, and Ronel's low
one coming back with the flat crack of a woman who has been travelled four days and owes no one a
soft answer, *and you'd side with anyone who'd have you, boetie, that's the whole of what you are*
— wound on wound, the two of the family who acted first and thought after, finally saying the things
the teasing had carried for two years; and Marika stood in the dark with her scrap bucket and made the call every
matriarch makes nightly and is never thanked for, which was: *not mine. Leave it.*

She left it. The voices dropped. She went in.

Nobody saw either of them until morning. The werf, which saw everything, decided formally to have
seen nothing; and Steve, at breakfast, studied the two empty places, and the barn, and the quality
of the silence, and closed his mouth on the best material of his entire career, and banked it,
which — Marika told Hettie at the wash-line — was the single greatest act of self-denial the family
had ever witnessed, and would cost Steve years off his life.

And before first light, before either throne had reconvened its table, Ronel's kit was gone off its
hook, and her rifle, and four days of biltong; and the gate stood the way she always left it,
latched, exact, and no note — Ronel had never once left a note — and the veld had her, the way the
veld always had her, except that this time every soul on the werf who looked at the empty hook knew,
without one word being traded, that it was not the lines she had gone out to check.

Marika stood at the window with the morning tea going cold in her hands, and counted her family the
way she counted it every dawn — the sick-room empty now, thank God and the counting man's shelf;
Tomas singing badly somewhere; the smallest girl's voice on the school path, arguing; Dawid already
out at his line, straight as a post nobody would relieve; one place empty at the far end of the
grain; one packet sealed on the table; one huntress somewhere north with a rifle and a thing in her
chest she would rather outwalk than name — and found the count, for the first time since the fetch,
short.

Her hands, banking the stove, were not quite steady. She noticed it the way you notice weather on
the far ridge — a fact for later, someone else's later — and put the kettle on, and set the bread,
and began, because somebody had to and the somebody had a name, the long day's work of holding a
house together with two thrones cracked and one chair empty and the grain still standing, heavy,
patient, indifferent as the light, waiting to be brought in by whatever family was left by Friday.

*Ons lag*, she told the kettle, dry-eyed, in her mother's voice.

The kettle, in its own time, sang back.
