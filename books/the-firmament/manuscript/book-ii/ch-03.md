# Chapter Three
### *Book II · The manuals · close-third, Jorik*

The founders had left them everything except the knowing.

That was how Jorik came to understand it, the summer the sky spoke: that the old world had died rich.
The Kloof's deep stores were full — not with treasure, or not what the stories called treasure, but
with the true kind: tools in oiled paper, wire on wooden spools, glass valves in their hundreds packed
in straw like eggs, machines with their handles worn bright by hands that were dust before his
grandmother was born. And with the machines, on shelf after shelf after shelf, in the driest room of
the deep stores, the founders had left the *manuals* — and it was the manuals, Ivo said, that were the
real inheritance. "A machine without its manual is a dead man's tool," he told Jorik, the first
morning, laying one flat on the bench with both hands the way Marika laid out dough. "A machine *with*
its manual is a dead man teaching."

Jorik had grown up on the book, and he knew a holy text when he saw one handled. They were bound
heavy, printed close, full of words nobody living could say and numbered verses
nobody living could follow — *section four, three, one: before energising the bus, verify* — and Ivo
read them the way the elders read the book, which is to say slowly, aloud, with his finger on the line
and a terrible patience, and would not skip, and would not guess, and when he came to a word he did not
know — *impedance*; *capacitor*; *earth*, which never meant the ground you stood on but some deeper
idea of it — he wrote it in his own grey notebook with a question mark, and did not pretend, and that
was the thing about Ivo that Jorik had decided to grow up into, if he could manage it. The elders, when
they hit a hard verse, had a way of making their voices rounder, as if sound could stand in for sense.
Ivo just said, "I don't know what this word is yet," and turned the page. The manual did not mind you
not knowing. The manual waited, like the book, like the
veld, for you to catch up.

"Why do they write it like this?" Jorik asked him once, over a page of numbered warnings. "Like they're
cross with you?"

Ivo considered the page. "They're not cross," he said. "They're afraid. Every line like that, somebody
died learning it." He tapped the verse that said, in the old close print, WARNING: LETHAL VOLTAGES ARE
PRESENT EVEN WHEN EQUIPMENT IS SWITCHED OFF. "That one's a grave," he said, and went on reading, and
Jorik looked at the line and the line looked back and he understood — the way understanding came to
him, slow and once and for keeps, like a post going into ground — that the manuals were exactly what
the elders always said the book was, and that nobody had ever needed to tell him to believe the book,
either. *Written by the dead, at cost, so the living wouldn't have to pay it twice.*

He was fifteen, that summer. He would keep the phrase his whole life. He put it in the good log that
night with the day's weather, because it seemed to him it belonged to the record of the world.

---

The werf, meanwhile, wanted water, and did not care that the sky had spoken either.

That was the summer's other truth: the spring-line was pulling thin, the lands were thirstier than
two summers of guessing had planned for, and down in the deep stores stood a pump — a beautiful iron
ox of a pump, the founders' own, with BOREHOLE 2 stencilled on her flank and her manual in the dry
room, complete — that would, if she ran, put the water problem to bed for a generation. Dawid wanted
the pump running the way other men want salvation. And up on the hill in the radio shack there was
the other work, the work with no water in it and no bread: the finding of one thin voice in all the
round world's worth of night.

The werf could not have both at once. It had one Ivo.

Jorik stood in the yard the morning it came to words — Dawid with the pump manual under his arm like
a writ, Elsabé with her slate, half the table gathered the way the werf always gathered when two
right things met head-on — and he watched the grown people argue priority, and noticed, because his
job those weeks was noticing what Ivo needed before Ivo did, that Ivo himself said nothing at all.
Ivo stood at the edge with a loop of heavy wire over his shoulder like a strange halo, waiting for
the argument to finish the way you wait for weather, and when Marika finally said, "Ivo. You're the
one being divided. Divide yourself," he blinked as though the question had woken him and said:

"The pump is three days. The loop is one night. And the pump doesn't drift." And then, when the yard
looked at him: "The water is under the ground. It stays. The skip changes with the seasons — the sky
that carries her is a summer sky. We find her *now*, while the finding's good, or we maybe lose the
hour that works and start over in the spring. Then I do the pump." He shifted the wire on his
shoulder. "Three days," he said again, to Dawid, in Ivo's version of diplomacy, which was arithmetic.

Dawid looked at him a long moment. "One night," he said. "Then the pump owns you."

"Then the pump owns me."

And that, Jorik wrote in the good log, was the whole of the great pump-and-loop quarrel of the summer
the sky spoke, which lasted four minutes and was settled by a man counting days out loud; though he
also wrote, because the log deserved the whole truth, that Tomas had stood at the kitchen door through
all of it with his arms crossed, saying nothing at enormous volume, because one night at the loop
meant one more supper with Ivo's chair empty, and some quarrels on the werf were never about pumps.

---

The loop was a thing from the manuals, and building it was the best night of Jorik's life to date,
which he was old enough to know said something about his life and young enough not to mind.

It was, Ivo explained, with the manual open between them on the shack bench, an aerial that *pointed* —
a great flat coil of wire on a frame you could turn, taller than Jorik, on a pivot Steyn had welded
that afternoon with the new-old welder that itself had come alive out of a manual a month ago; and the
pointing worked backward from what any sensible person would guess, which delighted Jorik beyond
reason. You did not turn it until the voice was loudest. You turned it until the voice was *gone.*

"The null," Ivo said. "The loop hears everything except along its own axis. Loudest is wide —" he
swept his hand flat, a whole horizon of maybe — "but *nothing* is narrow. You find the place she
disappears, and that's the truest arrow in the world."

Jorik turned this over. Outside, the light was going; the hour that worked was coming up the sky.
"You find her by losing her," he said.

Ivo stopped, with his hand on the frame. He looked at Jorik the way he looked at a clean solder
joint. "Write that in your log," he said. "That's better than the manual has it."

She came up at the top of the hour, faithful as the rota, thin and far and laughing at something
Steve had said the night before and been made to repeat. The hour that worked had walked earlier
through the summer as Ivo learned the band, until the whole werf could listen on the shack's speaker
of an evening, the way Jorik imagined the old world had sat around fires — and while she
talked, Ivo turned the great loop, gently, gently, an inch and an inch and an inch, with his eyes
shut and the phones on over one ear, and Jorik watched the strangest and holiest thing he had yet
seen in the new age of the world: a man swinging a door of wire through the dark, hunting the one
narrow place where a woman vanished.

He did not find it. Not on the first pass, nor the second — the frame had a wobble the pivot had
promised not to have, and every time it settled, the null smeared wide: a whole dark quarter of the
world where she was merely fainter, which was useless. "A mood," Ivo said through his teeth, "not a
measurement." So they took the great frame down, the two of them, with the hour thinning and the
voice still talking somewhere above their bent backs, and shimmed the pivot with a strip of tin cut
off the tea-caddy, and hung it again; and Jorik learned, kneeling in the dark with cold fingers, the
other thing the manuals never said in so many words: the dead teach you the shape of the work, and
the work teaches you the rest, mostly by refusing.

On the third pass, Ivo's hand came off the frame. His eyes opened.

"There," Ivo said, hoarse, to the shack, to the werf, to the summer night. "Hold there. Mark it."

The frame's pointer stood on the scale Elsabé had painted that afternoon in her small exact figures.
Jorik read it off and wrote it in the good log with a hand that shook a little, the first bearing of
the new world, and under it, because the log deserved the whole truth, the manual's own dry verse for
what had just happened, which Ivo made him copy out exactly:

*The null is bidirectional. A single loop cannot distinguish a signal ahead from a signal behind.*

"Meaning she's that way," Jorik said, pointing up the dark veld, "or exactly that way," pointing back
over his shoulder at the Kloof and everything behind it, and the arrow of the world split in his hand
even as he held it.

"Meaning," said Elsabé, from her crate, without looking up from the slate, "the manual is honester
than a hopeful boy with a pointer. Two lines cross. We take the second bearing from the ridge camp —"
she was already drawing it, the werf, the ridge, the long angle between — "or we build the little
aerial in the next section and the null gets a *front*. Either way: arithmetic, then walking. Not
walking, then hoping."

"Sense antenna," Ivo said, half to himself, already three pages ahead in the manual, his supper
untouched again by his elbow, and Jorik bent over the good log in the valve-light and entered the
summer's accounts as they truly stood: one pump, promised, three days. One bearing, held, one of two.
One woman, laughing, somewhere up one of two arrows in the dark. And the dead teaching the living to
find her, out of a dry room, at cost, so that nobody would have to pay it twice.

He dated the line. He was fifteen. It did not seem to him, that night, that the world had ever been
anything but this good.
