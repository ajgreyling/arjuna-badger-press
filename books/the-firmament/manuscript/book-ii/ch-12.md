# Chapter Twelve
### *Book II · Ronel gone · close-third, Ronel*

The veld did not ask her how she was.

That was the whole of why she went to it, had always gone to it, since she was nine years old and
first understood that the Kloof was a box full of voices and the voices fixed nothing. The veld
asked you real questions — water, direction, wind, how much light was left and what you meant to do
about it — and if you answered them right you lived, and if you answered them wrong it killed you
without once pretending it was for your own good. She trusted that. It was the only bargain she had
ever met that came with all its terms showing.

She made twenty kilometres the first day and did not think about the barn.

She thought about the barn on every one of them, which she filed under the same heading as weather:
present, noted, not negotiable, not now. What had happened had happened because she had let it, and
that was the unforgivable part — not him; her; the latch she had lifted herself — and the thing in
her chest was not shame and was not regret and she was not going to stand still long enough to learn
its right name. Her mother had a saying, from the Kloof, from the long tables where the voices had
argued a year about a dying light while her father sat by his lamp getting greyer and quieter and
was gone before the walking-out he had argued for: *talk is the roof leaking upward.* Her mother had
said it bitterly, and stayed at the tables. Ronel had heard it clearly, and left. Hands fixed
things. Voices promised. She had buried the first promise she ever meant to depend on when she was
fourteen; her father's, in the arguing year, was only the last. Her two hands had kept her alive
every day since and never once said a word about it.

So she walked her lines, and reset the far snares, and slept in the rock overhang above the second
river with her back against stone and her rifle across her knees, and was, in the manner of a woman
outrunning a name, entirely fine.

On the third morning she went up the spur to glass the northern country, and saw the sky, and
stopped being fine, because the sky had terms showing.

It stood along the whole north-west rim like a bruise coming up under skin — not cloud yet, not to
a kitchen eye, just a colour the horizon had no honest business wearing in harvest season, and under
it the air had gone still in the particular way she had felt exactly twice in her life, both times
before hail. She stood on the spur a long time, reading it, giving it every chance to be wrong. It
was not wrong. Two days, maybe less if the wind came round early. Hail on standing grain. The
whole werf's winter, flattened into mud in an afternoon — the beans, the bread, the winter of every
soul at that long loud arguing table she had walked out of.

Nobody commanded her. That fact stood with her on the spur, the way it always stood with her, her
oldest companion. She could stay out the storm in the overhang, dry, provisioned, beholden to
nobody; the werf was not hers to save; the werf had Dawid's rotas and Elsabé's slate and a boy who
wrote everything down, and voices, so many voices, and she had told them all with her feet what she
thought of voices. Nobody would blame her. Nobody would know.

She was off the spur and moving south inside the minute — not for the voices; she made herself say
it plainly, in her mother's bitter grammar, somewhere in the first running kilometre — for the
beans. For the small girl's bread. For the winter. Because the werf could not read sky and she
could, and a hand that can fix a thing and doesn't is just a voice with fingers.

The rain caught her at the second river at dusk, the first outriders of it, fat and cold; and the
riverbed stones, which had kept faith with her boots for two years, turned their coats in the wet,
and she went down hard in the crossing with her leg opened on a split edge from knee to shin — a
long ugly shallow-deep gash, the kind that argues — and lay in the shallows a breath with the rain
on her face, doing the arithmetic she had done her whole life: alone, hurt, twenty kilometres out,
weather coming.

"Ag," said Ronel, to the river, to the rain, to the name she was not learning, and got up, and
bound the leg with her spare shirt, walking already, because the arithmetic had one line in it that
had never been there before and she did not look at it directly: *they need to know about the sky
more than I need to be fine.*

She made the werf two hours before midnight, wet through, grey with the leg, and put her head into
the kitchen where the family was sitting up not-talking about her, and said, from the doorway,
dripping, in the voice she used for terms:

"Hail. Day after tomorrow, maybe sooner. All of it. Get the grain in."

And then, because the room had come up out of its chairs at the sight of her, and because the leg
chose that moment to finish its own argument, she added, conversationally, "Somebody catch the
lamp," and sat down on the floor.

---

She woke in the sick-room with her leg stitched and dressed and one lamp lit, and Hettie in the
chair, sewing, not looking at her.

Ronel took stock the way she took stock in the veld: rifle — corner, cleaned, out of the wet. Boots
— dried, by the stove, stuffed with paper. Leg — throbbing, properly; twelve stitches, she judged,
maybe fourteen; the dressing tight and right. Whole werf — audible through the wall, a controlled
roar of lamplit purpose, Dawid's parade voice carrying over it, the yard full of wheels and hands:
the harvest going in by night, every soul on the lands, the rotas redrawn around her sky the way
the whole house had once crowded into a shack around a voice. They had believed her before the
lamp hit the floor. Something in her chest shifted its weight at that, and she filed it, not now.

"Fourteen stitches," said Hettie, to her sewing, having felt her wake the way Hettie felt
everything. "You'll keep the leg and you'll keep the scar. I've had worse off the plough." She bit
her thread. "There's meat and bread on the sill. The tea's the willow one, drink it anyway."

That was all. That was, for a long time, entirely all. Hettie sewed. The lamp burned. The werf
roared softly on the far side of the wall, getting the winter in. And Ronel lay in the clean bed
with her leg full of another woman's exact and careful work, waiting for the rest of it — the
questions, the *why did you go*, the *you could have died*, the *talk*, the leak in the roof — and
it did not come, and it went on not coming, and she understood, somewhere past the second cup of
bitter tea, that it was never coming. That the woman in the chair had known her whole shape from
the first week — had fought her treaty-silent over a warm bowl for two years — and had decided,
long ago, in whatever ledger she kept, that this one you feed and stitch and never ask. That care,
from Hettie, could arrive with all its terms showing and not one of them named *need.*

Her mother had been wrong, Ronel thought, drifting, the willow-tea winning. Not about the roof.
About the direction of the leak.

"Hettie," she said, at the edge of sleep.

"Hm."

"Fourteen's good work. On a moving patient."

"You held still," Hettie said, "for you," and bit her thread again, and that was the whole of the
conversation, and it was the longest one Ronel had had in years.

---

The hail came a half-day early and found the near lands stripped and the grain in the barn — wet,
half of it green-cut, racing its own damp now on every drying floor the werf could clear, but IN,
out from under the sky, brought in by lamplight on the word of a woman who had walked twenty
kilometres on an open leg to deliver a weather report. The far field they could not lift in time
went down under the ice where it stood, ten minutes, a winter's tithe flattened into the mud; and
the drying floors would need hands for a fortnight that the werf did not have, which nobody said
aloud in the barn door, because the strip with its three proven hands still lay on the adjourned
table, and everybody could count. But the most of it was in. The werf stood in the barn door
together and watched the sky fall on the stripped lands in white sheets, hammering the stubble
flat, beating the roofs like the drill-count of something enormous, and there was cheering,
and there was Steve announcing that Ronel had personally arranged the entire storm for the
attention, and there was, at the sick-room window, one huntress on one chair with her leg propped,
watching the ice eat the world that had not got her family after all.

Steyn brought her plate that evening. Knocked on the doorframe first, which he had never once done
in his life, at any door. Set it on the sill, said nothing about the barn, nothing about the gone
days, nothing about anything — just, "The small one saved you the crust. Apparently you're the
reason there's bread," — and left, the way you leave a rock-rabbit skin folded over a rail: a thing
given, deniable, in the only currency the receiver banks.

Ronel ate the crust. Outside, the hail wore itself out against the stones of the yard, and melted,
and steamed; and the name she had walked twenty kilometres to outrun sat down quietly in her chest
next to the stitches, and stayed, and she let it, not now being, she found, a smaller country than
it used to be.
