# Chapter Nine
### *Book II · The resource cliff · close-third, Hettie*

The fever came up the valley with the cut grass, the way fevers do, and it went for the children
first, the way fevers do, and Hettie met it at the door the way she met everything: with clean cloth,
counted breaths, and the ledger nobody knew she kept.

It was not a book, the ledger. It was not written anywhere except where it was written, which was in
Hettie, all the way down, in the place other people kept their prayers: every soul she had ever
nursed, and what had come of them. The column of the mended was long and she never read it. The other
column had four names in it, and she knew the weight of each to the grain — the two old ones the
first winter out, that nobody counted against her except herself; the baby that had never properly
started; and the first name, the oldest, the one from inside the Kloof, from before, the one she had
sat with for eleven nights when she was seventeen and newly trusted with the nursing, and lost on the
twelfth, and had been paying for ever since, in the currency of everyone else. Nobody on the werf
knew the first name. Marika knew there was a name. That was as far as anyone had ever been let in.

So when the first child came in hot — the school's smallest, a girl of six with opinions, whom the
whole werf privately considered its own — Hettie put her hand on the small burning forehead
and felt, under her own steadiness, the old ledger open to its short column, and said, aloud, to the
kitchen, in the voice that ended discussions: "Right. Boiled cloth and the willow-tea, and somebody
fetch me the school register. We'll have the lot of them by Friday."

They had the lot of them by Thursday.

---

It was the worst possible week, which Elsabé said afterward was not luck: harvest and fever travel
together, cut grass and close quarters and tired bodies, the old books said so. The lands stood
heavy and ready and the hands to bring them in were sponging children in the long room instead, or
were children, or were down themselves — Tomas went down on the second day, dramatically, protesting
love for everyone from his cot until Hettie threatened to dose him for that too — and for the first
time since the walking-out, the werf met the arithmetic that has no mercy in it: too few hands, too
many needs, and the light going down on standing grain.

Dawid did what Dawid did. The rotas redrew overnight — harvest crews halved and doubled up, the
sick-room its own watch now with its own roster, the ration tightened one notch "before we need it,
so we never need two" — and the werf obeyed, and grumbled, and obeyed; and the blocs, which the
vinyl night had melted into one warm room, hardened again along their old seams under the pressure,
quietly, the way ice comes back to a pane. Steyn fought the quarantine line twice — not out of
foolishness; out of Steyn: there were hands needed in the lands and he had two and the cordon said
no — and lost twice, publicly, to Dawid's flat "The line holds or the fever harvests us instead,"
and took it badly the second time, in front of the crews. And Ronel — Ronel simply did not attend
the cordon. She came and went by the veld as she always had, left meat at the kitchen and medicine
plants from the kloofs at the sick-room window, slept in the barn away from everyone, and when Dawid
confronted her at the gate with the line and the list and the law of it, she looked at him with
those unhurried yellow-brown eyes and said, "Your line is for people who live inside it," and was
over the fence and gone before the sentence found its verb.

"She's right and she's wrong and I cannot afford either," Dawid said at the table that night, to
nobody, with his knuckles on his eyes; and Marika, who had been on her feet since the first hot
forehead — cooking for the well, sitting with the sick, holding the whole frightened werf together
with the big laugh gone quiet — Marika said, "Eat, Dawid," and he ate, and Hettie watched the Queen
burn one more candle-width down and said nothing yet, and moved her along the private ledger's
watch-list one place nearer the top.

---

The white tablets were on the Laer's list. That was the thing. Everyone knew it, and nobody said it,
for three days.

Hettie had read that list the way Ivo read manuals — once fast, once slow, once at night by herself
— and she could have recited the fever-page from memory: the founders' clinic-wing had made and
sealed the things her own stores gestured at, the true febrifuge, the lung medicine, the small white
mercy her willow-tea imitated the way a lamp imitates the day-track. Between the werf and that shelf
stood a mountain range, a man who counted souls before he asked names, and the family's whole
careful doctrine of *slowly.*

On the fourth day, with the small girl's fever standing at the top of its arc and not coming down,
Hettie put her cloth in the basin, walked to the long table where the Court sat not-saying it, and
said it.

"I want the Laer's medicine. I want it on the wire tonight."

"Hettie —" Elsabé began.

"I know what it costs." She heard her own voice and let it be what it was. "I have heard every word
said in this house about that man, and I have said some of them. He counts. He keeps. His mercy
scales, God help us all. And I am telling this table that I have four names in me already, and if
that child's fever does not break by Sunday there will be five, and I will not —" and there it was,
out at last, seventeen years old and never once said aloud in the daylight, "— I will *not* lose
another one to a principle. Vote your caution on the grain, on the maps, on the survey, on anything
that is not breathing. Not on her."

The table was quiet. Neva was looking at Hettie with an expression Hettie could not read and did not
try to; she had her own dead world behind her, that one, and her own arithmetic about keeping and
losing. It was Dawid who spoke, and what he said surprised the table, though it did not, somehow,
surprise Hettie, who had watched him eat with his knuckles white all week.

"We ask tonight," Dawid said. "But we do not receive. We *buy.*" He looked around the table, and his
voice had the parade flatness that meant the thing underneath it was anything but flat. "Filtered
diesel. Seed. Salt. Labour at their harvest if they'll take it — they are short-handed too; nobody
out here is not. We pay a fair price and we set it ourselves and we put it on paper. Because the day
this werf takes a gift from that voice," and nobody at the table needed him to finish it, and he
finished it anyway, quietly, into the quiet: "is the day the roster starts learning our names."

---

Reuel took the proposal with what sounded, over the wire, like genuine professional pleasure — "A
people who insist on paying. Sister Hettie, your table does you credit" — and named a price so fair
that Elsabé spent an hour after sign-off trying to find the trap in it and reported, with the deep
suspicion of a woman handed a clean audit, that there wasn't one. The trucks would meet the Laer's
vehicle at the old crossroads on the founders' maps, two days out, neutral ground, three souls a
side. The Laer would bring the medicine, sealed, with the clinic-wing's own assay sheets. The Kloof
would bring the diesel and the seed.

"Three souls a side," Steyn repeated, at the table, flexing his grievance from the cordon like a
man testing a mended bone. "Whose three?"

"Mine," said Dawid, "to decide," which settled nothing and postponed everything, and the table broke
up into the night's watches with the first meeting between the living colonies of the recovered
world set, dated, and priced — a thing that should have felt like history, Hettie thought, and felt
instead like weather coming.

She went back to the sick-room. The small girl was asleep, hot, breathing in that shallow way that
Hettie's hands knew better than her hopes did; and Hettie wrung the cloth and laid it on, and sat,
as she had sat all her life, in the low lamp, in the space between the mended column and the other
one, keeping the watch that was never on any of Dawid's boards — the longest rota on the werf, and
the oldest, and the only one with a single name on it, written seventeen years deep, renewed nightly,
never once relieved.

Two days, she told the fever, the way you'd tell a wolf you saw its eyes. Two days. Hold the line.
