# Chapter Sixteen
### *Book II · The choosing · close-third, Neva*

The Strand answered on a Thursday, in the first week of the new cold, and it answered laughing.

They had gone back to the scan the day after the Laer's dust settled — Ivo's proposal this time,
seconded from the sick-room chair, minuted by the boy: *the net is not one wire between two houses;
work the log* — and the fourth voice of the recovered world came up out of the roar not stiff, not
counted, not careful, but mid-argument, two speakers fighting over their own handset, a man and a
woman talking across each other about whether the calling voice was real, until the woman won the
instrument and said, breathless, delighted, in an accent that stretched every vowel like a net being
paid out: *"Kloof station, Kloof station, you are the third miracle of my life and my brother still
thinks you are the weather. Say something rude to him. He deserves it."*

They were fisher people, the Strand. Their vault was a dome under a headland, and their book — this
came out in the first hour, offered as freely as everything else they said — their book had taught
them the world was a bowl of water under a lid of sky, and the dry land a blessing floating on it;
and when their lid had cracked with age and they had walked out onto the actual shore of the actual
sea, their eldest had looked at the horizon curving away wet and enormous in every direction and
said — the woman told it like a family joke, like the best thing that had ever happened to anyone —
*"Well. The bowl is bigger than the bowl."*

Neva sat in the crowded shack hearing her own story told in a stranger's mouth — another
box, another true book, another walking-out; the third shape of the one kept thing — and caught
Ivo's eye across the valves, and understood that he was thinking what she was thinking, because on
this one wire they had always thought the same thing: *the log is full of these. The whole round
world is a shelf of sealed books, each one true, each one small, every one of them waiting to be
read into the commons.*

And the Laer ranges, said the cold new thought underneath, in a voice with no doubt in it. The Laer
ranges, and the Laer catalogues, and the Laer keeps.

Which was the night Neva decided to open the packet.

---

She did it the way the house did everything that mattered now: at the long table, all chairs, out
loud, and slowly. The packet had sat squared to the table's edge for a season, and she stood over it
that evening and said, "This came to me with my name on it, and I have let it teach the table what
patience looks like for as long as it has anything to teach. Now I want witnesses," and she slit the
seal with Marika's bread-knife, in front of everyone, and unfolded what the man in the mountains had
judged worth two days of road.

It was a page. Hand-copied, old, soft at its folds — the softness she knew better than any soul
alive, the softness of a thing carried against bodies through years of fear — and the hand that had
copied it was no hand she knew, and the verse on it was no verse she knew, and yet she knew it, the
way you know a cousin at a stranger's funeral. *...and the waters are gathered under the bowl of the
world, and the deep is His, and the dry land stands upon the deep, and it does not founder...* A
round book. A wet book. Some drowned vault's kept and cut and carried scripture, catalogued out of
the dark by the men who resealed the doors "out of respect" — the Strand's cosmology, or a sister of
it, in a dead cousin's hand.

Under it, one line, in the clear even script she had first seen on a packet a season ago:

*There is more book than any one vault kept, sister. The Laer ranges. Come range with it. — R.*

The table was very quiet. It was, Neva thought, the finest lure ever fitted to a fish: not power,
not safety, not even company. *The rest of the sentence.* He had watched her across a fence for one
evening and read her to the floor of her — the woman who had walked through the ice for half a
verse — and he had baited the hook with the one thing on the round earth she was hungry for, and
the terrible generous accuracy of it was that the bait was real. The pages existed. The Laer had
them. The Laer would go on having them, vault by resealed vault, until the last kept book on the
round world sat catalogued in a mountain school, read by appointment, under discipline.

"He's offering you the libraries," Elsabé said quietly. "Every dead vault's book. Yours to read —
inside his walls."

"Ja," Neva said. She was looking at the drowned verse, the cousin-hand, the softness of the folds.
*Somebody had counted the cost of the woman with no name and paid it again, and again.* Every page
that had ever mattered in her life had come to her through hands that gave it away at risk, for
nothing, because a true thing kept secret goes small. That was the whole war, she understood,
standing at the table with the Laer's gift in her fingers: not gates, not physicians, not grain.
*Who holds the books.* The pen would keep them safe and dole them out as wages. The net would set
them loose and trust the world to carry them.

"Ivo," she said. "At the top of the hour, the common frequency. Everyone who can hear."

Elsabé looked at the page a long moment — the woman whose slate had taught the net that the wire
lies. "The wire still lies," she said at last. And then: "Read it twice."

And that night, with the whole werf in the shack and the Strand's far couple listening and whatever
else listened in the roar, listening, Neva of the two worlds took the handset and read the drowned
vault's verse onto the net aloud, slowly, twice — Elsabé's twice — for any hand that wanted to copy
it — provenance, cousin-hand,
folds and all — and then said, to the open air, to every sealed and waking box on the curve of the
world, in the voice her caste had trained on frost-lines and her family had trained on arguments:

"The Kloof holds this page in trust for whoever lost it. It is not ours. It is not anyone's. Any
station that holds pages, of any book, is asked to read them onto the net at the top of any hour —
the Kloof will keep the common copy, open, and read it back to any soul who asks. The books belong
to the net. That is the Kloof's answer, and the Kloof's invitation, and the whole of its terms."
She paused, and the roar breathed, and she finished it, dry as Elsabé, kind as Marika, in galgehumor
learned at this very table: "Rangers welcome. Bring pages. Leave boots at the gate."

Steve laughed out loud in the lamplight. The Strand laughed on the far end of the wire, both of
them, over each other. And somewhere in the mountains, if the schedule held — and his schedule
always held — a man with the calmest eyes on the round earth was listening to his own lure being
read into the commons; and she had meant him to hear every word of it, the whole conversion, page
by page, treasury into library, in the voice of the one soul he had judged worth two days of road.

The Laer's answer came at its exact scheduled hour, and it was one word:

NOTED.

"He's furious," Elsabé said, with satisfaction, reading the strip.

"He's *filing*," Neva said. "It's worse."

---

They planned the spring at that same table, in the weeks that followed, and Jorik minuted it all,
and the shape of the minutes was the shape of the family the year had made — the old sides of the
table not dissolved; grown into offices. Dawid and Elsabé built the net's slow scaffolding: the protocol
published open on the common frequency — challenge-phrases, vouching, the papered trust, the whole
of it given away exactly as Neva had given the page, so that no station, ever, would stand at the
head of the net the way a keeper stands at a gate. Steyn and Ronel — who argued now the way other
people danced, and fooled nobody — mapped the road north for the spring expedition: the vault, the
seeds, the radio room in the flank of Neva's sleeping world, and whatever waited past it. Hettie
provisioned everything twice. Tomas planned the singing. Marika, walking a little further each
week with the small girl for a warder, presided over all of it from whatever chair the sun had
warmed, spending her rationed strength like a woman who had finally learned the price of the
currency; and Steve took formal odds on whether the Strand's brother and sister would kill each
other before midsummer, and gave them out loud, on the net, to the parties concerned, who bet
against each other immediately.

And Neva, at the top of every hour, read pages into the dark — theirs, the drowned vault's, and
then, in the third week, a page that came *back*: the Strand's woman reading onto the
net a verse of the flat disc's book — *he hangeth the earth upon nothing* — copied from the common
stock, carried now in a fisher's mouth on a shore Neva would never see, alive again in the world,
uncatalogued, unkept, loose.

She sat in the shack a long time after that one, with the phones warm on her ears.

*Keep,* she had told her sleeping world, with her palm on its flank. She had thought, then, that
keeping meant holding. It didn't. She had it in the log by morning, and the log keeps it, and the
net does too, because the net heard her say it at the top of the hour, in two languages, laughing:

The sentence is not kept by the vault that holds it. It is kept by every mouth that lets it go.
