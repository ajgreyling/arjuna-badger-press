# Chapter One
### *Book II · The far end of the wire · close-third, Ivo*

The set was honest. That was the whole of why Ivo loved it, and the whole of why he was there at
midnight instead of asleep: a radio did not say anything it had not heard.

People said things they had not heard all day long. People told you it would be all right, and that the
harvest would hold, and that they were fine, thank you, and half of it was hope wearing the clothes of
knowledge, and you could not fault them for it and Ivo did not — he simply preferred the bench. The set
had one virtue and it was total: silence meant silence. Two years he had sat the night watch in the
hardened room the founders had built into the flank of the Kloof, with the valves warming the dark like
a row of small patient suns, and the set had never once lied to him. It had given him the roar — the
vast soft breathing roar of the open air between the worlds, which he had loved from the first night
like a man hearing the sea in a shell the size of the sky — and it had given him nothing else, because
there was nothing else, and it was honest about that, every night, all night, no matter how long he
listened.

*Nil heard.* He had written it in the log so many times the letters had worn into a single tired shape.
The log went back further than the Kloof's own memory: column after column in the founders' hands, the
call-signs of the other stations — a world of them, once — and beside each, down the years, the same
patient terrible word. *Silent. Silent. Silent.* The last founder to keep the log had kept it to the end
of an old shaking hand, and after that there were no entries for a very long time, because after that
nobody in the Kloof had remembered the room existed; and then there was Ivo's hand, two years of it,
young and cramped and stubborn: *nil heard, nil heard, nil heard.*

"You can't miss what answers," he told the set, most nights, companionably. "And you never answer. So."

He said such things aloud because the room was the one place nobody marked him for it. Machines were
honest and they did not mind you talking; it was people who wanted the talk to *go* somewhere. The werf
was full of good people who wanted his chair at the long table and his face at their fires, and Ivo
loved them the way he loved the veld — sincerely, from a workable distance — and near all of them he
felt the old tiredness of a man being read; and here, in the valve-glow, with the roar in the phones and
the night band open over the dark of the whole turned world, he felt what he had never once managed to
explain at that table: *company.* The roar was not nothing. The roar was the sky itself, breathing. It
carried no voices, but it was the kind of silence that could — and a silence that could is a different
thing entirely from a silence that can't, and if that made no sense to anyone but him, well. It was
midnight. The bench was his.

The Kloof stood behind the werf in the dark, and Ivo did not look at it, the way none of them looked at
it much anymore: the great buried curve of the old world, grass growing on its flank now, its doors
standing open to the weather like something exhaled. He had been born inside it, in the long lamplit
valley of it, where the light-line ran down the roof of the world from end to end and the ground curved
up on both sides, because the world was a kloof — everyone had known that; it was in the book — a deep
kept valley cupped in the maker's two hands, and you lived in the palm of it, and the walls curved up
because hands do. Then the light-line had begun to fail. Not all at once; the way old things fail: a
flicker at the far end, a cold morning, a dimming you could argue about until you couldn't. And the
Kloof had done the thing Ivo was proudest of his people for, prouder the older he got: they had argued
about it, at the long tables, for a year — and then they had gone and *looked.* All of them. Not one
heretic alone in the dark; the whole stubborn lot, elders in front, children on shoulders, up the dead
end of the valley to the place the book called the world's end, with lamps and bread and the arguing
still going — and they had found the door, and opened it, and walked out onto the wide flat shining
veld under the roofless sky, and stood there, hundreds of them, in the wind and the terrible light.

His mother had told him what the eldest said, that first morning, into all that size. The old man had
looked at the veld running flat away forever — flat, *flat*, where every verse said the world curved up
— and at the sky coming down to meet it in a great pale bowl at the edge of seeing, and he had stood
there a long time with his book in his hand, and then he had said, in the voice he used for the
lighting of the lamps: *"He cupped His hands. We had only never seen the size of His hands."* And the
Kloof had taken the world the way it took everything, arguing, all the way down to the good soil —
some saying the book had lied, and some saying the book had been cut, for the old ones' books did not
all agree and there were pages the elders would not read aloud; and most, like his mother, like Ivo,
saying the book had been *true of the valley*, and the valley had been true of nothing but itself, and
a true thing was not a lie just because it turned out to be small.

Two summers, now, they had been out. The werf built, the lands broken, the flock got up from the wild
sheep of the heights; Ronel's snares on the night-lines, Marika's kitchen the new centre of the world,
Dawid drilling his watch rotas that guarded against nothing anybody had ever seen. And under it all,
for Ivo, this room — found the first winter, wept over, resurrected coil by coil — and the log with its
columns of the silent dead, and the roar of the empty listening sky, and *nil heard*, in his own hand,
two years deep.

He had the phones on and the tea going cold when Tomas came with the plate.

"You missed supper," Tomas said, from the doorway, in the way he had of making a fact sound like a
wound. "Again. Hettie kept it back for you before Steyn got his third helping, which is love, Ivo, that
is what love looks like in this family — it looks like mutton kept back from Steyn."

"Put it on the bench."

"On the bench, he says." Tomas came in and put it on the bench, and then did not leave, because Tomas
never left; he stood warming his hands at the valves as though the set were a hearth, looking at the
dials the way Ivo looked at people: fondly, and without the first idea how they worked. "One of these
nights," Tomas said, "you'll come sit at the table like a Christian, and the sky will just have to
manage without you."

"The skip's best after dark. You know this. I've explained the layer —"

"You've explained the layer." Tomas said it gently, and he touched Ivo once on the shoulder, the way he
touched everyone, the way Ivo never quite got out from under in time, and went to the door. "Eat the
food. If the dead want to talk to you, my friend, they've had two years of chances."

And it was there — with Tomas's boots still on the step, with the plate steaming by the key and the
tea cold and the log open to its tired column and Ivo half-turned in his chair to say something dry
about the dead keeping farmers' hours — it was exactly there that the roar broke.

A voice came out of the sky.

It came small and cracked and impossibly far, riding up out of the noise the way a lamp shows at the
end of a long dark lands-road, there and gone and there — a woman's voice, thinned to a whisper by
half a turning world, saying words in the old radio-tongue of the founders, and Ivo's whole body went
still except his hands, which were already moving, which had trained two years for a moment he had
stopped believing in, easing the dial a hair, holding her, holding her —

*"— anyone there,"* the sky said. *"This is one who walked out. Onto the round world."*

The plate steamed. The valves hummed. On the step, Tomas had stopped like a man shot.

*"The book is true,"* said the voice, from the far end of the wire, from the far end of everything,
cracked with distance and steady, so steady, the way you are steady when you have gone past all the
lesser fears into the one at the bottom. *"It hangs upon nothing, and it holds. If you can hear me —
if anyone can hear me — the ends of the earth go on forever, and it is good, and you are not alone."*

The roar closed back over it like water.

Ivo sat with his hand on the dial and did not breathe. Afterward — for the rest of his life, at every
telling, and there would be tellings until they buried him — he would say the same thing about that
moment, and it was the truest thing he knew how to say about himself, and the room, and the two years
of *nil heard*: that he did not doubt it for a second, and that this was the set's last gift to him
before everything changed. Anyone else in the werf, hearing a voice out of the empty sky, would have
had to fight down the thought that they had dreamed it, hoped it, made it up out of loneliness. Ivo
had no such thought. The set was honest. The set did not say things it had not heard.

He took the key. His hand was shaking so hard that the first press was nothing, a stutter of noise,
and he took his wrist in his other hand the way you steady a tool, and pressed again, and heard his
own voice go out into the roar sounding nothing like himself:

"Ja," Ivo said, to the sky, to the dead band, to the one who walked out, wherever on the round world
she stood. "Ja. We hear you."

And then, because two years of night watches had after all built the discipline for exactly this, even
while the water stood in his eyes: "This is the Kloof station. Say again your position. Say — " and
then the discipline failed, and what came out instead, keyed white-knuckled into the dark between the
worlds, was: "You are not alone either. Do you hear. Whoever you are. You are not alone either."

---

Tomas's boots went off the step at a dead run, and Tomas's voice went up across the whole sleeping
werf like a bell: *"THE RADIO — IT'S THE RADIO — SOMEBODY'S ON THE RADIO —"* and lamps came alive in
doorway after doorway, and the room that had held one man and the roar for two years filled, in the
space of ten minutes, with the whole argumentative heart of the Kloof.

They came the way they were. Ivo marked it even then, even half-deaf with his own blood: that a thing
like this went through people like a lamp through glass and showed you their insides. Marika came
first, barefoot on the cold floor with her coat over her nightdress and took one look at Ivo's face
and did not ask *is it real*; she asked, "Is she well? The one talking — is she safe, is she fed?" —
because that was Marika, the whole werf could have told you, the sky itself opens and the Queen wants
to know if the sky has eaten. Dawid came dressed. Nobody could say how; the man slept in his rank. He
stood at the back with his arms folded and waited for the excitement to make a mistake, and when the
room got loud he said, in the parade voice, "One at a time. Ivo has the key. Nobody touches anything.
Jorik — the good log, and a pencil," and the room, which loved him and mocked him and needed him,
went quiet and orderly, and was glad of it, and would never once say so.

Steve arrived with his braces hanging and surveyed the crowded shack, the steaming forgotten plate,
Ivo's wet unashamed face, and said, "Two years. Two years of *nil heard*, and the night it finally
talks back, it waits till Ivo's supper is in front of him. That, friends, is how you know the universe
is run by a woman with a sense of humour." Which was for the room, to bleed the pressure off, and it
worked, the way it always worked; and under it, quietly, he put his hand on Ivo's shoulder and left
it there, and that was for Ivo.

Elsabé came last and stood in the doorway where she always stood, at the edge of things, where you
could see the whole board. She listened to the fragments Ivo had caught — *walked out · the round
world · the book is true · not alone* — and while the room lifted around her like a field in wind she
said, to nobody, in the cool flat voice that the Kloof had learned to be grateful for and had never
once enjoyed: "A voice is not a colony. We know what she said. We don't know what she is."

"Elsabé," said Tomas, wounded, wet-faced, glowing.

"I hope she's exactly what she sounds like," Elsabé said. "Hope is free. The watch rota isn't." But
she came in, all the same, and found a crate at the edge of the lamplight, and sat, and did not leave
either; and Ivo, who was the only one placed to see it, saw her write the frequency on the back of
her hand.

And Jorik — Jorik stood holding the good log and the pencil like the elements at a lighting, and
looked around at all of them with his young face wide open, and said the thing that the rest of them
were too old and too careful to say, the thing Ivo would put in the log verbatim because it deserved
keeping: "It was never just us. All this time. It was never just us."

---

She came back at the top of the hour, as the skip steadied, and they talked — if you could call it
that, the long-spaced hollering across the dark of the world, her voice surfacing and sinking in the
roar, Ivo working the dial like a man keeping a lamb alive through a cold night. Her name was Neva.
She had walked out of a world that was *flat* — flat, with a roof, a lit lid over a sealed disc, and
at that the whole shack turned as one to look at the dark window where the Kloof lay open on the hill,
and nobody said anything, because what was there to say; you heard the shape of your own story told in
a stranger's voice and the floor of you moved. Her people were still inside. Still sealed. Reciting —
she said it without bitterness, which silenced even Steve — reciting the door and calling it a wall.
She was alone. She had been alone on the round world for — the roar took the number, and Marika made a
sound low in her throat, the sound she made when a child on the werf fell badly, and reached over and
gripped the back of Ivo's chair.

Dawid leaned in once, took the key with a look that asked and a nod that granted, and said into the
sky, in the parade voice, which was the only voice he had for tenderness: "Neva. This is Dawid of the
Kloof. You will listen for us at this hour every night. Whatever else happens, we are on this
frequency at this hour every night. That is not a hope, that is a rota. Confirm."

The roar breathed. And then the voice came back, and there was something new in it, something that had
not been in it even in the steadiness, and Ivo heard what it was and had to look hard at the dials:
she was laughing. Cracked, far, half-drowned — laughing. *"Confirmed,"* said the one who walked out,
first soul of a dead world, from the far side of the night. *"A rota. You're real. Nobody would invent
a rota."*

"There's the proof Elsabé wanted," Steve said. "Send her Dawid. No ghost could survive him."

They lost her at the greying of the sky, mid-word, the way the band closes — no farewell, just the
roar folding back over the place her voice had been, and Ivo working the dial a long quiet minute
before he would admit the night was done. The shack emptied slowly, talking low, the way you leave
after a birth or a burial; Marika already planning aloud what could be *sent*, as though kilometres
were a river you could get a basket over; Tomas not even pretending he wasn't crying; Dawid last at
the door, looking back once at the set, and at Ivo, and saying only, "Good watch," which from Dawid
was a hug and a medal and a speech.

Then it was Ivo, and the valves, and the cold mutton, and the log.

He sat a long time before he wrote it. The lamp hissed. Outside, the werf's first fires were going up
against the grey, and somebody — Tomas, it could only be Tomas — was singing. Ivo turned back through
the old columns once, the way you touch a grave on the way past: the founders' clear hands, the
call-signs of the world that was, and beside them, down the long years, *silent, silent, silent*, and
the last old shaking entry, and the long blank dark, and then two years of his own cramped stubborn
*nil heard*.

He took up the pencil, and dated the line, and wrote, in the best hand he had ever managed in his
life:

*Heard: one who walked out. The book is true. She is not alone.*

And then, because the set was honest, and because the log was the set's memory and deserved the whole
truth, he added, in small letters, at the end of the line, where the founders used to put the signal
strength:

*Neither are we.*
