# Chapter Thirteen
### *Book II · Marika at the end of her strength · close-third, Tomas*

The morning Marika did not get up, the kitchen fire did not get lit, and Tomas learned that the sun
of a world can fail quietly, on an ordinary Tuesday, between one breath and the next.

She had gone down at the drying floor the evening before, mid-shift, mid-sentence — turning wet grain
with the second crew, doing the work of two because the werf was a fortnight short of hands and
telling nobody, being nobody's business but everyone's mother — and she had put out one hand to the
rail, and said, in a puzzled, reasonable voice, "Just give me a —", and not finished it, and folded.
Steyn had carried her in at a dead run. Hettie had met them at the sick-room door with her face
already wearing its working stillness, and the door had closed, and the werf had stood in the yard in
the dusk, all of it, the whole family, not talking, while behind that door the woman who held every
one of them was held, for once, by the only hands on earth with the right stubbornness for the job.

The heart, Hettie said, when she came out. Strained past its rated load and gone on strike — not
burst, not broken; *striking*, Hettie said, with the bitter precision of a woman naming a thing she
had watched coming up her private list for a season and been forbidden by its owner to say aloud.
Survivable. Mendable, even. "But the mending is rest," Hettie said, to the yard, to the family, in
the voice that closed subjects, "weeks of it, real rest, and rest is the one thing that woman does
not know how to draw from the stores. So we are going to learn to be a werf she can rest in. Starting
now. Go to bed."

Nobody went to bed. Everybody went to bed eventually, which is different; and Tomas lay in the dark
listening to the house not-sound the way a man listens to a stopped clock, and understood, with the
part of him that had always done the family's feeling ahead of the family, what was actually about to
be asked of them. Not grief. Grief they knew; grief had a liturgy. This was worse and stranger:
*maintenance.* The engine of the house had stopped, and every soul in it was about to find out
exactly which of their daily mercies had been that engine running — and they were going to have to
become, together, out of spare parts and bad guesses, the thing one woman had been alone.

---

They found out about the bread first.

Nobody knew the yeast. That was the discovery of the first morning — that the werf's bread had not
been a recipe, it had been a *relationship*, conducted at dawn between Marika and a crock of
something living that she fed and scolded and knew by smell, and in her absence the crock sulked and
the first three batches came out as bricks that Steve ceremonially tested by knocking on them and
declaring the barn now fully defensible. They found out about the small girl next: that she had been
doing her letters at the kitchen table every morning at Marika's elbow, and would not do them at
anyone else's, and sat instead outside the sick-room door with her slate, teaching herself, fiercely,
in exile. They found out that six different quarrels on the werf had never actually ended — they had
just been *held apart*, daily, by a woman who knew exactly whom to seat next to whom; the quarrels
came creeping back like damp. They found out the hens had a feeding order. They found out Dawid — this
one Tomas would keep — they found out Dawid could not reconvene the adjourned table without her,
would not, stood at the head of it on the second evening with the Laer's strip in his hand and
looked down its length at her empty chair at the far end, and said, "It keeps," and put the strip
away; because a man may argue with his opposing throne, but he does not vote while she is lying
down. Even the fracture, Tomas thought, had run on her.

So Tomas did the thing he was for.

He could not rota it — he tried to explain it to Dawid, who wanted to rota it, who wanted to rota
his own helplessness into some shape that would obey him — you could not put *warmth* on a board.
But you could do it. He got up before light because someone had to talk to the yeast; he had watched
her do it half his life; the fourth batch was bread. He sat the small girl at his own elbow and drew
her letters wrong on purpose until she corrected him, which got them done. He learned the hens'
order by trial and insult. He seated the quarrels apart at supper by dint of enormous, transparent,
unashamed manipulation that everyone saw through and everyone allowed, because it was Tomas, and
Tomas's schemes had a licence Marika's tact had never needed. He wept openly at least once a day and
made no apology, which turned out to be its own service: the werf's grief needed somewhere visible
to happen, and Tomas, who had never guarded a feeling in his life, stood in for all of them the way
a lightning rod stands in for a roof.

The house held. Badly, sideways, at triple the effort for half the grace — but it held, and
somewhere in the second week Tomas caught the shape of the thing they were all building without
naming it: not a replacement for her. An *answer* to her. Proof, laid up day by burnt-porridge day,
that everything she had spent herself keeping had taken root — that the warmth was not hers on loan
to them but theirs now, clumsy and self-sustaining, and she could stop burning because the fire had
caught.

---

Steve broke on the ninth evening, and it was the bread that did it.

He had been immense for nine days. That was the only word Tomas had for it — immense: the jokes
riding shotgun on every hard hour, the barn-brick routine, the running gag of the betting book now
taking odds on which hen ruled the yard; nine days of a man deflecting a family's terror with his
own hands, joke by joke by joke, the way he had deflected everything since before anyone could
remember. And on the ninth evening at the long table, with the fourth batch — the good batch, the
bread that had finally come out as bread — steaming on the board in front of the Queen's empty
chair, Steve stood up with his mug to give the toast, the obvious toast, the *she'd be proud of
this brick, at least it's a soft one* toast, and Tomas watched him reach for it, the way a man
reaches for a rail that has always been there.

It was not there.

"To —" Steve said, and stopped. The room waited, grinning, cued. "To —" said Steve again, and his
face did the thing Neva had once described to Tomas from the vinyl night, the grin going out like a
lamp — except this time it did not come back, there was no record turning, no cover of horns, only
the good bread and the empty chair and nine days standing behind him with their full weight, and
Steve put his mug down very carefully, and put his hand over his eyes, and cried, at the table, in
front of everyone, the way a dam does not so much break as simply arrive at full.

Nobody laughed. Nobody moved to make it smaller. It was Neva who got up — Neva, who had been taught
to dance by this man at the end of the world, *it's the one thing they'd never forgive us* — and
went and stood behind his chair and put her arms down around him and her chin on his head, and said
nothing at all; and the family sat with its broken register in the bread-smelling lamplight and let
the man who laughed for all of them cry for all of them, at last, at the right cost, in the only
house on the round earth where both were the same work.

"Ag," said Steve, finally, wetly, from inside Neva's arms, to the table, to the bread, to the empty
chair. "Ons lag, né."

"Want anders huil ons," said the whole table, quietly, together, like the answer at a Lighting.

And both halves, that night, were true at once, which was the whole doctrine, and nobody had ever
had to live it all the way down before.

---

They brought Marika the evidence on the morning she was first allowed to sit up: the good loaf, the
small girl's slate of self-taught letters, the rotas kept, the hen politics stabilised, the werf
alive — carried into the sick-room in relays and laid on her blanket like tribute at a court, which
it was. She looked at all of it a long time. She looked longest at the bread.

"Who talked to the yeast?"

"I did," Tomas said. "It's rude. I see why you like it."

And Marika laughed — carefully, one hand flat on her own chest, minding the striker within, but the
big laugh all the same, the one that was half the reason the family held, rationed now like
everything precious — and then she lay back on the pillows and looked at them all crowded in the
door of the sick-room, her whole clumsy self-sustaining answer of a family, and did the hardest
thing the werf had ever watched her do.

She let them keep her.

"All right," said the Queen, closing her eyes, the fire caught, the watch relieved. "All right.
Show me tomorrow."
