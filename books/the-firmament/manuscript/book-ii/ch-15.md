# Chapter Fifteen
### *Book II · He refuses · close-third, Jorik*

Jorik had recorded every table the werf had ever held, and he knew before the first word that this
one would fill pages, because for the first time in the history of the good log, every chair was
full.

Marika sat at the far end in a nest of pillows, carried in by Steyn under Hettie's supervision and
Hettie's conditions, which had been negotiated like a treaty and were enforced from the chair at her
right hand. Ronel sat on the window sill with her leg propped and her back, for once, half-turned to
the room instead of fully. Neva sat opposite the sealed packet, as she had all season. Ivo had been
fetched from the shack and had come without argument, which measured the night better than any
instrument; Steve had his mug and none of his jokes out; Tomas could not sit at all, and stood
against the dresser with his arms wrapped around himself like a man holding a parcel of himself
together; and Dawid stood at the head of the table with the Laer's three strips laid out in front of
him in date order, straight as soldiers, and called the table to order by saying nothing until it
was quiet.

Elsabé fired first. Elsabé had been waiting all day to fire.

"Before anyone argues mercy or grain," she said, "arithmetic. His strip said *Reuel and four.* At
the gate he offered four hands for the drying *and* a physician. With himself that is six souls
camped at our fence, from a man who counts children in bands and has never in one season wasted a
word or miscounted a gram." She let it stand a breath, the way she let all her verdicts stand. "The
count is wrong because the count is a door. Four workers, a physician — and a sixth soul with no
named work, who is meant to come in with the rest and *stay.* A liaison. A resident. A pebble in
the shoe of this house forever. He is not offering help. He is offering a graft, and the graft is
wrapped in help, and he expected nobody at this table to count past the kindness." She sat back.
"Somebody else may now argue mercy and grain."

They argued mercy and grain. Jorik wrote it all, and the log would keep it: that it was a true
argument, all sides shown, nobody made small. Steyn — quieter since the barn, in a way Jorik had
noted in the margin and did not fully understand — spoke for the grain one final time and then, when
Elsabé's count sat unanswered in the middle of the table, said, "Ag," and folded his own case with
his own hands, which cost him, and everyone saw what it cost, and Dawid inclined his head to him
across the table as to an equal, which Jorik also recorded, because the log kept what mattered.
Hettie spoke last and shortest: "I want that physician the way the lands want rain. I have wanted
him since the fever." A breath. "That is exactly how I know what he is. You do not send a hook to
people who are not hungry. Nee." And she folded her hands, and it was done, and the table had not
voted so much as arrived, together, out loud, slowly, at the place its whole life had been walking
toward.

Then Dawid told them about the leak, because the answer at first light would have to answer that
too; and Jorik's world came apart in the space of one sentence.

*The boy's bulletins.*

He heard the rest through a roaring — Tomas's choir request, the open evenings, the whole warm
season of the werf on the wire filed name by name in a mountain school — but the words that stood
were his own: his proud reports, read into the dark at the top of the hour, the harvest counts and
the hen politics and *Marika is mending, God be thanked, she sat up today* — he had told them. He
had told the counting man everything, in joy, in his own careful successor-hand of a voice, and the
file the Laer kept on his family had Jorik's fingerprints on more pages than anyone's; and he sat
at the table he was recording with his ears on fire and waited, fifteen years old, for the shame to
be given its right name and its right owner.

It was Marika who saw his face. Of course it was Marika.

"Jorik." The Queen's voice, rationed and clear from the nest of pillows. "Look at me. You told the
sky that we sing, and that our grain came in, and that your Queen was mending. Every word of it
true, and every word of it *ours to be proud of.*" Her eyes went around the table, gathering it.
"The shame in this room does not belong to the boy who was glad out loud. It belongs to the man who
filed it. And if this family answers that man by teaching its children to be afraid of their own
joy, then he will have got through the gate without ever setting foot in the yard. Ja?"

"Ja," said the table, all of it, at once; and Neva, across from her packet, said, quietly, to
Jorik, in the voice of the one soul present who knew exactly what it was to have loved a keeper
with her whole heart before she knew what kept her: "Write that in your log. That one's for both
worlds."

He wrote it. His hand shook, and he wrote it.

---

They went down to the gate at first light all together, which was the answer before the answer, and
Jorik carried the good log, because Dawid had ordered it — "The record attends," was the whole of
the order — and the Court of the Kloof stood at its own fence line in the grey and gold of the
morning: the two thrones, one on a chair carried down over Hettie's formal protest and under her
armed supervision; the huntress on a crutch; the fixer, the heart, the sage, the magician, the
warrior, the walker, and the boy with the book. Reuel came up the track alone to meet them, and
Jorik, watching him take in the assembly — all of them, together, nobody's spokesman sent out
alone — saw the calm eyes do their counting, and saw the count, for once, come out in a shape the
man had not filed in advance.

"Brother Reuel," Dawid said. "The table has argued. Here is its answer."

And Jorik wrote the answer into the good log at the gate as it was spoken, and the log keeps it,
word for word, under the date, in a fifteen-year-old's careful hand gone steady:

"The trade stands. Grain for medicine, priced and papered, at the crossroads, as neighbours. The
gate does not open. Not to four hands, not to five, not to your physician, and not to the sixth
soul your strip forgot to count — the table counts too, brother. We are a house of argument. You
saw truly: it is slow, and it is loud, and one winter it may kill us. But it is *ours to risk.*
Every soul behind me stands at this fence because they argued their way to it and chose it — and
that choosing is the whole of the difference between a family and a flock, and we will not trade it
for hands, or medicine, or rest." A breath, parade-flat to the last. "Discipline is a thing I offer
this werf, Reuel. It is not a thing I do to it. That is the answer. Go well on the road."

Silence, in the grey and gold. And Jorik, pencil still moving, watched the calmest eyes on the
round earth look down the fence line — at the thrones, the crutch, the mugs, the boy — and find,
for the second time in two days, a fact that talked back.

"Well argued," said Reuel, at last, warm to the very end, and there was, Jorik would swear to it
all his life, something almost like regret in it — the regret of a keeper watching good stock
decline the pen, already patient for the winter that would change their minds. "The Laer keeps the
trade. The Laer keeps the schedule. And when the arguing fails you, brother — one bad winter, one
bad vote — the gate I keep is never shut." His eyes crossed the line once more and stopped, one
last time, on Neva. "Sister. The packet keeps."

"So does the table," Neva said.

And Reuel of the Laer inclined his head one degree, to her, to all of them, and walked back down
the track to his vehicles in the risen morning; and the engines woke, and the Laer went home along
the bones of the old road, correct to the last metre of it; and the Court of the Kloof stood at its
own fence in the gold light and watched the dust settle back onto the world, together, in the loud
slow arguing freedom they had just paid a physician and four harvests' worth of hands to keep.

"Right," said Marika, from her chair, into the enormous morning. "Breakfast. All of it. Jorik —"
the Queen looked at the boy with the book, and the boy looked back — "read us yesterday. From the
top. Loud."

He read them yesterday. From the top. Loud. And when he came to the line that was for both worlds,
the whole table said it back, the way you'd say amen — and that, too, the log keeps.
