# Chapter Ten
### *Book II · The crossroads · close-third, Neva*

Dawid chose himself, and Ronel, and — over Elsabé's flat opposition, delivered in front of the whole
table with both hands on her slate — Neva.

"He asks after her by name," Elsabé said. "She is the one thing on this band he has wanted since the
first contact. You propose to drive her two days up a dead road and park her within reach of his
people."

"Ja," said Dawid. "Where I can watch what his people do about it." He looked at Neva, and did her the
courtesy he did every soul he commanded, which was to hand her the true price openly. "You're bait,
walker. Bait that reads keepers better than any of us. I won't dress it prettier than that, and I
won't take you against your will."

"You couldn't leave me against my will," Neva said, which settled it, and Steyn, passed over a third
time in one season, stood up from the table and went out to check the trucks with a thoroughness that
frightened the chickens, and nobody went after him yet, because some debts have to be paid in their
own coin and Steve was already counting out the jokes it would take.

They drove out at first light: one truck, three souls, the trade goods roped under canvas — filtered
diesel in the founders' drums, seed in sealed tins, salt — and the werf seeing them off in
the yard the way the caste had once seen off the Lighting, which is to say everyone pretending it was
ordinary and nobody managing it. Hettie put a provision sack on the seat without one word about what
else rode on this journey, and her eyes said Sunday, and Neva took the sack and said, "By
Sunday," and they went.

The dead world by truck, with company, was a different world from the one she had walked. That was
the thing she had not expected, and she rode the first hours in silence with it: the same green
ruin, the same roofless enormous sky that had once nearly swallowed her whole — and it could not
touch her now, because Ronel sat on the load reading the country like a page and Dawid drove like a
man auditing the road, and grief shared out three ways, it turned out, was carriable in a way that
grief alone had not been. She had walked this world as its last soul. She rode through it now as a
member of something, and the difference was the whole of everything.

"Lion," said Ronel, from the load, mid-afternoon, pointing with her chin at nothing Neva could see.
"Old spoor. They're back, this side of the river." And then, because Ronel spent words the way
Elsabé spent hope: "Centuries with nobody shooting them. They won't know to be afraid of us."

"Are we afraid of them?" Neva asked.

"We're careful of them," Ronel said. "Fear's for things you don't understand." She looked out at the
green a while. "I understand lions."

---

The Laer's three were at the crossroads before them, and Neva knew everything the wire had not told
her in the first half-minute of watching them wait.

They were not soldiers the way the old books drew soldiers; no two of their coats matched. It was in
how they *stood*. Three men at rest by a vehicle in open country, and there was not a lazy line in
any of them: spaced apart without having discussed it, angles covering angles, the youngest turning
once every few breaths to sweep the dead ground behind — and when the Kloof's truck came up the
broken road, the three of them moved, without hurry and without one word, into a shallow arc that
put the sun behind their shoulders. It was courteous. It was competent. It was, Neva understood with
a long cold drop in her stomach, the *forms* — the same turns, the same low set of the weight, the
same guarding hand past the ear that her whole caste had danced at every Lighting of her life,
beautiful and empty under the gold ice, meaning forgotten, kept for gratitude's sake.

The Laer had not forgotten. The Laer's forms had never been allowed to become a dance. Twelve
generations sealed, the same founders' drill in the body — and where her people had kept it as
prayer, and the Kloof, by Ivo's telling, had let it thin to a half-remembered children's game, the
Laer had kept it as what it was made to be, against a day like every day, and it showed in the way
three polite men stood beside a truck as though the veld itself reported to them.

She had carried the keeping out through the ice in a bundle of pages, and loved it, and grieved
it. She had never once thought to be afraid of it.

The trade itself was correct to the minute and the gram. Their officer — grey-bearded, courteous,
introduced as Brother Alwyn — checked the diesel against the assay the way Elsabé would have,
approved it, and handed over the medicine chest with its seals unbroken and the clinic-wing's sheets
clipped to the lid; and if his eyes went to Neva more than the goods, and they did, he was correct
about that too: "You would be the walker. Reuel sends his regard, and this—" a flat packet, sealed,
her name on it in a clear even hand "—with his compliments. No answer is looked for." Dawid's face
did not change and his weight did. Neva took the packet the way you take a coal, and did not open
it, and Brother Alwyn inclined his head as though something had been confirmed, and the two trucks
parted before the light got long, each side watching the other out of sight, politely, in shapes.

"He wasn't here," Neva said, on the road south. "Reuel."

"No," said Dawid, driving. "The Laer does not risk its head on roads. You heard the man." His hands
were easy on the wheel and his voice was not easy at all. "An army that stands like that to trade
salt," he said, after a kilometre, "is not an army that fears the road."

---

The lioness came at the night camp, an hour before the false dawn, and she came for the meat.

They had done everything Ronel required: fire banked high, truck at their backs, the trade-meat from
Alwyn's parting courtesy slung high in a tree away from camp — and the lioness, centuries unshot,
unknowing of man the way the whole recovered world was unknowing of man, walked past the tree and
came to the fire to see what the fire was, at a low curious flat-headed walk, all that patient
weight with her eyes full of flame-light, and she was between Neva and the truck before Ronel's
low voice said, from the dark, without one sharp edge in it: "Nobody run."

What happened next happened in three breaths, and Neva would spend the rest of her life being asked
for it at fires and getting it, she suspected, slightly wrong, because her mind had not been present
for it. Only her body had.

The lioness, curious, unhurried, put her head low and made her small testing rush — not a charge,
Ronel said after, a question, *what are you* — and Neva's body answered it in the old tongue. The
left arm went over and down, hard at the finish, the way you close a valve; the weight dropped low
and sideways through the turning step; the right hand came flat past her ear — guarding, at last,
after four hundred years, the *throat* — and she was not where the rush went, and she was making,
without deciding to, the deep flat shout that the count had always put at the drill's dark centre,
*HAMA*, the syllable that had gone down the generations wearing its meaning away; and the lioness,
answered — met by a thing that stood its ground and turned and shouted in shapes older than her
whole rewilded world — checked, and stood, and swung her great head between the fire and the shout
and Ronel's advancing dark shape with the brand; and decided, in the way of cats, that the question
had not been worth the evening; and was gone.

Silence. The fire cracked. Neva stood in the finished form — weight low, arm out and down, breath
sawing — in the middle of the sleeping veld, and began, helplessly, to laugh, the cracked laugh,
because she understood, at last, all the way down:

Every Lighting of her life. Every child in the rows, wobbling. Every elder saying the forms were for
gratitude. Four hundred years of her people dancing, in perfect faith and perfect ignorance, the
exact grammar of surviving this exact night — keeping, keeping, keeping the thing whole with the
meaning gone, so that one daughter of the caste could stand on the far side of the ice at the end of
the world and have her body remember what her whole civilization had forgotten it knew.

"The prayer," she said to Ronel, still laughing, still shaking, while Dawid came around the fire
with his iron and his heart in his throat. "Ronel. The prayer *works.*"

Ronel looked at her a long moment over the brand — the woman who needed no one, reading the woman
whose dead had just saved her — and delivered the longest speech Neva had yet heard from her.

"Then your people," Ronel said, "kept the right things."

---

They rolled into the werf's yard on Sunday, in the morning, with the medicine chest roped on the
seat between them like a passenger; and Hettie met the truck the way she had met the fetch, and
had the chest open and the seals verified and the first white tablets into the small girl
before the engine had finished ticking; and the fever, which had held its line at the top of the arc
like a thing listening for the sound of a truck, broke that afternoon.

Jorik entered it all in the good log — the trade, the packet with Neva's name, which she had laid on
the table herself, unopened, where the whole Court could keep it in view, the lioness, the forms, Ronel's verdict — and
at the bottom of the day's page, in his careful successor-hand, the boy wrote the sentence that
Neva, reading over his shoulder, touched once with one finger, and let stand for the record of the
new world:

*The dance was a drill. The old ones knew. Nothing kept whole is ever wasted.*
