# Chapter Seven
### *Book II · Efficient data over analog · close-third, Elsabé*

The third voice came up out of the roar a month after Neva did, and it came up broken.

They had been scanning the dead log's frequencies by rota since the vinyl night — Elsabé's proposal,
adopted at table the way her proposals were always adopted, which was after twenty minutes of everyone
else discovering the idea for themselves — working down the founders' column of silent call-signs one
band at a time, transmitting the Kloof's identity and the hour, and logging *nil heard* in Jorik's
tidy successor-hand to Ivo's. Elsabé had costed the campaign out loud at its adoption: months of
nothing, most likely, for a world of dead ends — "we are knocking," she had said, "on the doors of
graves; be ready for the graves to be graves" — because somebody at that table had to hold the price
of hope steady while the rest of them spent it, and the somebody was always going to be her.

She had also, privately, on the slate she showed nobody, worked the other arithmetic: that the Kloof
had answered on the first knock, and Neva's vault had answered Neva's, and that two for two was not
a graveyard's odds. She did not say that part aloud. Hope from Elsabé carried like a verdict; she
spent it like a woman spending gold in a war, which is to say almost never, and always to effect.

Then, on a Tuesday, at the far cold end of the fourth band, the roar broke — and what came up was
not a voice the way Neva had been a voice. It came in *pieces*: a syllable, a wash of noise, half a
word, gone, back, a fragment that might have been a call-sign's tail, gone again — a station at the
very rim of the possible, thinned past understanding by distance or power or both, so that the whole
shack full of them stood straining at the speaker like people trying to read a face by lightning.

"Somebody's talking," Tomas said, wet-eyed on the instant, holding the doorframe. "Somebody's
*talking* out there —"

"Somebody's transmitting," Elsabé said. "What they're saying, no ear on this earth is going to pull
out of that." She looked at Ivo. Ivo was already looking at her, and there passed between the two of
them, not for the first time that season, the particular current of two cold minds arriving at the
same shelf of the same library at the same moment.

"The machine with the keys," Ivo said.

"The machine with the keys," said Elsabé.

---

The machine with the keys stood under a canvas in the corner of the hardened room, and it had stood
there since the winter he woke it, because it was the one machine in the Kloof that Ivo had
resurrected and then, to the werf's silent astonishment, walked away from. It was a teleprinter — the manual said so, and
said it as though introducing royalty — a great black iron typewriter with ambitions, that chattered
when it worked like a hail on a roof, and its purpose, the manual said, was this: that a letter
pressed here became a pattern of tones on the wire, and the pattern became the same letter *there*,
printed, on paper, at any distance, in any noise a voice would drown in. Ivo had woken it the second
winter, fed it a sentence, watched it chatter the sentence back, and covered it with the canvas.

"Why," Neva had asked him, when the story was told her — she was learning the werf by its stories
now, the way she had learned it by its voices.

"Because there was nobody to print to," Ivo had said, as though this were obvious, which it was, once
he said it, in the way of most things Ivo said. A telephone with one end is furniture. He had built
the beautiful thing and it had nothing to say to, and so it had waited under its canvas the way
everything in this world waited — trucks, seeds, verses — for the other end of itself to arrive.

Now the other end was arriving in fragments out of the fourth band, and the canvas came off, and
Elsabé watched Ivo and the boy fall on the machine and its manuals like men uncovering a well in a
drought, and set herself, at the bench's other end, to her own half of the problem, which no manual
in the dry-room addressed directly, and which she had privately concluded was the harder half.

The tones, the codes, the printed letter — that was Ivo's war, and she watched him win it the way he
won everything, slowly, aloud, at cost. It took eleven days. There were pages of the manual on the
five-hole code — every letter of the world reduced to five yes-or-noes, which delighted Jorik so
much he perforated a week of the good log's margins with hand-punched words until Dawid stopped him
— and pages on the check-sums, the little arithmetic that rode at the end of a line so the far
machine could add up what arrived and know if the noise had eaten any of it. *The wire lies*, the
manual said, in its graveside way. *Assume the wire lies, and make the message confess.* Elsabé had
copied that verse onto her public slate the first evening, because it was the truest sentence the
founders had left them and it was not about wires at all.

Ivo strung the tones across the yard first, shack to kitchen, and the kitchen machine — the second
printer, the lighter one, carried down with ceremony by Steyn — chattered out ONS LAG WANT ANDERS
HUIL ONS onto a strip of founders' paper while the supper table crowded round it, and Marika put the
strip up on the wall beside the wash-line rota, where it stayed. Then he strung them further, up the
ridge, through noise Steyn manufactured on purpose with the truck's ignition — "somebody has to be
the storm," Steyn said, gunning it, enjoying his work — and the check-sums caught what the noise ate
and asked for it again, and again, patient as frost, until the message came through whole or knew
itself broken, which — Elsabé wrote it on the slate under the founders' verse — was the entire moral
sophistication the wire itself would ever need: *it cannot be made honest. It can be made to know
when it is lying.*

The wire could. Her war was the other one.

---

Because on the twelfth night they turned the machine's ear to the fourth band, and the fragments
that no human ear could read began, out of hail and patience and arithmetic, to *print.*

It did not come fast, and it did not come whole. It came a character at a time, over three nights,
the check-sums discarding, demanding, discarding — the far station was weak and its sending was odd:
not in the keying, which any machine made even, but in the habits — transmissions to the exact
minute, retries identical to the character, never one wasted word — as though whoever set the far
schedule never tired and never varied — and the strip
that crept out of the iron machine, letter by letter under the eyes of the whole Court, said, when
Jorik at last carried it to the table flattened under glass like a relic:

**KLOOF STATION CONFIRMED. WE HOLD THE LOG ALSO. STATE YOUR STRENGTH. HOW MANY ARE YOU.**

The table read it in silence. Neva, at the table's far end, put her bread down and did not pick it
up again. It was everything they had knocked on graves for a month hoping to
hear: another world, alive, ordered, answering, holding the same founders' log — the net not dead
but waking, the distributed architecture of the careful dead knitting itself back together across
the round world exactly as built. Tomas had his hand over his mouth. Jorik was radiant. Steyn was
half out of his chair with somewhere to *go* at last, and even Dawid had gone very still in the way
he went still at good news, which was indistinguishable from his bad-news stillness to anyone who
had not lived with him.

"It's real," said Tomas. "Ivo — it's *real*, it's another Kloof —"

"It is a station that holds the founders' log and sends like a rock falls," said Elsabé. She laid
the strip's glass flat and turned it once, all the way around, the way she had turned Neva's pages,
looking for the sweat in the margins, and found no sweat in these margins to find, and minded it. "And it
asked one question." She looked around the table, at her family, at the hope running loose in the
room like spilled water, and did the job that was always going to be hers, and said it plain:

"A neighbour asks your *name.* A census-taker asks how many you are."

The room went quiet.

"Elsabé," Marika said — not a rebuke; a request for the price.

"I'm not saying don't answer." Elsabé turned the strip back face-up, squared it to the table's edge,
and looked at it a long moment, the werf's whole warm loud living roll call reduced on a far cold
key to a number someone wanted. "I'm saying we are about to meet the other kind of kept people. The
kind the keeping did something *to.* And I want us to have decided, before we tap one letter back
into that machine, what we tell strangers about how much of us there is to lose."

Nobody answered. On its bench in the corner, patient, iron, honest, the machine with the keys sat
waiting to send whatever they chose, and to make it confess whatever the wire did to it on the way;
and no arithmetic the founders had left in any manual on any shelf could ride at the end of that
line and check what arrived on the far side, in the far room, in the heart of whoever it was that
kept the schedule and wanted, before anything, the count.

The Court sat with its miracle and its price, at the long table, under the strip on the wall that
said ONS LAG WANT ANDERS HUIL ONS.

"We answer," Dawid said at last. "Slowly."

"Slowly," Elsabé agreed.
