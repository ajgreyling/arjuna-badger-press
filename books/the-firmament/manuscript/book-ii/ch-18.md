# Chapter Eighteen
### *Book II · Under one sky · close-third, Marika*

They kept the first Night of the Voice on the anniversary, in the long room, with the vinyl and
both worlds' bread, and Marika did what she did at every gathering of her life, which nobody had
ever once caught her doing: she read the heads.

Roll call, her mother had called it, underground, in a world ago. The reading was long, this year.

Ivo at the valve set, priest of the evening's two liturgies, the record and the rota, arguing
quietly with the Strand's brother about aerials — the Strand pair had come south for the feast from
the north watch, trading stations with Steyn for the month, and the brother argued exactly as
advertised and was exactly as beloved as his sister had promised. Tomas everywhere, undefended,
conducting the room like weather. Hettie by the food, fists on hips, running the werf's shadow
government of second helpings. Elsabé in the doorway that was hers, one shoulder already conceding
to the music, her slate at home on its hook for the one night of the year the count could mind
itself. Jorik with the good log open on his knees even now, fifteen going on archivist of the human
race, writing the feast into the record while the feast happened to him. Ronel — in the room. In
the room, on a chair, her stick within reach and her back to no wall at all, which those who knew
how to read her read the way you read a banner headline. And Steyn beside her, home from the
farthest watch with a beard the small girl had formally disapproved of, sitting with the stillness
of a man who had stood a season of nights at the wall of a sealed world listening to the roar and
had come back with something settled in him that no amount of Steve's investigation had yet named.

And Neva. Neva at the set's side, where she had lived all year, the walker of two worlds with her
family's whole noise around her; and past her, at the table's far end, the one head Marika counted
that was not there: the chair nobody had set and everybody saw, the place where a calm warm voice
out of the mountains would have sat if the world had gone another way — kept empty not in fear, and
not in honour; in *argument*. The house's standing answer, one supper at a time. He had chosen the
pen. The chair would keep.

The count came out long, this year. Marika stood at her kitchen door with the whole loud room in
front of her, the striker in her chest keeping its mended time, and let the length of the count be
what it was: the year's whole answer, in heads.

---

Steve gave the toast at the turn of the evening, and it was the one he owed.

He stood up with his mug when the first record's side ran out, in the hiss and crackle of the
needle riding home, and the room cued itself and went quiet; and Marika watched him take hold of
the rail that had not been there on the ninth evening of the bad month, and find it there now, and
grip it.

"To the empty chairs," Steve said. The room stilled further. He let it. "All of them. The ones we
came from and can't go back to. The ones up the dead roads we haven't knocked on yet. The one at
the end of this table, kept empty by the best argument this family ever won." He raised the mug an
inch. "May we fill them slowly, and never with people who count us." A breath, and the grin arrived
on schedule, in full armour, and under it the ache rode visible and carried, the way this family
carried everything now, out loud, together: "And to the woman who talked to the yeast so I didn't
have to give this toast to a brick. *Ons lag.*"

"*Want anders huil ons,*" said the whole room — both worlds' accents, the Strand's stretched vowels
in it, the small girl's piping over it — and the needle dropped on the hot side, and the dead were
singing again, and the living got up to dance with them.

Marika danced one slow one with Dawid, who had asked her with a formal inclination of the head that
was its own paragraph of their year; and the room made space and pretended not to watch, and failed
completely; and the two thrones turned slowly in the lamplight, mended and mendable, while the
found-family they had argued into existence made a wall of warmth around them.

"The table holds," Dawid said, at the turn.

"Ja," said Marika. "It does that now."

And Ronel — the room's held breath, the betting book's richest page — Ronel, at the fast one, put
out her hand *first*, palm up, to the man with the disapproved beard; and Steyn looked at the hand,
and at her, and took it; and Steve opened his mouth across the room, and Ronel, without turning her
head, said, "One word, Stoffel, and I dance with your hat instead," and Steve closed his mouth and
pointed, mutely, at everybody, at the record, at history — and the log, Jorik's pencil flying,
kept it.

---

At the top of the hour they lifted the needle for the rota, because the rota was the floor and the
feast stood on it like everything else.

It was the werf's oldest liturgy now, one year old: the room quiet, the set warm, the family
gathered at the shack-speaker's kitchen twin while Ivo worked the dial. The Kloof called; the north
watch answered — the Strand's sister, keeping Steyn's wall for the month, reporting the lamp lit
and the roar rich and nothing heard, *nil heard*, in the cheerful voice of a woman to whom nil
heard was a night's honest work and not a sentence; and the Strand's home station answered, the
brother's other sister, with the sea behind her voice; and the net's three lamps stood confirmed
across the dark of the world, and the room began to turn back toward the music.

And the roar opened.

Faint. Far. New. A voice none of them had ever heard, on the common frequency, halting, careful,
reading — not calling, *reading*, the way you read a thing you have copied out and practised alone:
*"...in the beginning... was the firmament... and the light upon its track..."* — the commons copy,
the first page, the words exactly as written, coming back out of the dark of the round world in a
stranger's mouth, in a stranger's accent, from a station that did not say its name.

Nobody in the long room moved. The needle hung lifted in Ivo's hand. The small girl's mouth was
open. And Marika watched Neva close her eyes and stand in the middle of her whole found family
listening to the sound of a soul somewhere on the curve of the earth practising the sentence — some
reader, some walker, some keeper of some box they had not met yet, who had been out there in the
roar all along, copying, and had chosen the Night of the Voice to try the words aloud.

The reading stopped. The roar breathed. Then, shy, half-swallowed: *"...is anyone there."*

Neva took the handset the way she took every threshold of her life, first and gently, and Marika
would set what she said beside anything the books of either world kept, and so would the log, which
has it, word for word, under the date:

"Ja," said Neva, of the two worlds, of the one sky. "We hear you. You read it beautifully. Say your
name when you're ready — there's no hurry on this frequency. The net keeps the light on, and the
book has no gate, and you are not alone. None of us ever were. We just couldn't hear it yet."

---

The party spilled into the yard at midnight, the way the werf's parties had learned to, and both
worlds' people stood in the warm dark with their drinks and their arguments under the whole
uncounted blaze of the stars — the roofless up that had once nearly swallowed a lone walker whole,
and now held still over her family like a roof too big to see the edges of; and somewhere under it,
north, a lamp burned at the wall of a sealed world with a book waiting at its threshold; and
somewhere under it, unmapped, a stranger sat by a set with a practised page and a name not yet
said; and somewhere under it, in the mountains, a man who counted was listening too, and the chair
stood empty at the table, and the argument stood open, and the gate stood shut, and the frequency
stood open, and that — Marika thought, reading her long count one last time by starlight — that was
the whole of the year, and the whole of the doctrine, and the whole of what they had built: every
door in the world, shut or open, *chosen*, out loud, together, slowly.

She went in before the last dance to bank the stove, because some liturgies outrank even feasts,
and stood a moment at the window with the coals settling, counting her family through the glass,
out loud, unashamed, the whole long count of it, and it did not come out short.

Far to the north, the lamp was lit. Far past counting, under the same one sky, somebody they had
not met yet was practising the words.

The kettle, in its own time, would sing.
