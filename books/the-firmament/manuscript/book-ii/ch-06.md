# Chapter Six
### *Book II · The vinyl party · close-third, Neva*

Her first days at the werf, Neva mostly ate.

This was not her plan. She had arrived with plans — to be useful at once, to earn her place at the
wire and the wash-line, to be no one's burden; she had rehearsed them in the truck the whole last
hour, the way you rehearse for a world you have only heard through a wall. And then Hettie had met
the trucks at the gate with her fists on her hips, taken one look at her over Marika's shoulder, and
assumed command of Neva's body as though it were a neglected garden that had come into her
possession by law. There was mutton. There was bread with real butter, which made Neva cry, to her
own fury, at the table, in front of everyone — over *butter* — and nobody laughed, and Steve said
kindly, "Ja, it does that," and passed her the dish again. There was more bread in the morning and
rusks at mid-morning and something at every hour a bell would have rung, until Neva understood that
this was not hospitality but doctrine: Hettie had packed that provision sack before there was a
bearing — on faith and mutton, as Steve had testified at the campfire — and now the faith had a body
in front of it, and the body would be
fed, and the only theology anyone was prepared to argue at that table was whether she was getting
enough.

"You'll stop fighting it sooner or later," Marika advised her, watching Hettie load the plate a
third time. "The rest of us stopped years ago. It's how she says it."

"Says what?"

"Everything," said Marika.

Elsabé came to her on the second evening, with the lamp lit, and asked — politely, precisely, and
with a formality that told Neva this had been decided on, weighed, and worded in advance — whether
she might see the pages.

Neva brought the bundle in its oiled cloth and put it into her hands, and watched the coldest mind
on the werf undo the wrapping the way Ivo laid out a manual, and then sit, and read, and not get up.
She was still reading when Neva went to bed. She was reading when Tomas banked the kitchen fire. In
the morning the pages were wrapped and squared on the table, the cloth folded to its old creases
exactly, and Elsabé, grey at the edges with the night, delivered her verdict to the breakfast table
with the slate under her hand, in the flat voice of a woman who had checked everything checkable and
would not insult the rest by pretending.

"Five hands," she said. "Five different copyists, at least, across those folds — different inks,
different years, one of them shaking with age or cold or both. Folded and refolded past counting.
Carried against skin — you can see where the sweat took the margins." She looked at Neva, and it was
not a soft look, because Elsabé did not own one, but it had shifted somewhere at the back. "People
kept these a long time, at cost, in secret, in relay. That part I can verify with my own eyes. The
rest of it" — the round world, the wall, the roofless sky — "drove in my gate by truck." She pushed
the bundle two inches across the table toward Neva. "I'm satisfied. Eat your bread."

Ronel she did not properly meet at all, that first while. Ronel was in from the veld twice in four
days; she looked at Neva once across the yard, the whole of her still, the way she went still at a
sound in the grass, and then nodded — one nod, which Neva would learn was a paragraph — and went
on about her lines; and on the third morning there was a rock-rabbit skin, tanned soft, folded over
the rail outside Neva's door, and nobody would say it had been anybody, and Hettie, pressed, said
only, "That's how *she* says it," and the matter, like everything in this family that mattered most,
was closed without one word being spent on it in the open.

---

Ivo found the crate the way he found everything: by refusing to leave a locked door alone.

It had taken him three days and a bent bar and most of Steyn's patience to get the store open, and when
the door finally gave, the smell that came out was old paper and older dust and something underneath it
that Neva did not have a word for yet — the smell of a room that had been shut before any of them were
born and had kept its own weather since. Ivo went in with the lamp. He was quiet for long enough that
Steyn leaned in after him and said, "Well? Treasure, or another cupboard of the founders' underpants?"

"Better," Ivo said, from the dark. "Come look."

The crate was vinyl. Neva did not know the word; she learned it that night, the way she was learning all
the words for the world's real furniture, one object at a time. Flat black discs in paper sleeves, heavy
as slate, and with them a machine — a low wooden box with a needle on a hinged arm and, behind a grille,
a row of glass valves like little upturned bottles. Ivo lifted one of the discs to the lamp and turned it,
and the light ran round and round the fine black grooves, and his face did the thing it only did for dead
machines: it opened.

"It's *music*," he said. "It's — the whole thing's analog. No chips. Nothing to burn. You could drop a
flare on this and it'd still —" He stopped. He turned the disc again. "It'd still play," he said, more
quietly, and Neva understood that he had just said something true about more than the disc.

---

Getting it to play was its own evening, and it was the best kind — the kind where the work was the party
before the party. Ivo would not let anyone touch the valve set until he had opened it and read its insides
like scripture, and Hettie brought him tea he did not drink and stood over him anyway, because that was
Hettie, and Steve narrated the whole operation from a crate in the corner as though it were a birth.

"He's going in," Steve said. "He's — ag, look at him, he's in love. Ivo, do you want the room? We can
give you and the amplifier the room."

"I want the room," Ivo said, not looking up.

"Typical Aquarius," said Elsabé, from the doorway, and it got a laugh, because it was true and because
Elsabé so rarely spent a joke that when she did it landed like a coin on a table.

They ran it off the truck. That was Steyn's contribution, and it was a real one under the clowning — a
battery hauled in on its own sledge, terminals scraped bright, and Ivo's careful cabling from the twelve
volts up through a thing he had built that afternoon and would not explain. Neva watched him work and
thought: this was the thing no ledger of this family would ever quite hold — that Ivo, who could not
sit at a table with them for a whole meal, would spend six hours in the cold making a machine so that
the rest of them could be warm together. His care came out through his hands or it did not come out at
all.

"Ready," Ivo said.

"You're sure?" said Dawid. The Sergeant had come in without anyone marking it, and stood at the edge with
his arms folded in the posture that meant he did not know what his hands were for at a thing like this.

"No," Ivo said, honestly, and dropped the needle.

---

There was a hiss. A round, warm, breathing hiss, and under it a crackle like a small fire, and Neva felt
the whole room lean toward the sound the way the fields had once leaned toward the light on the track.

And then it came.

She had no name for it either — later Ivo would tell her the founders had names for all of it, that this
was the *hot* music, the party music, the sound the world made when it was young and did not know it was
going to end — but she did not need the name. It was bright. It was fast. It had a heartbeat you could put
your own heart next to, and horns that laughed, and a woman's voice riding over the top of it who sounded
like she had never in her life been afraid of anything.

For a second nobody moved. Nobody breathed. The dead were singing in the room and the living stood still
to let them.

Then Steve stood up off his crate, held out one hand to nobody, put the other on his own heart like a man
about to make a speech, and began — with enormous, terrible dignity — to dance.

That broke it. Marika laughed first, the big warm laugh that was half the reason the group held together,
and then she was up and had Steve's hands and the two of them were turning, badly, gloriously, and Tomas
was up, Tomas was *always* going to be up, Tomas danced the way he did everything, with his whole undefended
chest — and Jorik, the boy, watching them with a face that had not yet decided whether this was allowed;
and Marika saw the face and did not let him decide, just reached out and pulled him in.

Neva stood at the edge with Dawid and did not dance, because she did not know how, because no one in the
flat world had ever danced where she could see it. The keepers did not dance. You did not, the way you did
not touch the wall.

"You don't have to," Dawid said, beside her, watching the room the way he watched everything, for exits.

"I know," she said.

They stood a while. Steyn had got hold of Ronel — no one saw how, it was the bravest thing Neva had seen
him do and she had seen him walk into the dark past the perimeter — and Ronel was letting herself be turned,
once, twice, her whole body saying *this means nothing* and her feet saying otherwise, and Steve called out
over the horns, "There she goes! Claws in for once! Somebody write it in the record!" and Ronel showed him
a finger without missing the step, and even Elsabé, in the doorway, was moving now, one shoulder, barely, a
woman conceding a single degree.

"They're good people," Neva said. She had not meant to say it aloud.

Dawid was quiet. Then he said, "Yes." And then, lower, in the voice she was only beginning to be trusted
with: "That's the whole trouble, isn't it. If they were bad it would be simpler."

She looked at him. But he had already stepped forward — not to dance, Dawid would sooner have walked back
through the ice wall than dance — but to stand nearer the warmth of it, which for Dawid was the same
surrender, and Neva let him go and did not name what it cost him, because the room had taught her that much
already: you do not caption the thing a hard man does when he lets himself stand closer to the fire.

---

The ache found her late in the night, and no one could have told her it would be Steve who carried
it in.

The record turned over — Ivo did it, reverent, a priest at an altar he did not believe in — and the second
side was slower, a song for the end of a night rather than the middle of one, and the dancing gentled into
something closer, people holding on now more than turning. And Steve, who had danced with everyone and made
every joke and been the engine of the whole glad wreck of it, came and sat down by Neva at the edge, out of
breath, grinning, and the grin — she watched it happen — the grin *failed*.

It went out like the day-track at twelfth bell. One moment it was there and the next it was a man's face
with nothing held up in front of it, and he was looking at the room, at Marika turning slow with the boy,
at Tomas with his eyes shut, at the black disc going round and round with the dead woman's voice still
riding over the top of it, unafraid, four centuries and a whole ended world after she had sung it.

"They're all gone, you know," Steve said. He said it lightly. His voice did not match his face at all.
"Every one of them. Whoever played in that band. Whoever bought this record and put it on and — danced to
it, in a kitchen somewhere, before. The woman singing. Her mother. Everyone she ever —" He stopped. He
pressed the heel of his hand hard against one eye, once, the way you'd stop a machine you didn't want the
others to hear fault. "And here's us," he said. "The last stupid lot of us. Dancing to it anyway."

Neva did not say anything. She had learned that too, in her short time among them: that there were things
Steve said that were not asking to be answered, only to be *heard*, so that he would not have to be the
only one holding them.

"*Ons lag,*" she said finally — one of the founders' phrases, from the un-redacted pages, that she had not
understood until exactly this moment — "*want anders huil ons.*"

Steve looked at her. Something moved across his ruined grinning face, and then the grin came back — real
this time, smaller, the kind that has been through the other thing and chosen to come back anyway.

"Ja," he said. "That's the one. Who taught you that?"

"Dead people," Neva said.

And Steve laughed — a proper laugh, cracked right down the middle, half of it grief and no way to tell you
where the seam was — and he stood, and he held out his hand to her, the newest and strangest of them, the
one who had walked out of the flat world into all of this.

"Come on then," he said. "You can't just watch. It's the one thing they'd never forgive us — coming all
this way and not dancing."

And Neva, who did not know how, who came from a world that had cut dancing out of the book along with the
sky, took the hand of the man who laughed so he would not cry, and let him show her, badly, under a light
they had stolen back from the dark, to the sound of everyone who had ever lived before them.
