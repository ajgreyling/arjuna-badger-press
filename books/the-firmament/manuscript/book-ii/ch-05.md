# Chapter Five
### *Book II · The road · close-third, Neva*

The night they found which arrow she lived at the end of, Neva was keeping her fire small against the
wind and mending the aerial guy that the same wind had been worrying all week, and she nearly missed
the top of the hour doing it.

She did not miss it. She had not missed it once. The rota was the floor of her world now — she, who
had grown up inside a track the sky ran on rails, had walked out of one schedule and built her whole
life around another within a season, and she knew it, and let herself be wry about it in the hour
before sleep, because the alternative was to think too hard about what the hour meant to her, and a
person alone does not pull on the one rope holding the roof up. At the top of the hour the roar
turned into voices. That was the whole of it. Every night, out of the vast soft breathing nothing,
the far end of the wire opened like a door in the dark, and there was the low ordered voice, and the
warm big one, and the young one reading out the day from the good log if she asked, and the dry one
that could make her laugh from the far side of the sky — and for the hour that the band held, Neva,
first soul of the dead world, walker-out, carrier of the sentence, was none of those things. She was
company.

Ivo's voice came up different that night, and she heard it before his words: quicker, pitched a shade
high, a man being careful with a full cup.

"We built the little aerial," he said. "The sense. The null has a front now. Neva — we swung it
tonight. Three times. Elsabé made me do it three times."

She sat very still by her small fire, with the mended guy in her hand.

"North of you," she said. Her own voice came out strange. "I'm north of you."

"You're north of us." Behind his voice the shack — she knew its sounds now like a street she had
never walked — erupted, was shushed, erupted anyway; she heard Steyn whoop, and Marika's laugh riding
over everything, and under it all Dawid's parade voice already dividing the noise into lists. "Neva,"
Ivo said, and his careful cup spilled at last, and what spilled out was so simple it took her breath:
"We know where you are."

Nobody had known where she was since the wall.

She had stood on the far side of everything, on a height in the wind, and been vindicated, and been
alone in the vindication; and she had made her peace with the aloneness the way you make peace with
weather, by working; and now a voice out of the sky said *we know where you are*, and Neva put her
hand over her mouth in the firelight, alone, so that the sky would not hear what the sentence did to
her; and did not entirely succeed.

"Stay by your set," said Dawid's voice, taking the handset with that asking look she could hear by
now, and issuing tenderness the only way he knew, as orders. "Do not move your camp. Keep your fires
regular. We are coming to fetch you."

"That's not a hope," Neva said, when she could. "That's a rota."

"Confirmed," said Dawid, of the Kloof, solemn as scripture; and the far end of the wire laughed, all
of it at once, and she sat by her fire at the end of the found arrow and laughed with them, on the
roofless earth, under all the cold high fires of the dark.

---

They drove out of her sky before they reached her. Ivo had warned her it would happen — had explained
it twice, the layer, the skip, the shadow under the arc; the night voice was a stone thrown over a
mountain, he said, and the nearer the thrower walked, the farther over her head the stone would land
— so that when the hour came and the roar stayed roar, she knew it was arithmetic and not disaster,
knew it was the sound of them *arriving*, and sat through the silent hour anyway with her arms around
her knees, keeping the rota alone, because the rota was the floor, and you do not stop keeping a
thing just because it has stopped answering. Her caste had taught her that, at least, and taught it
deep.

Two days she kept the silent hours. She mended what did not need mending. She talked to the set the
way Ivo talked to his — she knew he did; she had heard him, nights, forgetting the handset was open —
and told it, companionably, that they were two days out, then one, that the roads were half what the
maps promised, that Steyn was certainly driving too fast wherever the surface allowed and Dawid was
certainly not allowing it; and on the third evening, at the grey hour when the light went long and
gold across the ruin and the green, the wind shifted, and carried up the valley to her camp a sound
the recovered world had not held in centuries.

An engine.

Neva stood up so fast she knocked the pot off its stone. The sound came and went on the wind, nearer
and gone and nearer — an old hammering diesel taking the bad road slowly, patiently, the way you walk
a frost-line — and she found that she was already running, down from the camp through the wet grass
in the gold light, toward the mouth of the valley, where presently, unbelievably, out of the green
that had eaten every road on earth, came a flat-faced grey truck with her bonnet catches rusted and
her headlamps on in the daylight like a thing come looking with its eyes open; and behind it a
second; and the first truck saw her — she heard the engine catch its breath — and stopped, and its
doors opened both sides at once, and the dead world was over.

Afterward, at the werf, they would all tell it differently, and she would let every version stand,
because each of them was true and none of them was complete. Tomas told it that she stood in the
grass like a ghost until he reached her, and that he reached her first. He did; he came over the
veld like weather, wide open, weeping already, and did not slow down at the end, and took her into
the kind of embrace her caste had not practised in twelve generations and Tomas had never once
rationed in his life, and Neva — who had last been touched by a frightened old man patting her hands
at a frost-wall on the other side of the world — stood in it rigid as a post for one breath, two,
and then broke, and held on, and was held, in the gold light, in the wet grass, by a man whose face
she had never seen, while over his shoulder the rest of the far end of the wire climbed down out of
the trucks and came toward her through the grass, slow, giving her time, every one of them exactly
and nothing like their voices.

Marika did not embrace her at first. Marika stopped at arm's length and took her face in both hands
and looked at her the way you read a page, and said — the first words spoken face to face between
the two worlds, and Jorik would enter them in the good log that night and the log would keep them —
"There you are. Skin and bone. We're fixing that tonight," and *then* embraced her, and the matter
of Neva's place in the family was thereby closed, permanently, without a meeting.

Steve shook her hand with enormous formality, looked her in the eye, and said, "So. The book is
true, the world is round, and you kept a rota alone in a dead world for a season. You'll fit in
fine. We're all mad here too." And Neva laughed — cracked, half a sob, the laugh from the height in
the wind — and Steve's grip tightened just slightly, one oom's worth, which she would learn was how
he caught people.

Dawid stood back until the others had done, then came forward and inclined his head, formal as a
lighting, and said, "You kept your fires regular. Good discipline," which she understood — having
learned the family by radio, which strips everything but the truth of a voice — to be Dawid for
*welcome, we are glad you are alive, you are one of ours now.* "Thank you," Neva said, gravely, in
kind, "your rota held me together," and watched the words land somewhere behind his parade stillness,
in the place where his father kept the argued-over book.

And Ivo. Ivo had not left the first truck. He stood by the open door with one hand still on the
wing, the man who had heard her first, who had answered — *ja, ja, we hear you* — who had swung a
door of wire through the dark until the sky gave up the place she stood; and now that she stood
there in the flesh, ten paces off, the wire between them gone and nothing in its place, he had
plainly no idea on earth what to do.

So Neva went to him. And she did not embrace him, because she knew him — knew him the way you know
a hand you have read a thousand pages of — and instead she did the thing her caste did, the thing
Sarel had done at the rim and Aldous had done at the wall with the bundle between their four hands:
she took Ivo's hand in both of hers and patted it, once, the old blessing, the gesture for *go, and
be more than we were brave enough to be*, which had crossed the ice and the veld and the whole round
world to arrive here, at a truck door, in the gold light, between the two ends of the wire.

"You found me by losing me," she said. "Jorik read me that, from your log."

"The null's narrow," Ivo said, hoarse. "Loudest is wide. It's — most people think you'd hunt the
loudest." He looked at her hands around his. "Nobody hunts the quiet."

"Your people did," Neva said. "Twice."

---

They stayed that night at her camp, because the light was going and the road was bad, and it was
the strangest and best campfire the recovered world had yet seen: eight souls and two trucks at the
foot of a sealed sleeping vault at the end of a found arrow, eating Hettie's provision sack —
"packed before we had the bearing," Steve testified, "on faith and mutton" — while Neva told them,
in her own voice, with the fire on her face, the whole of it: the wall, the hatch, the between, the
no-roof; the height and the curve and the laughing; the cliff, and the page, and *upon nothing*,
and how it held. Nobody interrupted. Even Steyn did not interrupt. The fire burned down and she
talked, and the far cold fires came out overhead in their thousands, and the family she had heard
through a wall of static for a season sat around her in the flesh and listened the way her caste
had listened at the Lighting: to the whole of a true thing, told into the dark, by the one who had
been let out to see it come true.

In the morning, before they loaded, she walked up alone to the flank of her world and put her palm
flat on it, an inch off, then flat on.

Inside, in the warm gold lie, her mother was kneading dough under a made sun, believing her in the
deep fields. Below her palm, in the cold sleeping heart of the wall, the seeds lay banked in their
long dark, waiting, as they had waited through everything. She was not abandoning either of them.
She made it a promise this time, not a grief: the trucks had changed the size of the world; a day
and a night of road was not the ice, was not forever, was a *distance*, and distances were what
this family ate for breakfast now.

"Keep," she told her world — keeper's word, the whole of the law of her old life, given back to it
at last as a blessing instead of a cage. "I'll come back with hands."

Then she went down the hill to the trucks and the family, and they turned south onto the bones of
the old road, toward the werf, toward the lands and the long table and the whole loud rest of her
life; and Neva rode in the first truck with the window down and the wind of the moving world on her
face, between Ivo, who did not talk, and Tomas, who never stopped, and found — somewhere in the
first hour, without deciding it — that she had stopped counting the hours to the next contact.

She was inside the signal now.
