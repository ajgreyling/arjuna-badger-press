# Chapter Four
### *Book II · Diesel and dirty oil · close-third, Steve*

The founders had left them four trucks, and the trucks had waited the way iron waits: badly.

Steve had known about the shed for two years and stayed out of it, and if you had asked him why he
would have given you four different jokes on four different days, which was his system for the
questions that had no funny answers. The truth was that the shed was the one place on the whole werf
that smelled like his father. Not his own father — his father was born in the Kloof, same as
everyone — but the *before*-fathers, the ones in the manuals' photographs with their sleeves rolled
and their hats pushed back, standing beside machines they owned the knowing of: grease and cold iron
and rubber gone chalky with the years, the smell of a world of men who could fix anything and had not
been able to fix the one thing, and had parked their trucks anyway, brakes on, tanks drained, tools
wiped and hung in size order on the wall — parked them *carefully*, Steve noticed, the first morning
he finally went in with the lamp, and that was the detail that sat down in his chest and stayed. They
had known they would never drive out again. They had left the trucks ready to be driven anyway.

"Ag, look at you," he said to the nearest one, a flat-faced grey army thing with her bonnet catches
rusted shut, standing on four flat tyres like an old cow asleep. "Left at the altar. Forty — what,
four hundred years, my lammetjie? And not one letter." He patted her wing, and the dust came off on
his palm like flour, and under the dust the paint was good. "Right," said Steve, and hung up his hat
with the phrase that on the werf served for prayer, rifle-drill, and declaration of war all at once:
"Let's see what the oud-ooms left us."

---

What the oud-ooms had left them, in the fuel store behind the shed, was diesel by the drum — rows of
it, sealed, dated in stencil — and every drum of it dead.

That was Ivo's word, delivered kneeling over the first opened drum with the lamp, wearing the face he
otherwise kept for lost causes and Tomas's questions about the radio. The fuel had not kept. Time had
got into it the way time got into everything down here: water sweated in through four centuries of
breathing, and something had *lived* in it, in the black seam where water met oil — lived, and died,
and settled — so that what the drum held now was a clear enough top, a rotten black floor, and between
them a smell that emptied the store in a hurry and became, by supper, one more werf legend: the day
the oud-ooms' diesel fought back.

"The manual says it grows," Jorik reported, awed, from the dry-room that evening, finger on the verse.
"In the fuel. A thing grows *in the fuel.*"

"Of course it does," said Steve, comfortably, over his pipe. "This is Africa, boetie. Everything grows.
You put water and darkness and four hundred years together in a tin, something in this country will
farm it."

But — and this was the part Steve loved, the part that had him whistling by the second morning in a
way that made Marika look at him sideways over the wash-line — the oud-ooms had *known.* The dead men
had known their fuel would die, the way they had known everything else, and the manual for the fuel
store said so in its dry graveside voice: said *settle*, said *decant*, said *filter*, said — Ivo read
it out twice for the pleasure of it — *fuel may be reclaimed*; and on the top shelf of the store,
crated, in oiled paper, the founders had left the tools of the resurrection: felt filters by the dozen,
funnels, hand pumps, and one machine that stopped Steve's whistling dead when Ivo unwrapped it,
because Steve knew it. Every child of the Kloof's dairy sheds knew it. A cream separator. The same
hand-cranked iron cone his own mother had skimmed the milk with — and the fuel-store manual, in its
close cross little print, instructing the living to crank their dead diesel through it, exactly so,
because what slings cream out of milk will sling water and rot out of oil.

Steve stood in the fuel store holding his mother's machine repurposed for the end of the world, and
laughed until he had to sit down on a drum. "The same machine," he kept saying, wiping his eyes, to
Ivo, who did not see the joke and never would. "Butter on Sundays and diesel at the resurrection. The
same blessed machine. Ag, I *love* these people, Ivo. They thought of everything and they made it all
out of the dairy."

---

It took nine days, and the werf shaped itself around the work the way the werf shaped itself around
any work, which was the thing — Steve thought it and then spent the week not-saying it, because you
did not caption a good thing while it was running — the thing the voice on the radio had actually
changed. Nothing on the werf was different. Everything on the werf was pointed now.

They settled fuel in relays: drum after drum decanted down the clear top, the black floors left to
their private wars. Steyn cranked the separator until his arm gave out, then swore a formal oath
against dairy equipment and cranked on, because the one thing you could always get out of Steyn was
more, especially if Ronel was in earshot, which — Steve noted, in the betting-book he kept behind his
teeth — she somehow, that week, generally was. The filtered fuel came through the felt at last in a
thin bright amber line, and Jorik held the first jar of it up to the lamp like the elders held — like
a man holds a thing he is proud of, and wrote the yield in the good log, gills and pints, every
evening, a harvest record for a crop of light.

And Ivo paid his pump debt. Three days, as contracted — Dawid stood over the paperwork of it like a
man collecting rent — and BOREHOLE 2 went from a stencilled promise to a shaking, hammering, living
iron ox that pulled cold water up out of the dark of the world and spat it silver into the new-laid
furrow, and the whole werf stood around the borehole in the evening light and cheered the water the
way their grandparents had answered the lamps; and Hettie, standing next to Steve with her fists on
her hips and her eyes wet, said the truest grace ever said over that ground: "Now the beans will make
it," which — Steve told the table that night — was the difference between this family and every story
in the old books: other people's miracles came with trumpets; theirs came with beans.

But the trucks were the thing. The whole werf knew the trucks were the thing, and knew *why* without
one meeting being held about it, which was how the werf decided everything that mattered: a woman was
out there past the rim of the maps with a bearing and a half pointing at her, and four hundred
kilometres — call it more; Dawid did — was a death on foot and a fortnight's argument by ox-wagon,
and a *day and a night*, the manuals said, by road, if the roads were half what the maps promised.
Nobody said *we are cleaning four centuries of rot out of forty drums so that we can go and fetch
Neva.* Everybody knew it. Even the trucks knew it, Steve maintained, patting the grey cow's wing each
morning: "Patience, my lammetjie. You waited four hundred years for a reason. She's up that road."

---

On the ninth day, in the evening, with the whole werf pretending to have other business in the yard,
they tried her.

Steyn was in the seat, because some honours you give the young or you never hear the end of it. Ivo
stood at the open bonnet with the manual, primed and bled and checked and re-checked, muttering the
starting drill like the verse it was. Steve stood where a wise oom stands, which is where he can see
the fuel line, the boy's face, and the shortest way to run. And Dawid — Dawid stood at parade rest
with his hands behind his back and ruined it completely by being unable to keep still, rising on his
toes and down again, on his toes and down, a sergeant-major at a christening.

"Glow," said Ivo. Steyn held the plugs. They counted it out loud together, all of them, the whole
yard, because the manual said count and the werf said *together* — "…nege, tien" — 

"Crank."

She turned over with a sound like a giant clearing his throat of four centuries, caught for half a
breath — and died. The yard let out its air. Steyn's face fell like a bad soufflé, and he reached for
the switch again, because Steyn's answer to everything was again and harder, and Ivo said, "No," in
the flat bench voice that even Steyn had learned to obey around machinery, and stood there with his
eyes shut, listening to the echo of the failure the way he listened to the roar.

"Air," he said. "In the line still. She's drinking and getting foam." And to Steve, without opening
his eyes: "Crack the injectors. One turn. We bleed her at the top."

So Steve cracked them, one by one, his mother's separator-callus finding the old work as if forty —
as if four hundred years were a long weekend; and Steyn cranked, and the air came weeping out of the
steel in little silver beads, oil rising behind it, and Ivo went down the line closing them off in
order, and there was a moment — Steve would tell it at the vinyl night, and at the wedding-feasts of
people not yet met, and one day, an old man, to children who knew it by heart and demanded it anyway
— a moment when the whole yard was silent, and the light was going gold over the heights, and the
grey cow stood primed and bled and ready with a boy's hand on her switch, and the werf held its
breath around her like a family around a bed where the news could go either way.

"Now," said Ivo.

And the truck started.

Not the cough — the cough and *then* the catch and then the deep, ragged, evening-out hammer of an
old mechanical diesel taking up her work again, four hundred years after careful men had parked her
ready, shaking the dust of all of it off her bonnet in soft grey smoke, running, *running*, and the
yard came apart. Steyn was standing on the seat roaring like the engine's own echo; Tomas was weeping
on Marika, who was laughing the big laugh with her arms full of him; Jorik was writing in the good
log at a dead sprint of the pencil; Hettie was crying into her apron and denying it; and Dawid — the
whole werf saw it and the whole werf loved him for it and no one ever once let him forget it — Dawid
was up on his toes with both fists in the air like a boy at his first sheep-race, shouting something
that was lost forever in the hammer of the engine, which was as well, Steve reckoned, since it had
every appearance of being the word *logistics*.

Steve stood in the smoke and the gold light and the noise with his hand on the warm shaking wing of
the resurrected world, and let the tears come — the yard was loud enough that nobody would ask, which
was the only condition on which he permitted them, ever — for the careful dead men who had wiped
their tools and parked their trucks facing outward, ready, tanks drained, brakes on, against exactly
this evening, which they had believed in, and provisioned, and would never see.

"Ja, ooms," he said, under the hammer of it, patting the wing. "We got one going. Sleep now. We'll
drive."
