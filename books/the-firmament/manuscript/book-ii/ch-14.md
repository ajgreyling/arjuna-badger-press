# Chapter Fourteen
### *Book II · Dawid at the gate · close-third, Dawid*

The strip came up at the top of the hour, and it was three lines, and Dawid read it alone in the
shack because the rota that night was his, and sat with it a long time before he rang the yard bell.

THE LAER COMES TO THE KLOOF. REUEL AND FOUR. TWO DAYS. WE BRING HANDS FOR YOUR DRYING AND OUR
PHYSICIAN FOR YOUR QUEEN. THE ROAD IS WORTH IT, BROTHER. SOME THINGS ARE.

Every line of it was a gift, and every line of it was a blade, and Dawid, who had spent a season
learning to read the man the way the man read everyone, could parse the wounds it was fitted to
without moving his lips. *Hands for your drying* — the fortnight they were short, the lever still
lying on the adjourned table. *Our physician for your Queen* — and there it was, the thing he would
carry up to the house like a stone: they knew. The Laer knew Marika was down. No soul on the werf
had *reported* it; he had stood the rotas himself since the drying floor and kept the contacts to
grain-counts and weather; and the answer, when he made himself find it, was worse than a spy and
simpler: a season of open-hearted evenings, and one bad first week before he took the key himself.
Tomas asking their choir for a get-well song. Jorik's proud bulletins. The werf had been telling the Laer its whole life all along, in joy, the
way this family told everything — and somewhere in the mountains a man who never wasted a word had
been filing it, name by name, wound by wound, against the day the file would be useful.

The day was here. *The Laer does not risk its head on roads.* He had said it himself, on the road
south, with the forms standing in his mirrors. For this, the head would risk the road.

That was the most flattering sentence Reuel had never sent.

---

They came at dusk on the second day, two vehicles with their lamps lit, and stopped a courteous
fifty metres from the gate, and only one man walked forward into the werf's lamplight; and Dawid,
standing at the closed gate with his hands behind his back, met, at last, in the flesh, the best
answer he had ever heard to any question.

Reuel was ordinary. That was the first thing, and the worst. Dawid had built him tall in the mind's
dark — the voice demanded it — and the man at the gate was middle-sized and middle-aged, a
schoolmaster's build under a good plain coat, bare-headed, grey at the temples, with lines at his
eyes that said he laughed often and meant it; and the famous voice, in the open evening air, was
exactly as warm as the wire had promised, and the eyes above it were the calmest Dawid had ever
seen on a living man. Not cold. Calm. The eyes of a man who had done all his arguing long ago, in
some sealed room in some hungry winter, and had never since met a fact that talked back.

"Brother Dawid." He did not offer his hand across the gate, which was correct, and the correctness
was the point. "You stand a gate well. I'd have been disappointed by less."

"You've come a long road."

"Some things are worth it." He looked past Dawid's shoulder at the werf — the lamplit yard, the
barn with its wet treasure, the sick-room's small window — and he looked at it the way Dawid had
watched men look at a good map: with pleasure, and with placement. "So this is the table that
argues," Reuel said, warm as ever. "I've admired it a season, brother, I'll admit it freely. The
voices, the singing, the boy's bulletins. Sister Hettie's stores. Brother Tomas, who asked my choir
to sing for your Queen — they've practised, tell him." Name by name, gently, like a man laying out
tools, each one clean, each one exactly where he had filed it. "And how is she? Marika. The one who
holds it all."

The stone sat in Dawid's chest where he had carried it two days. *The record had a voice like
that, Dawid. It just never needed a body before.* Neva's voice, in the dark of the shack, a season
ago. He had thought it a warning about a future. It had been a report about the present. The body
stood at his gate.

"Mending," Dawid said.

"God be thanked," said Reuel, and the terrible thing, Dawid would tell the table later, the thing
the table had to understand about what stood at their gate — was that he meant it. There was not
one false coin in the man. He was glad the child had lived; he was glad the Queen was mending; he
had driven two days on a dead road to bring help he did not need to bring, and every gram of the
help was real, and all of it, the gladness and the gifts and the road itself, sat inside a single
arithmetic that had long ago stopped being cruel or kind: what stands, joins; what joins, is
counted; what is counted, is kept. He was not a wolf at the gate. He was a keeper at it, offering
the pen's own mercy, certain to his calm eyes that a pen is what a flock is for.

"You're short hands for the drying," Reuel said. "I have four on those vehicles who can work grain
in their sleep, and a physician your Sister Hettie can put to use or put to shame, her choice. No
wage, no terms, no paper. Neighbours, brother. It is what I came to be." Four hands and a
physician, from a strip that had said *Reuel and four*; Dawid filed the arithmetic without touching
it, for the table. And then, with the first
and only weight he put on anything that evening, quiet as a bolt going home: "You are a man of
order in a house of argument, Dawid. You know what that costs. I am the only other man on this band
who knows what you know. Open the gate."

And Dawid stood at his closed gate in the dusk, with everything he loved lamplit and short-handed
at his back, and wanted to.

He would say it plainly, later, at the table, because the table deserved it and because his father
had raised him to bill his own account: he wanted to. The wanting had a sound, and the sound was
rest — the rotas kept without argument, the watch relieved on time, the weight of the werf's whole
loud contrary life shared with the one man on earth who spoke the language of it; and under the
wanting, in the place where the file was kept, a boy stood at a post in the dark of a failing
world, waiting for an order that never came, holding a line nobody else would hold — and Dawid
looked at the calm certain eyes of the man who would have given that boy the order, every order,
forever, and understood the exact price of never standing an unrelieved post again.

The order comes. The order always comes. And the boy never learns what he holds when nobody holds
him to it — and a werf full of people who argue about water becomes a kept thing, name by name.

"The gate stays shut tonight," Dawid said. "Not for insult. We are a house of argument, as you say.
The house will argue it, all of it, at one table — our Queen included, if Hettie allows her the
chair. You'll have our answer at first light. There's water and fodder to the fence line; you'll
want for nothing while you wait."

Reuel looked at him a long moment, and something moved behind the calm that was not anger and was
not surprise; it was, Dawid thought, *interest* — the interest of a strong man meeting a tool that
declined the hand — and then he inclined his head, one degree, the schoolmaster acknowledging a
correct answer he had not taught.

"First light," said Reuel, of the Laer. "Argue well, brother. It's a fine thing to watch, an
arguing house. While it stands."

He walked back to his vehicles through the dusk, unhurried, and his lamps banked down to parking
glow; and Dawid stood at his gate until the dark was whole, and then walked up
through the yard, past the barn with the winter in it, past the long table where the strip still
lay, to the sick-room window, where a lamp was lit; and Marika was sitting up against her pillows,
awake, watching him come, having plainly heard all of it in the way she heard everything, and she
looked at him through the glass a long moment — the two thrones, one standing, one mending, the
whole werf dark and breathing around them — and she raised her hand, and beckoned, and said,
through the glass, in the voice of the true authority of the Kloof:

"Convene my table, Dawid. All of it. Tonight."
