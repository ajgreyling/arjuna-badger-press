# Chapter Seventeen
### *Book II · What we rebuild · close-third, Ivo*

They went north in the spring, two trucks and eight souls, on roads that had names now.

That was Jorik's doing — the naming. The boy had spent the winter drawing the net's first true map
from the bearings and the drive-logs, and he had named the roads the way the werf named everything,
after what had happened on them: the Fetch Road, the Trade Road, the Hail Road where Ronel's river
ran. Ivo had watched the map grow all winter on the kitchen wall beside the ONS LAG strip and the
wash-line rota, and had understood, in his own grammar, what it was: an aerial. A great slow
antenna of roads and stations, laid across the recovered world one journey at a time, for carrying
the only signal that had ever mattered.

He rode the whole way north with the good valves packed in straw on his knees, and did not let
Steyn take the corrugations at speed, and was not sorry.

The vault came up out of the green on the fourth morning the way Neva had described it at the
campfire a year ago and the way it had stood in Ivo's mind every day since: the great curved white
flank in the hillside, breathing its cold, humming its patient sealed hum — the far end of the
wire, the box the first voice of the new world had walked out of; and Ivo stood in the wet grass
where the trucks stopped and looked at it a long time, a wall with a whole world inside it, every
soul of them reciting the door and calling it a wall; and beside him Neva stood looking at it too,
and neither of them said anything, because between the two of them, on this one subject, everything
had been said a year ago at the top of an hour, and the saying was why they were here.

They had come to do three things. Jorik had them in the log as a list, because the expedition was
an instrument of the net now, minuted and mandated at the long table: *re-man the north station.
Draw seed. Leave what she leaves.*

---

The radio room took two days, and they were the best two days of Ivo's working life, and he knew it
while they were happening, which he had read in the manuals was rare.

The room was as she had left it a year ago — the dials, the log, the dead reels, the dust of
centuries she had disturbed once and the year had settled back — and Ivo stood at the bench where
the first walker of the recovered world had coaxed a dead machine to live with a frost-lamp and her
father's iron and no manual at all, and took his hat off, which Steve, carrying the battery in
behind him, marked and for once did not tease. They rebuilt her lash-up properly: the good valves,
the new aerial run up the flank on Steyn's rigging, the battery bank, the spare parts crated and
labelled in Elsabé's hand against the years. Jorik copied the founders' log — the whole column of
call-signs, the *silent, silent, silent*, the last shaking entry — into the net's common records,
"so the dead stations stay asked after," and then ruled a fresh page in the old book and wrote the
north station's first new line:

*RE-MANNED. The net keeps the watch now. — For the one who walked out, and the ones who will.*

Because that was the third thing about the room, the thing the table had argued longest and
warmest: it would not stand empty. The Strand was sending two of its own overland in the summer —
their first journey, their share of the watch — and until then a Kloof pair would keep the
station's hours; and so the room in the flank of the sealed world, built by the founders to knit
the arks together and silent for centuries, went back on watch for good: a lit lamp at the Vault's
own wall, listening, so that if ever — tonight, or in ten years, or in none — another soul read
too long, and walked the riveted dark, and found the seam, and turned the frost-locked wheel, she
would step out of the ends of the earth not into an empty green silence but into range of a warm
patient voice saying *ja, we hear you*, before her first night fell.

"You found me by losing me," Neva said, in the doorway, watching Ivo set the rota card above the
bench in his cramped stubborn hand. "They'll be found by our waiting."

"It's better arithmetic," Ivo said. "Waiting scales."

---

The seed took a day, and it was the day Ivo would tell worst and remember best, because the words
for it were all in the old register and he distrusted them, and the thing itself was only this: they
went in through the between — the airlock the founders had built even the leaving of carefully —
and into the cold sleeping heart of the wall itself, and there, in the long dark racks the frost
had kept for four hundred years, was the green world's whole insurance: the seeds, tens of
thousands of them, banked in their tins and their trays, waiting in the cold the way everything in
both their worlds had waited — for hands.

They drew what the net had voted: not a raid, a *loan against planting* — Elsabé's papers, Hettie's
lists, every tin logged out in the good log with its count and its promise, because the vault
belonged to the sealed world that kept it, and the net took nothing it did not mean to return
tenfold in living fields. Crates for the Kloof's lands, for the Strand's terraces, for the two dead
stations' valleys if anyone ever came to plant them; the first seed-post of the recovered world,
to travel the named roads and the summer routes, common as the pages, open as the frequency —
because Neva had stood in the racks with a tin of some ancient stubborn wheat in her two hands and
said the doctrine of the age into the frost-smoke of her own breath: "The books belong to the net.
So does the bread."

And Ivo, logging tins by lamplight in the cold heart of her world, had his one heresy of the
expedition, and kept it, and would tell it only to the wire: that the founders had built better
than their own plan. They had banked the seeds against the sky's fire. They had never guessed the
harder thing — that the seeds would outwait the *forgetting*, too; that the vault would keep the
bread safe from the very people it was keeping it for, until a walker came round the outside of
the world to draw it in trust. Nothing kept whole is ever wasted. The boy had written it. The racks
were the proof of it, ten thousand tins deep.

---

What she left, she left alone.

Ivo watched her go into the between with the wrapped bundle under her arm — the whole of it, he
knew, because the werf had spent the winter helping her make it: the un-redacted book entire,
copied fair by every hand at the long table, Jorik's straight sevens and Marika's strong loops and
Steve's surprising careful print, page after page after page, the sentence whole, in the
handwriting of a family — and he watched the door, because watching doors was the whole of what he
was for at such a time, and she came out the far side of an hour later without the bundle, lighter
in a way that had nothing he could log about it.

She did not tell them where in the between she had set it, or what she had weighted it with, or
what she had written on the wrapper. The log records only what she reported to the table, which
was: "The next one through that door finds the book waiting at the threshold. Before the sky.
Before the green. Before anything can frighten them, the sentence, whole, in a warm hand. What was
given me at the cost of everything, given on. That's all. That's the whole of what I went in to
do."

But Ivo was at the north station that evening, alone with the hours, when she came and stood at
the flank of her world one last time before the trucks turned south; and he did not watch, and saw
anyway, because the window was the window: Neva of the two worlds, palm flat on the white curve of
the wall, saying something he could not hear and did not need to — her mother in there somewhere
under the made and even light, kneading dough, believing her daughter safe in the deep fields, kept
and warm and small; and the daughter outside with her hand on the world, not opening it, because
you do not open a sealed world from the outside, because the door only means anything walked to
from within — leaving the book at the threshold and the lamp lit at the wall and the watch kept,
and choosing, at the cost only she could count, to let her whole first world take its own long
patient time to be born.

She stood back from the wall. She said one word to it — Ivo saw the shape of it through the glass,
and it was the old word, the whole law of both their lives, *keep* — and then she came in out of
the evening and sat down at the bench beside him and put the phones on one ear, her hour, the
rota's hour, and took the handset, and read the first page of the whole sentence into the open
frequency, from the flank of the sealed world, out across the named roads and the green ruin and
the curve of the round earth, to every soul and station listening, and to one who couldn't, yet:

*In the beginning was the firmament, and the light upon its track — and the ends of the earth went
on beyond all seeing, and it was good.*

The roar breathed. The wall hummed. Far south, faint, the Kloof answered, and the Strand after it,
laughing; and the log keeps the hour, and the net keeps the page, and the wall — the wall keeps
what it has always kept, patient, warm, and lit from the outside now, for as long as the watch
holds.

Which will be, the rota says, in Ivo's cramped stubborn hand, above the bench, at the far end of
the wire: *every night. Both ends. Until.*
