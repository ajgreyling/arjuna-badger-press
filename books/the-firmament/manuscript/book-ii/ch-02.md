# Chapter Two
### *Book II · Roll call · close-third, Marika*

The sky had spoken in the night, and the sheep did not care.

That was the first thing Marika thought, standing in her kitchen doorway in the grey before the light
with the fire already going at her back: that whatever had happened in Ivo's little room on the hill —
and everything had happened; she had stood in that crowded lamplight and heard a dead world talk — the
werf's day was still the werf's day. The bread still wanted kneading. The ewes in the sick-pen still
wanted looking at. The water still had to come up from the spring-line, and the beans did not know
there was a woman alive on the far side of the night, and would not have held their growing for her if
they had. There was a comfort in that which Marika did not examine too closely, because she suspected
that if she examined it she would find it was the whole of her religion: *the work goes on. Feed what's
in front of you.* You could carry any size of miracle if somebody kept the porridge from catching.

So she kneaded, and the werf woke around her the way it always did, in ones and twos and complaints,
and the news went through it like yeast through dough — slow, and then everywhere at once, and rising
as it went.

Hettie came first, of course. Hettie had been up before her — Hettie was always up before her; the two
of them had a running quarrel about it that was really a running courtship — and had already been to
the sick-pen and had already decided, Marika could see it in the set of her, what the night's news
meant, because for Hettie all news meant the same thing.

"She'll be hungry," Hettie said, tying her apron. Not *good morning*; not *did you hear.* "A woman
alone out there since — however long. You heard how thin her voice was."

"That was the radio, Het."

"It was thin," said Hettie, in the voice that ended discussions, and took the other bowl, and that was
Hettie: the whole impossible night reduced, by first light, to a person insufficiently fed, which was a
thing you could *do* something about. Marika loved her the way you love the other ox in your own yoke.
They worked, and the light came up over the heights the colour of skimmed milk, and the kitchen filled
with the smell that meant the day had properly started, and the men began to drift in — and this
morning, for the first morning since the walking-out, not one of them talked about the weather.

---

Steyn came in like a thrown door. He had been at the shack half the night, everyone had; the difference
was that Steyn had evidently spent the hours since converting wonder into plans.

"We go and get her." He said it before his backside touched the bench, to the room in general and Dawid
in particular. "That's it. That's the whole discussion. There's a woman alone in the veld somewhere —"

"Somewhere," said Dawid, from the head of the table, not looking up from the harness he had brought in
to mend, because Dawid's hands could not hear an argument start without picking up work to do while he
won it. "Somewhere is not a place, Steyn. You cannot walk to somewhere."

"You can walk toward it."

"In which direction?"

Steyn opened his mouth, and shut it, and Steve — who had come in behind him with his hat still on and
straw in his collar, having plainly slept in the barn nearest the shack rather than walk all the way
home from a miracle — said comfortably, into the gap, "North. It's always north in the stories. Or was
that up. Ivo!" — this out the door, at the hill — "which way's the dead world? Steyn's leaving before
breakfast!"

"He is not," said Marika, and put the porridge down on the table like a magistrate putting down a
gavel, and the morning meeting of the werf came to order the way it always did: around food, over
work, everybody talking, nobody chairing, and her at the end of the table where she could see every
face and top every bowl.

Roll call, her mother used to name it, in the Kloof, at tables underground: not the counting of heads
but the reading of them. Marika read them now, over the steam. Dawid at the head with the harness in
his lap, mending order into leather because the world had gone too interesting overnight. Steyn beside
him burning like a lamp with the wick too high, ready to walk to *somewhere* on an empty stomach out
of pure uncontainable act. Steve opposite, hat finally off, deflecting the night's size into jokes the
way a roof deflects hail, and watching, under the jokes, everyone else's weather. Tomas with his eyes
still red from the shack, radiant, unable to eat for telling it all again to Jorik — and Jorik, who
had heard it, who had *been* there, listening to the retelling anyway with his bowl going cold,
because some things improve with saying. Elsabé at the corner she preferred, squared to the door, one
line written small on the back of her hand and a list growing on a slate beside her bowl. And the two
empty places: Ivo's, because Ivo was asleep across his bench with the phones still on his ears — Jorik
had gone up at dawn and reported this in a whisper, as though describing a saint in a shrine — and
Ronel's, because Ronel was where Ronel was, which was the veld, which would give her up when it gave
her up and not one minute sooner.

*Mine,* Marika thought — she counted the sleeping and the absent and the far thin voice in the
counting; she had begun doing that in the night without asking herself why — *one short of a dozen,
though she could not have said why the count felt short. God help the world; it's got us for a rescue
party.*

---

Ronel came in mid-argument, which was the only way anyone ever came into that kitchen, with two hares
and a francolin hanging gutted from her fist and the veld still on her boots. She hung the game by the
door, washed her hands at the pail, took the bowl Hettie had kept warm without being asked and without
thanking her — the two of them had their own treaty, negotiated entirely in acts — and ate standing,
shoulder to the wall, while the table laid the whole night out for her in six voices at once: the
voice, the round world, the flat world with its roof, the woman alone, the rota, the everything.

Ronel ate, and listened, and when the six voices ran down enough to leave a gap, she asked her first
question of the new age of the world.

"How far off is she?"

The table looked at Ivo's empty place. The table looked back at Ronel.

"We don't know," Marika said.

"Then you know nothing," Ronel said — not unkindly; Ronel said everything the way she skinned game,
quick and clean and without malice, because doing it slowly was the cruelty — "you know a voice. Nice
for you. Wake me when it's a *distance*," and she took her bowl to the step and sat with her back to
the room, which was Ronel's way of staying.

"She has a point," said Elsabé, from her corner, and the fact that it was Elsabé agreeing meant the
table actually went quiet. She turned the slate so the room could see the list. "What we learned last
night: one voice, one frequency, one hour that works. What we did not learn: where she is, how she
lives, whether she can move, and whether anyone else is listening." She let that last one sit a
half-breath — nobody but Marika seemed to mark it — and went on. "Before anybody walks anywhere,
there are questions that cost nothing but a night's asking. Ivo can take bearings. If the old books
in that room are what he thinks, there are ways to make a direction out of a voice. A direction and
days-of-walking is a map. A map is a plan. A plan," and she looked at Steyn the way a schoolmistress
looks at a boy she has decided to save time on, "is what the grown people will be doing."

The slate had one more line at the bottom, below the list, in Elsabé's small exact hand, and she did
not read it aloud; Marika, coming past behind her with the pot, read it upside down, and carried it
around the table with her the rest of the morning like a stone in her shoe: *and if there are more of
them — how do you rebuild a world? what with?*

"And while the grown people draw maps," Steyn said, "she's alone in the middle of a dead world."

"She has been alone in it long enough to be alive and talking," Elsabé said evenly. "She is
demonstrably good at it. Another
fortnight of alive-and-talking beats one gallant idiot dead in the veld in the general direction of
north. Ja?"

It was brutal and it was true and it was Elsabé to the bone, and Steyn took it the way Steyn took a
fence — badly, at speed, and without lasting damage — and the argument rolled on over the cold
porridge, and Marika sat at the end of the table and let it roll, because this was the other thing her
mother taught her about roll call: you learn more from the argument than from the answers. She watched
the lines the night had drawn. Dawid and Elsabé had already, without one word between them, become a
committee: rotas, bearings, lists, the slow correct machinery of doing a thing right. Steyn was a
straining rope, and Steve had stationed himself, joke by joke, between Steyn and the door — clowning,
you would have said, unless you watched him do it twice. Tomas and Jorik had simply adopted her, this
Neva, sight unseen, a sister already; Tomas was making plans out loud about what she should be told
about them all before she arrived, *warn her about Steve at least*, as though her arriving were a
settled fact of the calendar. And Hettie had begun, quietly, at the far counter, with the face of a
woman who did not care to be observed doing it, to put things aside into a small sack. Rusks. The hard
sausage. The things that keep.

Faith, on this werf, looked — as the saying went — like mutton kept back from Steyn; and hope looked
like a provision sack packed for a stranger nobody could yet point to on any map in the world.

---

Dawid found her alone in the last light, at the wash-line, and stood beside her in silence for a
while, folding nothing, which for Dawid was almost lounging.

"They'll want to go," he said finally. "The moment the boy has a bearing. You know that. Steyn first
and half the young ones after him, and Tomas will talk himself aboard, God defend us all."

"I know."

"Call it four hundred kilometres. Call it more — I plan on worse and I'm seldom disappointed. Through
country nobody's walked in living memory. For a voice." He said *a voice* the way Elsabé said it, and then stood there a
moment, and then he said the other thing, the thing she knew had brought him to the wash-line, in the
flat parade voice he used when a sentiment had to be moved under guard: "She said the book is true.
That it hangs on nothing. And holds."

"Ja," Marika said.

"My father used to fight about those verses," Dawid said. "In the Kloof. The ones the elders wouldn't
read aloud. He held there was more book than we were given. He held it at some cost." He looked out at
the dark coming up over the heights, where somewhere beyond the rim of everything a woman with a thin
voice was sitting by a radio in a dead world, and Marika watched the harness-mending stillness of his
face and understood, with the jolt you get when a locked room shows a lit window, that the Sergeant of
the werf had not slept last night either, and not for logistics. "If she's real," Dawid said, "then
my father was right. That's all. I'd like to know it in his lifetime. He's old."

He straightened the wash-line pole, which did not need straightening, and went in.

And Marika stood in the blue dark hearing the first bats go over, and let herself, at last, at the end
of the whole impossible day, feel the size of it: the sky peopled again. The dead world with a woman
in it, and the woman would come to them — Marika knew it the way she knew when a child on the
werf was about to fall; the girl had a rota and a voice that laughed at the word *rota*, and she
would come. They would all come, in the end — however many voices were still out
there in the roar, however many sealed worlds full of argued-over books and kept-back suppers. The
world was going to get bigger now, and hungrier, and more complicated, and it was going to arrive at
her table, all of it, wanting feeding.

She went in and set the second batch of dough, the big one, to prove overnight.

You carried a miracle further, her mother used to say, on fresh bread.
