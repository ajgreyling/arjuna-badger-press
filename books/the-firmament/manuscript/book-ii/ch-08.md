# Chapter Eight
### *Book II · Who's on the wire · close-third, Dawid*

Dawid believed in order the way other men believed in the book, and he knew what that belief was made
of, and did not look at it, which is a thing a man can manage for years if his rotas hold.

Order was not the boot. He had said it to Steyn a hundred times, to Ronel with less hope, to himself
in the bad hour before the early watch: order was the *shape you built so that the weight never came
down on one man alone.* A rota was not a cage; a rota was the whole werf agreeing, in advance, in
daylight, who would carry which end of the night — so that no one stood a watch out of fear, and no
one slept out of luck, and when the wolf came, if it ever came, it would find not one tired man but a
structure. That was order. That was the whole of what he wanted in the world, and the werf teased him
for it and obeyed him in it and loved him through it, and if there was a reason he could not sleep
until he had heard the watch change — a reason with a sound, a particular sound, of a chain of
command coming apart around him long ago in the dark of the Kloof, of orders that stopped coming and
seniors who stopped answering and a boy standing at a post nobody would ever relieve him from — then
that reason was his own, and filed, and the file was closed.

The Laer reopened it. Not with a threat. That was the thing he would keep coming back to, after, in
the long wars of the table that were coming: the Laer never once threatened. The Laer *complied.*

---

They had answered slowly, as agreed. Name, not number: WE ARE THE KLOOF. FARMING PEOPLE. WHO ARE YOU.
Elsabé had drafted it and the table had passed it, every word weighed like ration-flour, and the
machine had tapped it out into the dark; and the fourth band had gone quiet for two nights, which
Elsabé said meant either nothing or deliberation, and which turned out to mean deliberation, because
what came back on the third night was not an answer. It was a *report.*

WE ARE THE LAER. It came through clean now — they had done something to their aerial or their power;
Ivo's eyebrows had climbed and stayed — and it kept coming, orderly as a manifest, while the printer
chattered on and the strip piled up in loops at Jorik's feet. Their numbers: given, unasked,
to the soul — two hundred and forty-one, with ages banded. Their site: a fortified farm-school in
the mountains, named on the founders' maps, coordinates offered. Their stores, their wells, their
herd counts, their two working vehicles, their dead — listed, dated, causes given. Their government:
one line, no apology. THE LAER IS UNDER DISCIPLINE. IT HAS SERVED US.

"They've answered everything we didn't ask," said Elsabé, reading the loops with the face she kept
for a move she had not predicted. "Location, strength, order of battle. Either it's the truth freely
given, or it's a truth designed to make us ashamed of having been careful."

"Can't it be both?" Neva said quietly, and Elsabé looked at her a moment and wrote something small on
the slate.

The report ended with the first request the Laer had made: PROPOSE VOICE CONTACT. YOUR HOUR. WE WILL
FIT OURS TO IT.

*We will fit ours to it.* Dawid read that line twice. It was precisely engineered to his own hand: the courtesy of a commander who could afford courtesy, deference offered
from strength, the shape of good order extended like a handshake. He proposed the hour. Of course he
proposed the hour.

---

The voice of the Laer was a man who introduced himself as Reuel, and he had the best radio voice any
of them had ever heard — better than the founders' recordings, Steve said afterward, and Steve did
not mean it entirely as praise. Level, warm, unhurried; a voice with all the time in the world and no
doubt in it anywhere; a voice that arrived, that first night, bearing gifts.

They had medicine. The Laer's vault had been a school with a clinic wing, and their stores held what
Hettie had been praying toward for two years — the names made her sit down slowly with her list
forgotten in her hand — and Reuel offered the pharmacopoeia list unprompted, complete, with expiry
judgements, *we will trade or we will give, your physician may choose.* They had seed the Kloof
lacked, and the varieties named were the right ones, the hard-country ones. They had visited — this
brought the shack to absolute stillness — two other stations off the founders' log, in person,
overland: both dead, documented, sealed again out of respect, artifacts catalogued. THE NET SHOULD
KNOW WHAT REMAINS, the follow-up strip said. WE WILL SEND THE SURVEY.

And Reuel asked for nothing. That night, nothing at all. He answered Marika's questions about their
children — forty-one under fifteen; a school running; he named the teachers — and Tomas's about
their music, which made him laugh, a real laugh, warm as the rest of him, *we have a choir, friend,
and it argues like a parliament* — and it was in that same glad hour, the table would establish
afterward, gently, and without blame that stuck, that Tomas told the Laer the whole story of the
fetch, unasked, unweighed, because joy in Tomas had no customs post; Elsabé's chalk paused, and moved
on — and when the hour thinned he thanked them for their discipline in
contact, complimented the werf's signals work — Ivo went stiff at the exactness of the compliment;
the man had read their operating habits like a page — and signed off on the dot of the schedule,
leaving behind him a shack full of people who had expected a census-taker and met, apparently, a
neighbour: competent, open-handed, and sane.

It was the following week, in the third voice contact, that Dawid asked the question he had been
saving — one commander to another, the shop-talk the others' questions never reached — of how the
Laer had come through its first winter out, which everyone on the wire by now knew had been a hungry
one for everybody.

"I'll tell you what I tell our own young ones," Reuel said. The warmth did not go out of the voice;
that was the thing Dawid would remember, that it never went out, not once, not even here. "We came
through because we did not vote about water. We drilled the well rota in the first week and we kept
it under discipline through the worst of it, and every soul who stood down a watch was fed at the
same table as every soul who stood one. Order is a mercy, brother. It is the only mercy that scales.
The colonies that voted about water are the ones I sealed the doors on last summer, out of respect."

There was a silence in the shack of a very particular kind.

"And who," said a voice — Neva's; she had not spoken on the wire to the Laer before, and Dawid saw
Elsabé's chalk stop moving — "keeps the roster of who is fed?"

"The Laer does," said Reuel, warm as ever, missing nothing, and Dawid could hear that he had turned
toward the question with real interest, the way a strong man turns toward a good tool. "You would be
Neva. The walker. Your story reached us before your voice did; you are the best news on this band.
To your question: the roster keeps the roster, sister. That is what a roster is *for* — so that no
man's mood and no man's favourite stands between a soul and her supper. You of all people know what
a world looks like when the keeping answers to nobody. Ours answers to me, and I answer for it. Name
me a better arrangement and the Laer will adopt it by morning."

It was, Dawid thought, the best answer he had ever heard to that question.

That was the moment — he would be an old man before he could say it aloud, and when he finally said
it, it would be to Marika, at a fire, with the whole of the story long over — that was the moment
the war for Dawid's soul began: not when the Shadow threatened, because it never threatened, but
when it answered well. When it stood on the far side of the wire in a voice with no doubt in it and
described, fluently, generously, in the vocabulary of mercy, the exact world Dawid had spent two
years building rotas to hold at bay — and Dawid's own heart, in the dark of the shack, under the
strip that said ONS LAG, had leaned toward it like a plant toward a lamp.

After the sign-off, in the ordinary noise of the shack standing down, Neva stayed in her corner, and
Dawid, securing the log, found her looking at him.

"I know that voice," she said. Not accusing. Worse: kind. "Not the man. The voice. It kept my whole
world, and it never once threatened, and it never once lied outright, and it was always, always
reasonable." She got up, and at the door she said the thing he would carry up the hill and into his
sleepless hour and down all the long wars of the table to come:

"The record had a voice like that, Dawid. It just never needed a body before."
