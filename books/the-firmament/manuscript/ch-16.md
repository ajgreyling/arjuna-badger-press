# Chapter Sixteen

She did not go back through the door.

She meant to. She walked the long way back across the green heights to the wall, the whole three days of it in
reverse, and came at last to the place where the torn ice went up out of the world into the roofless sky — and
it was strange beyond telling to see it from *outside*, the great curved white flank of the thing she had lived
her whole life inside, a vast frost-sealed dome set into the green like a held breath, humming its low labouring
hum, tending its lit warm kept moment on, indifferent, patient, exactly as it had for twelve generations and an
ocean of years. Her whole world. From the outside, a machine in a field. She stood and looked at it a long time.

But she did not go to the between-door she had come out of. Because circling the base of the dome, reading it now
from the outside the way she had read it from within, she found — where the green had not yet climbed, where the
old makers had set it into the living rock — a second door. Lower. Older. Heavier. Not a keeper's between-hatch
but something else, marked with symbols she could not read, and around it, half-swallowed by the patient green,
the ruin of a made place: fallen masts, a collapsed roof of some long grey stuff, and a squat hardened structure
built into the flank of the wall itself, sealed, shielded, made to last past the end of the world.

She got it open the way she got everything open now: with her father's iron and her keeper's patience and the
still cold certainty that there was always a back to the wall. And inside, in the dark, under decades — centuries —
of dust, was a room.

Not a between-room. Not a seedbank. A room with a *purpose* she had no name for. A long bench, and on the bench
a made thing — dials, a great many dials, and a grille, and a thing on a cord that fit the hand, and a book. She
lit the frost-lamp. The made thing was dead, dark, cold; but it was analog, all of it, dials and coils and glass,
no different in kind from the valve-and-wire of the world she knew, and her hands understood it before her mind
did — that it was a thing for *listening*, and for *speaking*, across a distance; a thing the founders had built,
hardened, into the very flank of the sealed world, and then — she saw it in the dust, in the dead dark of it —
left.

She opened the book. It was a log. Column on column, in the old careful founders' hands — clear hands, loving
hands, hands that believed a clear hand was a kind of love — of names that were not names but call-signs, and
beside each a mark, a date, a word. And she could read enough of it, enough of the shape of it, to understand
what she was looking at, there in the dust, in the dark, in the hardened room in the flank of her sealed world:
that there had been *others.* Other rooms like this. Other sealed worlds, other lit kept moments, other caste
gone into the ground the same year hers did, each under its own borrowed name — and that these rooms had *spoken
to each other*, once, across the dead green world, a nervous system of hardened voices keeping the pieces of
everything in touch so that whatever came, they could find one another again when it was over.

And then, down the log, the marks changed. One by one, turning by turning, the call-signs went to a single word,
in a hand that grew older and shakier and at last stopped: *silent. Silent. Silent.* One by one the other worlds
had gone quiet — not destroyed, she understood, but *sealed*, tended, their people forgetting, the way her people
had forgotten, that there was a room in the flank of the world with a voice in it. Forgetting they had ever been
able to reach each other. Forgetting they were not alone. The last entry was so long ago the ink had gone to a
ghost.

Neva sat down at the dead bench in the hardened room with the log of the silenced worlds open under her hands,
and understood the last and largest of the things the round world had come to teach her: that she was not the
first one awake in *one* world coming back. There were others. Sealed, forgetting, reciting their own doors in
their own lit rooms — but *there*, out there, around the curve of the round earth that had no end, other worlds
full of kept and folded and salt-hidden people, each one waiting, without knowing it waited, for someone brave
enough or broken enough or loving enough to walk out and read the rest of the sentence.

And here was a voice to reach them.

She did not know how to wake it. It was dead, and dark, and centuries cold, and she was one keeper with a frost-
lamp and her father's iron and a bundle of pages against her heart. But she was a keeper of drift; she had
resurrected the frost-line on her wall a hundred times; and the thing on the bench was analog, dials and coils
and glass, a thing that could be understood by a patient hand, a thing that — she found the truck of it, the
heavy dead battery-heart of it, under the bench, and knew, the way Ivo would one day know, that a dead machine
is only a machine waiting — a thing that could be *made to live again.*

It took her the rest of that day and most of the dark. She would not remember, after, all of what she did — the
cleaning, the coaxing, the patience, the keeper's listening to a machine that did not yet want to speak. But she
would remember, all the rest of her life, the moment it lived: the hum, first, the round warm breathing hum she
knew from the world, rising in the dead room; and then a glow, deep in the glass; and then, out of the grille, out
of the centuries, out of the silence of all the sealed and forgotten worlds around the curve of the roofless
earth — a sound. Not a voice. Only the sound of the listening itself, the vast soft roar of the open air between
the worlds, waiting.

Neva took the thing on the cord in her cold-scarred hand. She did not know the words. She knew only that somewhere
out there, around the round of the earth that had no end, under the same roofless brimming sky, was another sealed
lit world with another hardened room, dark and forgotten in its flank — and that maybe, maybe, someone in it had
read too long, and walked out, and was sitting even now at a dead bench with a frost-lamp, learning how to make a
dead machine live.

She pressed the thing that made it speak, the way you press a wound you need to know is still bleeding.

"Is anyone there," Neva said, into the roar of the open air between the worlds, in her own voice, the first new
words the sentence had reached. "This is — " and she stopped, because she did not have a call-sign, she had only
what she was — "this is one who walked out. Onto the round world. The book is true. It hangs upon nothing, and it
holds. If you can hear me — if anyone can hear me — the ends of the earth go on forever, and it is good, and you
are not alone."

And she let go of the thing that made it speak, and sat in the hum and the glow in the hardened room in the flank
of her sleeping world, at the edge of the round earth, under the roofless sky, and listened, with a keeper's
whole patient listening, into the roar of the open air.

For a long time there was nothing.

The nothing held.

And then — faint, and far, and cracked with centuries and distance, from somewhere around the curve of the round
world that had no end, from another lit sealed forgotten world where someone, someone, had also read too long and
walked out and learned to wake the dead —

a voice answered.
