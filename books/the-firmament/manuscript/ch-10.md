# Chapter Ten

Up close the wall was not a wall.

She had known it her whole life as a wall — the flat grey machine-face on the working side, the frost-line
you read with your palm, the cold border of the world — but that was the near wall, the keepers' wall, the
tame face of it. Here, past the fields, past the lamps, at the true rim where no one came, the ice went up
out of the little lamp's reach into a dark she could not measure, and it was not flat and it was not a face.
It was *cliffs.* Ranked frozen cliffs, blue-white where the lamp caught them, rising and rising, seamed with
the black lines of the machinery that ran inside them, breathing out cold in a slow steady exhalation that
was almost, she thought, almost like something sleeping. This was the seedbank. She understood that dimly,
without the word — that the cold itself was the point of the wall, that the ice was not the edge of creation
but the great sleeping cold heart of it, and that everything green the caste had ever grown had come, once,
from something laid down to sleep in this cold, long ago, by hands that were dust.

She put her palm an inch off it, the keeper's habit, reading the cold — and stopped, because the cold at the
true rim was not the cold of the working wall. It was deeper and older and it had a *structure* to it, a
grain, and reading it was like putting your hand near a page in a language you half-knew, and the thing it
said, the thing the deep ice said to a keeper who could read cold like her father, was not *the end of
creation.* It was *a made thing. Sealed. Held. Cold on purpose. Cold with a reason.* And under that, fainter,
the thing that stopped her breath: *there is a far side to this. Cold has two sides. Everything cold has a
warm it is keeping something from.*

She stood a long time with her palm near the ice, in the dark, in the labouring hum, and let the wall talk to
her the way it had talked to her father, and did not look away.

---

It was here that the world began to thin.

She could not have said it better than that, then or ever. It was not that anything changed; it was that she
*saw*, for stretches, the way you see a familiar face suddenly as only bone and skin — saw the wall not as
the wall but as *material*, as riveted plate and frozen coolant and running machinery, a made thing made by
someone, and once she had seen it that way she could not unsee it, and the seeing spread. The floor under her
boots: plate and rivet. The dark overhead where the firmament came down to meet the ice: a *ceiling*, a
seam, a place where two made surfaces were joined. The very air, scrubbed and even and breathed by twelve
generations, going in and out of vents she could hear now, ticking, cycling, tended. The whole of the world,
for a reeling stretch there at the rim, stopped being *the world* and became a *room* — an enormous, ancient,
lovingly-built room, sealed, tended, running itself over the bones of the people who built it — and Neva
stood in the middle of the thinning with her hand near the ice and thought she might be sick, or might laugh,
or might simply come apart, because there is a particular horror in watching your whole world resolve into a
thing with edges, and it is very close to the particular joy of it, and she felt both, at once, in the cold
dark, and understood that this was the place. This was what her father had reached. This was the thinning he
had lived in at the end, reading too long, seeing the room behind the world — and *not looking away*, and
loving it anyway, both ends of it, the world and the room, the lie and the truth, the warm gold caste and the
riveted dark.

*It made him love it more,* Sarel had said. She understood that now too. Because a world you think is
creation you take for granted; it simply is, the way you are. But a world you can see was *built* — built
against the cold, built to last, built with a light on a track so the children would have days and not be
afraid — a *built* world is a world someone *made you a gift of*, at the cost of their lives, and you cannot
take a gift for granted once you have seen the hands that gave it. The room did not make the world less holy.
It made it holy in a different way — the way a thing is holy that someone died to give you.

She took her palm from the ice. Her hand was aching with the deep cold. She was weeping, a little, without
having noticed starting, and it was not fear.

"You built us a world," she said, to the ice, to the dust in the cold, to whoever had sealed the hatch on
the last true daylight with a child's hand in theirs. "You built us a whole world so we'd be safe. And you
were so good at it we forgot it was you."

The wall breathed its slow cold breath and did not answer, because walls do not, because the people who
could have answered were twelve generations of dust in the cold they had made.

But she had felt it, reading the ice: *a far side.* Cold has two sides. And a room, however lovingly built,
however holy, is still a room — and a room has a door.

She lifted the frost-lamp, and raised her father's bright iron, and began to walk the rim, close in against
the breathing cliffs, reading the cold as she went, looking — for the first time in twelve generations of
her line, on purpose, without looking away — for the seam that was a seal, and the seal that was a door, and
the door that opened on whatever the ends of the earth turned out, at last, to mean.
