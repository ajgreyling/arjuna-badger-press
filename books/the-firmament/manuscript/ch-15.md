# Chapter Fifteen

It was the road that told her how long.

She had turned back inland from the edge of the water, because a person cannot live at the lip of the world,
and because something in her — the keeper, the reader of drift — wanted now to *understand* the round world
the way she had understood the flat one, and you did not understand a world by kneeling at its edge weeping,
however true the weeping. So she walked back into the green, and on the second day of the walking back she came
upon the road.

She knew it was a made thing at once; she had learned to see made things. But it was a made thing the way
nothing in her world was made — not tended, not even, not kept. It was a long straight scar of grey stone laid
across the land, cracked all along its length, buckled and split, and out of every crack in it grew the green —
grass, and saplings, and here a whole tree, decades old, risen up through the middle of the road with its roots
prising the grey stone apart. She stood on the broken road and understood, slowly, in her body, the way she had
understood the rain and the circle and the nothing, a thing she had no way to have understood before: that this
road had been made, and used, and then *left* — and that the leaving had been very long ago. Long enough for a
tree to grow up through stone. Long enough for the green to take back a road.

She followed it, and it brought her, by the end of that day, to the bones of a town.

She had no word for it. Buildings — but ruined, roofless like everything out here, their windows empty, their
walls green with the slow patient climbing life; a whole vast tangle of them, more dwellings than her whole
caste had, gone to ruin and green together, silent, emptied, held in the same enormous quiet as the road and
the height and the sea. She walked its broken ways in the failing light and understood where she was. This was
the world the founders had sealed the caste away from. This was *outside*, not the wild green outside of the
heights but the outside where the people had been — the people who made the roads and the buildings and the
music the founders must have loved, the people of the last true daylight, her people, the ones the flare had
come for. And they were gone. All of them. Long, long gone — long enough for the trees to come up through the
roads, long enough for the towns to go green, long enough that she, walking their broken streets, was the first
living soul to walk them in more turnings than she could hold.

She went into one of the buildings because the doorway stood open and the light was going and some old
keeper's habit would not let her pass a door unread. It had been a small room once, low-ceilinged, and
the green had not fully taken it; enough roof held that the floor was dry, and on the floor, half-buried
in fallen leaf-litter gone to soil, was a shoe. A child's shoe, small enough for her own two hands to
cup, its leather gone stiff and dark with age but its shape still whole, still laced, the lace tied in a
bow by fingers that had known how, once, for someone who could not yet tie it themselves.

She did not touch it. She knelt over it a long while in the going light, and thought of the woman at the
last hatch with a small hand in hers, and understood, kneeling in the green-fallen room, that the woman
had been someone's mother before she was anyone's founder — that somewhere in this town or one like it a
child this shoe fit had run and fallen and been picked up and had the lace done up again a hundred
ordinary times before the last one, and that all of that — the running, the falling, the doing-up of
laces — had gone into the same ground the shoe was sinking into now, unmourned by anyone left to mourn it,
until her.

She mourned it. She had no other name to give it, no ledger entry, not even a guess at a name; she gave it
only her kneeling, and her tears, and the plain fact of being the first living soul in centuries of turnings
to notice a small shoe on a floor and understand what it had cost someone to lace it. Then she left it
exactly where it lay, because it was not hers to carry and not hers to disturb, and went back out into the
dying light with one more grief added to the ledger she was keeping now, the last and gentlest one: not
grief for the flat lie, not grief for her mother's kitchen, but grief for strangers she would never have a
name for, who had loved a child, once, in a room the green was slowly, patiently taking back.

That was when the size of the leaving reached her. Not the size of the world — she had met that at the height.
The size of the *time.* She had stepped out of a sealed room into the round world and she had thought, in the
first shock of it, that she had walked out into *space* — into the up, the green, the enormous where. But she
understood now, in the green bones of the town, that she had walked out into *time* — that the room had been
sealed not just against the sky but against the years, that it had kept the caste in the last true daylight
while out here the daylight went on and on, turning after turning after turning, the flare's dead world slowly,
patiently, greenly coming back to life without a single soul to see it — and that she had stepped out of the
kept moment into all the years that had run past it, centuries of them, an ocean of time, and she was as far
from the people who made this road as she was from the far curved line of the sea.

She sat down in the green ruin of a doorway, in the last of the light, and grieved that too. There was so much
to grieve. She was getting used to the largeness of what there was to grieve.

---

But grief was not the end of it, and she knew it was not, because she was her father's daughter and her
father had loved the world at both ends, and because the pages against her heart were not only a verse about
nothing. They were a *seedbank's* scripture. She had carried out the sentence of a people whose whole purpose,
whose whole reason for sealing themselves into a lit room against the burning sky, had been to *keep something
alive to plant again.*

She understood it there, in the green doorway, as the light went down over the world that had come back to life
without anyone. The ice wall was cold because the cold kept the seeds. The caste was sealed because the sealing
kept the people. The whole vast lovingly-built room — the firmament, the track, the ration, the tended even
world she had loved and fled — had been built for exactly one thing, held in reserve across twelve generations
and an ocean of years, waiting: for the day someone walked out, onto a world gone quiet and green and ready,
and began again.

That was the vocation. It came to her not as a voice, not as a verse, but as a plain cold understanding of what
she was for — she, Neva, keeper of drift, carrier of the sentence, first soul in centuries to walk the green
bones of the world. The caste could not do it; the caste did not know it needed doing; the caste was sealed in
the kept moment reciting the door. But she was out. She had the pages. She had, sleeping in the cold heart of
the wall she had walked through, a whole world's worth of green things laid down to wake. And she had a roofless
brimming ready Earth, grieving and green and alive, turning upon nothing, holding, waiting to be planted.

She was not the last of anything. That was the turn, there in the doorway, the thing that lifted her up off the
grief the way the nothing had lifted the world. She had thought, in the first days, that she was the last one
awake in a dead world. She understood now that she was the *first* one awake in a world coming back — that the
seedbank's long patient purpose, the reason her line had been kept and folded and hidden and smoothed to
*nominal*, the reason for the wall and the cold and the whole sealed gold lie, was *her*, walking out, now, with
the pages and the seeds and the whole green ready earth in front of her.

"All right," she said, into the green ruin, into the falling light, the way she had said it to the wall. Her
voice did not break this time. "All right. I'll plant it."

And she got up, in the last of the light, in the bones of the old world, and turned back the way she had come —
toward the wall, toward the cold sleeping heart of it, toward the seeds; because a person who has understood
what she is for does not sit in a doorway, she goes and begins; and the round world turned under her, upon
nothing, carrying her toward the work, and held.
