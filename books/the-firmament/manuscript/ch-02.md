# Chapter Two

Her mother was of the opinion that Ines had gone to the deep fields and that was the end of it, and she
said so while she worked the dough, which was how she said everything that mattered — sideways, with her
hands busy, so that a thing could be spoken without having to be looked at.

"People get reassigned," she said. "Your father was reassigned."

"My father died," Neva said.

"Reassigned first." Her mother turned the dough. "There's the deep fields, and there's further than the
deep fields, and a keeper who reads too long at the cold wall goes one and then the other. You know this.
Everyone knows this. It's not a mystery, Neva, it's a *job.*"

It was the reasonable thing to say and her mother was a reasonable woman, and Neva loved her, and did not
believe her, and could not have said why. She only knew that *reassigned* had begun to sit in her chest
like a stone she'd swallowed — a word that had always been furniture, that you walked past a hundred times
a day without seeing, and that had now, for no reason she could name, turned and shown her it had a back
she had never looked at.

She helped with the bread. She did not say the other thing, which was that she had gone to the record
again that morning, early, before her shift, and read Ines's last three days of frost-readings one more
time, slowly, the way her father had taught her to read cold — not for what it said but for what it was
trying not to say. And the readings were perfect. That was the thing. Ines had never in her life written a
perfect column of readings; Ines rounded, Ines guessed the middle bells when she'd been talking instead of
listening, Ines was a *person* at the wall. And the record now held three days of her work in a hand that
never rounded and never guessed and never once, in three days of readings, made the small human error of
a woman doing a long cold job while thinking about her supper.

Somebody had done Ines's work better than Ines. In one night. And then filed it under her name.

"You're somewhere else," her mother said.

"I'm here," said Neva, and floured her hands, and was not.

---

The deep fields were down. Everyone knew that the way everyone knew everything — without having been told,
without having gone. Down the freight lift at the south gate, down past the pump galleries where the water
went round again, down into the warm dark under-half of the world that the track had never lit: the root
crops, the mushroom sheds, the deep tanks, the long galleries where things grew that did not need the sky.
The work down there was warm, people said, which was a kindness in the usual manner of kindnesses — warm
meant dark, and wet, and out from under the light. Hard hands and good soil. Half the caste had somebody
down there. Nobody had ever visited one.

She noticed that on the second day, the way you notice a hole in a thing you have owned your whole life.
Nobody had ever visited one. The deep fields fed the world — the dark flour in the loaves, the winter
roots, Ouma Renske's beer, brewed to a strength no ration explained — and the world sent people down to
them, and tallies came up with the crates every morning, weights and counts in a round clerk's hand, and
in the whole warm even world there was no road you could walk that arrived there. The lift carried crates.
The lift was not for riding. Not forbidden — nothing was ever forbidden — just not for riding, the way
the wall was not for touching.

On her rest-day she walked the spoke road down to the south gate anyway, because a thing that is not done
is not the same as a thing that cannot be done — she was still young enough to think so — and
found the lift-man in his warm little hut beside the gates, an oiled, cheerful man with his tea and his
tally-spike, who heard her out with his head tipped and did not once look at her strangely.

"Course you can, love," he said. "Write it small."

So she wrote it small — *Ines. It's Neva. Send word you're settled. The wall is boring without you* —
and the lift-man read it without pretending not to, the way of men who handle other people's words all
day, and spiked it with the morning tallies, and said, "Goes down with the crates. Anything comes back up,
I'll send it along the row." And then, kindly, over the tea: "Don't expect fast. They're on soil-time down
there. Things come up when they're grown."

Nothing came up. Not in three days, not in six. Which was not strange — everyone agreed it was not
strange; settling in took time; soil-time, said the lift-man, kind as ever, spiking her second note beside
the first.

Beside the first. She saw it as he did it — her own small folded word, three days on the spike, going
soft at the edges the way paper goes in frost-air — and saw what she had not let herself see before:
that the spike was thick with notes, dozens deep, in dozens of hands, the newest crisp and the oldest
weathered grey. Write it small, love. The crates went down and the tallies came up, and she went through
it carefully, walking home, the way you walk a frost-line: no word had ever come back from the deep
fields. Not to her. Not to anyone she could name. No letter. No message at the turning. No keeper riding
up on the lift to dance at the Lighting. Word came up when word was needed, and it was always the same
kind of word: a line in the census at the turning, a notice when a keeper died of the warm damp — word
about her own father had come up that way, in a fair hand, regretting the loss to the caste. Official
word. Word with its collar buttoned.

There was also the draw. The stretch's draw rode with the stretch, so carrying Ines's panels took her to
the office by the field gate, and to the clerk — a soft, pleasant woman with ink on her thumb — who
turned the great roll around on the counter unasked so that Neva could see for herself: Ines's line
struck through, clean, single-ruled, carried over to the deep-fields roll with the date and the mark. A
fair hand, of course.

"Went through the morning after she was posted," the clerk said, approving, tapping the line with her
inky thumb. "They're quick with the rolls. Whatever else, you can say that — nobody falls between the
pages in *this* world." She said it with real satisfaction, a woman proud of the books she kept, and
Neva thanked her, and meant it, because the clerk was kind and the office was warm and there was nothing,
nothing anywhere, to be anything but grateful for.

Every door she tried stood open. Nobody stopped her. Nobody asked why she was asking. The lift-man spiked
her notes and the clerk turned the roll around and everyone answered every question she dared to put —
and every open door gave onto the same soft furnished nothing. She had gone looking for a locked door,
and there was no locked door in the world. By the sixth day she understood, without words, the shape of
the thing she was pushing on. It was like pressing on wool. You could press as long as you liked. The
wool did not press back, and the wool did not stop you, and the wool went down forever.

---

The day-track lit and crossed and the world went about itself, and Neva went about the world, and tried
to make the stone go back to being furniture. For a while it half-worked. There is a great and quiet power
in the ordinary, and the Vault was nothing if not ordinary; it wrapped you in the warm hum and the even
light and the smell of the growing fields and the small kindnesses of people who had known you since you
were born, and it asked you, gently, all day long, to be content, and most days you were, because what was
the alternative? You could not be discontent with *creation.* There was nowhere for the discontent to
stand.

And the ordinary was not nothing; that was the trap of it. Wash-day came, and the mending circle in the
long room, her mother's needle flashing in the lamp-warmth while Ouma Renske retailed the row's news —
the autumn apprentice-taking, and which young keepers might put in for it, Karel's name said with the
particular relish the old kept for a boy pretending he could leave the wall — and the bells rang the
world through its turns, and her nine panels and Ines's two were eleven panels of honest cold that had
to be read whatever else was true. The work held you. The work was made of holding you. She read her
frost and wrote it fair and was, for whole bells together, only a keeper at her wall, and grateful to
be nothing more.

She took her supper that evening in the long room with the others of her caste, and it was good, and it
was warm, and Ouma Renske told the story about the ration clerk and the two hens that everyone had heard
four hundred times and everyone laughed at anyway, because that was what the story was *for*, and old
Tobias said, the way he always did when the laughing died down, "Well. We're not dead yet," and everyone
said it back — *not dead yet* — the way you'd say amen. It was the nearest thing the keepers had to a
spoken prayer that wasn't in the book. *Not dead yet.* Neva had said it all her life without hearing it. She
heard it now. She thought: *there are people who* were *dead, then. That's what the joke is standing on.
Every joke we have is standing on something.* And she looked around the warm long room at the faces she
loved, laughing, *not dead yet*, and for one bad moment the warmth of it felt exactly like the even light
of the track — too regular, too kind, arranged.

There was an old keeper at the far end of the long table that night, down from the deep shift for his
supper — one of the very old ones, stooped, with ruined hands around his cup, whose stretch she did not
know and whose name she had never needed. He did not laugh at the hens, though his face was not unkind
about it. And once, when she looked up from her plate, he was looking at her — quietly, with nothing in
it she could put a name to; the way a keeper looks at a panel that has begun to run warm out of grace.
Then Ouma Renske said something outrageous and he was only an old man again, drinking his tea with both
hands.

She put the moment down. You could not live in that moment. She ate her supper.

---

At home, at lamp-out, her mother read the page.

Every house had the book, and every house read it the same way, which was hardly at all and completely:
a page at the lamp, at the day's end, the same pages coming round in their season like the light coming
round its track. Her father used to read it. Now her mother did, sitting under the lamp with the book
flat on the table, her finger moving because that was how she had been taught, and Neva sat across from
her with the mending in her lap and said the lines along with her in the low voice you used for it,
because you did not exactly read the book; you kept it company.

*In the beginning was the firmament,* her mother read, *and the light upon its track, and the ice at the
ends of the earth. And it was good.*

*And it was good,* Neva said, in the place where you said it.

And it was. That was the thing she would have wanted to be understood, the thing the words held even now,
with the stone in her chest and the wool at every door: the lamp was warm, and the bread was proving
under its cloth, and her mother's voice was her father's cadence worn smooth, the way a daughter wears a
father's boots, and the words were the oldest words there were and every one of them was *true* — the
firmament stood over them; the light kept its track; the ice held the ends of the earth. She said *and
it was good* and meant it, all the way down. The book had never once lied to her.

She fell asleep that night to the sound of her mother banking the stove, and did not know why she lay a
while first, listening, in the dark, under the thin line of banked light along the floor, to the world
humming to itself behind its walls.

---

On the next wash-day she went to see Ines's mother, who lived at the warm end of the row, and brought a loaf
of her own mother's baking, because you did not go into a grief empty-handed, even a grief with good
news at the bottom of it.

The house was tidy the way a house goes tidy around a missing person — everything squared, everything
wiped, the chairs pushed in — and Ines's mother was glad of her, gladder than Neva could bear, and made
the tea and talked. The deep fields would be the making of the girl, she said. The warm would be good for
her chest. And the soil-time would settle her — "Twenty years I couldn't get that child to fold a blouse,"
she said, wry and fond, with the laugh keepers' mothers keep by the door. "They'll have her tidy inside a
turning. They'll manage what I never could." And Neva laughed with her, in the tidy kitchen, with the tea
between them, and if either of them heard how the laugh rang against all that wiped and squared-away
quiet, neither of them said.

Then Ines's mother showed her the kit.

It had come up on the lift on the third morning — Ines's frost-kit, the wall tools she'd have no use for
below — and it sat now on the shelf by the door where a keeper's kit sits, and Neva looked at it and the
kitchen went very far away. The mittens were paired. The sounding-lines were coiled, each in its own flat
loop, tied off. The pencil torch, the margin-rule, the little brass gauge her father's generation had
called a frost-finger — each in its loop, each in its place, the whole kit squared away like a drawer in
the record room. Ines had carried that kit for nine years. Ines did not coil lines; Ines's kit was a
famous midden, a joke of the line, a thing her master had wept over in front of witnesses. Somebody had
packed this kit the way somebody had written those readings — fair, even, patient, in a hand she could
not have named — and sent it up to stand on a shelf by a mother's door and be a comfort.

"They're careful down there," Ines's mother said, following her look, and her voice was full, and grateful,
and proud. "Whatever else. You can see it. They take such care."

Her father's boots had come up the same way, Neva remembered — suddenly, whole, the memory coming up
through her like cold through a boot-sole: brushed and blocked, the laces in two neat coils, and everyone
saying how it spoke well of the fields, that they sent a man's things up so beautifully kept. She had
been glad of the care, then. She was wearing the boots now, at Ines's mother's table, with the tea going
cold in her hands.

"Yes," Neva said. "They take such care."

She walked home along the row with the light going down rim-ward, past the squared warm windows of the
people she came from, and she did not cry, and she could not have said, if you had stopped her under the
dimming track and asked, what there was in the whole reasonable open-doored world to cry about.

---

It was Tobias, in the end, who gave her the thread that held, though he did not mean to, and did not know
he had, and would have been frightened if he'd known.

He was the oldest keeper still on the wall, older than her father would have been, and his hands shook now
so that they'd taken him off the frost-line and put him on the record instead — a soft reassignment, the
gentle kind, the kind that ended in a chair and not in the further-than-the-deep-fields. He liked Neva. He
had liked her father. And when she brought him his tea in the record room that night — she had volunteered
for it; she told herself it was kindness and it was, and it was also that the record room was where the
question lived — he was in a talking humour, and he talked.

"Your father," he said, "could read cold like scripture. Better than me. I'd walk his line behind him
sometimes just to feel how he did it. You have it. You know you have it."

"I know," she said. It wasn't pride. It was just true, the way the wall was true.

"He read too long," Tobias said, comfortably, sipping. "That's the trouble with the good ones. The wall
talks and the good ones can't stop listening, and there's things at the wall a person's better off not
hearing." He said it the way her mother said *reassigned* — sideways, hands busy, a thing spoken so it
wouldn't have to be looked at. "You be a middling keeper, girl. Middling keepers live to sit in this chair
and drink bad tea and bore the young ones. You hear me? Be middling."

"What did he hear?" Neva said.

And Tobias, who was old and tired and warm with tea and had known her since she was born, opened his mouth
to answer — she saw it, she saw the answer *right there*, some shape of it, in the old man's face — and
then something went across him, some old trained flinch, deeper than thought, the way your hand comes off
a hot thing before you've decided anything, and he shut his mouth, and looked at his tea, and when he spoke
again his voice had gone careful in a way she had never once heard from him in her whole life.

"Nothing," Tobias said. "There's nothing at the wall, Neva. That's the whole point of it. Past the ice
there's nothing at all." He looked up at her, and his old eyes were wet, and she did not know if it was age
or fear or the tea. "You go home now," he said. "You go home and you be middling and you forget you asked
me that. Will you do that for me. Will you forget you asked."

She said she would.

It was the first lie she had ever told a keeper of the wall, and she told it looking straight into the eyes
of a frightened old man who loved her.

She took the long way home, down the line, under the parked track. Far along the grey, on the deep shift,
a single lamp burned at a panel: one of the very old ones, stooped over his ledger, filing his frost in
the cold hours. He raised his head as she passed — too far to greet, too dim to know — and held it raised,
and did not lower it again until she had gone by; and Neva, whose head was full of a frightened old man's
wet eyes, thought nothing of it at all.

And she walked home under the parked and dimming track with the stone in her chest gone suddenly, terribly
light — because a stone that heavy has no business floating, and this one had just begun to, and she
understood, walking, that things only float when there is nothing under them holding them down.

*Nothing at all,* Tobias had said, *past the ice.*

He had said it like a man throwing a blanket over a fire.
