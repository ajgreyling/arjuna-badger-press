# Chapter Twelve

The first thing was the cold, and the second thing was the size, and there was no third thing for a long
while because the size took everything she had.

She had lived her whole life under a roof. She had not known it was a thing she knew until it was gone. All
her days the firmament had come down close and dark overhead, near enough that the eldest could read the verse
up into it, near enough that the made sun crossing its track was the whole of the sky — and now there was no
firmament, there was no near, there was no overhead at all, there was only *up*, and up, and up, farther than
the eye could climb, an up with no top to it, a blue so deep and so high and so utterly without ceiling that
Neva fell to her knees in the wet cold and put her hands flat on the ground and held on, because a person with
no roof over her has nothing to keep her from falling into the sky.

That was the first terror. That she would fall up. She knelt with her hands pressed to the cold living ground
and shook, not with cold now, and could not look up again, could only look at her own white knuckles against
the ground and breathe the impossible green rotting living air in gasps, while over her and around her and
past her in every direction the round world simply, enormously, indifferently *was.*

When she could, she looked.

She was on a slope, in a grey wet light, and it was raining — she did not have the word; water was falling
out of the roofless up, softly, everywhere, and she turned her freezing face into it and it ran down her skin
and she understood *the waters above* in her body a whole breath before the verse surfaced in her, understood
it as sensation, as wet, as the sky itself coming down gently to touch her, the waters above the firmament
that the caste had recited for twelve generations and never felt, and she was feeling them now, on her face,
in the ruined green light at the ends of the earth.

And there was green. That was the thing that broke something open in her that would never quite close. Green
everywhere, more green than the whole of the fields, green gone wild and vast and tangled up the slope and
down it and away to a distance she could not hold — trees, though she had only the word from the old songs,
huge and wet and living, and grass to her waist, and small bright things she would later learn were flowers,
and over all of it and through all of it a sound she had never heard: not the labouring hum, not the even
quiet of the sealed world, but *life* — wind in the wet green, water running somewhere, and, high and sudden
and piercing, a call, an animal call, a *bird*, another word from the songs, something alive and free and
loud in the roofless morning, singing for no reason, singing because it was alive, in a dead world that had
gone on living without anyone to keep it even.

Neva knelt in the wet grass in the rain under no roof at all and wept the way she had wept at the Lighting,
easily, wholly, the way you weep at a thing too big and too true to do anything else with — except that at
the Lighting she had wept for her small warm world proving faithful, and now she wept for how small it had
been, how warm and how small, how sealed and gold and loved and *small*, a lit room at the ends of an earth
so vast and so green and so brimming with careless life that she could not, kneeling in it, take in even the
single wet slope she could see, let alone the round of it, the whole round unroofed circling green of it, going
on and on past any wall, past any rim, past any ends at all.

*The circle of the earth,* she thought, or her father thought in her, or the pages against her heart said
themselves through her — *the circle of the earth* — and she did not yet know what it would mean, standing up,
walking out, watching the ground curve away and the horizon refuse to end; she only knelt in it and let the
first of it in, the cold and the size and the green and the rain and the bird, the whole indifferent living
roofless enormity that her caste had cut out of the book because a world this big will not fit inside a wall,
and had kept in the book, folded small under the salt, passed hand to hand at the cost of everything, because
a truth this big must not be lost even when it cannot be borne.

She had carried it out. She was here. The sentence was in the place it fit.

After a long time, in the rain, she stopped shaking. She got her feet under her. She stood — carefully, low,
one hand still near the ground, not yet trusting the up not to take her — and she lifted her wet face to the
roofless grey brimming sky, and she did not fall into it.

The ground held her. Whatever held the ground held her too.

She began, on legs that did not yet believe the world, to walk out into the round of it — to find out, now,
at last, in the place the words came true, what her whole dead line had been right about.
