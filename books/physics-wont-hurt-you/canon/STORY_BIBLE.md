# *PHYSICS WON'T HURT YOU (WELL, KIND OF)* — Story Bible

> **A standalone popular-science history.** An **original narrative synthesis** of documented
> history: physics from Galileo's tower to CERN's collider and past it, into the field's live
> open questions and speculative frontiers. Shares the house voice (plain, warm, unafraid of
> technical material handled simply) but its own register: funny, digressive, character-driven,
> in the tradition of Bryson's *A Short History of Nearly Everything* and Harari's *Sapiens*.
> Not part of any other shelf title — its own book — though it shares subject matter with, and
> should stay factually consistent with, this library's standalone Feynman biography
> (`richard-feynman`) and should cross-reference rather than duplicate that book's chapters on
> Los Alamos and QED.

> **Tone (binding, the author's charge):** *wonder first, jokes earned, humility at the edges.*
> The book should make a reader who has never taken a physics class feel smart, not stupid — every
> equation gets a plain-language handle before (or instead of) the symbols. When the book reaches
> Part Five's frontier material (interpretations of quantum mechanics, the simulation hypothesis,
> string/loop quantum gravity, consciousness-and-physics crossover), the register is **sympathetic
> exploration**: curious, open-minded, wonder-forward, giving speculative ideas real air time and
> real respect — while still, at every turn, telling the reader plainly whether a given idea is
> mainstream consensus, a live open scientific question, or a speculative/fringe idea taken
> seriously by *some* physicists and philosophers but not settled science. That labelling is never
> dropped, even in the most exploratory passages — sympathetic does not mean uncritical.

---

## 0. The shape of the book

**Part One — The Old Certainties.** Galileo, Newton, Faraday, Maxwell, thermodynamics/Boltzmann.
Classical physics as a triumph of "just go look" — and its own internal cracks (the ultraviolet
catastrophe, the aether) that Part Two will blow open.

**Part Two — Everything Falls Apart.** Planck, Einstein (1905 + general relativity), Rutherford/
Bohr's atom, Schrödinger, Heisenberg, the Bohr-Einstein debate, Dirac. The quantum and relativistic
revolutions, told through the specific, very human weirdness of the people who made them.

**Part Three — Splitting the Atom, Drawing the Universe.** The Manhattan Project (condensed —
defers to `richard-feynman` for the full Los Alamos/Arline story, cross-referenced rather than
retold at length), Feynman/Schwinger/Tomonaga's QED (condensed, same deferral), Gell-Mann and the
Standard Model, the four forces, the Higgs.

**Part Four — The Universe Gets Big.** Hubble, Lemaître and the Big Bang, Hawking and black holes,
dark matter and dark energy — cosmology as the zoom-out.

**Part Five — Where Physics Goes to Die.** The interpretations war (Copenhagen/Many-Worlds/Pilot
Wave), the simulation hypothesis, string theory/loop quantum gravity and the multiverse,
consciousness and panpsychism (Penrose-Hameroff, IIT, and honest scepticism about "quantum" being
bolted onto things it doesn't belong to), the ethics of physics (the bomb, AI, geoengineering), and
a closing epilogue that ties the title's joke to Feynman's "pleasure of finding things out" as the
book's real thesis.

## 1. What this book invents

Scene-setting, staged dialogue, and character colour, reconstructed from the documented record
(letters, biographies, the physicists' own popular writing, the historical consensus) — never
contradicting the documented frame, never borrowing a sentence from an existing book on the same
subject. Where the historical record disagrees on a specific quote or detail (e.g. exact wording
of "God does not play dice"), the book uses the most widely attested version and treats it as
received anecdote, not forensic transcript.

## 2. Register

One narrating voice throughout: warm, funny, digressive in the Bryson mode, willing to admit what
it doesn't fully understand, translating every hard idea into a concrete picture before trusting
the reader with the abstraction. Chapters are character-driven — each one enters through a person
and a scene, not a bare list of facts.

## 3. Guardrails

- No pseudoscience presented as settled fact. Part Five's sympathetic, wonder-forward tone is a
  register, not a licence — every speculative claim is labelled as speculative in the same breath
  it's explored.
- No flattening the ethical material (the bomb, surveillance/AI physics, geoengineering) into a
  simple morality tale; the book sits with the genuine difficulty.
- No duplicating `richard-feynman`'s Los Alamos/Arline chapters at length — condense and
  cross-reference instead.
- Historical figures are drawn as real, flawed people (Newton's feuds and alchemy, Boltzmann's
  suicide, Heisenberg's ambiguous wartime record, Schrödinger's unconventional personal life) with
  the same care and non-sensational honesty the Feynman biography uses.
