# Chapter 12 — The Uncertainty of Werner Heisenberg

A year before Schrödinger wrote his wave equation on that Alpine Christmas holiday, a twenty-three-
year-old German physicist named Werner Heisenberg, suffering badly from hay fever, retreated to the
treeless, pollen-free island of Helgoland in the North Sea to think, and came back with an entirely
different, considerably stranger route to what turned out to be the same underlying theory —
different enough, in fact, that it took physicists over a year to prove Heisenberg's and
Schrödinger's approaches were, despite looking almost nothing alike on the page, mathematically
equivalent descriptions of the same reality.

Where Schrödinger had built his physics around a wave spreading continuously through space — an
approach that felt, to most physicists trained in the classical tradition, comfortingly familiar,
even if what the wave actually represented remained genuinely strange — Heisenberg took the
opposite and, at the time, far more radical approach. He decided that physics should stop trying to
picture what an electron is "really" doing inside an atom at all, on the grounds that nobody can
ever actually observe an electron's continuous path or its precise orbit directly, and that a
genuinely rigorous science should restrict itself entirely to quantities that can, in principle, be
directly measured: the frequencies and intensities of the light an atom actually emits, and nothing
else. Working this way, using an unusual mathematical technique involving grids of numbers called
matrices — a branch of mathematics Heisenberg himself did not fully know when he started and had to
learn, essentially reinventing pieces of it independently before discovering, to his own surprise,
that mathematicians already had a well-developed toolkit for exactly what he needed — he built a
version of quantum mechanics that made no attempt whatsoever to picture what an electron looked like
mid-orbit, and simply calculated, correctly, what would show up on a spectrometer.

Max Born, the same physicist whose probability interpretation of Schrödinger's wave this book's
previous chapter credited, was in fact the one who first recognized what Heisenberg's strange grids
of numbers actually were, mathematically speaking — Heisenberg himself, by his own later admission,
had reinvented matrix multiplication from scratch without knowing the term for it, and it was Born,
recognizing the pattern from his own earlier mathematical training, who told his younger colleague
he had unknowingly rebuilt a well-established branch of mathematics and who then helped, together
with a third young physicist, Pascual Jordan, turn Heisenberg's rough sketch into the fully rigorous
theory that history remembers simply as Heisenberg's, a small further entry in this book's honest,
recurring tally of collaborative work quietly folded into a single more famous name.

It was a colleague of Heisenberg's, working through the philosophical implications of this
studiously picture-free approach a couple of years later, who helped Heisenberg arrive at the single
idea that would make his name a household word in a way "matrix mechanics" never quite managed to:
the uncertainty principle, published in 1927. Stated plainly, it says that there is a fundamental,
unavoidable limit to how precisely you can simultaneously know certain pairs of properties of a
particle — most famously, its position and its momentum (a measure combining its mass and velocity).
The more precisely you pin down where a particle is, the less precisely you can possibly know how
fast it's moving, and vice versa, and this is not — and this point genuinely cannot be overstated,
because it is the single most commonly misunderstood idea in all of popular physics writing — a
limitation of our measuring instruments, a problem that better engineering could someday fix. It is
a fact about the universe itself. A particle simply does not *possess* a perfectly definite position
and a perfectly definite momentum at the same time, in the same way that a note played on a piano
cannot be made arbitrarily brief in time and arbitrarily precise in pitch simultaneously — the
shorter and more percussive the note, the fuzzier and less precisely defined its exact musical pitch
becomes, and this isn't a flaw in anyone's piano or anyone's ear; it's a mathematical fact about
what a wave is. Heisenberg's insight was that matter, per de Broglie and Schrödinger, has this same
wave-like character, and inherits this same fundamental fuzziness, baked into the structure of
reality at the smallest scales, whether or not anyone is around with an instrument to measure
anything at all.

The uncertainty principle killed, permanently and without appeal, the old Newtonian dream — the
dream Einstein himself, as the next chapter will show, never fully gave up hoping to somehow
resurrect — of a universe that is, at bottom, a clockwork machine whose every future state could, in
principle, be calculated with perfect precision if only you knew the exact position and momentum of
every particle in it right now. Heisenberg's uncertainty said that "exact position and momentum,
both at once" is not merely difficult to know. It is not a thing that exists to be known, for
anyone, ever, about anything. This is one of the two or three deepest philosophical shocks in the
entire history of physics, on a par with Galileo dethroning the Earth from the center of the
universe or Darwin dethroning humanity from a separate creation, and this book will spend the whole
of Part Five wrestling with exactly how far that shock's implications actually extend.

Heisenberg's own biography carries a weight that his tidy, elegant physics gives almost no warning
of, and it is the most morally complicated single chapter in this entire book, more complicated even
than the Manhattan Project chapter still to come, precisely because the facts of it remain genuinely,
irreducibly contested among serious historians to this day, in a way the Manhattan Project's basic
facts are not. Heisenberg stayed in Germany throughout the Nazi era and became, by 1942, the
scientific head of the German nuclear weapons research program — the effort, parallel to and
racing against the Allied Manhattan Project this book will meet properly two chapters from now, to
determine whether Nazi Germany could build an atomic bomb before its enemies did.

Germany never built one. The reasons why remain the subject of serious, unresolved historical
argument, and the two competing explanations sit at almost opposite moral poles. One account, which
Heisenberg himself promoted for the rest of his life and which some of his defenders still maintain,
holds that Heisenberg deliberately slow-walked and sabotaged the German program from the inside, out
of moral conviction that Hitler must never be handed such a weapon, while carefully avoiding any
action so overt it would get him executed for treason — a story Heisenberg himself dramatized in a
famous, mysterious, and to this day disputed 1941 wartime meeting with his old friend and mentor
Niels Bohr in Nazi-occupied Copenhagen, a conversation both men later gave conflicting and only
partial accounts of, and that neither ever fully clarified before their deaths. The competing
account, supported by a good deal of documentary evidence including secretly recorded conversations
among captured German physicists at a British country house called Farm Hall in the months after
Germany's surrender, holds something considerably less flattering: that Heisenberg and his colleagues
genuinely tried to build a bomb, were not slowed down by any moral scruple, and simply made a real
scientific miscalculation — a specific, documented technical error in Heisenberg's own calculation
of the critical mass of uranium needed to sustain a chain reaction, an error that, if corrected,
would have shown the Germans they were closer to a workable weapon than they believed, rather than
that Heisenberg had cleverly steered the program away from one.

Historians who have spent entire careers on this question, sifting the Farm Hall transcripts and
Heisenberg's own postwar statements against each other, have not reached full consensus, and this
book will not pretend to resolve a dispute better and more thoroughly-informed scholars have not
resolved. What can be said plainly is this: Heisenberg did not defect, did not refuse to work for the
Nazi state's nuclear program the way some of his own former students and colleagues — including
several who happened to be Jewish and had no choice at all in the matter, forced out of German
universities and, in several tragic cases, into exile or worse — were compelled by circumstance to
do. He remained a prominent, publicly visible figure within Nazi Germany's scientific establishment
throughout the war, traveled to occupied countries on cultural-diplomacy missions the regime used
for propaganda purposes, and never, at any point during the war itself, took any action that
unambiguously and verifiably sabotaged the program from within, whatever his own private
calculations and private conscience may genuinely have been doing behind the scenes. The most honest
statement available, a full three-quarters of a century on, is that the moral truth of Werner
Heisenberg's war is simply not fully knowable, and that this uncertainty — a man's actual intentions,
existing in some genuine sense as an unresolved superposition of hero and collaborator until history
opens a box that may never fully open — is, in its own dark and entirely unintended way, a rather
apt final footnote for the physicist who told the rest of us that some things, at the deepest level
reality permits, simply cannot be pinned down with perfect precision at all.
