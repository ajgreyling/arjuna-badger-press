# Chapter 21 — The 95% We Can't See

Here is a fact about the universe that would have struck every physicist in this book, from Galileo
through Hawking, as almost too strange to be taken seriously, and that is nonetheless the settled,
repeatedly confirmed consensus of modern cosmology: everything this book has discussed so far — every
star, every planet, every atom in your own body, every particle the Standard Model of Chapter 17 so
carefully catalogued — makes up, by careful measurement, only about five percent of the total mass
and energy content of the universe. The remaining ninety-five percent is something else entirely,
something physics can measure the gravitational and cosmological effects of with real precision, and
that nobody has yet directly detected or fully explained.

The first serious hint that something invisible was out there came, like several of this book's
biggest discoveries, from a scientist whose full due took decades to arrive. Fritz Zwicky, an
eccentric, famously blunt, and by most contemporary accounts rather difficult Swiss-American
astronomer working at Caltech in the 1930s, studied the motion of galaxies within a large cluster
called the Coma Cluster, and found that the individual galaxies were moving far too fast, whipping
around the cluster's center at speeds that should, by Newton's own well-established gravity, have
long since flung them apart entirely — unless the cluster contained vastly more mass than could be
accounted for by the visible light of all its galaxies combined. Zwicky, in 1933, proposed that some
additional, invisible form of matter — he called it, in German, *dunkle Materie*, dark matter — must
be present, providing the extra gravitational glue holding the fast-moving cluster together. Zwicky's
own prickly, combative reputation among his colleagues (he referred to scientists he disagreed with,
in print, as "spherical bastards" — bastards, he explained, however you looked at them) meant his
proposal was, for decades, treated as an interesting curiosity from a difficult man rather than a
serious cosmological puzzle demanding urgent attention.

It took until the 1970s, and the painstaking observational work of the American astronomer Vera
Rubin, to make the case impossible to keep ignoring. Rubin, who had faced her own long, documented
struggle simply to be taken seriously as a woman in a field then even more male-dominated than most
of physics — turned away from Princeton's graduate astronomy program in the 1940s for the explicit,
stated reason that the university did not admit women to that department at all — spent years
carefully measuring how fast stars orbit at different distances from the centers of spiral galaxies,
including our neighbor Andromeda, the very galaxy Hubble had used to discover the wider universe
half a century earlier. By ordinary Newtonian expectation, stars far out at a galaxy's edge, orbiting
a smaller share of the galaxy's visible mass, should circle noticeably slower than stars closer to
the dense, bright center — the same way Pluto orbits the Sun far more slowly than Mercury does.
Rubin found, again and again, across dozens of galaxies, that this simply wasn't happening: stars at
a galaxy's outer edge were orbiting almost as fast as stars much closer in, a "flat" rotation curve
that made sense only if each galaxy were embedded in a far larger, invisible halo of unseen mass,
extending well beyond the visible disk of stars, providing exactly the additional gravitational pull
needed to keep those outer stars from flying off into intergalactic space. Rubin's meticulous,
repeated, hard-to-dispute measurements, combined by the 1980s with several independent lines of
supporting evidence, finally moved dark matter from Zwicky's disreputable fringe proposal into
mainstream cosmological consensus — though Rubin herself, despite work now recognized as among the
most consequential in twentieth-century astronomy, was never awarded a Nobel Prize before her death
in 2016, one more name to add to this book's uncomfortably long list of women whose foundational
contributions the prize committees of their era simply failed to recognize in time.

The leading candidate particle physicists spent the most effort chasing for decades goes by the
acronym WIMP, for Weakly Interacting Massive Particle — a hypothetical particle considerably heavier
than a proton but interacting with ordinary matter only through gravity and the weak nuclear force
this book's Chapter 17 introduced, which would explain both dark matter's gravitational effects and
its near-total invisibility to every other kind of detector. Enormous, exquisitely sensitive
underground experiments — tanks of liquid xenon shielded by kilometers of rock from the ordinary
cosmic-ray noise that would otherwise swamp any real signal, in facilities with names like
XENONnT and LUX-ZEPLIN — have spent over two decades hunting for the extremely rare, extremely faint
flash of light a WIMP should produce on the rare occasion it collides with an ordinary xenon nucleus.
None has yet found one, and the steadily narrowing range of WIMP properties still consistent with
that persistent silence has led a meaningful fraction of the field to widen its search toward other
candidates: axions, a much lighter hypothetical particle originally proposed for an unrelated reason
in particle physics; primordial black holes, tiny black holes formed in the earliest fractions of a
second after the Big Bang rather than from a collapsed star; and a small number of more exotic
proposals that would require modifying gravity itself at very large scales rather than adding any
new particle at all, a minority position within cosmology that most, though not all, of the field
considers less well supported by the overall body of evidence than genuine dark matter. What dark
matter actually *is*, at the level of fundamental particles, remains one of the genuinely
open questions this book will carry into Part Five. It is not, physicists are now quite confident,
simply ordinary matter that happens to be dark — not black holes, not dim stars, not vast unlit
clouds of ordinary gas; a combination of gravitational lensing studies (the bending of distant light
around galaxy clusters, a direct application of the same general-relativistic light-bending this
book's Einstein chapter described) and the fine details of the cosmic microwave background's tiny
temperature variations have together ruled out ordinary matter as the answer with real confidence.
Whatever dark matter is made of, it appears to interact with the rest of the universe almost
exclusively through gravity, passing through ordinary matter, including the entire planet Earth, in
staggering quantities every single second, without leaving so much as a trace in any detector built
so far, despite decades of increasingly sensitive, increasingly expensive searches conducted deep
underground in old mines and tunnels around the world specifically to shield the detectors from
every other kind of interference. It remains, in the plainest and most honest sense this book can
offer, an open mystery: physicists know, with real confidence, that dark matter is there, and know a
great deal about how much of it there is and how it's distributed. They do not yet know what it is.

Dark energy, discovered later and turning out to be even stranger and even larger a piece of the
cosmic pie, emerged from a discovery that stunned the two independent research teams who made it in
1998, both of whom had actually set out expecting to confirm the opposite result. Both teams,
studying a particular class of exploding stars called Type Ia supernovae — objects reliable and
bright enough to serve as, in the astronomers' own term, "standard candles" for measuring vast
cosmic distances with real precision, extending Hubble's original Cepheid-based method far further
out into the universe — were trying to measure exactly how much the universe's expansion, established
back in Chapter 18, was gradually slowing down over cosmic time, since gravity, pulling all that
matter and dark matter together, should logically be acting as a brake on the expansion Hubble had
discovered. Instead, both teams found, independently and to their own considerable initial disbelief,
that the expansion is not slowing down at all. It is speeding up. The universe's expansion is
accelerating, and has been for roughly the last five billion years, meaning some kind of repulsive
effect, working against gravity's pull, is actively pushing space apart, faster and faster, on the
very largest scales.

Physicists gave this repulsive effect a name that is, on reflection, at least as much an honest
admission of ignorance as it is an actual explanation: dark energy. It behaves, in the equations that
successfully describe it, remarkably like the cosmological constant Einstein had once inserted into
his own general relativity equations purely to force a static universe, and then tore back out,
calling it his biggest blunder, once Hubble showed the universe was expanding rather than static. It
may be, physicists increasingly suspect, that Einstein's supposed blunder was not a mistake at all,
merely applied for the wrong reason — a genuine property of empty space itself, a kind of intrinsic
energy that space carries even when totally devoid of any matter, that only became the dominant force
shaping the universe's overall behavior once matter had thinned out enough, after billions of years
of expansion, for its own gravitational pull to weaken and let this steady, ever-present repulsion
finally win out. The team leaders behind this discovery, Saul Perlmutter, Brian Schmidt, and Adam
Riess, shared the 2011 Nobel Prize for a finding that, more than the discovery of the Higgs boson,
more than the confirmation of gravitational waves, arguably represents the single most genuinely
disorienting result in observational cosmology since Hubble first showed the universe was expanding
at all.

Put the two mysteries together, and the honest inventory of the entire universe's contents, as best
modern cosmology can currently measure it, comes out to roughly this: about five percent ordinary
matter — stars, planets, gas, every atom in this book and its reader alike — around twenty-seven
percent dark matter, and a full sixty-eight percent dark energy, the strange, accelerating,
space-stretching majority stakeholder in the cosmos that nobody, a century after Einstein first
inserted and then regretted its mathematical ancestor, has yet managed to explain. Physics spent four
centuries, from Galileo's ramp to the Higgs boson's discovery at CERN, building an increasingly
precise, increasingly successful account of the small five percent slice of the universe that happens
to be made of the kind of matter humans are built from and can directly detect. It now knows, with
real confidence and no comfortable way to explain it away, that this entire magnificent, exhaustively
tested body of work — everything from Newton's apple to the Standard Model's carefully completed
particle zoo — is a detailed account of a rounding error. What the other ninety-five percent
actually is remains, this book must say plainly and without false comfort, one of the largest open
questions in the whole of science, and it is exactly this kind of honest, unresolved not-knowing —
vast, humbling, and, this book will argue in its final part, genuinely exciting rather than merely
embarrassing — that marks the real, current edge of what physics can presently tell you for certain.
