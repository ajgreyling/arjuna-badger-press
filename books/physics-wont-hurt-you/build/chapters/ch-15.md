# Chapter 15 — The Bomb

Every idea in the previous eight chapters — Planck's reluctant quantum, Einstein's mass-energy
equivalence, Rutherford's dense little nucleus sitting mostly empty space, Bohr's institute in
Copenhagen training a generation of the century's best physicists — had, by the late 1930s, been
sitting quietly in physics journals, argued over at conferences, treated by nearly everyone involved
as a magnificent intellectual adventure with no obvious practical application beyond, at most, a
better understanding of starlight. Then, in December 1938, two German chemists working in Berlin,
Otto Hahn and Fritz Strassmann, bombarded uranium with neutrons and found, to their own considerable
confusion, that the uranium appeared to have split into much lighter elements — a result that made
no sense under the physics anyone understood at the time, and that Hahn, in some distress, wrote to
his former colleague and close friend Lise Meitner to ask for help interpreting.

Meitner is one of the great, quietly enraging injustices of twentieth-century physics, and this
book will not pass over her the way the 1944 Nobel committee did. She was an Austrian-born Jewish
physicist who had worked alongside Hahn in Berlin for three decades, co-discovering with him the
element protactinium and doing much of the theoretical groundwork behind the very uranium
experiments Hahn was now confused by — and who had been forced to flee Nazi Germany in the summer of
1938, mere months before Hahn's discovery, smuggled across the Dutch border with ten marks in her
pocket and her own scientific career effectively uprooted overnight for the sole crime of being
Jewish. Working in exile in Sweden, corresponding with Hahn by letter and meeting her physicist
nephew Otto Frisch over a Christmas walk in the snow, Meitner worked out the correct physical
explanation for what Hahn had observed: the uranium nucleus, struck by a neutron, was not merely
losing a small fragment the way earlier, better-understood radioactive processes did. It was
splitting apart entirely, into two roughly equal, lighter nuclei, releasing an amount of energy that
Meitner, applying Einstein's E=mc² to the small but measurable difference in mass between the
uranium and its fragments, calculated with startling precision. She and Frisch named the process
fission, borrowing the term from biology's word for how a living cell splits in two. Hahn published
the experimental discovery under his own name alone, as was then common practice for the (male)
laboratory chief; Meitner's essential theoretical explanation appeared soon after in a joint paper
with Frisch. In 1944, the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for the discovery of nuclear fission went to Otto
Hahn alone. Meitner, whose physics had made sense of what Hahn's chemistry had merely observed, and
whose exile from her own country had been necessitated by exactly the ideology the resulting weapon
would eventually help defeat, received nothing.

The physicists who understood immediately what fission implied were not primarily thinking about
weapons at first; they were thinking about chain reactions, and specifically about whether a
splitting uranium nucleus released enough spare neutrons to trigger further splittings in
neighboring nuclei, in a self-sustaining cascade that could, in principle, release an almost
unimaginable amount of energy very quickly. It did. By 1939, with the world sliding into what would
become the Second World War, a small group of physicists — several of them Jewish refugees who had
personally fled the same Nazi regime Meitner had, and who understood, with a clarity that comfortable
American-born colleagues did not always share, exactly what it would mean if Germany's own excellent
physicists (among them Werner Heisenberg, whose own contested wartime record this book examined a
few chapters ago) got there first — persuaded Albert Einstein, whose name alone carried the weight to get
a letter read at the highest level of government, to sign a warning to President Franklin Roosevelt.
The letter, largely drafted by the Hungarian physicist Leo Szilard, urged the United States to begin
its own research into the possibility of an atomic weapon before Germany could build one first.

Leo Szilard himself, the physicist most responsible for drafting the letter and for badgering
Einstein into signing it, deserves a fuller word than this condensed chapter can otherwise spare
him, because his own restless, unconventional career is one of this book's more vivid illustrations
of a mind that simply could not stop seeing consequences several moves ahead of everyone else in the
room. Szilard had conceived of the basic idea of a nuclear chain reaction as early as 1933, crossing
a London street, years before fission itself was even discovered, and had quietly patented the
concept, assigning the patent to the British Admiralty specifically to keep it classified and out of
open scientific literature where a hostile government might read it — an act of pure, prescient
caution taken half a decade before there was any concrete fission discovery to be cautious about.
He would go on, after the war, to abandon physics for molecular biology almost entirely, driven by
the same restless conviction that biology was where the next urgent, world-altering questions were
waiting, a late-career pivot few physicists of his stature have ever attempted with anything close to
his success.

What followed was the Manhattan Project — the largest, most secretive, and most expensive scientific
undertaking in human history to that point, eventually employing well over a hundred thousand people
across dozens of secret sites, its central theoretical work concentrated at a hastily built
laboratory on a remote New Mexico mesa called Los Alamos, under the scientific direction of J.
Robert Oppenheimer. This book has already told a considerable part of that story in detail — the
youngest group leader in the Theoretical Division, cracking safes and drumming through the nights
while his young wife lay dying in a sanatorium a hundred miles down the mountain; the calculations
run and rerun by a small army of physicists and their human "computers"; the test in the New Mexico
desert on the morning of 16 July 1945 that split the light of dawn a second time before dawn itself
had properly arrived — and rather than repeat that story here, this chapter points the reader
directly toward it: the full account, the full cast, the full weight of what it cost the people who
built it, lives in this library's standalone biography of Richard Feynman, *The Man Who Drew the
Universe*, and deserves to be read there in full rather than compressed a second time into a
paragraph.

What belongs here instead is the wider reckoning — the one that extended beyond any single
physicist's own conscience, into the whole discipline's understanding of itself. Fewer than four
weeks after the Trinity test lit up the New Mexico desert, atomic bombs were dropped on the
Japanese cities of Hiroshima and Nagasaki, on 6 and 9 August 1945, killing an estimated 110,000 to
210,000 people, the overwhelming majority of them civilians, most within the first day and the rest
over the following months and years from burns, radiation sickness, and related illness — figures
that remain, to this day, disputed within a wide range by different historical methodologies, and
that no single number can make less staggering. Japan surrendered days later, ending the Second
World War, and the physicists who had built the weapon — brilliant, in many cases genuinely gentle,
funny, morally serious people, the same men and women populating every earlier chapter of this
book — were left, individually and as a profession, to work out what they had actually done.

Some, like Szilard, had tried before the weapon was even finished to redirect its use — circulating a
petition among Manhattan Project scientists in the summer of 1945 urging President Truman to
demonstrate the bomb's power to Japan in some uninhabited location first, rather than use it directly
on a populated city, a petition that never reached Truman's desk before the decision to bomb
Hiroshima was already made. Oppenheimer himself, in a now-famous account, said that watching the
Trinity test brought to his mind a line from the Bhagavad Gita: "Now I am become Death, the destroyer
of worlds" — a quotation whose exact circumstances and sincerity historians still debate, but whose
essential truth about Oppenheimer's own subsequent torment is not seriously disputed; he spent the
rest of his career advocating, at real professional and personal cost that would eventually cost him
his own security clearance during the McCarthy era, for international control of nuclear weapons and
against the far more destructive hydrogen bomb that followed. Einstein, whose own 1905 equation had
made the weapon's energy release calculable and whose signature on the 1939 letter had helped start
the whole project, though he himself never worked on it directly and was in fact denied the security
clearance to do so, called signing that letter the one great mistake of his life, and spent his
remaining years a prominent voice for nuclear disarmament and world government as the only sane
long-term answer to a weapon physics could no longer un-invent.

The bomb did not end physics's relationship with weapons of mass destruction; if anything it began a
new, more dangerous chapter, as the fission bomb was followed within a decade by the vastly more
powerful hydrogen bomb, built on nuclear *fusion* rather than fission, and as the Soviet Union,
having obtained its own bomb by 1949 partly through a small number of Manhattan Project scientists
who had spied for Moscow out of ideological conviction, kicked off a decades-long arms race that, at
its Cold War peak, left the two superpowers holding enough combined warheads to end organized human
civilization several times over, a capacity that, this book must note honestly, has never actually
gone away and that this book's final chapters, in Part Five, will return to directly, in the
company of a physicist who spent the last years of his own life trying to make one more
institution — this time NASA, not the Pentagon — tell an uncomfortable truth as plainly as the
Manhattan Project physicists, in their different ways, eventually tried to.

What the bomb settled, permanently and without any further argument needed, was the question this
whole book opened with in a cathedral in Pisa: whether the plain, checkable, patient method Galileo
had insisted on — go and look, go and measure, trust the ramp over the received wisdom — actually,
truly worked on the deepest structure of reality. It did. It worked well enough to end a war in
days and well enough, in the decades since, to hold the entire species in a standoff neither side has
yet found a way fully to stand down from. Physics, this chapter's plain and uncomfortable lesson,
was never a morally neutral hobby practiced safely behind university walls. From the summer of 1945
onward, it has been one of the most consequential moral forces in human history, for exactly the same
reason it is one of the most beautiful: it is astonishingly, terrifyingly good at being right.
