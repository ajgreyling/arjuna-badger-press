# Chapter 2 — The Man Who Broke the Universe Open

Isaac Newton was born on Christmas Day, 1642, by the calendar England was still using at the time,
premature and small enough that nobody expected him to survive the week — his own mother said
later he could have fit inside a quart pot. His father, a prosperous but illiterate farmer, had
died three months earlier. His mother remarried when he was three, to a man who did not want a
stepson underfoot, and left the boy to be raised by his grandmother while she moved a few miles
away to her new husband's house, close enough to visit and far enough to feel, to a small child,
like abandonment. Newton kept a list, as a teenager, of sins he felt he needed to confess to God,
written in a private shorthand he thought no one could read; among them, discovered centuries later
by scholars who did learn to read the shorthand, was "threatening my father and mother Smith to
burne them and the house over them." He was talking about wanting to burn down the house of the
stepfather and the mother who had left him there. This is not, it should be said clearly, a
detail included for cheap psychoanalysis. It is included because the single most important fact
about Isaac Newton, more important even than the laws that carry his name, is that he was
constitutionally, permanently, and quite obviously wounded, and that almost everything strange and
great and unpleasant about him afterward makes considerably more sense once you know it.

He was, by every account of his own schoolmasters, an unremarkable student until, in his mid-teens,
something clicked, and he went from the bottom of his class to the top with a speed that alarmed
people. He entered Cambridge in 1661 as what was called a subsizar — a poor student who paid his
way partly by acting as a servant to wealthier students, cleaning their rooms and running their
errands, a humiliation he never once mentioned in later life and clearly never forgot. And then, in
1665, the plague closed Cambridge, sent the students home, and handed Isaac Newton — twenty-two
years old, alone at his mother's farm in Lincolnshire with nothing to do and no one checking his
work — eighteen uninterrupted months that he would later describe, without noticeable modesty, as
the most productive of his entire life. Historians of science, only slightly less immodestly, have
taken to calling it his *annus mirabilis*, his year of wonders, though it was closer to eighteen
months than one.

In that stretch, alone, with no supervisor and no peer to argue with, Newton worked out the basic
mathematics of what we now call calculus — the tool needed to describe anything that changes
continuously, which turns out to be almost everything in the physical world, from a falling apple
to the orbit of a planet to the flow of heat through a bar of iron. He worked out, using a prism he
bought at a country fair, that white light is not pure and simple but is actually made of all the
colors of the rainbow mixed together, overturning two thousand years of assumption about the
nature of light itself. And he began the work — not finished, not even close to finished, for
another twenty years — of figuring out why the Moon does not fall into the Earth the way an apple
falls into the ground, when by every intuition available in 1666 it plainly ought to.

The apple story is, unlike the tower story about Galileo, probably substantially true, or at least
close to true — it comes from multiple independent accounts, including one written down by a
contemporary who heard it directly from Newton himself in old age, describing a specific apple tree
at his mother's farm that visitors can, remarkably, still go and see a descendant of today. But the
popular version of the story — apple falls, Newton shouts "gravity!", laws of motion instantly
complete — badly misrepresents what actually happened, which is both less cinematic and far more
impressive. What the apple gave Newton was not the answer. It was the question, correctly framed
for what may be the first time in human history: if the force that pulls an apple to the ground is
the same force, at root, as the force that holds the Moon in its orbit around the Earth, then that
one force ought to obey one mathematical law, everywhere, without exception — on the ground, in the
sky, on the surface of a distant planet nobody had ever visited. It took Newton the better part of
twenty more years, working mostly alone, obsessively, often not eating or sleeping for days at a
stretch when a problem had its hooks in him, to actually prove it.

He was, by the testimony of nearly everyone who had to deal with him, a genuinely difficult person
— not in Galileo's witty, cutting way, but in a colder, more paranoid, more permanently aggrieved
register. He accused Robert Hooke, a brilliant and equally difficult rival, of stealing ideas about
gravity and optics that Newton considered his own, and pursued the feud for decades with a
vindictiveness that outlasted Hooke's own life: when Newton became president of the Royal Society
after Hooke's death, the only known portrait of Hooke disappeared from the Society's collection
during Newton's tenure, and no one has ever found it, and it is difficult, given everything else we
know about the man, not to suspect he had something to do with it. He fought an even longer and
uglier battle with the German mathematician Gottfried Leibniz over who had really invented
calculus first — both of them had, independently, at almost exactly the same time, which is itself
one of the recurring small miracles of the history of science, an idea whose time had simply come
— and Newton, using his position as president of the Royal Society, secretly wrote an official
"impartial" report exonerating himself and condemning Leibniz, and had it published anonymously
under the Society's authority as though it were the judgment of a neutral panel. It was not
discovered for over a century that Newton had written the whole thing himself.

And then there is the alchemy, which the polished modern image of Newton — wig, apple, three tidy
laws of motion — tends to leave out entirely, because it is deeply inconvenient to a story about
the birth of rational science. Newton spent, by some estimates, more of his working life on alchemy
and on biblical chronology and prophecy than he ever spent on physics. He filled a million words of
private manuscript trying to work out the exact date of the biblical apocalypse (he settled,
eventually, on no earlier than 2060), trying to transmute base metals into gold, trying to decode
hidden numerological messages he believed were embedded in the architecture of the biblical Temple
of Solomon. He kept almost none of this secret work published in his lifetime, aware — correctly —
that it would have scandalized the very scientific establishment he dominated, since alchemy had
already begun to be seen, even in his own era, as closer to superstition than to the emerging
mathematical physics he himself was inventing. It is one of the odder facts in the whole history of
science that the man who did more than any other single human being to establish that the universe
runs on precise, checkable, mathematical law spent the majority of his working hours hunting for
hidden messages from God in the dimensions of an ancient temple. He did not, as far as anyone has
ever established, see any contradiction in this. The universe, to Newton, was a puzzle God had
built and half-hidden the key to, and gravity and alchemy and Scripture were simply three different
rooms in the same locked house.

The actual physics, when it finally arrived in 1687 in a book called *Philosophiæ Naturalis
Principia Mathematica* — usually just called the *Principia*, written in Latin, in a mathematical
style so dense that even most of the small number of people qualified to read it at all found it
close to impenetrable — is almost absurdly simple to state and was almost unimaginably difficult to
arrive at. Newton proposed three laws of motion: an object in motion stays in motion, and an object
at rest stays at rest, unless a force acts on it; the acceleration of an object is proportional to
the force applied to it and inversely proportional to its mass; and for every action there is an
equal and opposite reaction. And he proposed one law of gravitation: every object in the universe
attracts every other object with a force proportional to the product of their masses and inversely
proportional to the square of the distance between them. Four sentences. Four sentences that,
worked through with the calculus Newton himself had invented to work them through, correctly
predicted the orbit of every planet then known, the timing of the tides, the path of comets, the
slight flattening of the Earth at its poles — a bulge nobody had measured yet, that Newton's math
predicted before anyone went and checked, and that turned out, when they finally did check nearly a
century later by sending expeditions to Lapland and Peru to measure the actual shape of the Earth,
to be exactly right.

This is the single most important thing this book will ever say about what physics actually is, and
it is worth saying plainly, right here, at the hinge between Galileo's ramp and everything that
follows: a law of physics is not a description of what has already been observed. It is a machine
for predicting what has not yet been observed, built from what has, and it is only real physics —
as opposed to a good story that happens to fit the facts so far — if it makes a prediction daring
enough that it could, in principle, be proven wrong. Newton's gravity said the Earth bulges at the
equator by a specific, calculable amount, before anyone had measured it, and it was right. That is
the whole difference between physics and everything that came before it, and Newton, whatever his
personal failings, is the man who proved beyond any argument that the universe would answer that
kind of question if you asked it precisely enough.

He died in 1727, wealthy, knighted, revered, and — this detail is almost too on the nose to include,
except that it is entirely true and entirely characteristic — apparently still a virgin, having by
every surviving account never formed a romantic or sexual attachment to another human being in his
eighty-four years, pouring everything the rest of us spread across a whole life into mathematics,
grudges, and the hidden architecture of Solomon's Temple. He is buried in Westminster Abbey under a
monument that took years to design and that shows him reclining on a pile of his own books,
attended by cherubs holding a prism, a telescope, and the mathematical instruments of the calculus
he invented alone, in eighteen months, in a farmhouse, hiding from the plague, grieving a childhood
he never once, in eighty-four years, wrote about with anything other than that one small, damning,
private confession of wanting to burn the house down.
