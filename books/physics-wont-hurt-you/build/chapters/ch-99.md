# What's Real, What's Open, and What's Frankly Speculative

*A note from the author, and an invitation.*

Every physicist, date, experiment, and discovery named in this book is real and drawn from the
documented historical and scientific record. Every equation and every physical mechanism described is
this book's own plain-language account of settled or actively-researched physics, not a reproduction
of any other book's explanation of it. Scene-setting, staged dialogue, and character colour are
reconstructed where the record gives the fact but not the exact words — kept true to the documented
frame throughout, and clearly flagged in the text itself wherever a famous quote (Galileo's "*Eppur si
muove*," Bohr's "stop telling God what to do") is probably or certainly apocryphal rather than
verified. This is an **original narrative synthesis**, not a derivative retelling of Bryson,
Rovelli, Greene, Carroll, Gleick, Hawking's own popular writing, or any other existing book on this
subject.

## The honest map: consensus, open question, speculative

Because this book's whole final Part asks the reader to sit with real uncertainty, here is a plain,
one-place summary of where each major territory it covered actually stands, for a reader who wants
the map without re-reading the full chapters.

**Settled scientific consensus**, confirmed by repeated, independent experimental or observational
evidence: classical mechanics and gravitation (Chapters 1–2); electromagnetism (Chapters 3–4);
thermodynamics and statistical mechanics (Chapter 5); the discovery of radioactivity (Chapter 6); the
existence of quanta, the photoelectric effect, special and general relativity (Chapters 7–9); the
nuclear atom and the basic framework of quantum mechanics, including the reality of Heisenberg
uncertainty and quantum entanglement as confirmed by Bell-inequality experiments (Chapters 10–14);
nuclear fission and the physical mechanism of the atomic bomb (Chapter 15); quantum electrodynamics as
the most precisely verified theory in the history of science (Chapter 16); the Standard Model of
particle physics and the Higgs mechanism (Chapter 17); the expansion of the universe, the Big Bang,
and the cosmic microwave background (Chapters 18–19); the existence and basic behaviour of black
holes and Hawking radiation as a theoretical (not yet directly observed) consequence of
well-established physics (Chapter 20); the existence of dark matter and dark energy as measured
gravitational and cosmological effects, though not their underlying composition (Chapter 21); the
existence of confirmed exoplanets by the thousand (Chapter 22); the basic physics and real,
demonstrated feasibility of quantum computing (Chapter 25).

**Live, genuinely open scientific questions**, actively studied by the mainstream field with no
settled answer yet: which interpretation of quantum measurement is correct, if any single one is
(Chapter 23); the composition of dark matter and the mechanism of dark energy (Chapter 21); the exact
process by which information escapes an evaporating black hole (Chapter 20); whether time is
fundamental or emergent, and the block-universe/presentism debate (Chapter 24); whether any other
technological civilization exists, i.e. the Fermi paradox (Chapter 22); the biological and
philosophical basis of consciousness (Chapter 28, "hard problem" framing).

**Serious minority positions and speculative frontiers**, taken seriously by real, credentialed
researchers but not accepted as mainstream consensus and not yet supported by confirming experimental
evidence: string theory and loop quantum gravity as candidate theories of quantum gravity (Chapter
27); the simulation hypothesis and the holographic principle's cosmological extensions (Chapter 26);
Orchestrated Objective Reduction and panpsychism as accounts of consciousness (Chapter 28); Gödel's
closed-timelike-curve solutions to general relativity as a mathematical (not physically demonstrated)
possibility (Chapter 24). The 420 Code (Chapter 31) sits further out: it is a substantial independent,
published and falsifiable speculative framework, not a mainstream or peer-confirmed physical theory.
Its public formulae are computationally reproducible; that is not the same as showing that its axioms
uniquely force those formulae or that new experiments confirm the framework. Readers are encouraged
to treat any popular claim that confidently resolves any of these as settled fact — in either
direction — with real skepticism.

AJ Greyling's independent verifier reimplements the published formulae and matches G's own script to

The full-SPARC diagnostic and the Lucid/CongoSky reader, knowledge graph and Coherence Ledger

physical derivations.

## The people (real)

Every physicist named across this book's thirty-one chapters is a real, documented figure,
and this book has tried, throughout, to give particular and deliberate attention to contributions
history under-credited at the time — **Marie Curie**'s original Nobel nomination, which initially
omitted her from the citation her own husband Pierre insisted the committee correct; **Lise
Meitner**'s theoretical explanation of nuclear fission,
excluded from the 1944 Nobel Prize awarded to Otto Hahn alone; **Henrietta Leavitt**'s Cepheid
period-luminosity relation, without which Hubble's distance measurements would have been impossible;
**Vera Rubin**'s galaxy rotation-curve measurements establishing dark matter, never recognized with a
Nobel Prize before her death in 2016. Their fuller stories deserve books of their own; this one has
tried, at minimum, not to repeat the omission.

## A note on apocryphal quotes

Several famous quotations attributed to this book's subjects are widely repeated without solid
primary-source verification, and this book has tried to flag each one honestly at the point it
appears rather than silently borrowing the improved, more quotable version: Galileo's "*Eppur si
muove*" (Chapter 1), Bohr's "stop telling God what to do" (Chapter 13), and the phrase "shut up and
calculate," popularly but almost certainly wrongly attributed directly to Feynman (Chapter 23), all
fall into this category. Where a quote is solidly documented — Einstein's letter on the "stubbornly
persistent illusion" of time (Chapter 24), Oppenheimer's Bhagavad Gita recollection (Chapter 15,
itself contested in its precise circumstances though not in its substance) — this book has tried to
say so with equal clarity.
