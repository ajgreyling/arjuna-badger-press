# Chapter 27 — Strings, Loops, and the Multiverse

Physics, by the end of Part Three of this book, had two extraordinarily successful theories that
refuse, so far, to speak to each other. General relativity describes gravity beautifully at the
scale of planets, stars, and galaxies, treating space and time as a smooth, continuous, curving
fabric. Quantum mechanics describes everything else — electromagnetism, the strong force, the weak
force, the entire Standard Model of Chapter 17 — beautifully at the scale of particles, treating
energy and matter as fundamentally discrete, probabilistic, and quantized. Both theories are, within
their own domains, verified to extraordinary precision. And when physicists try to apply both of
them at once, to a situation extreme enough to genuinely need them both together — the center of a
black hole, or the very first instant of the Big Bang, where an enormous mass or an enormous amount
of energy is compressed into an almost impossibly small space — the two theories do not merely
disagree. The mathematics breaks down entirely, spitting out meaningless infinities, refusing to give
any answer at all. Finding a single, unified theory of "quantum gravity" that can handle both regimes
smoothly, without contradiction, is widely considered the single deepest unsolved problem in all of
fundamental physics, and this chapter introduces the two most seriously developed, most
scientifically ambitious attempts anyone has yet made to solve it — attempts this book will treat
with the same open, curious respect it gave the simulation hypothesis, while being equally honest
about how far each one currently falls short of being actually confirmed.

String theory's core proposal is disarmingly simple to state, however forbiddingly complex the
mathematics needed to work out its consequences: perhaps the point-like particles that the Standard
Model treats as truly, indivisibly fundamental — electrons, quarks, photons — are not points at all.
Perhaps each one is, at a scale far too small for any conceivable experiment to directly probe, an
extremely tiny, vibrating loop or strand of energy, a "string," and the different particles that the
Standard Model catalogs as separate, distinct entities are actually the very same kind of underlying
string, simply vibrating in different specific patterns, the way a single guitar string can produce
many different musical notes depending on how it vibrates. It is, in its founding ambition, a
genuinely beautiful idea, promising the kind of deep unification this book has celebrated again and
again, from Maxwell folding electricity and magnetism into one phenomenon to the electroweak theory
folding electromagnetism and the weak force into one — string theory dares to ask whether literally
every particle and every force in nature, gravity fully included, might be different notes played on
one single, common, more fundamental kind of string.

String theory's actual origin, it is worth noting, had nothing at all to do with gravity: the earliest
version, developed in the late 1960s, was a promising but ultimately unsuccessful attempt to describe
the strong nuclear force this book's Chapter 17 covered, before the discovery of quarks and quantum
chromodynamics rendered that original application obsolete. It was only in 1974, when the physicists
John Schwarz and Joël Scherk noticed that one of the theory's supposedly unwanted particles — a
massless, spin-two particle the strong-force version of the theory had no obvious use for — had
exactly the properties physicists would expect of a graviton, the hypothetical particle that would
carry gravity itself in a fully quantized theory, that the whole project was reborn with a
considerably grander ambition: not a theory of one force among several, but potentially a theory of
gravity itself, folded in alongside the other three. That accidental redirection, from a failed
theory of the strong force to a candidate theory of everything, is itself a small, instructive
lesson in how theoretical physics sometimes advances — not always by successfully answering the
question originally asked, but by noticing, honestly, that the mathematics has quietly been trying
to answer a different and considerably larger question all along.

The theory's mathematics, developed with real seriousness since the 1970s and 80s by an enormous
international community of extremely capable physicists, turns out to require something that sounds,
to anyone encountering it for the first time, more like abstract fiction than physics: the equations
only work out consistently if the universe has considerably more spatial dimensions than the three
we actually experience — most commonly, in the theory's best-developed versions, ten total
dimensions of space and time, with the extra six or seven spatial dimensions somehow "compactified,"
curled up so impossibly small and so tightly folded in on themselves that they have simply never been
detectable at any scale humans have yet managed to probe, the way a garden hose, seen from far enough
away, looks like a simple one-dimensional line, even though its surface actually curves around a
second, tightly rolled dimension too small to see from a distance.

The honest, necessary caveat, and this chapter will not soften it: after roughly half a century of
extraordinarily sophisticated theoretical development, string theory has not yet made a single
distinctive, testable prediction that current or realistically foreseeable technology could actually
check, and it has not yet settled on one single, specific version of itself as the correct
description of our actual universe rather than one of an almost incomprehensibly large number —
commonly estimated at somewhere around 10^500, a number so large it dwarfs the total number of atoms
in the observable universe many times over — of mathematically consistent alternative versions, each
describing a universe with different fundamental particles, different force strengths, potentially
even a different number of large dimensions, all equally permitted by the theory's own underlying
mathematics with no obvious way, so far, to determine which one, if any, corresponds to genuine
reality. This vast collection of mathematically possible alternate universes is what physicists have
in mind when they invoke the term "multiverse" in this particular scientific context — a usage worth
distinguishing clearly, for an honest reader, from the very different Many-Worlds multiverse this
book met two chapters ago in the argument over quantum measurement, and from the entirely separate
"multiverse" some cosmologists derive from certain models of the universe's very early, extremely
rapid inflationary expansion, in which different regions, expanding away from each other faster than
light could ever connect them again, might have settled into different physical constants of their
own. All three ideas get called "multiverse" in popular science writing, and this book wants its
reader to leave this chapter able to tell them apart, because conflating them is one of the more
common ways popular physics writing quietly overstates how much any one of them has actually been
established.

The physicist Peter Woit, in a pointedly titled 2006 book, and the physicist Lee Smolin, in a
companion critique published the same year, both made the case, from within the physics community
itself rather than from outside skeptics, that string theory's four-decade inability to produce a
single testable prediction represented a genuine crisis for the discipline — not because the
mathematics was wrong, necessarily, but because a theory that cannot, even in principle, be checked
against experiment sits uncomfortably close to the boundary this book has insisted on since Galileo's
ramp: the boundary between real physics and elegant, unfalsifiable philosophy dressed up in physics's
own vocabulary. Woit's book carried the blunt title *Not Even Wrong*, borrowing a phrase the physicist
Wolfgang Pauli reportedly used, decades earlier, to describe a piece of work so vague it failed to
even qualify as a testable, falsifiable claim in the first place — not wrong, in Pauli's cutting
formulation, but not even wrong, a category error more damning, in a working scientist's eyes, than
simple error. String theorists have pushed back, forcefully and with real technical substance,
arguing that the theory's mathematical consistency and its remarkable, unplanned ability to
reproduce general relativity itself as a natural low-energy consequence of string vibrations
represent a real form of indirect evidence, and that half a century is not, in the context of a
problem this fundamentally difficult, an unreasonable amount of time to still be searching for the
specific testable prediction that would finally settle the argument either way.

Loop quantum gravity, the second major contender, takes a genuinely different route toward the same
destination, and is worth understanding partly because its very different starting assumptions
highlight, by contrast, just how open the whole question of quantum gravity still is. Rather than
proposing new fundamental objects (strings) vibrating within an assumed, pre-existing continuous
spacetime, loop quantum gravity, developed principally by physicists including Abhay Ashtekar, Carlo
Rovelli, and Lee Smolin (making his second appearance in this chapter, this time as a builder rather
than a critic) starting in the late 1980s, applies the quantization procedure that made quantum
mechanics work for matter and energy directly to space and time themselves, and finds that space, on
this approach, is not smooth and continuous at the very smallest scales at all — it is built, instead,
from an unimaginably fine-grained lattice of discrete, quantized loops of gravitational field,
woven together into a structure the theory calls "spin networks," with a genuine smallest possible
unit of area and volume below which the very concept of geometric distance simply stops applying.
Space itself, on this picture, is not the smooth stage Newton, Einstein, and every physicist in
between assumed it to be. It is granular, all the way down, the way a photograph that looks smooth
and continuous from across the room turns out, examined closely enough, to be built from individual,
discrete pixels.

Loop quantum gravity shares string theory's central, honest limitation: it too has not yet produced a
distinctive, currently testable prediction capable of definitively distinguishing it from its rival,
though its proponents note, fairly, that unlike string theory it requires no extra spatial dimensions
and no vast landscape of alternate universes, achieving its quantization using only the three
dimensions of space general relativity already describes — a more conservative mathematical
commitment that its supporters consider a real point in its favor, and that string theorists
generally consider a symptom of the theory's more limited ambition rather than a genuine advantage.

This book's honest summary, offered in the same spirit of open, sympathetic, carefully-labeled
wonder it promised at this Part's outset: humanity has built two remarkably sophisticated, internally
consistent, and so far entirely unconfirmed mathematical frameworks for what the universe's deepest
fabric might actually be made of, developed by thousands of the most capable minds currently working
in physics, over five decades of extraordinarily hard, genuinely brilliant effort — and neither one
has yet cleared the one bar every single previous chapter of this book has insisted mattered more
than any other: a specific, falsifiable, checkable prediction, tested against nature and found to be
right. This is not, this book wants to be entirely clear, a criticism of the physicists doing the
work, who are, by any reasonable measure, attacking one of the hardest problems human curiosity has
ever posed itself, with the tools currently available. It is simply the honest state of the frontier,
offered without either the breathless overselling some popular treatments indulge in or the dismissive
sneer some skeptics prefer instead: a genuinely open question, awaiting, perhaps, its own Eddington
eclipse expedition, its own accidental pigeon-nest antenna discovery — the unplanned, patient, ordinary
kind of breakthrough that has settled nearly every other great open question in this book's four
hundred years, and that nobody, including the physicists working hardest on the problem, can yet
promise or predict the timing of.
