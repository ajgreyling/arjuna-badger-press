# Chapter 24 — Is Time Even Real?

Every chapter of this book so far has quietly assumed something that this chapter now wants to put
directly on the table and examine: that time flows, that "now" is a real, special, moving moment
sliding forward through an otherwise fixed sequence of past and future, the way a spotlight moves
along a stage while the rest of the set sits waiting in the dark on either side of it. This is not,
this chapter must report honestly, a settled question in physics. It may be one of the least settled
questions in the entire field, and the honest reason it unsettles physicists more than almost
anything else in this book is that the disagreement is not really about missing data, the way dark
matter's composition is missing data. It is a disagreement about what Einstein's own, already
thoroughly confirmed equations actually imply, once you take them completely seriously.

Chapter 9 of this book showed that special and general relativity dissolve Newton's assumption of a
single, universal, ticking cosmic clock — simultaneity itself, the very idea of two distant events
happening "at the same time," turns out to depend on how fast the observer judging them is moving.
Push that dissolution to its logical conclusion, as a number of serious physicists and philosophers
of physics have done, and you arrive at a picture called the block universe, or eternalism: past,
present, and future, on this view, are not stages a single, privileged "now" passes through in
succession. They are equally, permanently, simultaneously real, all of it, laid out together the way
every point along a physical stick's length is equally real all at once, with no special, moving
point that counts as the stick's "true" location. Your birth, this sentence, and your eventual death
would, on this picture, all exist with exactly the same fixed, permanent reality — there simply is no
"now" that is more real than any other moment, only a subjective, brain-generated impression of
motion through a landscape that, considered from outside any single observer's particular vantage
point, does not actually move at all. Einstein himself, writing a letter of condolence in 1955 to the
family of his recently deceased close friend Michele Besso, offered a version of exactly this
consolation, one of the more quietly moving passages of correspondence in this entire book: "People
like us, who believe in physics, know that the distinction between past, present, and future is only
a stubbornly persistent illusion." Einstein himself would die less than a month after writing it.

There is a plain, everyday physical fact this book has already established that makes the
block-universe picture considerably less exotic than it first sounds, worth pausing on before the
argument gets stranger still: this book's Chapter 9 also showed that relativity's dissolution of a
single universal "now" is not merely a philosopher's abstraction. It is already, quietly, an
engineering requirement of the satellite navigation system carried in most readers' own pockets.
GPS satellites orbit fast enough, and sit far enough up in Earth's weaker gravity, that their onboard
clocks run at a measurably different rate from clocks on the ground — a combination of special and
general relativistic effects that would, left uncorrected, cause the whole system's position
calculations to drift by several kilometers within a single day. Every GPS satellite's clock is
therefore deliberately built to run at a very slightly different rate before launch, precisely
compensating in advance for the relativistic drift its orbit will introduce, so that the "now" a
phone on the ground computes from four satellites' signals stays usable. Relativity's dissolved,
observer-dependent present is not, in other words, waiting at some remote theoretical frontier for
philosophers to argue about. It is already built, correction by correction, into the device most
readers used to find their way to wherever they are reading this sentence.

Not every serious physicist accepts this reading, and the opposing camp, generally called
presentism, deserves an equally honest hearing rather than being waved away as merely comfortable
common sense dressed up in philosophy. Its most sophisticated modern defenders, including physicists
working specifically on quantum gravity, point out that relativity's dissolution of a single universal
"now" is itself already complicated, and arguably partly undone, once you take quantum mechanics's
own measurement problem — the very puzzle this book's Chapter 23 left honestly unresolved — seriously
alongside it: something that a great many interpretations of quantum theory treat as a genuine,
irreversible, one-way event (a measurement "happening," a superposition "collapsing") does not sit
entirely comfortably inside a block universe where nothing, strictly, ever happens at all in any
privileged, ongoing sense, because everything, past and future alike, already simply exists. Some
physicists working at the intersection of these two great, famously incompatible theories — general
relativity's frozen block and quantum mechanics's seemingly genuine, ongoing collapse — have proposed
that reconciling them may require treating time's flow as considerably more physically real, not
less, than the pure block-universe picture allows; others have proposed the opposite, that quantum
collapse itself is merely an artifact of an observer's limited, forward-looking perspective within
an already-complete block, no more physically fundamental than a river's current looks, from a fixed
point on the bank, "flowing" past a spot that, described from above, isn't moving relative to the
whole river at all. This argument, unlike several others this Part has surveyed, has genuinely
divided working physicists rather than merely philosophers, and shows no sign, as of this writing, of
resolving in either direction.

The physicist Carlo Rovelli — whose own work on loop quantum gravity this book's earlier Part Five
chapter on quantum gravity has already introduced — has spent much of his later career arguing for a
version of the block-universe picture pushed even further, proposing what he calls the "thermal time"
hypothesis, developed together with the mathematician Alain Connes: that time itself, rather than
being a fundamental ingredient physics must simply assume from the outset the way Newton did, might
actually *emerge* from the second law of thermodynamics this book met all the way back in Chapter 5
— that what we experience as time's forward flow is nothing more mysterious, at bottom, than the
statistical tendency toward increasing entropy Boltzmann worked out for gas particles in a box,
scaled up to the whole universe, with the specific direction we call "the future" simply defined as
whichever direction entropy happens to be increasing in. On this view, a genuinely, fundamentally
timeless underlying reality could still produce every single one of the ordinary, felt experiences
this book's reader has of time passing — memory only running one direction, causes always preceding
their effects, the ordinary lived sense of past and future — purely as a consequence of the universe's
overall drift toward disorder, without time itself needing to be written into physics's most basic
equations at all. It is, this book would suggest, one of the more genuinely mind-bending
possibilities in this entire Part: not that time is an illusion exactly, but that it might be a real,
emergent, thermodynamic phenomenon, the way temperature is a real, emergent property of jostling
atoms even though no single atom, on its own, has a temperature at all.

There is a still stranger wrinkle waiting inside general relativity's own mathematics, discovered, in
one of the more quietly startling collaborations in this whole book, by the mathematician Kurt Gödel
— already one of the towering figures of twentieth-century logic for his incompleteness theorems,
the same theorems a later chapter of this book will show Roger Penrose leaning on, more
controversially, in his own theory of consciousness — during his years as a close friend and
frequent walking companion of Einstein's at Princeton in the 1940s. Gödel,
working through Einstein's field equations as a kind of intellectual
gift for his friend's seventieth birthday, found an entirely valid, mathematically consistent solution
describing a hypothetical universe that rotates as a whole, and showed that in such a rotating
universe, general relativity permits genuine closed timelike curves — paths through spacetime that,
followed consistently forward at every point, eventually loop back around to their own starting point
in the past. In plainer language: Gödel's solution describes a mathematically legal universe in which
time travel, in the fullest, most literal sense the phrase can carry, is not merely a science-fiction
convenience but a rigorously permitted consequence of Einstein's own equations. Our actual universe,
current cosmological measurement shows with real confidence, does not rotate in the way Gödel's toy
model requires, and no physicist takes Gödel's solution as a serious prediction that time travel is
actually achievable in the world we happen to live in. What it establishes instead, and this is the
honestly unsettling part, is that general relativity itself, taken as seriously and as literally as
this whole book has insisted every genuinely fundamental theory deserves to be taken, does not
forbid such loops on purely mathematical grounds — meaning something beyond the equations themselves,
some additional physical principle nobody has yet identified and confirmed, must be doing the work of
ruling them out in the actual universe we inhabit, if indeed they are ruled out at all. Einstein,
reportedly presented with Gödel's solution as a birthday gift, found it genuinely disturbing rather
than delightful, by his own account unsettled that his own theory, which he had built specifically to
tame Newton's absolute space and time into something more coherent, had returned the favor by handing
him a mathematically valid loophole back into the one thing time seems, to ordinary lived experience,
most obviously and stubbornly never to permit: a path back to yesterday.

This book will not pretend to resolve, in one chapter, a question serious physicists have not
resolved in over a century of taking it exactly this seriously. What it will say, plainly, before
moving on, is the same thing it has said at every other genuinely open frontier in this final Part:
that the ordinary, unexamined confidence most people carry through their entire lives — that "now" is
obviously, self-evidently real, and the past is obviously, self-evidently gone — turns out, once you
actually build the mathematical ramp and roll the physics down it the way this book's very first
chapter insisted you always must, to be considerably less secure a foundation than it feels from the
inside. That is not a reason for despair. It is, this book would argue, one final, fitting
demonstration of the entire method's power: even the single most intimate, most seemingly
unquestionable feature of anyone's own lived experience — the plain feeling that this particular
moment, right now, reading this particular sentence, is the one truly real one — turns out to be a
question physics can meaningfully ask, has not yet fully answered, and, this book is confident, will
keep patiently, honestly working on long after every name in this book has become, on either
reading of the argument, simply one more fixed point on the block.
