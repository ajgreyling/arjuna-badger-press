# Chapter 7 — The Reluctant Revolutionary

Max Planck did not want to start a revolution. This needs to be said plainly, right at the start of
this chapter, because Planck's role in the story that follows is almost the exact opposite of the
usual physics-revolutionary archetype: he was fifty-two years old when it happened, a conservative,
formal, deeply traditional Prussian professor who believed, by his own later account, in the
essential completeness and elegance of the classical physics that Newton, Maxwell, and Boltzmann
had built. He did not set out to overturn it. He was dragged into overturning it by a single stray
piece of data that refused, no matter how he massaged it, to fit the equations he was certain had to
be correct — and when he finally found a formula that did fit the data, he spent years trying to
explain it away as a mathematical trick rather than a real physical fact, because the alternative
was too strange for even him to want to believe.

The problem was called, with a certain grim Victorian understatement, the ultraviolet catastrophe,
and it emerged directly from Maxwell's beautiful, unified theory of electromagnetism — the very
achievement the previous chapter celebrated as one of the great triumphs of nineteenth-century
physics turned out to contain, buried in its own success, a genuine absurdity. Physicists knew that
any object, heated up, glows — first a dull red, then orange, then white, then, if you could get it
hot enough, blue-white. This is called blackbody radiation, and classical physics, using Maxwell's
equations plus Boltzmann's statistical mechanics, made a very specific and very testable prediction
about exactly how much energy a hot object should radiate at each frequency of light. At low
frequencies — red, infrared — the prediction matched the measurements from real physics laboratories
beautifully. But as physicists calculated what should happen at higher and higher frequencies,
climbing up through blue light and into the ultraviolet and beyond, the classical equations
predicted that the energy radiated should climb without any limit at all — that any hot object,
including, awkwardly, the reader of this book, should be radiating an *infinite* amount of energy
at ultraviolet and higher frequencies. This is obviously, checkably, laughably false; you are not
currently glowing with infinite ultraviolet radiation, and neither is anything else, ever, anywhere.
The problem was not merely academic embarrassment, either; Planck's own institute in Berlin had a
direct, practical stake in solving it, funded in part by the German lighting and electrical
industry's very concrete interest in knowing precisely how much light, and at what cost in energy,
a given filament or gas lamp ought to produce at a given temperature — Planck's blackbody puzzle
sat, in other words, considerably closer to nineteenth-century industrial engineering, in its
immediate origins, than to the abstract philosophical territory quantum mechanics would eventually
be associated with, one more entry in this book's running theme that some of its deepest discoveries
began life as unglamorous, practical questions about better light bulbs and more efficient steam
engines rather than any grand cosmic ambition. The equations that had correctly predicted radio waves, the equations that Hertz had called
"Maestro Maxwell" for getting so beautifully right, were producing an answer of infinity for a
quantity that plainly, obviously, was not infinite. Something in the foundations was wrong.

Planck, working in Berlin in 1900, found a mathematical formula that fixed the problem — a formula
that matched the actual, measured, non-infinite data at every frequency, low and high alike,
beautifully. The formula worked. What Planck could not initially explain, and what made him deeply
uncomfortable for years afterward, was *why* it worked, because the assumption buried inside it was
one that nobody in the two-hundred-and-fifty-year tradition running from Galileo through Newton
through Maxwell had ever needed to make: that energy, at the level of light being absorbed and
emitted by matter, does not come in a smooth, continuous flow, the way you might pour water from a
jug. It comes in discrete, indivisible packets — Planck called them, borrowing a Latin word for
"how much," *quanta* — and the size of each packet depends on the frequency of the light: a small
packet for red light, a much larger packet for ultraviolet. Once you assumed energy could only be
exchanged in these fixed-size lumps rather than in any amount whatsoever, the mathematics that had
been predicting infinite ultraviolet radiation suddenly, cleanly, correctly predicted the finite
glow that laboratories actually measured.

Planck himself called this a "purely formal assumption," a mathematical trick he'd reached for out
of what he later described, with the kind of honesty that makes him one of the more sympathetic
figures in this book, as an act of desperation — he did not, for years afterward, believe that
energy actually came in real, physical, indivisible lumps. He assumed, or at least hoped, that some
future, more complete theory would explain his formula in terms that preserved the smooth,
continuous universe of Maxwell and Newton that he had spent his whole career believing in and that
had, by every account of his students, formed something close to his religious faith in the
essential rationality and elegance of the physical world. He spent much of the next decade trying,
and failing, to find that more comfortable, continuous alternative. It was a young patent clerk in
Switzerland, unknown to the physics establishment, publishing four papers in a single year that
would each individually have made most physicists' careers, who told Planck — and told the rest of
physics, with considerably more conviction than Planck himself was prepared to muster — that the
quantum was not a mathematical trick at all. It was real.

Planck's own derivation, worth a moment's closer look for what it reveals about how theoretical
physics is actually practiced under pressure, was less a single flash of insight than a piece of
careful mathematical reverse-engineering: he already had, sitting in front of him, a curve of actual
measured blackbody data supplied by colleagues, and he worked backward from that curve, trying
different mathematical forms until he found one that fit the measurements at every frequency,
arriving at the correct formula, by his own later account, in a single evening at his desk — and only
afterward, over the following weeks, hunting for a theoretical justification, a physical story that
could explain why that particular formula and no other should be the right one, which is what
eventually forced the quantum assumption on him. It is a useful corrective to the popular image of a
theorist working purely from first principles outward: Planck found the answer first, by fitting the
data as honestly as he could, and only then went looking for the reason — a sequence closer to
Galileo's ramp than to pure deduction, however abstract the finished mathematics of quantum theory
would eventually become.

That patent clerk gets the next chapter of this book entirely to himself, because 1905 deserves it.
But it's worth pausing here, before he arrives, on the shape of the man who opened the door he
walked through, because Max Planck's later life carries a weight that the tidy textbook story of
"discovered the quantum, 1900" leaves out almost entirely. Planck lived to be eighty-nine, long
enough to see the quantum theory he'd been dragged into founding grow into the strange, thriving,
probabilistic edifice of quantum mechanics that occupies the next several chapters of this book —
an edifice whose central implications, the abandonment of strict determinism at the deepest level
of physical reality, troubled him for the rest of his life nearly as much as they would trouble
Einstein. And he lived through, and lost almost everything to, the Second World War: his home in
Berlin destroyed by Allied bombing, his life's papers and library burned; his younger son, Erwin,
implicated in the failed July 1944 plot to assassinate Hitler and executed by the Gestapo in early
1945, a loss from which Planck's biographers agree he never recovered. He had already lost his
first wife to illness and two daughters, both named Grete after her, to complications of
childbirth years apart, and an elder son to the First World War. He was, by the time of his death in
1947, a man who had watched almost everyone he loved die before him, having spent his working life
as the reluctant, uncomfortable midwife to the single strangest and most successful theory in the
history of science — a man who believed, to the end, in an orderly, rational universe, who had
nonetheless been the one to first crack it open and let the disorder in.

He received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1918, "in recognition of the services he rendered to the
advancement of Physics by his discovery of energy quanta," a sentence that undersells, by a
considerable margin, just how much of the rest of this book depends on the number he was so
reluctant to believe in himself: the size of that smallest indivisible packet of energy, now called
Planck's constant, turns up — small, fixed, unavoidable — in nearly every equation that follows
from here forward, in every chapter about atoms, particles, uncertainty, and the strange rules that
govern the smallest scales of the universe. Planck spent a decade hoping it would go away. It has,
instead, turned out to be one of the small handful of numbers the entire structure of physical
reality actually runs on.
