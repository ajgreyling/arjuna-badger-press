# Chapter 11 — The Cat That Wouldn't Stay Dead or Alive

Erwin Schrödinger wrote down the single most important equation in the history of chemistry and
much of the rest of physics, the one that determines the shape of every atom, the behavior of every
electron, the structure of every molecule in your body and everyone else's, while on a Christmas
holiday in the Swiss Alps in December 1925, in the company of a woman whose name history has not
reliably preserved, who was not his wife. This is not a scandalous aside included for cheap effect;
it is, by Schrödinger's own account and the consensus of his biographers, more or less exactly how
he did most of his best work, and understanding the man requires understanding this about him from
the outset, because his personal life and his physics were never, for Schrödinger, kept in separate
rooms.

He was Viennese, formally married to a woman named Annemarie Bertel, called Anny, in an arrangement
both of them understood from early on would not be sexually or romantically exclusive on either
side, and that lasted, in its unconventional way, until his death forty years later. Schrödinger
kept detailed, unembarrassed diaries recording his many affairs and infatuations; Anny had her own,
including a long relationship with the mathematician Hermann Weyl, who briefly employed Schrödinger
himself; the household Schrödinger eventually settled into in Ireland, decades later, when he ran
the Dublin Institute for Advanced Studies, openly included his wife and one of his mistresses living
together with him, along with a daughter from that relationship, in an arrangement his Dublin
neighbors found, by the polite standards of mid-century Irish Catholic society, considerably more
scandalous than the physics community ever much minded. None of this is included here to titillate;
it is included because Schrödinger himself, asked late in life to account for the extraordinary
burst of creative work that produced his equation, pointed more than once to exactly this kind of
romantic and intellectual restlessness as inseparable from how his mind actually worked — a mind
that, by his own account, needed the charge of a new infatuation the way other physicists needed a
strong cup of coffee.

The physics itself grew out of an idea proposed the year before, in 1924, by a French aristocrat and
physics student named Louis de Broglie, who made a proposal so strange and so apparently
unjustified that his doctoral examiners at the Sorbonne were reportedly ready to fail his thesis
outright, and only relented after quietly sending it to Einstein for a second opinion; Einstein
wrote back that de Broglie "has lifted a corner of the great veil," and the thesis was approved.
De Broglie's idea was this: if light, which everyone had always assumed was a wave, could also
behave like a stream of particles — exactly what Einstein's 1905 photoelectric effect paper had
shown — then perhaps the reverse was equally true, and particles of matter, like electrons, could
also behave, under the right circumstances, like waves.

Louis de Broglie's own examiners were not simply being obstructive when they balked at his 1924
thesis; the proposal genuinely had no experimental support behind it at the time, only a chain of
elegant analogical reasoning, and physics has never rewarded elegant analogy on its own without
demanding a checkable prediction to go with it. That prediction arrived just three years later, in
1927, when the American physicists Clinton Davisson and Lester Germer, at Bell Labs, fired a beam of
electrons at a crystal of nickel and found the electrons scattering off in a distinctive pattern of
peaks and troughs — a diffraction pattern, the unmistakable fingerprint of wave behavior, of exactly
the kind light produces when it passes through a narrow slit, and matching de Broglie's predicted
electron wavelength precisely. Solid, ordinary matter, fired at a metal surface, behaved like a wave.
De Broglie received the Nobel Prize the following year, and Davisson shared a later prize of his own
for the confirming experiment, but it is worth remembering, in this book's spirit of honest
labeling, that the idea Einstein once called "lifting a corner of the great veil" spent three years as
pure, unconfirmed theoretical daring before nature obliged.

Schrödinger took this seriously in a way almost nobody else initially did, and asked the obvious
next question: if an electron really does have a wave associated with it, what equation governs how
that wave behaves and changes over time, the way Maxwell's equations govern how a light wave
behaves? The equation he wrote down over that Alpine Christmas holiday — now simply called the
Schrödinger equation, engraved, among other places, on his own tombstone in the Austrian village of
Alpbach — answered exactly that question, and it worked, beautifully, precisely, correctly
predicting the same hydrogen spectrum lines that Bohr's cruder, ad hoc model of quantized orbits had
gotten right by a kind of educated guesswork, and going considerably further, correctly predicting
the behavior of atoms and molecules far more complex than hydrogen, in a mathematically unified,
elegant framework that made Bohr's earlier planetary-orbit picture look, by comparison, like the
scaffolding you take down once the real building is finished.

But the equation immediately raised a question Schrödinger himself never fully made peace with, and
that physics as a whole has arguably still not fully made peace with a century later: what, exactly,
is doing the waving? A wave on the ocean is water, physically rising and falling. A sound wave is
air, physically compressing and expanding. What is Schrödinger's electron-wave actually a wave *of*?
Schrödinger's own initial hope — and it was a hope that fit comfortably with the same classical,
picture-in-space intuitions this whole book has tried to build in the reader from Galileo's ramp
onward — was that the electron itself was, quite literally, smeared out through space like a small
cloud of electric charge, genuinely spread thin rather than sitting at any one point, with the wave
equation simply describing the shifting shape of that cloud.

It was a German physicist named Max Born, working out the mathematical implications more carefully,
who proposed the interpretation that ultimately won out, and that Schrödinger himself never fully
accepted or much liked for the rest of his life: Born argued that the wave is not a cloud of real,
physically smeared-out charge at all. It is a wave of *probability*. The mathematical square of the
wave's height, at any given point in space, tells you the probability of finding the electron there
if you actually go and measure its position — not a description of where the electron physically,
diffusely is, but a description of the odds of where you'll catch it, if you look. Before you look,
on this interpretation, the electron does not have a definite location at all; it exists, in some
genuinely strange sense that a century of physicists and philosophers have argued about ever since
without full resolution, as a spread of possibilities, a fog of *could-be*, that only resolves into
one definite, particular place at the instant a measurement actually happens.

Schrödinger loathed this idea, considered it a retreat from the plain, checkable, cause-and-effect
picture of reality that four hundred years of physics, from Galileo's ramp onward, had been built on,
and in 1935 he constructed, in a thought experiment published in a philosophical paper rather than a
technical physics one, the single most famous illustration of just how strange the probability
interpretation gets if you follow it all the way to its logical conclusion. Imagine, Schrödinger
proposed, a cat sealed inside a closed steel box, along with a tiny amount of a radioactive
substance, a Geiger counter, and a mechanism rigged so that if the Geiger counter detects a single
radioactive decay — a genuinely, fundamentally quantum, probabilistic event, with precisely a
fifty-fifty chance of occurring within, say, one hour — it triggers a hammer that shatters a vial of
poison gas, killing the cat instantly. According to the probability interpretation Schrödinger was
attacking, the radioactive atom, before anyone opens the box to look, exists in a genuine
superposition of having-decayed and not-having-decayed, both possibilities simultaneously real, not
merely unknown. And if the cat's fate is mechanically, deterministically linked to that atom's
undecided state, then the cat itself, by the same strict logic, ought to exist — until the box is
opened and the fact is checked — in an equally genuine superposition of dead and alive at once,
neither state resolved, both equally and simultaneously real, right up until the exact moment a
conscious observer lifts the lid and looks inside.

Schrödinger meant this as a reductio ad absurdum — a demonstration of just how obviously silly the
probability interpretation became once you scaled it up from an invisible electron to a macroscopic,
furry, sneezing cat, and used it to argue that something in the standard interpretation had to be
incomplete or simply wrong. Instead, and this is one of the great, slightly rueful ironies of the
whole history of physics, the thought experiment escaped its creator's intentions entirely and
became, over the following decades, the single most famous and most widely recognized image in all
of quantum mechanics, "Schrödinger's cat" repeated in classrooms and popular articles and internet
memes for a hundred years afterward — not usually as the devastating objection Schrödinger had
intended it to be, but as a cheerful, slightly cartoonish shorthand for quantum weirdness in
general, its original, pointed critical edge worn smooth by a century of casual repetition. The
question the cat was actually built to ask — what, precisely, counts as a "measurement" that
collapses the fog of probability into one definite outcome, and why should a conscious observer's
glance have any special power to do it that a Geiger counter's mechanical click does not already
have — remains, a century on, one of the genuinely open, unresolved questions at the foundations of
physics, and this book will return to it directly in Part Five, once several more strange men have
had their turn adding to the puzzle.

Schrödinger, for his part, spent much of the rest of his career trying, without full success, to
find an interpretation of his own famous equation that avoided the probability cloud entirely and
restored something closer to the plain, definite, cause-and-effect world he'd started out believing
in. He never fully succeeded, and by most accounts made his peace, in his later years, with being
remembered as the man who wrote the equation and hated what everyone else decided it meant. He
shared the 1933 Nobel Prize in Physics with Paul Dirac, whose own contribution to this story — an
equation stranger still, that predicted the existence of antimatter before anyone had ever seen a
trace of it — belongs a few chapters later in this book, once two other men, working in a very
different style from Schrödinger's own, have had their say about what an electron actually is.
