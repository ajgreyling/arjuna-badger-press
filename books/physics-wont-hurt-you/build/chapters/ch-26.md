# Chapter 26 — Is the Universe a Simulation?

Let this chapter say, before it says anything else, exactly what kind of chapter it is. Nothing that
follows here is settled physics, in the sense that the Higgs boson or the cosmic microwave background
are settled physics — confirmed, checkable, agreed upon by the overwhelming consensus of the field.
What follows is a live, seriously argued, and genuinely delightful philosophical question that a
number of real physicists, real philosophers, and — this being the twenty-first century — a number of
real Silicon Valley billionaires have taken considerably more seriously than a casual reader might
expect, and that this book intends to explore in the same spirit those thinkers bring to it:
wide-eyed, half-convinced, and honest about exactly how far the actual evidence currently reaches,
which, this chapter will show plainly, is not nearly as far as the argument's own cleverness might
suggest.

The modern version of the question was framed with unusual rigor in 2003 by the Swedish philosopher
Nick Bostrom, in a short, deliberately dry academic paper that has since become one of the most
discussed pieces of philosophy written this century. Bostrom's argument does not simply ask "could
we be living in a simulation," which is an old and largely unanswerable question going back at least
to Descartes's evil demon and, in different clothes, to Zhuangzi's butterfly dream in ancient China.
It asks something more specific and, in its way, more disciplined: given a few plausible-sounding
assumptions about the future, what should we actually expect, as a matter of simple probability, about
our own likely place within it?

The argument runs roughly like this. Suppose that civilizations, as they mature technologically,
eventually gain the ability to run extremely detailed, genuinely conscious computer simulations of
entire populated worlds — a capability that does not require any physics beyond what this book has
already established as real, just an enormous amount of computing power, applied to minds and
environments detailed enough to be indistinguishable, from the inside, from the "base" physical
reality that built the computer running them. Suppose, further, that at least some civilizations
that reach this technological level choose to run a very large number of such simulations — for
research, for entertainment, for reasons of historical curiosity, for any of the countless reasons
human beings already run smaller simulations of everything from weather systems to nuclear
explosions. If both of those suppositions are even roughly true, then the total number of simulated,
conscious minds, across all of reality, would vastly outnumber the comparatively tiny number of
"original," un-simulated minds that ever existed in the very first base reality where the whole
technological process got started. And if you are a conscious mind, with no independent way of
checking which category you belong to, simple probability suggests you are very likely one of the
overwhelming majority — a simulated mind — rather than one of the comparatively rare originals.
Bostrom's own conclusion, worth stating precisely because it's more careful than the argument's
popular retelling usually manages, is not "we are definitely in a simulation." It is a three-way
fork: either almost no civilizations survive long enough to reach this technological capability, or
almost none of the ones that do ever choose to run such simulations in large numbers, or — the
disquieting third option — we are, in fact, almost certainly living inside one right now.

Several genuinely eminent physicists have taken this seriously enough to write and speak about it at
length. The Swedish-American cosmologist Max Tegmark has explored versions of it as part of a much
broader personal research program on the mathematical structure of reality itself. The astrophysicist
Neil deGrasse Tyson has said, in public forums, that he finds the probability the argument suggests
uncomfortably persuasive. The physicist and Nobel laureate George Smoot — whose own work on the
cosmic microwave background this book celebrated back in Chapter 19 — has spoken favorably about it
in public lectures. This is worth noting plainly, because a skeptical reader could be forgiven for
assuming a question this strange must be pure internet speculation with no serious scientific
pedigree behind it at all, and that assumption would be wrong; it is speculation, but it is
speculation a genuine, credentialed slice of the physics community finds worth their public time and
reputation to engage with.

It is also worth being equally plain about the argument's real weaknesses, because a sympathetic
exploration is not the same thing as an uncritical one, and this book has promised, from its very
title page onward, never to let the fun of an idea erase the honest labeling of where it actually
stands. The argument rests on at least two genuinely large, entirely unverified assumptions: that
consciousness itself is a purely computational phenomenon, fully reproducible inside a sufficiently
detailed simulation running on non-biological hardware — a proposition Chapter 28 of this book will
show is itself hotly, unresolvedly contested — and that a simulated civilization's own physics would
even need to render, moment to moment, a perfectly consistent, infinitely detailed reality all the
way down to the subatomic level, rather than some far cheaper, more computationally efficient
approximation that only fills in detail where and when an observer actually happens to be looking
closely enough to notice a shortcut. Several physicists, hunting for exactly this kind of shortcut,
have proposed specific, in-principle testable signatures a simulated universe running on some
underlying computational grid might leave behind — a fundamental smallest possible length or energy
scale, baked into the simulation's own "resolution," that could in principle show up as a subtle
directional bias in the very highest-energy cosmic ray particles physics can observe. Searches for
exactly this signature have been conducted. None have found anything. This is not proof the
simulation hypothesis is false — a sufficiently sophisticated simulation could, this book must
honestly note, always be built cleverly enough to hide any specific signature you happen to think to
look for, which is precisely the frustrating, and to a rigorous scientific mind rather unsatisfying,
character of the whole hypothesis. It is simply the honest current state of the actual evidence: none
found, none currently expected, and a real, structural difficulty in ever designing an experiment
that could conclusively rule the idea either in or out.

A further, often-overlooked wrinkle in Bostrom's own argument is worth adding here, because it
cuts directly against the more breathless popular retellings: Bostrom himself has repeatedly and
publicly stressed that his argument gives no reason at all to think a simulated reality would be
lower-fidelity, glitchy, or somehow less real *to its inhabitants* than a base, un-simulated one —
a simulated mind, by the argument's own premises, experiences exactly the same rich, convincing
world a base-reality mind does, feels pain and joy and curiosity with the same subjective force, and
would have absolutely no reason, from the inside, to feel cheated or diminished even if the argument
turned out to be correct. The philosophical stakes of the question, in other words, are considerably
smaller than the science-fiction framing usually implies: it is a question about the deep metaphysical
architecture underneath experience, not a question about whether that experience, or the curiosity,
love, and grief that fill this book's own four-hundred-year story, would somehow matter less.

There is a gentler, less headline-grabbing cousin of this whole family of ideas that deserves its own
mention here, because it grows out of genuinely mainstream, well-supported physics rather than
Bostrom's probabilistic argument about future computing civilizations: the holographic principle,
developed by the physicists Gerard 't Hooft and Leonard Susskind in the 1990s out of serious work on
black hole thermodynamics (the very information paradox this book's previous chapter left
unresolved), which proposes that all the information describing everything happening inside any
given three-dimensional region of space can, in principle, be fully encoded on the two-dimensional
boundary surface of that region, the way a hologram encodes a full three-dimensional image on a flat
sheet of film. Taken seriously and pushed to its cosmic scale, some physicists have speculated that
our own three-dimensional universe might itself be, in some deep mathematical sense, a kind of
projection from information genuinely encoded on a distant boundary surface — not a computer
simulation in Bostrom's sense of a program running on some external hardware, but a hint that the
very question "what is truly fundamental, the three-dimensional world we experience or some
lower-dimensional information encoding it" may be less settled, at the deepest level, than four
centuries of Galileo's ramps and Newton's apples ever gave anyone reason to suspect.

What should a reasonable, curious person actually make of all this? This book's honest answer, and
its last word on the subject before moving on, is that the simulation hypothesis earns its place in
a history of physics not because it is likely true — there is, as this chapter has tried scrupulously
to show, no positive evidence that it is, and a real, structural difficulty in ever finding any — but
because taking it seriously, for the length of one chapter, forces a useful kind of honesty about
just how little this book, for all its four-hundred-year sweep of triumphant, checkable
predictions, actually knows about the single most basic question of all: not how the universe
behaves, which physics has gotten spectacularly, repeatedly right, but what the universe fundamentally
*is*, underneath the behavior. That is precisely the question the next chapter takes up directly,
in the company of two ambitious, unfinished, and so far entirely unconfirmed attempts to answer it.
