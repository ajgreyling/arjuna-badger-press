# Chapter 3 — Lines of Force You Cannot See

Michael Faraday left school at thirteen. His family was poor — his father was a blacksmith,
frequently too ill to work, and the household went hungry often enough that Faraday remembered, in
old age, being given one loaf of bread to last a week. He was apprenticed to a bookbinder in London,
which turned out to be, by pure accident, one of the luckiest breaks in the entire history of
science, because a bookbinder's apprentice gets to read the books he's binding, and the fourteen-
year-old Michael Faraday started reading the science encyclopedias that came across his bench, and
found that he could not stop.

He had almost no mathematics — never learned much beyond basic arithmetic, and would go his entire
career, one of the most consequential in the history of physics, essentially unable to follow the
kind of advanced calculus that Newton had used to lock up the laws of gravity. This should, by every
reasonable expectation of how science works, have been disqualifying. Instead it turned out to be
close to a superpower, because it forced Faraday to think about the physical world in pictures
instead of equations, in a way that mathematically trained physicists of his era simply didn't, and
those pictures turned out to be onto something real that the equations, for the moment, had missed
entirely.

He talked his way into a job as a bottle-washer and assistant in the laboratory of Humphry Davy, the
most famous chemist in England, after attending a series of Davy's public lectures, taking
meticulous illustrated notes, binding them into a beautiful book, and sending it to Davy along with
a request for employment. Davy initially turned him down — there were no positions — but hired him
months later after firing an assistant for brawling, and reportedly told a friend, near the end of
his own life, when asked what he considered his greatest discovery, that it was Michael Faraday.
This was not entirely a compliment paid without cost: Davy grew jealous of his former assistant's
rising fame within a decade, and actively tried to block Faraday's election to the Royal Society,
the very institution Davy himself led — one more entry in the long list of scientific mentors this
book will meet who could not quite bear watching the student surpass the teacher.

Faraday's actual work, once he had his own laboratory, circled obsessively around a single question
that a Danish physicist named Hans Christian Ørsted had stumbled into by accident in 1820: Ørsted
had noticed, demonstrating something else entirely to a lecture hall of students, that an electric
current flowing through a wire made a nearby compass needle twitch. Electricity and magnetism,
which everyone had assumed for two thousand years were entirely separate phenomena, were somehow
talking to each other. Nobody, including Ørsted, had any real idea why, or what to do with the fact.

Faraday kept a diary of his laboratory work for over four decades, numbering every single
experimental entry consecutively; by the end of his working life the count had passed sixteen
thousand, a plain testament, this book would suggest, to the same unglamorous, repetitive patience
that runs underneath every genuine discovery in this whole story — Galileo's ramp rolled again and
again, Curie's pitchblende dissolved batch after batch, and now Faraday's coils and magnets,
rearranged and re-tested, entry sixteen thousand and something, in pursuit of an effect he was
certain had to be there before he had any proof at all that it was. Faraday spent the next decade chasing it, and in 1831 found the piece Ørsted's discovery had been
missing: not just that electric current could produce magnetism, but that a *changing* magnetic
field could produce an electric current, out of nothing, in a wire that wasn't attached to any
battery at all — you just had to move a magnet near it, or move the wire near the magnet, and a
current would appear, for as long as the motion continued. He built the first electric generator,
essentially by accident, in the course of proving this — a simple copper disk spinning between the
poles of a magnet, producing a small but continuous electric current, the direct ancestor of every
power station on Earth today. He had, without doing a single line of the advanced mathematics that
would eventually be needed to fully describe what he'd found, discovered electromagnetic induction:
the fact, quite literally, that makes the modern electrical grid possible.

What makes Faraday matter to this book, though, is not just the discovery — a merely clever
experimentalist could have stumbled onto electromagnetic induction and called it a day. It's the
picture he built to explain it to himself, because he couldn't do the math, and the picture turned
out to be more physically real than anyone at the time, including Faraday, quite understood. He
proposed that a magnet doesn't just sit there being magnetic; it fills the space around itself with
something — he called them *lines of force* — an invisible structure radiating outward, curving
through the surrounding space, that a compass needle or a length of wire or another magnet could
feel and respond to, even across empty space, without anything visible connecting them. He
sprinkled iron filings on a sheet of paper held over a magnet to make the lines of force visible,
watching them snap into the familiar curved pattern that every science-class student has drawn ever
since, and became convinced — correctly, as it would take decades and Faraday's own math-free
intuition to be fully vindicated — that these lines were not just a convenient way of drawing a
diagram. They were physically real. Space itself, in the vicinity of a magnet or a charge, was
somehow altered, structured, filled with something, even where nothing you could touch or weigh
appeared to be present.

This was, at the time, a genuinely radical claim, and most of the mathematically sophisticated
physicists of the era regarded it with polite condescension, the way a modern physicist might
regard a talented amateur with a beautiful metaphor and no real theory behind it. Isaac Newton's
gravity worked through what physicists called "action at a distance" — the Sun simply reached out
and pulled on the Earth, instantaneously, across ninety-three million miles of empty space, with no
mechanism proposed or apparently needed, and the mathematics worked perfectly well without asking
how the pulling actually happened. Faraday's idea that a *field* — a real structure filling the
space around a charge or a magnet — was doing the actual work, rather than a mysterious pull
reaching directly from one object to another, seemed to most of his contemporaries like an
unnecessary and unmathematical complication. Faraday, without the tools to prove them wrong on
their own terms, kept insisting anyway, for the same reason Galileo kept insisting the Earth moved:
because his experiments kept behaving as though the field, and not the distant pull, was the thing
actually there.

He was right, and it would take a young Scottish mathematician who greatly admired him, working in
the years right after Faraday's own powers began to fail, to prove it in the language the
mathematically sophisticated physicists of the era would finally have to accept.

Faraday himself never lost the plain, almost childlike delight in the natural world that had first
gotten him hooked as a bookbinder's apprentice reading an encyclopedia over his lunch break. He
turned down a knighthood, reportedly telling a friend he preferred to remain "plain Michael Faraday
to the last," and turned down repeated invitations to develop chemical weapons for the British
government during the Crimean War on grounds of conscience, a small and often-overlooked act of
moral courage from a man whose discoveries would, a century later, make possible both the medical
imaging that saves millions of lives every year and the electrical grid that, in the wrong hands
and the wrong century, could power considerably worse things than that. He founded, and for decades
personally delivered, the Royal Institution's Christmas Lectures for young people — a tradition that
continues in London to this day, nearly two hundred years later, aimed specifically at children, in
the conviction that the whole of what he'd found belonged, by right, to anyone curious enough to
ask, whether or not they could do the mathematics. He never did learn much of it himself. It never
once stopped him from being right.
