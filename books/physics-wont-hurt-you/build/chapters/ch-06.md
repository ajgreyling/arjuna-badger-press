# Chapter 6 — Glowing in the Dark

Henri Becquerel found radioactivity essentially by accident, in a Paris drawer, in the spring of
1896, and the story is worth telling carefully because it is one of the cleanest examples in this
entire book of how much genuine scientific discovery depends not on a flash of pure genius but on a
scientist patient and honest enough to notice when an experiment has quietly gone wrong in an
interesting way. Becquerel was investigating a hunch, inspired by Wilhelm Röntgen's sensational
discovery of X-rays a few months earlier, that certain naturally fluorescent minerals — substances
that glow after being exposed to sunlight — might also emit X-rays as part of the same process. His
plan was to expose uranium salts to bright sunlight, then place them on top of photographic plates
wrapped in thick black paper, reasoning that if the sunlight-excited minerals emitted X-rays, the
rays would pass through the paper and fog the plate, while ordinary light could not. A stretch of
cloudy Paris weather interrupted the experiment, and Becquerel, rather than waiting indefinitely for
sun, set the wrapped plates and uranium salts aside together in a dark drawer to wait it out. When he
eventually developed the plates anyway, more out of a hunch than expectation, he found them
intensely fogged — even though the uranium salts had received no sunlight exposure at all, sitting in
total darkness the entire time. Whatever was fogging the plates had nothing to do with sunlight, or
fluorescence, or the hypothesis Becquerel had actually set out to test. The uranium itself, sitting
quietly in the dark, was spontaneously emitting some kind of penetrating ray, all on its own,
constantly, with no external trigger required at all.

It was a young Polish-born physicist and chemist working in Paris, Marie Skłodowska Curie, who took
Becquerel's accidental finding and turned it into an entire new field of physics, at a cost to her
own health that this book will not soften. Curie — she had married the French physicist Pierre Curie
in 1895, and the two would become, by every account of their close working partnership, one of the
genuine scientific marriages of equals in this whole book, each crediting the other publicly and
often, in an era when that kind of generosity between a husband and wife scientist was far from
guaranteed — set out to systematically test every known element and mineral for this same strange,
spontaneous emission Becquerel had stumbled onto, using an extremely sensitive electrometer Pierre
and his brother had built years earlier for unrelated crystal research. She coined the term
"radioactivity" for the phenomenon itself, and found, working through a mineral called pitchblende,
that its radioactivity was considerably stronger than the amount of uranium it contained could
account for — meaning some other, far more intensely radioactive substance, not yet identified by
anyone, had to be hiding inside it. Marie and Pierre spent the next four years, working in a
converted shed at the School of Physics with what Marie herself later described as primitive,
freezing, leaking conditions unfit for real laboratory science, physically processing tons of raw
pitchblende ore by hand — dissolving, filtering, crystallizing, batch after batch, in a process one
biographer memorably compared to needle-in-a-haystack alchemy conducted at industrial scale — to
isolate, in 1898, two entirely new chemical elements. They named the first polonium, after Marie's
native and then-partitioned Poland, a quietly political act of national pride from a woman who had
left home partly because Warsaw's universities would not admit women. The second, considerably more
radioactive still, they named radium.

Marie Curie became, in 1903, the first woman ever awarded a Nobel Prize, sharing the Physics award
with Pierre and with Becquerel — though even this triumph carried an ugly asterisk this book should
not paper over: the Nobel committee's original nomination, drafted by members of the French Academy
of Sciences, listed only Pierre and Becquerel, omitting Marie entirely from the citation for
discoveries that were, by any honest accounting, at least as much hers; it was Pierre himself, upon
learning of the omission, who wrote to a sympathetic committee member insisting his wife's name be
restored, a rare and genuinely admirable act of a celebrated man using his own standing to correct an
injustice done to his collaborator rather than quietly accepting the credit alone. Pierre died in
1906, struck and killed by a horse-drawn cart in a Paris street, and Marie, devastated, took over his
professorship at the Sorbonne, becoming the university's first woman professor in its six-
hundred-year history, and continued the work alone. In 1911, she won a second Nobel Prize, this time
in Chemistry, for the isolation and characterization of pure radium and polonium — still, more than a
century later, the only person of any gender ever to win Nobel Prizes in two distinct sciences.

The same year, 1911, the Nobel committee's Swedish secretary quietly wrote to Curie suggesting she
might prefer not to travel to Stockholm to collect the prize in person, in the middle of a vicious
public scandal then engulfing the French press over her relationship with a married former student of
her late husband's, the physicist Paul Langevin — a relationship Langevin's own estranged wife had
exposed through stolen letters leaked to sensationalist newspapers, which seized on Curie's status as
a foreign-born woman scientist to attack her with a viciousness that had nothing whatsoever to do
with her actual chemistry. Curie's reply to the committee, preserved and widely quoted since, stands
as one of the more quietly forceful statements of scientific principle in this entire book: she wrote
that the Prize had been awarded for the discovery of radium and polonium, that her private life had no
bearing whatsoever on the validity of that scientific work, and that she saw "no connection between
my scientific work and the facts of private life" — and she went to Stockholm and collected the
prize in person anyway, alone, a widow in her mourning black, while a French duel was nearly fought
in Paris over the same newspaper scandal in her absence.

It is not possible to tell Marie Curie's story honestly, in a book that has promised its reader
throughout to be plain about cost as well as triumph, without confronting directly what radioactivity
did to the woman who discovered it. Neither Curie nor almost anyone else working with radioactive
materials in that first pioneering generation understood, in the years before and even for some time
after her death, how dangerous sustained exposure actually was; Curie carried test tubes of radium
salts in her pocket for the pleasure of their faint, eerie glow in the dark, kept vials of it on her
desk as beautiful curiosities, and spent the First World War personally driving mobile X-ray units —
"petites Curies," the French troops called them — to the front lines to help army surgeons locate
shrapnel and bullets in wounded soldiers, work she considered urgent enough to teach herself to
drive and repair the trucks' engines for, undertaken with essentially no radiation shielding by
modern standards, for herself or for the many young women she personally trained to operate the
units. Marie Curie died in 1934, of aplastic anemia, a bone-marrow disease now understood to be a
direct and, in retrospect, tragically predictable consequence of decades of cumulative radiation
exposure. Her original laboratory notebooks, kept today in the French national library, remain too
radioactive to handle without protective equipment even now, nearly a century after her death, and
visitors who wish to examine them must sign a waiver acknowledging the risk.

Her elder daughter, Irène Joliot-Curie, working with her own husband Frédéric in the very laboratory
tradition her parents had built, discovered artificial radioactivity in 1934 — showing that stable
elements bombarded with the right particles could be made to become radioactive themselves, rather
than radioactivity being solely a naturally occurring property of a small handful of heavy elements
like uranium and radium — and won her own Nobel Prize in Chemistry for it the following year, making
the Curies, mother and daughter both Nobel laureates in the same discipline their own family had
essentially founded, one of the more remarkable single-family legacies in the entire history of
science. Irène, too, would eventually die of leukemia very plausibly linked to a lifetime of
radiation exposure inherited, in the most literal sense a family history can carry it, from the work
her mother had pioneered in that freezing, leaking Paris shed.

Radioactivity's discovery did more than earn its finders two generations of Nobel Prizes and, in the
end, take their lives. It handed the rest of this book's coming chapters a genuinely new physical
puzzle that neither Newton's mechanics nor Maxwell's electromagnetism had any framework to explain:
why should an atom, sitting perfectly still, with no external trigger, spontaneously and
unpredictably emit energetic particles and transform itself into an entirely different element? The
honest answer would not arrive until physicists had worked out the internal structure of the atom
itself — the nucleus this book's Chapter 10 will introduce through Rutherford's gold foil — and the
genuinely probabilistic, uncertain rules governing what that nucleus does and when, rules this book's
next several chapters will show turned out to require throwing out the whole tidy, deterministic
clockwork universe that had served physics faithfully since Galileo's ramp. Radioactivity, glowing
quietly and dangerously in a Paris drawer in 1896, was the first hard evidence that the clockwork had
a crack in it — a crack Marie Curie spent the rest of her too-short life prying open, at a cost she
paid herself, in full, so that the rest of physics could see what was hiding inside.
