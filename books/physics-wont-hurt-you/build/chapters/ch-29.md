# Chapter 29 — The Ethics of Knowing

This book has now told, across twenty-eight chapters, the story of physics getting things right — an
almost unbroken four-century run of daring predictions, checked against nature, and confirmed, from
Newton's bulging Earth to the Higgs boson's tearful discovery. It owes its reader, before it can
honestly close, one more chapter about what happens after physics gets something right, because
being right about the universe has never, not once in this whole story, been a morally neutral
achievement, and the physicists who did the getting-right have not always been given, or taken, the
chance to reckon with that honestly.

Chapter 15 already told the plainest version of this reckoning: the Manhattan Project, the bomb, and
the particular grief of physicists like Szilard, Oppenheimer, and Einstein watching a discipline
built on curiosity produce a weapon capable of ending civilization. That reckoning did not end in
1945, and it is worth tracing, briefly, where it went next, because the pattern it set — brilliant,
well-intentioned people building something true and powerful, only to discover, often too late to
fully undo it, that truth and power do not come with an attached user's manual for wisdom — has
repeated itself, in different clothes, in nearly every decade since.

Richard Feynman himself, whose fuller life this library tells at length, offers this book's clearest
single case study in what taking that responsibility seriously can actually look like in practice,
decades after the war that first taught him the stakes. In 1986, dying of cancer, he joined the
Rogers Commission investigating the *Challenger* space shuttle disaster, and found, once inside the
process, an institutional culture eerily reminiscent of what a physicist should never quite trust:
official risk estimates presented to Congress and the public that differed by orders of magnitude from
the far more sober numbers the working engineers who actually built the hardware were privately
using. Feynman's response — a piece of rubber O-ring seal, a clamp, and a glass of ice water, dropped
into a televised hearing to demonstrate, in thirty unmistakable seconds, exactly what a subcommittee's
worth of hedged testimony had failed to make vivid — was not merely good television. It was, this
book would argue, the whole of the scientific method's ethical core, distilled: refuse to let an
institution's preferred, comfortable version of events stand in for what a plain, checkable
measurement actually shows, especially, and especially urgently, when seven human lives had already
been lost to exactly that kind of comfortable evasion.

It is worth pausing, before this chapter turns to the harder and more contested cases, on a genuine
success story, because this book's whole final Part has insisted on honesty in both directions, and
the honest record on physics-and-ethics is not only a record of weapons and warnings. In the
mid-1970s, the chemists Mario Molina and Sherwood Rowland calculated, using physics and chemistry
that traces directly back to the quantum theory of molecular bonding this book's Part Two
established, that chlorofluorocarbons — CFCs, the chemically inert, commercially beloved refrigerant
and aerosol propellant gases that seemed, on every practical measure available at the time, perfectly
safe — would drift, essentially unchanged, up into the stratosphere and there, broken apart by
solar ultraviolet light, release chlorine atoms capable of catalytically destroying the ozone layer
that shields the whole of the Earth's surface from the same ultraviolet radiation. The finding was
disputed, sometimes viciously, by an industry with billions of dollars riding on the answer coming
out otherwise. It did not come out otherwise: a 1985 discovery of an actual, rapidly deepening
seasonal hole in the ozone layer over Antarctica confirmed the mechanism in the sky itself, and the
world's governments, in a rare and genuinely instructive display of physics translated swiftly and
almost unanimously into binding global policy, adopted the 1987 Montreal Protocol phasing out CFC
production worldwide. The ozone layer, measured today, is recovering, on a trajectory current models
project will return it to its pre-industrial state sometime around the middle of this century — a
plain, checkable, decades-long confirmation that the same method capable of building a bomb is
equally capable, when the political will meets it partway, of catching and fixing a slower-moving
catastrophe before it fully arrives. Rowland himself, reflecting late in life on the fight to get
the finding taken seriously, is widely quoted as having asked his own wife, after finishing the
original calculation, "what's the point of doing this atmospheric chemistry if, at the end, all we
can do is write papers about it" — a question this whole chapter would suggest every physicist
handed a genuinely consequential finding eventually has to sit with.

The pattern Feynman fought at NASA in 1986 has, if anything, only grown larger and higher-stakes in
the decades since, as physics-derived technology has moved from weapons the public could at least see
and fear directly into infrastructure most people never think to examine at all. The same statistical
and computational tools originally developed for particle physics and cosmology — the pattern-finding
methods this book's Chapter 17 used to describe how Gell-Mann found order in the particle zoo — now
sit underneath the machine-learning systems increasingly used to make decisions about who gets a loan,
who gets released on bail, and what content billions of people see online each day, deployed, in a
great many documented cases, with far less rigorous checking against real-world outcomes than any
physics journal would tolerate for a claimed discovery. The particular ethical discipline this book
has celebrated throughout — publish the specific, falsifiable prediction; check it honestly against
the world; report the result even when it embarrasses the institution funding you — has not
automatically traveled along with the mathematics into these newer, physics-descended technologies,
and a great many of the scientists and engineers now working at the frontier of artificial
intelligence have explicitly and publicly invoked this book's own history, Oppenheimer's reckoning
foremost among the examples cited, in warning that a comparably consequential technology may again be
outrunning the institutions built to govern it responsibly.

Geoengineering — the deliberate, planet-scale manipulation of Earth's climate, using physics
straightforwardly derived from the atmospheric science this book has not had room to cover in detail
but that rests on the same radiative and thermodynamic principles Chapter 5's Boltzmann would
recognize immediately — presents a newer and in some ways even starker version of the same dilemma.
Proposals to inject reflective aerosol particles into the stratosphere, mimicking the global cooling
effect scientists have directly observed following large volcanic eruptions, are technically
plausible, comparatively inexpensive relative to the cost of preventing climate change through
emissions reduction alone, and genuinely capable, by most serious physical modeling, of measurably
lowering global average temperatures within a few years of deployment. They are also unilaterally
achievable by a single sufficiently motivated wealthy nation or even, by some analyses, a sufficiently
wealthy private actor, acting without the consent of the rest of the planet whose weather patterns
such an intervention would also, unavoidably, alter in ways current climate models cannot yet predict
with full regional precision or full confidence about who, specifically, ends up bearing the
unintended costs. It is a vivid, present-tense illustration of a question this book's entire final
part has been circling from different angles: a technology can be physically real, scientifically
sound, and genuinely powerful, and still arrive years or decades ahead of the ethical and political
institutions capable of deciding, wisely and fairly, whether and how it should actually be used.

There is no tidy formula this chapter can honestly hand its reader for resolving any of this, and a
book that pretended otherwise would be selling something false, exactly the kind of overselling this
whole Part has tried to warn against. What this book can offer instead, drawn from four centuries of
watching physicists actually navigate versions of this same dilemma, is a short, hard-earned set of
working principles, none of them original to this book but all of them, in different words, repeated
by the physicists who lived through the sharpest versions of the choice. Tell the truth about the
risk, in plain numbers, even when a comfortable institutional story would be easier to deliver, the
way Feynman insisted on doing in front of a national television audience even as the commission's own
chair visibly wished he wouldn't. Do not let the elegance or the cleverness of a discovery excuse you
from asking, honestly and early, what it might be used for by someone considerably less careful than
you are, the way Szilard tried, imperfectly and too late, to redirect the weapon he had helped set in
motion. And remember, as this book's very next and final chapter will insist on remembering one more
time before it closes, that the same plain, patient, checkable curiosity that built the bomb also
built the vaccine, the MRI machine, the solar panel, and the satellite that lets a stranded hiker call
for rescue from the middle of a wilderness — that physics's moral weight has never run in only one
direction, and that the discipline's whole history, honestly told, is not a cautionary tale about
curiosity gone wrong. It is a four-hundred-year argument, still ongoing, still unresolved, about
whether human beings can be trusted with the true things they keep, against all reasonable
expectation, managing to find out.
