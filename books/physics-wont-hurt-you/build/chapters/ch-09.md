# Chapter 9 — Falling Elevators and Curved Space

Special relativity, for all its strangeness, had left one enormous problem sitting untouched right
in the middle of physics, and Einstein knew it the moment he'd finished the 1905 papers: it worked
beautifully for objects moving at constant speed, but Newton's theory of gravity, the one that had
correctly predicted the tides and the orbits of the planets and the bulge of the Earth for over two
hundred years, still assumed exactly the kind of instantaneous "action at a distance" that special
relativity had just declared impossible. Newton's gravity said the Sun pulls on the Earth right now,
this instant, across ninety-three million miles of space, with no delay whatsoever. Special
relativity said nothing — no signal, no influence, no information of any kind — can travel faster
than light. If the Sun suddenly vanished, Newton's equations said the Earth would feel the loss of
its gravitational pull instantly. Special relativity said that was impossible; the news would have
to travel to Earth at the speed of light at the fastest, taking a little over eight minutes to
arrive, the same delay it takes the Sun's actual light to reach us. Something had to give, and it
took Einstein the better part of a decade — years he later described, in letters to friends, with a
frustration and self-doubt that the confident 1905 papers give almost no hint of, as the hardest
sustained intellectual struggle of his life — to work out what.

The insight that finally broke the problem open, Einstein said later, arrived while he was sitting
at his desk at the Swiss patent office (he'd been promoted since 1905, but hadn't yet managed to
land the academic post his growing fame should by rights have secured him), imagining a man falling
freely off the roof of a house. Such a man, Einstein realized, would feel weightless during the
fall — he would not feel his own weight pressing down on anything, because there is nothing
underneath him yet to press against, and if he happened to have a set of keys or a hat in his
pocket, they would fall right alongside him, appearing, from his own falling point of view, to
simply float there. Einstein called this, in a letter to a friend years later, "the happiest thought
of my life," and the reason it deserves the billing is what it implies: if you cannot, from inside a
freely falling elevator with no windows, tell the difference between falling under gravity and
simply floating in empty space with no gravity at all, then gravity and acceleration are not two
different phenomena that happen to produce similar effects. They are, at some deep level, the same
phenomenon, viewed from different frames — an idea Einstein called the equivalence principle, and
built the next ten years of increasingly difficult mathematics on top of.

The version of this most often used to explain general relativity to a general audience — a person
standing in a windowless, accelerating rocket in deep space cannot tell the difference between the
force pressing their feet to the floor and ordinary gravity holding them to the surface of a planet
— captures the same idea from the opposite direction, and it's worth sitting with both versions for
a moment, because between them they contain the entire seed of the theory: acceleration and gravity
feel, from the inside, exactly the same, and if that is true without exception, then gravity cannot
simply be a force reaching out and pulling objects toward each other, the way Newton had assumed.
It has to be something about the shape of space and time itself.

What Einstein eventually worked out — with substantial mathematical help from his friend Marcel
Grossmann, who introduced him to a branch of geometry, developed decades earlier by mathematicians
with no interest whatsoever in physics, dealing with curved rather than flat spaces, that turned
out to be exactly the tool the problem needed — is that mass and energy do not pull on other masses
across empty space at all. They *bend* space and time itself, curving the very fabric that
everything else, including light, has to travel through. A planet does not orbit the Sun because
the Sun is reaching out and yanking on it. A planet orbits the Sun because the Sun's mass has curved
the space around it into a shape, and the planet, like a marble rolling on a curved sheet, is simply
traveling in the straightest possible line available to it through space that is no longer flat.
Gravity, in Einstein's picture, is not a force. It is geometry. The often-repeated summary,
attributed to the physicist John Wheeler decades later — "spacetime tells matter how to move;
matter tells spacetime how to curve" — captures the whole idea in ten words, though Einstein himself
never phrased it quite that neatly; getting there took him a decade of the hardest mathematics of
his career, several false starts he later had to publicly retract, and, by 1915, a genuinely
nerve-wracking race against a brilliant German mathematician named David Hilbert, who was closing in
on the same final equations from a more purely mathematical direction and who submitted his own
version to a journal within days of Einstein's — a near-photo-finish that historians of science
still argue over the precise timing of, though the consensus credits Einstein with the physical
insight and the correct final theory, whatever the exact sequence of submission dates.

Einstein's own personal life through this decade of struggle carried real cost that a book this
honest about every other physicist's private toll should not quietly skip over in his case alone:
his first marriage, to the physicist Mileva Marić, a fellow student from his own university years
and by several biographers' assessment a genuinely capable physicist in her own right whose exact
contribution to Einstein's early thinking remains debated but was very likely more than the
purely supportive role popular history has generally granted her, collapsed under the weight of his
consuming, near-total absorption in the general relativity problem, ending in a bitter separation
during the same years the theory was finally coming together and a divorce finalized in 1919, the
same year the Eddington eclipse expedition made him instantly, overwhelmingly famous. Their surviving
correspondence from these years is by turns tender and genuinely cruel, including a notorious list of
household conditions Einstein presented to Mileva as terms for continuing to live under the same
roof — among them that she keep his clothes and laundry in good order and not expect any personal
affection from him — a document that sits uncomfortably, and this book will not pretend otherwise,
alongside the same man's later, hard-won public reputation for gentleness and humane wit.

The theory, finished in November 1915, made a prediction bold enough to satisfy the same standard
Newton's gravity had set two centuries earlier: something specific, checkable, and not yet observed.
General relativity predicted that light itself, having no mass but still traveling through curved
space, should be bent by a massive object's gravity — starlight passing close by the edge of the Sun
should be deflected by a small but precisely calculable angle, twice the angle Newton's older theory
of gravity would have predicted if you naively assumed light had a tiny mass and calculated the
deflection the old way. This could only be tested during a total solar eclipse, when the Sun's
overwhelming brightness was blocked long enough to photograph the stars that appeared to lie close
to its edge.

The First World War delayed the test — a German attempt to observe a 1914 eclipse in Crimea was
called off when war broke out and the expedition's members were briefly detained by Russian
authorities as enemy nationals — and it fell, in the end, to a British expedition, in 1919, led by
the astronomer Arthur Eddington, traveling to the island of Príncipe off the coast of West Africa to
photograph a total eclipse and measure exactly how much the stars near the darkened Sun's edge had
shifted from their normal positions. There is a particular, quiet significance to the fact that it
was a British expedition testing a German physicist's theory, barely months after the two countries
had finished tearing each other apart in the deadliest war the world had yet seen; Eddington himself,
a Quaker and a committed pacifist who had faced real professional and legal jeopardy for refusing
conscription during the war, seems to have seen the expedition partly in exactly those terms — a
chance for science to reassert an internationalism the war had spent four years trying to destroy.

The eclipse photographs, developed and analyzed over the following months, showed a deflection
matching Einstein's prediction and not Newton's. When the Royal Society announced the results in
London in November 1919, the news reached far beyond the physics community with a speed and a
cultural force that is genuinely hard to overstate. *The Times* of London ran the headline
"Revolution in Science — New Theory of the Universe — Newtonian Ideas Overthrown." The *New York
Times* ran a headline calling the whole affair "a smash of all previous conceptions of the universe."
Einstein, who had been a respected but comparatively obscure figure among specialists the week
before, woke up world-famous, in a way that had genuinely never happened to a theoretical physicist
before and has arguably never happened to one quite so completely since — his face, with its wild
hair and its mixture of playful and melancholy expression, became one of the most recognized in the
entire world, a level of celebrity he found, by his own repeated and often wryly funny complaints in
letters to friends, considerably stranger and more burdensome than he had ever wanted, joking more
than once that he felt like King Midas, except that everything he touched turned not to gold but to
newspaper clippings.

It is worth being honest, in the spirit this whole book has tried to keep, about a complication in
this triumphant story that the popular retelling usually smooths over. Eddington's 1919 measurements
were, by later and more rigorous statistical analysis, genuinely at the very edge of what his
equipment could reliably distinguish — some of his photographic plates were affected by problems
that required Eddington to make judgment calls about which data to trust, and a purely skeptical
reading of the raw numbers, applying the more careful statistical standards physicists would later
adopt, leaves considerably more room for doubt than the triumphant newspaper headlines suggested at
the time. This does not mean Einstein was wrong; general relativity has since been tested, with
overwhelming and far more precise confirmation, in dozens of independent ways — from the precise
timing of GPS satellites, which must correct for relativistic time dilation every single day to
remain accurate, to the direct detection, a century later, of gravitational waves rippling outward
from colliding black holes, a phenomenon the theory had predicted in 1916 and that instruments would
not exist to actually detect until 2015. It means, rather, that the popular story of a single clean,
decisive eclipse proving Einstein right in one triumphant afternoon is, like Galileo's tower, a
better story than it is a strictly accurate one — and that the real history of how a theory earns
the right to be believed is nearly always slower, messier, and more statistically hedged than the
version that ends up in the newspapers, a pattern this book will meet again, more than once, before
it's done.
