# Chapter 13 — The Great Debate

By 1927, the pieces this book's last three chapters have laid out — Bohr's quantized atom,
Schrödinger's probability wave, Heisenberg's uncertainty principle, Born's statistical
interpretation of what the wave actually meant — had been stitched together, mostly by Bohr and
Heisenberg working together in Copenhagen, into something close to an official party line, which
came to be called the Copenhagen interpretation. Stripped to its plainest statement, it says: a
quantum system genuinely exists in a superposition of multiple possible states at once, right up
until the moment it is measured, at which point the superposition "collapses" — irreversibly,
unpredictably, governed only by probability — into one single, definite, actual outcome. Before the
measurement, there is no hidden fact of the matter about which outcome was "really" going to happen
all along. There is only the probability distribution, and then the measurement, and then one
result, chosen, as far as anyone has ever been able to determine, by nothing but chance.

Albert Einstein — the same man who had, twenty years earlier, been one of the first physicists on
Earth to take Planck's quantum seriously as a real physical fact rather than a mathematical trick,
and who had won his own Nobel Prize specifically for demonstrating that light quanta are real —
hated this conclusion, and spent the last three decades of his life trying, without ever fully
succeeding, to prove it had to be incomplete. His objection was not primarily a physicist's technical
quibble; it was closer to a philosopher's, or a theologian's, and he stated it, in a 1926 letter to
the physicist Max Born, in the single most famous sentence he ever wrote outside of relativity
itself: "Quantum mechanics is certainly imposing. But an inner voice tells me that it is not yet the
real thing. The theory says a lot, but does not really bring us any closer to the secret of the Old
One. I, at any rate, am convinced that He does not throw dice." God does not play dice with the
universe — repeated, paraphrased, and occasionally misquoted for a century since, right up through
this book's own chapter title two chapters ago — became the shorthand for Einstein's whole objection:
he could not accept that the deepest laws governing reality would be, at bottom, genuinely and
irreducibly random, rather than deterministic laws whose apparent randomness merely reflected some
deeper layer of hidden information that physicists simply hadn't found yet.

Niels Bohr's most famous reply, delivered at one of the several conferences where the two men argued
this out in person over the following years, was reportedly: "Einstein, stop telling God what to
do." Whether Bohr actually said exactly those words, in exactly that setting, is one more of this
book's recurring cases of a perfect quote that may be somewhat improved by retelling — but the
substance of the exchange, across a series of legendary confrontations at the Solvay Conferences in
Brussels through the late 1920s and early 1930s, is extremely well documented, because both men were
serious enough about the stakes to write detailed accounts of their own arguments afterward. Einstein
would arrive at breakfast with a new thought experiment, carefully constructed to expose some
apparent internal contradiction or loophole in the uncertainty principle — a clever mechanical
arrangement, in one famous case, involving a box with a shutter operated by a clock, designed to
weigh a photon precisely enough to violate Heisenberg's stated limits. Bohr would spend the day
working through the problem, often in visible distress, and by dinner would generally have found the
flaw in Einstein's setup — in the clock-and-box case, memorably, by pointing out that Einstein's own
general relativity, of all things, implied that the box's change in weight as the photon left it
would shift its position in Earth's gravitational field just enough to introduce exactly the timing
uncertainty needed to save Heisenberg's principle, a small, elegant irony that Bohr reportedly
relished pointing out to the man who had invented the tool being used against him. Round after round,
across nearly a decade, Bohr won essentially every specific technical skirmish. Einstein's deeper
philosophical unease never went away.

It found its most lasting and most scientifically productive form in 1935, in a paper Einstein wrote
with two younger collaborators, Boris Podolsky and Nathan Rosen, now simply called the EPR paper
after the three authors' initials. It proposed a thought experiment — take two particles that have
interacted and become, in the language physicists use, "entangled," then separate them by an
arbitrarily large distance, a mile, a light-year, it doesn't matter — and pointed out something that
seemed, to Einstein, to expose a genuine absurdity at the heart of quantum theory. If you measure a
property of one of the two particles, quantum mechanics predicts that you will instantly know the
corresponding property of the other particle, however far away it now is, with certainty — as though
measuring one particle here has somehow, instantaneously, forced a definite outcome onto its distant
partner there, faster than any signal, even light itself, could possibly have traveled between them.
Either quantum mechanics allowed some kind of "spooky action at a distance" — Einstein's own famous
phrase for it, dripping with the same distaste he reserved for the theory's other implications — that
violated the speed-of-light limit special relativity itself had established, or, Einstein argued, the
particles must actually have possessed definite, predetermined properties all along, hidden from
quantum theory's incomplete mathematical description, fixed at the moment they separated rather than
genuinely undetermined until measured. Quantum mechanics, Einstein concluded, must therefore be
incomplete — a correct but partial description of a deeper, fully deterministic reality that some
future, better theory would eventually uncover, restoring the plain, locally-caused, God-doesn't-
play-dice universe he had never stopped believing in underneath it all.

Niels Bohr's own response to the EPR paper, published within months in the same journal, is by
common agreement among historians of physics one of the least clearly written and most difficult to
parse major papers of his entire career — a fact Bohr's own students and biographers have generally
attributed not to any failure of the underlying physics, which the following decades would
overwhelmingly vindicate, but to the sheer difficulty of stating, in ordinary prose, why Einstein's
apparently airtight logical argument nonetheless failed to establish what it claimed to establish.
Bohr's core reply, distilled from pages of genuinely strained argument, was that Einstein's whole
setup smuggled in an assumption that seemed obviously true from ordinary experience but was not, in
fact, guaranteed by anything in physics itself: that a "measurement" performed on one particle could
be cleanly separated, as a matter of physical description, from the “disturbance” of a system it
was entangled with, however far away. Reality, on Bohr's account, simply did not divide up into
separately real, locally complete pieces the way Einstein's whole argument required it to — a claim
that sounded, in 1935, uncomfortably close to philosophical evasion dressed up as physics, and that
Bell's later theorem and the Aspect experiments would eventually show was, however awkwardly Bohr
himself had managed to state it, the more accurate description of how the universe actually behaves.

For nearly thirty years, the EPR paper was treated by most working physicists as a fascinating but
essentially unresolvable philosophical dispute — a disagreement about the *interpretation* of
quantum mechanics's undisputed mathematics, rather than a question with any actual experimental
answer, since nobody could see how you would ever design an experiment capable of distinguishing
"genuinely random, entangled correlation" from "hidden predetermined properties fixed in advance."
It took until 1964, nearly a decade after Einstein's death, for a physicist named John Stewart Bell,
working largely alone and considered something of an outsider by the mainstream of his field, to
find exactly such an experiment, at least in principle: a precise mathematical inequality, now
called Bell's inequality, that any theory built on Einstein's preferred picture of pre-existing,
locally determined hidden properties would have to obey, and that standard quantum mechanics
predicted would, under the right conditions, actually be violated.

It took nearly another two decades of steadily improving laboratory technique — most famously a
series of experiments run by the French physicist Alain Aspect and his team in the early 1980s,
followed by still more rigorous versions over the following decades that closed every remaining
technical loophole skeptics could raise — to actually go and check. The universe, tested this
specific, precise, previously untestable way, sided with Bohr and against Einstein: Bell's
inequality is, in fact, violated, exactly as standard quantum mechanics predicted, exactly as a
universe built on Einstein's preferred hidden, locally-determined properties said it could never be.
The work earned Alain Aspect, John Clauser, and Anton Zeilinger the 2022 Nobel Prize in Physics,
fifty-eight years after Bell's theoretical breakthrough and eighty-seven years after the original EPR
paper that started the whole argument, for experiments that had, by then, become the working
foundation of an entirely new field, quantum computing and quantum cryptography, that this book will
return to in its final part.

This does not mean the story ended cleanly, with Bohr simply declared the winner and Einstein the
loser, and it is worth resisting that tidy framing, because the actual moral of the whole thirty-year
argument is subtler and considerably more interesting than a scoreboard. Entanglement is real, Bell
proved and Aspect confirmed, beyond any remaining reasonable doubt: measuring one particle really
does instantaneously correlate with the state of its distant partner, in a way no theory of
pre-existing, locally hidden properties can explain. But — and this is the detail that keeps the
strangeness from becoming quite the free-lunch violation of relativity it first appears to be — this
correlation cannot actually be used to send any usable information faster than light; the outcome of
each individual measurement is still, on its own, entirely random, and it is only when the two
distant observers later compare their separate lists of random results, by some ordinary,
light-speed-limited method like a phone call, that the correlation becomes apparent at all. Nature,
it turns out, permits something considerably stranger than either Einstein or Bohr initially framed
the dispute around: a universe that is genuinely, deeply non-local in exactly the way entanglement
requires, while somehow still, at the level of any single measurement anyone can actually use to send
a message, respecting the speed-of-light limit special relativity demands. Both men, pushing as hard
as they each did, had been circling something even stranger than either of their two positions had
quite anticipated — which is, this book would gently suggest, more or less the recurring lesson of
the entire history of physics, and the reason the argument, unresolved in either man's own lifetime,
remains one of the most productive disagreements two human beings have ever had.
