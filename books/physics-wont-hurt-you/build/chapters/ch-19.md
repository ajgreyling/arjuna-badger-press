# Chapter 19 — The Day Without Yesterday

Georges Lemaître, the Belgian priest-physicist who gave the Big Bang its first serious mathematical
foundation, liked to describe the moment of cosmic origin with a phrase he thought captured its
strangeness better than any equation could: "a day without yesterday." It is worth pausing on that
phrase before this chapter moves on to how the theory was actually confirmed, because it points at
something genuinely disorienting that this book has not yet fully sat with: if the Big Bang theory
is right, then the question "what happened before the Big Bang?" is not simply a question science
hasn't answered yet. It may not, physicists increasingly suspect, be a coherent question at all —
because time itself, along with space, appears to be part of what came into existence at that
moment, meaning "before" may be as meaningless a phrase here as "north of the North Pole." This book
will sit with that particular vertigo more carefully in Part Five. For now, the more pressing
question two rival camps of mid-century physicists were actually fighting over was simpler and more
answerable: did the universe have a beginning at all, or has it always existed in more or less its
current form, the way Fred Hoyle's steady-state theory insisted?

The physicist who worked out what a definitive answer would actually look like was George Gamow, a
Russian-born physicist who had defected from the Soviet Union in the early 1930s (his escape attempts
are, on their own, a small adventure story — a failed attempt to kayak across the Black Sea to
Turkey, followed eventually by a successful defection during an international physics conference)
and who took Lemaître's primeval-atom idea and pushed it further, working out, with two younger
collaborators, Ralph Alpher and Robert Herman, what the universe's extremely early, extremely hot
conditions would actually imply. If the universe truly began in an unimaginably hot, dense state,
Gamow, Alpher, and Herman reasoned in the late 1940s, then that primordial heat should not have
simply vanished as the universe expanded and cooled over the following billions of years. It should
still be detectable today, everywhere, uniformly, as a faint, cooled-down bath of microwave radiation
permeating literally the entire universe — the afterglow, stretched by billions of years of cosmic
expansion into a very specific, calculable, low temperature, of the universe's own birth. They even
calculated roughly what that temperature ought to be: a few degrees above absolute zero.

Nobody went looking for it for nearly two decades — the prediction sat in physics journals largely
unnoticed by the wider astronomical community, one more entry in this book's recurring pattern of a
bold, checkable prediction made years or decades before anyone had the technical means, or in this
case simply the institutional attention, to actually check it. It was found, in the end, entirely by
accident, in 1964, by two radio astronomers at Bell Labs in New Jersey, Arno Penzias and Robert
Wilson, who were not looking for cosmic afterglow at all. They were trying to calibrate an extremely
sensitive radio antenna, originally built for satellite communication research, and kept finding an
annoying, persistent, low-level hiss of microwave static in their data that they could not get rid
of no matter what they tried. They checked every possible source of contamination they could think
of: they ruled out interference from nearby New York City; they climbed inside the antenna itself and
found, and carefully cleaned out, a pair of pigeons who had nested there and left behind what Penzias
later described, with the dry understatement of a man recalling a genuinely unpleasant afternoon of
work, as "a white dielectric material" — pigeon droppings, in plainer language, which they
scrupulously removed on the theory that it might be causing the interference. The hiss remained,
exactly the same, from every direction they pointed the antenna, at every time of day and every
season of the year, with a temperature almost exactly matching the few-degrees-above-absolute-zero
figure Gamow's team had calculated seventeen years earlier and that neither Penzias nor Wilson had
any idea existed.

There is a small, almost comic postscript to the pigeon story worth including here, because it says
something true about how unglamorous a genuine scientific breakthrough often looks from the inside
while it's actually happening: Penzias and Wilson, having failed to make the pigeons' mess or
anything else account for the hiss, seriously considered, for a period of some weeks, that the
antenna itself might simply be faulty, and continued their painstaking calibration work with no
sense whatsoever that they were sitting on what would become a Nobel Prize. Robert Dicke's rival
Princeton team, by contrast, had spent months building an instrument specifically hoping to find
exactly this signal and had not yet finished it when the phone call came — a reminder that being
first is sometimes less a matter of being cleverest than of simply already having, quite by
accident, the right piece of equipment pointed in the right direction when the universe happens to
answer.

A colleague eventually put the two Bell Labs engineers in touch with a team of cosmologists at
nearby Princeton University, led by Robert Dicke, who were, at that very moment, independently
building their own instrument specifically to search for exactly this predicted cosmic microwave
background radiation, having rediscovered Gamow's old, largely forgotten prediction on their own.
Dicke's team had not yet finished building their detector when Penzias and Wilson called; upon
hearing the description of the mystery hiss, Dicke is reported to have turned to his own team and
said, with a mixture of scientific excitement and entirely understandable professional
disappointment, "Boys, we've been scooped." The two groups published companion papers in 1965 —
Penzias and Wilson reporting the accidental discovery with careful, deliberately modest scientific
restraint, not even using the word "cosmology" in their own paper's title, and Dicke's Princeton
team, in the companion paper, providing the theoretical explanation of exactly what the two radio
astronomers had unknowingly found.

The cosmic microwave background is, without much serious competition, the single most compelling
piece of physical evidence ever obtained for the Big Bang, and it is worth being precise about why it
settles the argument so completely. It is not merely consistent with the Big Bang theory; the theory
had predicted its existence, its near-perfect uniformity in every direction, and even, roughly, its
specific temperature, nearly two decades in advance of anyone finding it. Fred Hoyle's rival
steady-state theory, by contrast, had no comparable mechanism to produce this uniform background
radiation at all, and the theory never recovered from the discovery, fading over the following years
from a genuinely serious scientific rival into a minority position held by an ever-shrinking handful
of holdouts, Hoyle himself prominent and unrepentant among them until his death in 2001. Penzias and
Wilson received the 1978 Nobel Prize in Physics for a discovery they had, by their own honest and
often-repeated account, initially regarded as an annoying engineering nuisance to be cleaned away
along with the pigeon droppings, rather than the single most important confirmation of the origin of
everything.

Later, far more precise measurements of the cosmic microwave background — first by NASA's COBE
satellite in the early 1990s, whose principal investigators John Mather and George Smoot received
their own Nobel Prize in 2006, and then by successively sharper instruments including the WMAP and
Planck space probes over the following two decades — revealed something even richer hidden inside
what had first appeared to Penzias and Wilson as a simple, featureless hiss: the background radiation
is not perfectly, exactly uniform after all. It contains extremely faint, precisely mapped
temperature variations, differing from point to point across the sky by only a few parts in a
hundred thousand, and those tiny variations turn out to be nothing less than the fossilized
fingerprint of the very first structures in the infant universe — the slight, quantum-mechanical
density fluctuations, present within the first fraction of a second after the Big Bang, that
gravity would spend the following billions of years amplifying into the galaxies, galaxy clusters,
and the vast cosmic web of structure that makes up everything astronomers observe in the sky today.
The universe's baby picture, in other words, taken not with a camera but with a radio antenna
originally built to bounce satellite signals off, still faintly showing, in the tiniest possible
variations of temperature, the seeds of every galaxy that would ever form — including, roughly
thirteen and a half billion years later, the one currently holding the solar system, the planet, and
the particular arrangement of atoms reading this sentence.
