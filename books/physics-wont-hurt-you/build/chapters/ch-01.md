# Chapter 1 — Falling Apples and Stubborn Priests

Before there was physics, there was a nineteen-year-old medical student in a cathedral in Pisa,
supposed to be paying attention to the sermon, watching a bronze chandelier swing on its chain
after a draft caught it, and timing the swings against his own pulse because he didn't own a watch
and hadn't yet worked out a better method. He noticed something that should not, on the face of
it, have been noticeable at all: as the swings got smaller and smaller, dying down toward
stillness, each one seemed to take the same amount of time as the last. A big swing and a small
swing, same duration. That is not an obviously true fact about the world. Most people, watching a
pendulum wind down, would tell you the swings are getting faster, because they're getting shorter
and more frantic-looking near the end. Galileo Galilei watched the same chandelier and, using
nothing but his own heartbeat as a clock, worked out that they weren't.

This is as good a place as any to start a history of physics, not because it was the first
observation of its kind — plenty of people before Galileo had noticed plenty of things — but
because of what he did next, which was the actually revolutionary part: he didn't just notice it,
write a poem about the harmony of God's creation, and move on. He went home and built a pendulum
of his own and tested it. He was not content to have seen a true thing about the world. He wanted
to know whether it stayed true when he pushed on it, changed the length of the chain, changed the
weight on the end, tried to break it. This turns out to be the entire difference between natural
philosophy, which had been going on for roughly two thousand years by the time Galileo showed up,
and physics, which had not yet been invented and which he is as responsible as any single human
being for inventing: the belief that a claim about the world is worth nothing until you have gone
and checked it against the world itself, repeatedly, on purpose, and written down what actually
happened even when it embarrassed someone important.

Galileo was born in Pisa in 1564, the same year, for what it's worth, that Shakespeare was born and
Michelangelo died, which is the kind of coincidence a book like this one is contractually obligated
to point out at least once. His father was a musician and music theorist who had his own minor
rebellion against received authority — he'd run actual experiments on strings and tensions to
disprove the ancient Greek theory of musical harmony that everyone had simply been repeating for
two millennia, and it is not hard to guess where his son got the habit of not simply repeating
things. Galileo trained in medicine because that's what his father wanted, discovered he liked
mathematics considerably more, and switched, over his father's understandable objections, in a
scene that will feel familiar to anyone who has ever disappointed a parent by choosing a subject
with worse job prospects.

He supported himself in these early years, before any university post secured his finances, partly
by giving private mathematics tutoring and partly by a genuine talent for practical invention that
this book's later chapters will show turning up again and again in physics's history as a kind of
recurring family trait: he designed and sold a military compass useful for aiming artillery and
calculating fortification angles, and, decades later, a thermoscope — a crude ancestor of the
thermometer, using the expansion and contraction of air in a sealed tube to give a rough visual
sense of temperature change, though without yet the fixed numerical scale a true thermometer would
need. Neither device made him rich. Both reveal a mind that could not resist turning an abstract
question into something you could actually hold, calibrate, and check against the world — the same
impulse that would, a few years later, send him rolling bronze balls down a wooden ramp rather than
simply arguing about Aristotle in a lecture hall.

The falling-tower story — Galileo dropping two balls of different weights off the Leaning Tower of
Pisa to show they'd hit the ground at the same time, disproving nearly two thousand years of
Aristotle's physics in one satisfying public thud — is probably not literally true, or at least
not verifiably true; the earliest account of it comes from Galileo's own assistant, writing after
Galileo's death, and no contemporary record confirms it happened exactly that way. It doesn't
matter, and the reason it doesn't matter is itself the whole point of this chapter. Galileo didn't
need the tower, because he'd already worked out the same result more carefully and more cleverly,
using an experiment that has the added virtue of being both real and considerably better science:
he rolled balls down a gently sloped, grooved wooden ramp, and timed them.

Here is why the ramp was smarter than a tower would ever have been. Aristotle's physics — the
received wisdom of the previous two thousand years, taught with total confidence in every European
university, including the one Galileo attended — held that heavier objects fall faster than light
ones, in direct proportion to their weight, because heaviness was understood as a kind of eagerness
to return to its natural place at the center of the universe. It is a perfectly sensible-sounding
idea if you have never actually measured anything, and almost nobody, for two thousand years, had
thought to. A cannonball dropped off a tower falls fast enough that the human eye, and certainly
the human pulse used as a stopwatch, cannot reliably tell whether it beat a musket ball to the
ground by a tenth of a second or a whole second or not at all. A tower is not a good laboratory. It
is a good story, which is a different thing, and the fact that history remembers the tower and not
the ramp tells you something true and slightly sad about which one is more fun to repeat at a
dinner party.

The ramp, tilted gently enough, slowed everything down to a speed Galileo could actually measure
with the tools of 1604 — his own pulse, later a water clock he built specifically for the purpose,
marking the passage of time by the weight of water collected in a container. He rolled a bronze
ball down the groove, over and over, at different angles of tilt, and found — carefully, patiently,
the same experiment run again and again until he trusted the pattern — that the distance the ball
covered was proportional to the *square* of the time elapsed. Not to the time itself. To the time,
squared. This is the kind of fact that looks like nothing on the page and is actually the hinge the
entire modern world turns on: it meant that falling — and, by extension he would work out over the
following decades, all motion under a constant force — followed a mathematical law, precise and
unforgiving and entirely indifferent to what Aristotle, the Church, or anyone's intuition thought
it ought to do.

And crucially: the weight of the ball didn't matter. A heavy bronze ball and a light wooden one,
rolled down the same ramp, covered the same distance in the same time. Two thousand years of
received physics, quietly and without a single dramatic public confrontation, turned out to be
simply, checkably wrong — and the checking was the whole revolution. Aristotle had reasoned from
first principles about what heavy and light things ought to do. Galileo built a ramp and watched
what they actually did. The gap between those two sentences is, more or less, the entire subject of
this book.

None of this made him popular with the people whose authority depended on Aristotle having been
right, which in early-seventeenth-century Italy included essentially the entire intellectual and
religious establishment. And Galileo, it must be said in the interest of the honest character
portrait this book promised, made the conflict considerably worse than it strictly needed to be, by
being — there is no more diplomatic way to put this — a genuinely difficult man to have as an
intellectual opponent. He was witty in the specific way that leaves scars. When he turned his
telescope on the sky and saw mountains on the Moon, four moons orbiting Jupiter that were plainly
not orbiting the Earth, and the phases of Venus that only made sense if Venus orbited the Sun and
not the Earth, he did not present these findings modestly. He wrote a book, *Dialogue Concerning
the Two Chief World Systems*, structured as a conversation between three characters — a clever
defender of the new Copernican, Sun-centered view; an open-minded questioner; and a not-very-bright
defender of the old Earth-centered view, whom Galileo named Simplicio, a name that means roughly
"simple-minded" in Italian and that Galileo's enemies at the Vatican quickly noticed bore an
uncomfortable resemblance, in his arguments if not his portrait, to the Pope who had personally
authorized the book's publication on the understanding that it would present both views fairly.

It did not present both views fairly. The trial that followed, in 1633, before the Roman
Inquisition, is one of the most famous confrontations between institutional authority and empirical
observation in the whole of Western history, and it is worth being precise about what actually
happened, because the popular version — brave scientist versus superstitious Church — flattens a
messier and more interesting truth. Galileo was not tried for believing the Earth moved; plenty of
churchmen, including some of his own friends inside the Vatican, quietly suspected it did too. He
was tried, in large part, for publishing a book that made the reigning Pope look like a fool in
front of all of Europe, at a moment when the Church, embattled by the Protestant Reformation and
desperate to project unwavering authority, could not afford to be seen backing down to a
mathematician. He was found guilty of "vehement suspicion of heresy," forced to publicly recant his
support for a Sun-centered universe on his knees, and spent the rest of his life — he lived another
nine years — under house arrest at his villa outside Florence, forbidden to publish, visited in
secret by admirers including, near the very end, a young Englishman named John Milton, who would
go on to write *Paradise Lost* and who described meeting the old, blind Galileo as one of the
formative encounters of his life.

He did not stop working. Under house arrest, blind in one eye and then both, Galileo dictated his
final and in some ways greatest book, *Two New Sciences*, smuggled out of Italy and published in
the Protestant Netherlands where the Inquisition's authority did not reach. It laid out the physics
of motion and of the strength of materials — the science of falling bodies, projectiles, the
mathematics of the ramp experiments, refined and generalized — in a form so complete and so
rigorous that it would still be the foundation Newton built on, fifty years later, when he set out
to explain not just how things fall but why.

The line usually attributed to Galileo at his recantation — muttering under his breath as he rose
from his knees, "*Eppur si muove*," "and yet it moves" — almost certainly never happened; no
contemporary source records it, and it first appears in print more than a century after his death.
It has survived anyway, told and retold for four hundred years, because it captures something true
even though the specific moment is invented: the plain, stubborn, checkable fact of the world does
not actually care whether an institution has forced you to recant it. The Earth kept moving.
Objects kept falling at the same rate regardless of weight. Galileo's own experiments — verified,
repeated, extended by everyone who came after him — kept being true, whatever the Inquisition's
official record said about it, and that gap between what an authority insists is true and what the
universe will actually show you if you build the ramp and run the experiment is the crack this
whole book crawls through, from a cathedral in Pisa in 1583 to a machine under the Swiss Alps four
hundred and some years later.

The Catholic Church did not formally acknowledge that Galileo had been right, and that the Earth
does in fact orbit the Sun, until 1992 — three hundred and fifty-nine years after the trial, in a
statement by Pope John Paul II expressing regret for how the case had been handled. Physics, for
its part, had not waited around for the apology. Within a hundred years of Galileo's death, an
Englishman named Isaac Newton would take the ramp experiments, the laws of falling bodies, and the
stubborn, checkable habit of mind that produced them, and use it to explain not just why an apple
falls, but why the Moon does not.
