# Chapter 20 — Black Holes and the Information Paradox

Karl Schwarzschild found the first exact mathematical solution to Einstein's general relativity
equations in 1915, within weeks of Einstein publishing the final theory itself, while serving as an
artillery officer on the Russian front during the First World War, calculating in his spare time
between duty shifts and mailing the result back to Einstein by military post. He would be dead
within the year, of a rare autoimmune skin disease contracted at the front, but his solution — a
precise description of the curved spacetime surrounding a perfectly spherical mass — contained,
buried inside its mathematics, an implication so strange that Einstein himself, and most physicists
for the following four decades, assumed it had to be a mathematical curiosity with no real physical
counterpart: if you compressed a large enough mass into a small enough radius, the equations said,
the curvature of space around it would become so extreme that nothing, not even light itself, could
travel fast enough to escape from within a certain boundary. Space and time, inside that boundary,
would become so warped that every possible direction, forward in time as well as through space,
would lead only further inward.

For decades this was treated, by nearly everyone including Einstein, as a mathematical pathology —
an artifact of the equations rather than a description of something that could actually exist,
partly because no known physical process seemed capable of compressing matter that extremely. That
changed as astrophysicists worked out, through the middle decades of the twentieth century, what
actually happens to a sufficiently massive star once it exhausts the nuclear fuel that has been
holding it up against its own gravity for millions of years: without the outward pressure of ongoing
fusion to counteract it, the star's own immense gravity has nothing left to resist it, and the
core collapses, in a matter of seconds, into an object of almost unimaginable density — and if the
original star was massive enough, that collapse does not stop at any of the intermediate,
extraordinarily dense but still finite states physicists had already identified (a white dwarf, or a
still denser neutron star, an object so compact that a teaspoon of its material would weigh billions
of tons). It keeps collapsing, without limit, straight through Schwarzschild's mathematical boundary
and into exactly the object his 1915 equations had described: what the American physicist John
Wheeler, in 1967, gave the vivid, instantly memorable name that stuck permanently in both physics and
popular culture — a black hole. Direct observational confirmation that these objects were not merely
theoretical curiosities took decades longer still to arrive in fully unambiguous form: astronomers
spent the latter half of the twentieth century building an overwhelming circumstantial case,
tracking stars and gas whipping around invisible, compact masses far too heavy to be anything else,
before the Event Horizon Telescope collaboration — a global network of radio observatories linked
together to function, in effect, as a single telescope the size of the Earth itself — finally
produced, in 2019, the first true image of a black hole's silhouette: a dark shadow ringed by a
glowing halo of infalling matter, sitting at the center of the galaxy M87, fifty-five million
light-years away. The 2020 Nobel Prize in Physics went jointly to Roger Penrose, for the mathematical
proof, developed with Hawking in the 1960s, that general relativity's equations robustly predict
black holes rather than merely permitting them under special idealized conditions, and to Reinhard
Genzel and Andrea Ghez, for two decades of painstaking observation tracking individual stars looping
around the supermassive black hole at the very center of our own Milky Way — proof, watched directly
rather than merely inferred, that one of these objects, four million times the mass of the Sun, sits
quietly at the middle of the very galaxy this book's own Chapter 18 spent so long establishing was
not, after all, the whole of the universe.

Stephen Hawking, who is unquestionably the single physicist most responsible for making black holes
a subject the general public came to find genuinely fascinating rather than merely exotic, deserves
his own honest introduction here, because his story carries a weight that the cheerful, wheelchair-
bound-genius popular image sometimes flattens. He was diagnosed, at twenty-one, with amyotrophic
lateral sclerosis, a progressive and at the time almost universally fatal motor neuron disease, and
given, by his doctors, roughly two years to live. He lived, instead, for another fifty-five years,
gradually losing nearly all voluntary muscle control, eventually communicating only through a speech-
generating computer controlled, in his final years, by the twitch of a single cheek muscle — and
doing, across those five and a half decades of steadily advancing paralysis, some of the most
important theoretical work on black holes that anyone has ever produced. This book has already, in
earlier chapters, resisted the temptation to turn genuine hardship into cheap inspirational
shorthand, and it will resist it here too: Hawking himself, in interviews throughout his life, pushed
back firmly against being treated primarily as an inspirational disability icon rather than a
scientist, insisting, with the same dry, characteristic wit that marked all of his public writing,
that his illness had cost him things — mobility, privacy, the physical ease of ordinary life — that
no triumphant narrative should be allowed to paper over, even as it left his mind, by every account of
the people who worked alongside him, undiminished and in some ways sharpened by the discipline of
having to think a problem through completely before dictating it, one laborious word at a time.

Hawking's single most important and most genuinely strange contribution to physics, worked out in
1974, took a subject that had, by that point, become associated almost entirely with the idea of
total, irreversible cosmic finality — nothing, famously, not even light, escapes a black hole — and
found a hole in that very certainty, using exactly the kind of quantum reasoning this book's earlier
chapters spent so long establishing. Hawking applied quantum field theory to the region right at a
black hole's boundary, its event horizon, and found that the intense, warped spacetime there should
constantly be producing pairs of particles and antiparticles out of the vacuum itself — a genuinely
quantum phenomenon, related to the same uncertainty this book met in Heisenberg's chapter, that
happens throughout empty space all the time but normally cancels itself out too quickly to matter.
At a black hole's horizon, Hawking showed, occasionally one particle of such a pair falls into the
hole while its partner escapes outward, carrying real, measurable energy away with it — meaning that
a black hole, against every previous assumption of total, permanent cosmic imprisonment, should
actually, very slowly, be radiating energy away and losing mass, evaporating, over spans of time so
vast for any realistically sized black hole that the number is difficult to state in any way that
feels genuinely meaningful to a human mind — but finite, and calculable, and, given long enough,
complete. This radiation, now called Hawking radiation, has never yet been directly detected — for a
black hole of stellar mass, the predicted radiation is fantastically fainter than the cosmic
microwave background this book's previous chapter spent so long establishing, and the predicted
evaporation time vastly exceeds the current age of the universe itself — but the theoretical
calculation behind it is considered, by the overwhelming consensus of the field, essentially solid,
one of the very few places where quantum mechanics and general relativity, the two great and
famously incompatible pillars of twentieth-century physics, can be made to talk to each other at all
and produce a definite, specific answer.

That answer, though, opened up one of the genuinely unresolved puzzles this book will carry forward
into Part Five: the black hole information paradox. Quantum mechanics, as this book's earlier
chapters on Schrödinger and Heisenberg established, has one absolutely non-negotiable rule running
underneath all of its strangeness and probability: information is never truly destroyed. Whatever
happens to a quantum system, however scrambled or randomized it becomes, the complete information
describing its original state is, in principle, always still there, recoverable if you could somehow
track every last detail of the process. But if a black hole slowly evaporates via Hawking radiation
and eventually vanishes completely, and if that outgoing radiation, as Hawking's own original
calculation suggested, carries no actual information about whatever specific matter originally fell
into the hole — just a featureless, random thermal glow, indistinguishable whether the hole had
swallowed a star, a spacecraft, or this very book — then it appears that real information has been
permanently, irrecoverably destroyed, in direct violation of one of quantum mechanics's most basic
and best-tested principles. Something has to give: either black holes really do destroy information,
meaning quantum mechanics itself is subtly incomplete in a way nobody has otherwise found any
evidence for, or Hawking radiation is secretly, in some way physicists have not yet fully worked out
how to calculate, carrying the missing information back out after all, encoded in correlations subtle
enough that Hawking's original approximate calculation simply missed them.

Hawking himself, in a move that generated its own small physics-world drama, publicly conceded in
2004, at a conference in Dublin, that he had likely been wrong on this specific point for thirty
years, and that information probably does escape after all — paying off a long-standing public bet
he'd made with fellow physicist John Preskill by presenting Preskill with an encyclopedia, "from
which information can be recovered at will," a characteristically wry Hawking touch even in
professional defeat. The precise mechanism by which the information actually gets out remains,
decades later, one of the most actively worked-on open problems at the true frontier of theoretical
physics, tied up closely with the attempts, examined in Part Five, to find a full theory of quantum
gravity capable of describing what actually happens inside a black hole's boundary rather than merely
approximating the physics just outside it.

Hawking died in March 2018, at seventy-six, having outlived his original two-year prognosis by more
than half a century, having written the best-selling popular physics book of all time in *A Brief
History of Time*, and having spent his final working decades circling, without ever fully catching,
the exact question this chapter has just laid out: what a black hole actually does to information,
and by extension, to the future of anything and everything that has the misfortune to fall inside
one. His ashes were interred at Westminster Abbey, between the graves of Isaac Newton and Charles
Darwin — a fittingly literal final resting place for a man whose own life's work sat, in its way,
directly between the two: the precise, mathematical universe Newton had first shown could be
calculated, and the deep uncertainty about what that universe ultimately, permanently keeps or loses,
that Darwin's own century could not yet have imagined asking.
