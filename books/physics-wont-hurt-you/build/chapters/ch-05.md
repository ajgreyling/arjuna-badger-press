# Chapter 5 — The Bookkeeping of the Universe

While Maxwell was busy proving that electricity, magnetism, and light were secretly one thing, a
parallel and in some ways even stranger revolution was happening in a much less glamorous corner of
physics: the study of steam engines, and specifically the study of why they never seem to work
quite as well as the mathematics says they ought to.

This sounds like an unpromising place to find one of the deepest truths in the entire universe, and
that is precisely why it's such a good place to find it. Physics, this book keeps insisting, is
what happens when someone takes a plain, unglamorous, practical question seriously enough to follow
it all the way down, and the plain, unglamorous, practical question in this case was: an engineer
named Sadi Carnot, working in France in the 1820s, wanted to know the theoretical maximum
efficiency a steam engine could ever achieve — not the best engine anyone had actually built, but
the best engine physics itself would ever permit, no matter how clever the engineering got. It is
the kind of question a patent office might ask, and the answer turned out to require rewriting the
rules of the universe.

Carnot's own life carries a small, sharp irony this book cannot pass over: he died in 1832, at
thirty-six, in a cholera epidemic sweeping Paris, and the strict quarantine protocols of the era
required that most of his personal effects, including a large number of his private papers, be
burned to prevent the disease's spread — meaning the man who worked out the theoretical limits on how
efficiently heat can ever be converted to useful work left behind a body of writing considerably
thinner than the size of his actual contribution, most of it inferred and reconstructed by later
physicists from the single memoir he had published nine years before his death rather than from any
fuller personal archive.

The rewriting is called thermodynamics, and its two central laws are, on the page, disarmingly
simple. The first law says that energy cannot be created or destroyed, only converted from one form
to another — heat into motion, motion into electricity, electricity into light — a strict cosmic
accounting rule with no exceptions and no overdrafts permitted, ever, anywhere. This one, though
profound, is not the strange part; it is, essentially, a grander version of the bookkeeping Newton
had already implied with his laws of motion. The strange part is the second law, and it says
something that Newton's clean, reversible, clockwork universe had given absolutely no warning was
coming: that in any real process, some energy is always, unavoidably, converted into a form that
can never fully be converted back — specifically, into the random, disordered jostling of atoms we
experience as heat spreading out and cooling down — and that this one-way conversion has a
direction. It only runs one way. A hot cup of coffee left on a table cools down to room temperature.
A room-temperature cup of coffee, left on the same table for a billion years, never spontaneously
gathers its own heat back together and starts steaming again, even though nothing in Newton's laws
of motion — reversible, symmetric, indifferent to the direction of time — technically forbids it.
Something else is forbidding it. The second law of thermodynamics is the name for that something.

Physicists gave this one-way tendency a name — entropy — and defined it, at first, in a purely
practical, engineering sense: a measure of how much useful, ordered energy has been irreversibly
lost to disordered heat in any given process. Entropy, the second law said, never decreases in a
closed system. It only ever increases, or at best, in some perfectly frictionless idealized case
that never actually happens in the real world, stays the same. Every real engine, every real
process, every real thing that has ever happened anywhere in the universe, has nudged the total
entropy of everything a little bit higher, and can never nudge it back down. This is, among many
other things it turns out to explain, the reason time seems to have a direction at all — the reason
you remember yesterday and not tomorrow, the reason a shattered glass never spontaneously reassembles
itself off the floor, the reason, ultimately, that the universe itself, incomprehensibly far in the
future, is expected to wind down into a maximally disordered, uniform, lukewarm nothing that
physicists call, with a certain grim professional restraint, "heat death." Newton's universe ran
like a clock, forward or backward, the equations not caring which way time pointed. Thermodynamics's
universe runs like a story: it only goes one way, and it is, very slowly, ending.

The man who explained *why* entropy behaves this way — not just that it does, which Carnot and
others had already established through the unglamorous business of steam-engine efficiency, but the
actual underlying mechanism — was an Austrian physicist named Ludwig Boltzmann, and his answer is
one of the most beautiful ideas in the whole of physics, and it cost him, by the most widely
accepted account of historians of science, his life.

Boltzmann's insight was to ask what heat actually *is*, at the smallest scale, at a time when many
serious physicists and philosophers of science still doubted that atoms were real objects at all
rather than a convenient mathematical fiction. Boltzmann insisted they were real — a gas, he argued,
is not a smooth, continuous substance but an almost unimaginably large number of individual
molecules, zooming around, colliding, bouncing off the walls of their container, and "heat" is
simply our large-scale name for the average energy of all that microscopic, chaotic motion. From
this one assumption, Boltzmann built statistical mechanics: the mathematics of what enormous
numbers of randomly moving particles will, on average, do, using the same kind of probability
Faraday would never have had the training to do but that Boltzmann, decades later, folded into
physics's actual foundations.

And from statistical mechanics, entropy stopped being a mysterious one-way arrow bolted onto
Newton's clockwork from the outside, and became something almost embarrassingly simple: entropy, in
Boltzmann's picture, is just a measure of how many different microscopic arrangements of atoms
correspond to the same macroscopic situation. A tidy room has very few possible arrangements of its
contents that would still look "tidy" to you — the books on the shelf, the clothes in the drawer.
A messy room has an almost incomprehensibly larger number of possible arrangements that would all
look "messy" — books anywhere, clothes everywhere, in any of a near-infinite number of specific
configurations. There is nothing in the laws of physics that forbids a messy room from spontaneously
tidying itself; it is simply, overwhelmingly, statistically unlikely, because there are vastly more
ways to be messy than to be tidy, and left alone, a system will drift, minute by minute, collision
by collision, toward whichever state has the most ways of happening. Entropy increasing is not a
mysterious cosmic force pushing the universe toward disorder. It is simply what you'd expect to
happen, on average, in a universe with an astronomical number of atoms bumping into each other at
random, for the same plain statistical reason a shuffled deck of cards is enormously more likely to
come up in a jumbled order than in perfect suit-and-rank sequence — not because shuffling has a
grudge against order, but because there is only one way to be perfectly ordered and there are
trillions upon trillions of ways to be jumbled, and random chance will, essentially every single
time, land you somewhere in that overwhelming majority.

This was, and to a real extent still is, a genuinely difficult idea for people to accept, because it
means the second law of thermodynamics is not an absolute law in the way Newton's laws of motion
are absolute laws — it is a *statistical* law, true not because it is forbidden to be otherwise but
because it is so staggeringly improbable to be otherwise that it might as well be forbidden. A
shattered glass could, in principle, spontaneously reassemble itself. The odds against it are so
large that you would need to wait many, many times longer than the current age of the universe,
watching an inconceivable number of shattered glasses, to have any realistic chance of seeing it
happen even once. Boltzmann's atoms and his statistics did not just explain heat. They quietly
smuggled probability itself into the bedrock of physics, decades before quantum mechanics would
make probability unavoidable at an even deeper level — and they did it against ferocious
resistance from senior physicists and philosophers who considered the very existence of atoms
scientifically unproven and Boltzmann's whole statistical approach a needless, unrigorous
complication.

The resistance was not gentle, and it was not brief. Boltzmann spent decades of his career
defending the atomic hypothesis against colleagues, some of them figures of real authority in
German-language physics and philosophy of science, who regarded his life's work as speculative at
best and simply wrong at worst. He suffered from what would today likely be diagnosed as bipolar
disorder, cycling between periods of tremendous productivity and periods of deep despair, made
considerably worse, by his own account in letters to friends, by the sense that his life's central
achievement was being dismissed by the field he'd given it to. In September 1906, while on holiday
with his wife and daughter near Trieste, Ludwig Boltzmann hanged himself in his hotel room while his
family was out swimming. He was sixty-two. The most widely accepted account among historians of
science attributes his death, at least in significant part, to this despair over the reception of
his work — though as with any suicide, the full truth of a mind at its darkest moment can never be
fully reconstructed from letters alone, and mental illness, not merely professional rejection, was
plainly also at work.

Within a year of his death, the atomic hypothesis he had spent his career defending was
conclusively vindicated. Albert Einstein — who appears properly in the next chapter of this book,
but who makes an early, quiet appearance here — published a paper in 1905 explaining Brownian
motion, the visible, jittery, random dance of tiny pollen grains suspended in water, first observed
under a microscope decades earlier by a botanist who had no idea what caused it. Einstein showed,
using Boltzmann's own statistical methods, that the jitter was caused by exactly the mechanism
Boltzmann had proposed: invisible water molecules, constantly and randomly colliding with the much
larger pollen grain from all sides, occasionally unbalanced enough in one direction to give it a
visible nudge. It was, in effect, direct experimental proof that atoms were real, individual,
countable objects, doing exactly what Boltzmann's statistics said they should do — arriving one
year too late for the man who had given his sanity and, in the end, his life to being right about
them before anyone would believe him.

Boltzmann's tombstone in Vienna's Central Cemetery carries a single equation, chosen after his
death by physicists who understood exactly what it meant even if the general public walking past it
never would: *S = k log W*. Entropy equals a constant, multiplied by the logarithm of the number of
possible microscopic arrangements. It is one of the shortest epitaphs of any major figure in this
book, and very possibly the truest one: a plain, checkable, statistical fact about how atoms behave
when nobody's looking, carved in stone over a man the field he founded could not, in his own
lifetime, quite manage to believe.
