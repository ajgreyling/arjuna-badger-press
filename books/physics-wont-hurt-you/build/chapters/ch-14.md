# Chapter 14 — Spin, Antimatter, and a Beautiful Equation

Paul Dirac said, by most careful counts, fewer words per day than almost anyone else who has ever
appeared in a physics textbook, and the words he did say were legendary, among the people who worked
with him, for a kind of literal-minded precision that reads today like something from a description
of autism, though no formal diagnosis was ever made in his lifetime and modern biographers and
some physicians who have studied the historical record are careful to note the limits of retrospective
diagnosis. Colleagues at Cambridge invented a unit, only half-jokingly, called "the dirac," defined
as one word per hour, to describe how little he said in ordinary conversation. Asked, at the end of
a public lecture, whether he had understood a question from the audience, he reportedly answered,
"Yes" — and then simply stood there, in total silence, until the moderator was forced to point out
that a spoken answer to the actual question would also be appreciated; Dirac, entirely literally,
had answered the question he'd actually been asked, which was whether he understood, not what the
answer was.

None of this is included to mock him. It's included because Dirac's peculiar, almost inhumanly
literal cast of mind turns out to be inseparable from the single strangest and most successful piece
of pure theoretical reasoning in this entire book — a discovery made not by running an experiment,
not by carefully massaging a stubborn piece of data the way Planck had, but by simply following an
equation's own internal mathematical logic wherever it insisted on going, past the point where any
less literal-minded physicist would have stopped to ask whether the destination made any physical
sense at all.

The problem Dirac set himself, in 1928, was to combine Schrödinger's quantum wave equation with
Einstein's special relativity — Schrödinger's original equation, elegant as it was, had been built
without relativity in mind, and did not correctly handle particles moving at speeds anywhere close
to the speed of light, an obvious gap given how central relativity had already become to the rest of
physics. Dirac found an equation that did the job, matching relativity's demands while still
correctly describing the quantum behavior of the electron — and in the process, discovered something
he had not gone looking for and did not, at first, know what to make of: his equation, worked through
honestly and without fudging, had two solutions for every electron, not one. One solution described
an ordinary electron, negatively charged, with a certain positive amount of energy, exactly as
expected. The other solution described what looked, mathematically, like an electron with *negative*
energy — a result that, taken at face value, appeared to be nonsense, since a particle with less
than zero energy ought to be able to fall to lower and lower negative energies forever, radiating
away an infinite amount of energy in the process, which is obviously not what real electrons do.

Most physicists confronted with an equation producing an apparently absurd extra solution would
assume the equation itself was flawed, patch it, or simply discard the unwanted solution as a
mathematical artifact with no physical meaning — the equivalent of solving for the length of a
rectangle's side and getting a negative number back, and simply throwing the negative answer away
because a length obviously can't be negative. Dirac, with the same literal, almost stubborn
insistence on taking mathematics at its word that made him such peculiar company at dinner, refused
to throw the answer away. If the equation said there was a second kind of particle implied by the
mathematics, Dirac reasoned, then physics needed to find out what that particle actually was, rather
than simply erasing the inconvenient half of its own best theory. After a period of genuine
uncertainty — his first proposal, that the negative-energy solutions might somehow correspond to the
already-known proton, quickly ran into insurmountable technical problems — Dirac committed, by 1931,
to the boldest possible reading: his equation was predicting the existence of an entirely new
particle nobody had ever detected, identical to the electron in mass but carrying the opposite,
positive electric charge. The antielectron. Antimatter.

This is worth sitting with for a moment, because it represents something close to the purest possible
distillation of what this whole book has been arguing physics actually is, going all the way back to
Galileo's ramp: a prediction, daring enough to be embarrassing if wrong, made entirely from the
internal logic of an equation, years before anyone had observed a shred of supporting evidence, laid
down as a specific, falsifiable claim rather than quietly withdrawn out of caution. Less than two
years later, in 1932, an American physicist named Carl Anderson, studying photographs of cosmic-ray
particle tracks in a cloud chamber — a device that makes the paths of charged particles visible as
thin trails of condensation, the way a jet's contrail marks its path across the sky — found a track
that curved the wrong way in the chamber's magnetic field for it to be an ordinary electron, and
whose other properties matched an electron in every respect except its charge. Anderson, working
independently and by his own later account not even fully aware of the specifics of Dirac's
prediction at the time, had photographed the first antielectron ever detected — soon renamed the
positron, the name that has stuck ever since. Dirac's equation, treated with total literal
seriousness rather than politely edited down to the comfortable half everyone had expected, had
correctly predicted an entirely new category of matter that nobody, before him, had ever imagined
might exist.

The implications, worked out over the following decades, turned out to run through the whole of
particle physics: every kind of particle, physicists came to understand, has a corresponding
antiparticle, identical in mass but opposite in charge and certain other properties, and when a
particle meets its antiparticle, the two annihilate each other completely, their entire combined
mass converting, exactly as Einstein's E=mc² requires, into a burst of pure energy. This is not
merely an exotic laboratory curiosity confined to particle accelerators, either — positron emission
tomography, the PET scan used in hospitals worldwide to detect cancer and study brain activity, works
by injecting a patient with a mildly radioactive substance that emits positrons, tracking the
gamma rays released when those positrons annihilate against ordinary electrons already present in
the patient's own tissue, a piece of Dirac's purely theoretical 1928 mathematics now quietly saving
lives in oncology wards around the world.

Dirac's own courtship and marriage, unlikely as it sounds for a man of his reputation, offers this
book one of its gentler small stories. Eugene Wigner's sister Manci, a lively, sociable Hungarian
divorcée with two children of her own, met Dirac at a dinner in Princeton and, by every surviving
account, simply decided she liked the strange, silent physicist enough to do the talking for both of
them; their courtship proceeded largely by letter, Dirac's own contributions notably brief and
literal even by the standards of a love letter, and the marriage that followed, in 1937, by most
biographers' assessment genuinely suited them both for the rest of Dirac's life — Manci, asked once
what it was like being married to one of the greatest physicists of the century, is said to have
replied simply that he was a good husband, which for a woman who fully understood exactly what kind
of man she had married, reads, in its own plain-spoken way, as a review considerably more glowing
than it sounds.

Dirac also gave physics a rigorous account of a property called spin — not, despite the name, any
kind of literal physical rotation of a tiny spinning ball, but a genuinely quantum property with no
real classical analogue, an intrinsic form of angular momentum that every fundamental particle simply
possesses as part of what it is, the way it possesses a fixed mass and a fixed charge, whether or not
it is moving or rotating in any ordinary sense at all. Spin turns out to be the deep reason behind an
enormous swath of everyday chemistry and materials science — it's the reason, worked out through a
principle called the Pauli exclusion principle, that electrons in an atom stack up into distinct,
separated shells rather than all collapsing down into the lowest possible energy level at once, and
that stacking-into-shells is, in turn, the entire reason the periodic table of elements has the
structure and the chemical behavior it does, the reason carbon bonds the way it bonds, the reason
your body's chemistry works at all rather than every atom in it simply piling into an
indistinguishable, chemically inert heap.

Dirac shared the 1933 Nobel Prize with Schrödinger — the two men whose approaches to quantum
mechanics had once seemed almost incompatible now formally recognized together — and reportedly
considered declining the prize entirely, on the grounds that the resulting publicity would be an
unwelcome intrusion on his privacy, before being talked out of refusing by Ernest Rutherford, who
pointed out that declining would attract considerably more attention than quietly accepting ever
would. He went on to live a long, famously reclusive life, married to Eugene Wigner's sister Manci —
a marriage that surprised nearly everyone who knew him, given his reputation, and that by most
accounts genuinely suited them both — and remained, until his death in 1984, one of the field's
quiet living legends: the man who found antimatter not in a laboratory but in the unforgiving,
literal logic of his own equation, and who trusted that logic completely, past the point where nearly
anyone else would have stopped and thrown the strange half of the answer away.
