# Chapter 18 — The Galaxy That Wasn't Ours Alone

For nearly all of recorded human history, right up through the first quarter of the twentieth
century, educated opinion held that the Milky Way — the hazy band of light visible across a dark
night sky, which telescopes had by the nineteenth century resolved into an enormous, disk-shaped
collection of stars — was, more or less, the entire universe. There were faint, fuzzy, spiral-shaped
smudges of light visible in telescopes, called "spiral nebulae," and the live, genuinely
unresolved scientific question of the early 1900s was simply whether these smudges were relatively
small, nearby clouds of gas within our own galaxy, or something almost too large to comfortably
imagine: entire separate galaxies in their own right, each containing billions of stars, so
staggeringly distant that their vast true size was reduced, across the intervening gulf, to a faint
smudge.

The man who settled the question, and who went on to make an even larger and stranger discovery in
the process, was an American astronomer named Edwin Hubble, working at the Mount Wilson Observatory
above Los Angeles, home to the largest telescope in the world at the time. Hubble had an
unconventional path into astronomy — a champion boxer and basketball player at the University of
Chicago who reportedly turned down an offer to spar professionally with the heavyweight champion of
the world, a Rhodes Scholar who studied law at Oxford and briefly practiced it, and who by his own
later, somewhat self-mythologizing account, affected a faintly theatrical English accent and a taste
for tweed and a pipe for the rest of his American life, in a way that some of his scientific
colleagues found grating and that his biographers have generally treated with a certain amused
skepticism. Whatever the truth of the persona, the astronomy itself was rigorous and, in short
order, era-defining.

The question was not actually as open, in scientific circles, as popular retellings sometimes
suggest, and the honest record deserves a fuller telling: in April 1920, three years before Hubble's
own decisive measurement, the astronomers Harlow Shapley and Heber Curtis held a formal public debate
at the Smithsonian, now remembered as the Great Debate of astronomy (not to be confused with this
book's own earlier chapter of the same informal name), arguing exactly this question in front of an
audience — Shapley for a single, large Milky Way encompassing the spiral nebulae, Curtis for
separate "island universes" beyond it. Neither man, judged by the standard of evidence available at
the time, was straightforwardly wrong to hold his position, and the debate itself is remembered
today less for producing a clean winner than for crystallizing, in public, exactly what evidence
would be needed to settle it — the same precise, calculable stellar distance measurement that Hubble,
three years later, would be the one to actually deliver.

In 1923, using Mount Wilson's hundred-inch telescope, then the most powerful in the world, Hubble
identified a specific type of star called a Cepheid variable inside the Andromeda spiral nebula — a
star whose brightness pulses in a very regular, well-understood pattern, and whose true absolute
brightness, unlike an ordinary star's, can be calculated directly from the timing of that pulse,
thanks to earlier work by an underappreciated Harvard astronomer named Henrietta Leavitt, another
entry in this book's honest running tally of women whose foundational work made a more famous man's
discovery possible. Comparing that calculated true brightness to how dim the star actually appeared
from Earth let Hubble work out its distance with real precision, using the same basic principle by
which you can judge how far away a lit candle is by how dim it looks compared to how bright you know
an ordinary candle actually burns. The Cepheid in Andromeda came out, by Hubble's calculation, to be
roughly 900,000 light-years away — a figure later refined upward to something closer to two and a
half million light-years by improved measurement techniques, but even at Hubble's original,
underestimated figure, catastrophically, unambiguously farther away than the entire known diameter
of the Milky Way itself. Andromeda was not a nearby gas cloud. It was another galaxy entirely, its
own separate island of billions of stars, and the universe, in a single measurement, became
staggeringly, almost incomprehensibly larger than anyone outside a small circle of speculative
astronomers had previously dared to insist on with hard evidence behind them.

Hubble did not stop there, and his next discovery, made over the following several years by
carefully measuring dozens more distant galaxies, is arguably the single most consequential
astronomical finding of the entire twentieth century. He measured, for each galaxy, not just its
distance but also its redshift — a stretching of the light's wavelength toward the red end of the
spectrum, caused by the same Doppler effect that makes a passing ambulance's siren drop in pitch as
it moves away from you, applied here to light instead of sound, and indicating that the galaxy in
question is receding, moving away from Earth. Nearly every galaxy Hubble measured showed this
redshift. And when he plotted each galaxy's redshift — its recession speed — against its distance, a
strikingly clean pattern emerged: the farther away a galaxy was, the faster it appeared to be moving
away from us, in a direct, linear, remarkably tidy proportion, now called Hubble's law.

The obvious, immediate, and genuinely dizzying implication is that the universe itself is expanding
— not that galaxies are flying outward through some pre-existing, static empty space away from a
privileged central point (Earth is not, this chapter should be clear, in any special position at
all; an observer in any other galaxy would see exactly the same pattern of every other galaxy
receding from them, for the same reason every point on the surface of an inflating balloon sees every
other point moving away from it as the balloon expands, with no single point anywhere on the
balloon's surface entitled to call itself the true center). Space itself, the very fabric between
galaxies, is stretching, carrying the galaxies apart with it, everywhere, all at once, uniformly.

And if you take that observation and simply run the film backward — a step of imaginative and
mathematical courage this book has now watched physicists take again and again, from Dirac's
antimatter to the electroweak theory's predicted W and Z bosons — a further, still more staggering
implication follows almost immediately: if everything is moving apart now, then at some finite time
in the past, everything must have been much closer together. Run the expansion back far enough, and
the entire observable universe, every galaxy, every star, all the matter and energy that exists,
converges toward a single point of unimaginable density, at a specific, calculable moment in the
past when the expansion itself apparently began.

This idea sat, for a while, on genuinely uncertain scientific footing, contested and actively
disliked by a number of serious physicists including, initially, Einstein himself, who had in fact,
before Hubble's measurements, deliberately inserted an extra fudge factor into his own general
relativity equations — he called it the cosmological constant — specifically to force his equations
to describe a static, unchanging, eternal universe, because a universe with a definite beginning
struck him, and struck a great many of his contemporaries, as uncomfortably, almost theologically
close to a creation story dressed up in physics. Einstein later called the cosmological constant, once
Hubble's expansion had made clear that the universe was not static after all, "the biggest blunder"
of his career — a piece of testimony historians of science treat with some caution, since the phrase
comes only from a secondhand recollection by the physicist George Gamow rather than any of
Einstein's own writing, but whose substance, that Einstein deeply regretted fudging his own beautiful
equations out of a prior philosophical preference rather than trusting where the mathematics
naturally led, is well corroborated elsewhere in his correspondence.

The idea of a definite cosmic beginning found its most important early champion not in an
observatory but in a rather more unusual setting for a scientific breakthrough: a Belgian Roman
Catholic priest and trained physicist named Georges Lemaître, who in 1927 — two years before Hubble
published his own redshift-distance results — had already worked out, independently and using
Einstein's own general relativity equations without the artificial cosmological-constant fudge,
that the universe should be expanding, and that running the expansion backward implied it must have
begun from what Lemaître, in characteristically vivid language for a physicist who also happened to
be a priest, called a "primeval atom" — an initial, unimaginably dense and hot state from which all
the matter and energy of the universe subsequently expanded, cooled, and, over billions of years,
condensed into the galaxies, stars, and planets that make up the universe humanity actually observes
today. It is worth noting plainly, because it complicates a lazy assumption some readers might
otherwise bring to this story, that Lemaître himself, throughout his life, firmly and repeatedly
rejected any attempt — including, at one point, a specific suggestion from Pope Pius XII — to read
his own scientific theory as direct physical confirmation of the biblical account of creation,
insisting that his physics and his faith operated as genuinely separate domains of understanding that
should not be collapsed into each other for either science's or religion's convenience.

The theory needed a name, and it got one, in 1949, from a physicist who did not believe it and meant
the name as an insult: Fred Hoyle, a brilliant British astronomer who favored a rival "steady state"
theory in which the universe had always existed, unchanging on the largest scales, with new matter
continuously and quietly created to fill the gaps as existing matter spread apart — Hoyle, on a BBC
radio broadcast, dismissively referred to Lemaître and Hubble's expanding-universe alternative as
"this Big Bang idea," intending the phrase to sound faintly ridiculous and unserious. The name stuck
anyway, adopted cheerfully and without any apparent embarrassment by the very physicists whose theory
it had been coined to mock, in one of the more satisfying small ironies in the history of scientific
nomenclature — and within two decades, a piece of accidental evidence, discovered not by cosmologists
searching for it but by two radio engineers trying to eliminate an annoying and persistent hiss from
their antenna, would settle the argument between the Big Bang and Hoyle's steady state almost
completely, and hand cosmology the next chapter of this book.
