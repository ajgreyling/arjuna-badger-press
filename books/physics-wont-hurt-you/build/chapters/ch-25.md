# Chapter 25 — Quantum Computing: Harnessing the Weirdness

Nearly every chapter of this book's final Part has treated quantum mechanics's strangest features —
superposition, entanglement, the genuinely unresolved measurement problem this Part opened with — as
philosophical puzzles first and technological curiosities second, if at all. This chapter exists to
correct that balance, because in the four decades since Richard Feynman first raised the idea in a
now-famous 1981 talk, at a conference on the physics of computation, physicists and engineers have
been steadily, patiently turning the very weirdness this book has spent so many pages trying honestly
to sit with into a working, buildable technology — one that may, within the coming decades, do more
to reshape ordinary life than any single discovery since the transistor.

Feynman's original observation, as this library's own fuller biography of him records, was
disarmingly simple once stated, in the same tradition of plain, physical reasoning this book has
tried to credit him with throughout: an ordinary classical computer, built from ordinary bits that
are always definitely either a 0 or a 1, struggles badly — the computational cost exploding
exponentially with every additional particle involved — when asked to simulate the full quantum
behavior of even a modestly sized physical system, precisely because a quantum system's own
description requires tracking every possible combination of superposed states simultaneously, a
bookkeeping burden that grows explosively and rapidly outpaces any classical machine's memory and
processing power. But, Feynman reasoned, a computer built out of components that were themselves
genuinely, physically quantum mechanical — that could exist in superpositions and exploit
entanglement directly, rather than merely calculating an approximation of a quantum system from the
outside — might not suffer that same explosive penalty at all, since it would be using the very same
quantum resource the simulated system itself was using. Nature, Feynman's insight amounted to, might
be the most efficient possible simulator of itself, if only you could build a machine willing to play
by the same rules.

The basic unit of a quantum computer is called a qubit, and the plainest way to picture what makes it
different from an ordinary classical bit is to return to Schrödinger's cat from Chapter 11: where a
classical bit is always definitely a 0 or definitely a 1, the way a light switch is always definitely
on or definitely off, a qubit, left undisturbed, can exist in a genuine superposition of both 0 and 1
at once — not merely an unknown mixture that secretly is one or the other and simply hasn't been
checked yet, but a real, physically meaningful combination of both possibilities simultaneously, in
exactly the same sense this book's Chapter 23 insisted the Copenhagen interpretation requires taking
seriously. A single qubit, on its own, is not obviously more useful than a classical bit for most
ordinary purposes. The real power arrives once you link many qubits together through entanglement,
the same genuinely non-local correlation this book's Chapter 13 traced from Einstein's uneasy "spooky
action at a distance" through John Bell's theorem to Alain Aspect's Nobel-winning experimental
confirmation: a set of entangled qubits can represent an exponentially large number of possible
combinations all at once, and certain carefully designed quantum algorithms can manipulate that whole
vast superposition simultaneously, extracting, at the final measurement, an answer that would have
required a classical computer to check each possibility one at a time, in a process that could
realistically take longer than the current age of the universe for a sufficiently large problem.

The clearest, most historically consequential example of exactly this advantage is a piece of
mathematics developed by Peter Shor in 1994, several years before any hardware existed remotely
capable of running it: Shor's algorithm, given a sufficiently large and reliable quantum computer,
can factor enormous numbers — break a large number down into its prime-number building blocks — 
exponentially faster than the best known classical method. This sounds, on first hearing, like a
narrow, abstract piece of mathematics with little bearing on ordinary life, and it is in fact one of
the more urgent, practically consequential results in this entire book, because the difficulty of
factoring very large numbers is precisely the mathematical foundation underpinning most of the
encryption currently securing the world's banking systems, private communications, and government
secrets. A sufficiently large, sufficiently reliable quantum computer running Shor's algorithm could,
in principle, break most of today's standard internet encryption in a matter of hours or less — a
prospect serious enough that governments and technology companies worldwide have already spent the
past decade developing and beginning to deploy new, "quantum-resistant" encryption standards, built on
different mathematical problems that even a working quantum computer cannot easily crack, specifically
to be ready before sufficiently powerful quantum hardware actually arrives. It is a rare and genuinely
striking case of civilization moving to defend itself against a technology's consequences years
before the technology itself is fully built — the geoengineering-style ethical foresight a later
chapter of this book, on the ethics of knowing, will argue for, already, in this one narrow case,
actually happening.

Building the actual hardware has proven staggeringly difficult, for a reason this book's own Chapter
24 on consciousness already previewed in a different context: quantum superpositions are extremely
fragile, and almost any interaction with the surrounding environment — stray heat, vibration,
electromagnetic interference — causes "decoherence," collapsing the delicate superposition into
ordinary classical behavior before any useful computation can be completed, the same basic physical
problem that a later chapter of this book will show the physicist Max Tegmark used to cast doubt on
Penrose and Hameroff's warm, noisy neurons sustaining quantum coherence at all. Building a working qubit therefore generally means isolating a physical
system — a trapped ion, a superconducting circuit, a particular electron's spin — inside an
extraordinarily controlled environment, frequently cooled to temperatures colder than the depths of
interstellar space, mere fractions of a degree above absolute zero, and shielded as completely as
current engineering allows from every outside disturbance. Google announced in 2019 that a
53-qubit processor, named Sycamore, had performed a specific, deliberately obscure calculation in
about 200 seconds that the company's own researchers estimated would take the world's most powerful
classical supercomputer roughly ten thousand years — a milestone the company called "quantum
supremacy," a term some in the field have since preferred to soften to "quantum advantage" given
both the narrowness of the specific task chosen and a subsequent, still-debated classical
recalculation by IBM researchers suggesting the classical comparison time might have been
considerably overstated. Whatever the exact scale of that particular claim, the field has continued
advancing rapidly since, with IBM, Google, and a growing number of well-funded startups all racing to
build processors with more qubits, longer coherence times, and lower error rates — the last of these,
error correction, widely considered by researchers in the field to be the single hardest remaining
engineering obstacle standing between today's experimental machines and a genuinely large-scale,
reliable, commercially transformative quantum computer.

The competing physical approaches to actually building a qubit are worth naming plainly, because
the field has not yet converged on one winning hardware platform the way classical computing
eventually converged on the silicon transistor. Superconducting circuits, the approach Google and
IBM have most heavily invested in, use tiny loops of wire cooled to near absolute zero so that
electrical current flows without resistance, letting engineers etch qubits directly onto a chip
using techniques adapted from ordinary semiconductor manufacturing. Trapped-ion approaches,
championed by companies including IonQ, instead suspend individual charged atoms in a vacuum using
electromagnetic fields and manipulate them with precisely tuned lasers, trading the superconducting
approach's manufacturing convenience for qubits that are, by most current measures, individually more
stable and longer-lived. A third approach, topological quantum computing, pursued most prominently
by Microsoft, tries to sidestep the decoherence problem at its root by encoding information not in a
single fragile particle's state but in the collective, more robust braiding pattern of exotic
quasiparticles — a more theoretically elegant solution that has, as of this writing, proven
considerably harder to actually build than either of its rivals. Which approach eventually wins, if
any single one does, remains as genuinely open a question as any this Part has raised, and this book,
in keeping with its own promise, will not pretend to know the answer before the engineers themselves
do.

What such a machine would actually be good for, once built, is worth being precise about, because
popular coverage sometimes implies quantum computers will simply be faster general-purpose computers
across the board, and that is not quite right: for the great majority of ordinary computing tasks —
word processing, web browsing, most everyday software — a quantum computer offers no advantage
whatsoever over an ordinary classical machine, and may in practice be considerably worse suited to
the job. The genuine, well-established advantage is narrower and, in its own way, more interesting:
problems that are themselves fundamentally about simulating quantum systems — designing new
pharmaceutical compounds by accurately modeling how their molecules actually behave at the quantum
level, discovering new materials with specific desired electronic or magnetic properties, optimizing
the notoriously complex quantum chemistry involved in more efficient industrial catalysts or better
batteries — are precisely the class of problem Feynman originally identified in 1981, where a quantum
machine, built from the same quantum stuff as the problem itself, has a genuine, provable, and
potentially enormous edge over any classical approach, however powerful.

It is a fitting image to sit with before this book turns to graver matters: the same strangeness this book has spent five chapters carefully, honestly
labeling — genuinely unresolved, genuinely unsettling, the subject of a century-long argument between
Bohr and Einstein that neither man lived to see fully settled — is not merely a philosophical puzzle
to be marveled at from a safe intellectual distance. It is already, quietly, in laboratories in
California and Zurich and Beijing, being wired directly into working machines, this book's own final
proof that the deepest, strangest truths physics has ever uncovered have a way, given enough patient
engineering, of eventually becoming simply one more tool sitting on the workbench, alongside the
electric generator Faraday built almost by accident and the radio waves Hertz detected purely to
confirm Maxwell had been right.
