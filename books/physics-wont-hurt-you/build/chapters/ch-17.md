# Chapter 17 — The Particle Zoo and the Four Forces

By the late 1950s, particle accelerators — machines that fling charged particles to enormous speeds
and smash them together, essentially industrial-scale versions of Rutherford's gold-foil experiment
from Chapter 10, run at energies Rutherford could never have imagined — had gotten powerful enough
to start finding new particles at an alarming rate. Protons and neutrons, which had seemed, for
thirty years, like the simple, solid, final building blocks of the atomic nucleus, turned out to
have a great many unstable, short-lived cousins, produced fleetingly in high-energy collisions before
decaying, within tiny fractions of a second, into more familiar particles. By the early 1960s,
physicists had catalogued so many of these — with names like pions, kaons, lambda particles, sigma
particles, xi particles — that the field started calling the whole confusing menagerie, with a mix
of genuine bewilderment and gallows humor, "the particle zoo." Enrico Fermi, one of the great
physicists of the era, is said to have remarked that if he could remember the names of all the new
particles, he would have become a botanist instead. Physics, which had spent three centuries
proudly simplifying — Newton unifying falling apples and orbiting moons, Maxwell unifying
electricity, magnetism, and light — appeared, for a genuinely uncomfortable decade, to be getting
messier rather than tidier.

The man who found the hidden order inside the mess was Murray Gell-Mann, whom this library's Feynman
biography has already introduced as Feynman's brilliant, formally precise, endlessly prickly Caltech
colleague and occasional rival. Working through the early 1960s, Gell-Mann noticed that if you
plotted the zoo's confusing collection of particles against two of their properties — their electric
charge and a somewhat more obscure quantum property called "strangeness" — they fell, again and
again, into small, tidy, symmetric geometric patterns: triangles, hexagons, octets of eight related
particles arranged in orderly rows. Gell-Mann, with a certain wry theatricality that his biography-
neighbor's showmanship would have appreciated, named the underlying mathematical symmetry the
"Eightfold Way," a deliberate, half-joking nod to the Buddhist Noble Eightfold Path, chosen because
the pattern kept organizing particles into families of eight. The pattern was elegant. It was also,
crucially, in the same predictive tradition this book keeps insisting is the actual test of real
physics: the pattern had a gap, a missing particle that the geometry said had to exist to complete
one particular family, with specific, calculable properties. Gell-Mann predicted it publicly in 1962.
It was discovered, with exactly the predicted mass, charge, and lifetime, at Brookhaven National
Laboratory in New York in 1964.

That success was impressive. What came next was stranger, and it is the single most important idea
in the whole of particle physics: Gell-Mann proposed, in 1964 — independently and almost
simultaneously with a physicist named George Zweig, who arrived at essentially the same idea using a
different mathematical route and originally wanted to call the particles "aces" — that the entire
zoo's confusing sprawl of protons, neutrons, and their many short-lived cousins was not, after all,
a collection of fundamental particles at all. They were all, Gell-Mann proposed, built from smaller,
more truly fundamental pieces, which he named, in one of the more famously whimsical acts of
scientific naming in this whole book, quarks — a word he lifted from a nonsense line in James
Joyce's novel *Finnegans Wake* ("Three quarks for Muster Mark!"), chosen, by Gell-Mann's own later
account, purely because it sounded right for a particle he suspected might come in sets of three.
Protons and neutrons, on this picture, are each built from exactly three quarks, bound together by
yet another force this chapter will get to shortly; the dizzying zoo of other short-lived particles
are built from various combinations of two or three quarks and their antimatter counterparts, and
the geometric Eightfold Way patterns Gell-Mann had first noticed turn out to be a direct, calculable
consequence of exactly this underlying quark structure — the messy zoo, once you had the right
pieces to build it from, snapping back into the same kind of tidy, unified simplicity Newton and
Maxwell had each found in their own centuries.

Quarks come, physicists eventually established through decades of further experiment, in six
varieties, given names that carry roughly the same whimsical spirit as "quark" itself: up, down,
strange, charm, top, and bottom. An ordinary proton is built from two up quarks and one down quark;
an ordinary neutron from two down quarks and one up. Quarks have never been, and current theory says
never can be, observed in isolation — the force binding them together, unlike gravity or
electromagnetism, actually gets *stronger* the further apart you try to pull two quarks, rather than
weaker, meaning that trying to rip a single quark free from a proton takes so much energy that the
energy itself simply condenses, via Einstein's E=mc², into a new pair of quarks, leaving you no
closer to an isolated quark than when you started, a genuinely strange property physicists call
"confinement" and that took years of further theoretical work, credited principally to David Gross,
David Politzer, and Frank Wilczek — work that earned all three the 2004 Nobel Prize — to properly
explain within a rigorous mathematical theory called quantum chromodynamics, the strong-force cousin
of Feynman's quantum electrodynamics from the previous chapter.

This is a good moment for this book to step back and give the reader the full inventory, because
everything from this chapter forward — cosmology, the frontier physics of Part Five — assumes a
working sense of it: physics has found exactly four fundamental forces governing the entire
universe, and no more, in nearly a century of increasingly precise searching. Gravity, Einstein's
geometry of curved space, by far the weakest of the four at the scale of individual particles but the
only one that dominates at the scale of planets and galaxies, because unlike the others it has no
opposite charge to cancel itself out. Electromagnetism, Maxwell's unified light-and-force, binding
atoms into molecules, letting electrons flow through wires, holding your body's chemistry together.
The strong force, the quark-gluing, confinement-producing force this chapter just introduced,
overwhelmingly the strongest of the four at nuclear scales but with a range so short it essentially
vanishes outside the nucleus. And the weak force, responsible for certain kinds of radioactive decay
and, as this library's Feynman biography recounts, the specific target of the Feynman-Gell-Mann
collaboration that briefly, productively, and rather contentiously united two of this book's most
important physicists in the late 1950s.

Murray Gell-Mann's own relationship to the eventual naming and acceptance of quarks is worth a
further honest word, because it mirrors, in miniature, the same tension between elegance and evidence
this whole book keeps circling back to. Gell-Mann himself, for years after first proposing them,
described quarks in his own papers with a certain public caution, at times almost implying they might
be no more than a convenient bookkeeping device for the underlying mathematical symmetry rather than
literal, physically real particles — a hedge some historians of science attribute to simple prudence
given how radical the proposal already was, and others attribute to Gell-Mann's own genuine
uncertainty at the time about whether anything so strange, permanently uncatchable, and confined
inside larger particles could really be said to exist in the way an electron or a photon existed. It
took direct experimental evidence — high-energy electrons fired at protons in the late 1960s at the
Stanford Linear Accelerator, scattering off what were unmistakably small, hard, point-like objects
inside, rather than off any smooth, structureless blob — to settle the matter and convert quarks from
an elegant organizing principle into a confirmed physical reality, a distinction this book's whole
final Part has insisted matters enormously and that even the theory's own inventor, for a time, was
not fully willing to commit to out loud.

Physicists have, since the 1960s, managed one genuine unification among these four: the electroweak
theory, developed principally by Sheldon Glashow, Abdus Salam, and Steven Weinberg, showed that
electromagnetism and the weak force, which look utterly different at the low energies of everyday
experience, are actually two faces of a single, unified "electroweak" force, that would have looked
identical at the almost unimaginably high energies present in the first fractions of a second after
the Big Bang, and that only splits apart into the two separate forces we observe today as the
universe cools. The theory made a bold and, this book's readers should by now expect, extremely
precise prediction: two new force-carrying particles, called the W and Z bosons, with specific,
calculable masses. Both were discovered, at those predicted masses, at Europe's CERN laboratory in
1983, earning Carlo Rubbia and Simon van der Meer the following year's Nobel Prize.

But the electroweak theory, worked through carefully, had an awkward gap of its own, structurally
similar to the gap Dirac's equation had left in Chapter 14, though this time the gap was not an
unexpected extra solution but a missing piece the mathematics needed and did not, on its own,
supply: the theory's equations, in their simplest and most elegant form, required every fundamental
particle to be massless — a flatly false prediction, since electrons, quarks, and the W and Z bosons
themselves all plainly have mass. In 1964, several physicists working mostly independently —
François Englert and Robert Brout, Peter Higgs, and separately Gerald Guralnik, C. R. Hagen, and
Tom Kibble — proposed a fix: an invisible field, now called the Higgs field, filling the entire
universe uniformly, that most fundamental particles interact with as they move through it, and that
interaction is what generates mass — a particle that interacts strongly with the Higgs field,
metaphorically speaking, wades through molasses and ends up heavy; a particle like the photon that
doesn't interact with it at all sails through unimpeded and stays perfectly massless. Crucially, the
theory predicted that this field, if real, ought to have its own associated particle, a ripple in the
field itself, that a sufficiently powerful accelerator ought eventually to be able to produce and
detect directly.

It took nearly half a century, and the construction of the Large Hadron Collider — a ring
seventeen miles in circumference, built a hundred meters underground straddling the border of
France and Switzerland, the single most expensive and most complicated scientific instrument ever
built by the human species — to finally check. On 4 July 2012, in a physics seminar at CERN followed
live around the world, two independent experimental teams announced they had found a new particle,
at almost exactly the mass the theory required, behaving in every measurable respect exactly as the
Higgs boson was predicted to behave. Peter Higgs, eighty-three years old, sitting in the audience,
was seen visibly wiping away tears as the announcement was made. He and François Englert shared the
2013 Nobel Prize; Robert Brout had died two years earlier and Nobel rules do not permit posthumous
awards, one more entry, alongside Lise Meitner two chapters earlier, in this book's running, honest
accounting of how imperfectly credit and recognition have actually tracked genuine discovery across
four centuries of physics.

The theory built from all of this — quarks and the leptons (the family that includes the electron
and its heavier, less stable cousins the muon and tau, along with three nearly massless, famously
elusive particles called neutrinos), bound and pushed around by the strong, weak, and electromagnetic
forces, all of it given mass by the Higgs field — is called, with a modesty that undersells its own
achievement, simply the Standard Model of particle physics. It is, by the same brutal standard of
checkable precision this book applied to quantum electrodynamics two chapters ago, extraordinarily,
almost unreasonably successful: every particle it has ever predicted has eventually been found, at
essentially every mass and every property the theory specified in advance. It is also, by the
consensus of nearly every physicist working on it, obviously and admittedly incomplete — it says
nothing at all about gravity, the fourth force this chapter listed and the only one general
relativity, not the Standard Model, currently describes; it has no explanation for the dark matter
and dark energy that the next several chapters will show make up the overwhelming majority of the
universe's actual content; and it requires a long list of specific numbers — the exact masses of the
quarks, the exact strength of each force — to simply be fed in from experiment, with no deeper theory
yet explaining why those particular numbers and not some others. Physics's zoo, in other words, has
been tamed into a beautifully organized, exhaustively verified cage. Nobody yet knows why the cage
was built to these exact specifications, or what, if anything, lies in the space just outside its
bars — and that unfinished business, along with an equally unfinished business at the largest scales
imaginable, is where this book turns next.
