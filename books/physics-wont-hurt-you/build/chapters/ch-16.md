# Chapter 16 — Pictures Instead of Equations

The physicists who came out of the Manhattan Project did not, for the most part, simply go home and
rest. Many of them, Richard Feynman prominent among them, spent the years right after the war in a
condition this book's companion biography of Feynman describes in some detail — burned out,
grieving, in Feynman's specific case still working through the death of his young wife Arline weeks
before the Trinity test, and uncertain whether the intense, urgent, deadline-driven physics of
wartime had used up whatever made them good at the slower, curiosity-driven physics of peacetime.
What pulled Feynman back, as that fuller biography tells at length, was not duty or discipline but a
wobbling cafeteria plate at Cornell, watched purely for the pleasure of it, that led him back, step
by unpressured step, into the single unsolved problem that had followed physics since the turn of the
century and that this book has been quietly setting up since Chapter 7: quantum electrodynamics, or
QED, the theory of how light and matter actually interact, which kept producing nonsensical infinite
answers no matter how carefully physicists tried to patch it.

This book will not retell that story scene by scene a second time — the 1947 Shelter Island
conference, the 1948 Pocono conference where Feynman's picture-based method landed badly against
Julian Schwinger's more rigorous formal mathematics, the young physicist Freeman Dyson proving on a
Greyhound bus that Feynman's approach and Schwinger's, though they looked nothing alike on the page,
were secretly the same theory — all of it is told in full in *The Man Who Drew the Universe*, and
this chapter exists to explain, in the plainest terms this book can manage, what the resulting
theory actually *is*, and why it matters to the shape of physics that follows.

Julian Schwinger himself deserves a fuller word here than a single condensed chapter can otherwise
spare him, because the popular telling of this story — a plainspoken, bongo-playing American genius
versus a stiff, formal rival — does real disservice to a man who was, by the measure of raw
technical power, arguably Feynman's equal and by some contemporaries' private judgment his superior.
Schwinger built his version of QED from rigorous, unbroken mathematical formalism, delivering
lectures so dense and so exactingly complete that students joked no living being other than Schwinger
himself could reliably follow one from the first line to the last without a night's sleep in
between; he arrived at Harvard as a professor at twenty-nine, having already earned a reputation, as
a teenage prodigy at Columbia in the 1930s, for turning in physics homework so far beyond the
course's actual level that his professors reportedly stopped grading it and simply let him work
independently. Sin-Itiro Tomonaga, the third laureate, developed his own equivalent formulation
essentially in isolation in wartime and immediate postwar Japan, working with damaged instruments
and a devastated economy, his results reaching the American physics community only after the war's
end through a colleague's persistent effort to get his papers translated and noticed — a reminder,
this book would add, that genuine simultaneous discovery, the same phenomenon Newton and Leibniz
fought so bitterly over with calculus, is less a strange coincidence in the history of science than
a fairly reliable sign that an idea's time has properly come, regardless of which side of a war or an
ocean the physicist chasing it happens to be sitting on.

Quantum electrodynamics is, by the measure every working physicist actually cares about — how
precisely a theory's predictions match reality when you go and check — the most accurately verified
theory in the entire history of science. It predicts, among other things, a specific number called
the electron's magnetic moment, essentially a measure of how strongly the electron behaves like a
tiny magnet, to an accuracy of better than one part in a trillion, a level of precision roughly
equivalent to measuring the distance from New York to Los Angeles to within the width of a single
human hair. No other theory in any field of science has ever been checked against nature and found to
agree this precisely. This is the concrete, checkable payoff of the strange, probability-soaked,
uncertainty-riddled quantum mechanics the last several chapters have built up piece by piece: however
philosophically uncomfortable it makes physicists and everyone else, it is also, by a very wide
margin, the single most accurately tested description of physical reality human beings have ever
produced.

Feynman's specific contribution — the diagrams that carry his name, sketched today on whiteboards in
every physics department on Earth — deserves a plain-language explanation here even for a reader who
has already met them, in passing, in the Feynman biography, because they are the direct ancestor of
the whole particle-physics picture the rest of this Part will build on. A Feynman diagram is a small
picture: straight lines for particles of matter, like electrons, moving through space and time;
wavy lines for particles of force, like photons, being exchanged between them; dots, called
vertices, marking the moments where a particle emits or absorbs another one. Two electrons repel each
other, in this picture, not because some invisible field mysteriously reaches across the gap between
them, but because they are constantly exchanging photons — trading force-carrying particles back and
forth, the exchange itself producing exactly the push you'd otherwise call "the electromagnetic
force." It is one of the great, quietly beautiful unifications this book keeps meeting again and
again: Faraday's lines of force, Maxwell's electromagnetic waves, and now, in this final quantum
form, force itself revealed to be nothing more mysterious than particles handing each other along.

The precision this chapter has already praised carries a specific, sobering asterisk that this
book's whole final Part will spend real time on: QED's calculations only produce finite, sensible
answers because of a mathematical procedure called renormalization, in which the theory's raw,
literally infinite quantities are systematically, carefully subtracted against each other in a
precise way, leaving a small, finite, checkable remainder that matches experiment beautifully. Dirac
himself, whose antimatter this book's previous chapters have already celebrated as one of the purest
examples of trusting an equation's own logic, never fully made peace with renormalization, calling
it in later life "just not sensible mathematics" — an ugly trick, in his view, dressed up as physics,
regardless of how well it worked. Feynman himself, by his own published admission, shared some of
that unease, once describing renormalization as a form of mathematical sleight of hand he was not
proud of, however necessary it had proven. That a technique its own inventors distrusted on
aesthetic grounds could nonetheless deliver the single most accurately verified prediction in the
history of science is, this book would suggest, one of the more genuinely humbling small lessons in
its entire four-hundred-year story: nature has never promised physicists that her deepest workings
would also strike them as beautiful.

This idea — that every force in nature is, at bottom, the exchange of some kind of force-carrying
particle — turned out to be exactly the right lens for the explosion of new particles physics was
about to discover in the postwar decades, as bigger and more powerful accelerators started smashing
particles together at higher and higher energies and finding, to the considerable exasperation of the
physicists trying to keep track of them all, that the tidy little zoo of proton, neutron, electron,
and photon that had sufficed since Dirac's day was nowhere close to the whole story. That
exasperation, and the extraordinary act of pattern-finding that eventually resolved it, is where this
book turns next.
