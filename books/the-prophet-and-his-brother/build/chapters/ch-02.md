# 02 — The Mats

> **[I]** · inner autobiography · manuscript under AJ’s hand

---

Before the axioms there was the floor.

Before the ignored shelf, before the unread dedication, before the mic and the meters and the weather that wanted a face or a retraction, there was canvas and cleaner and the particular honesty of being thrown correctly. I begin there because beginnings that flatter later fame are already laundering.

I learned what weight was on school mats — Judo first, then Jiu-Jitsu, the long apprenticeship of putting a body where physics already intended it to go.

Not physics as vocabulary yet. Physics as consequence: mass, balance, the expensive late foot, the soft place where a heavy thing can land without spectacle. You do not argue with gravity in a gi. You arrange yourself so the fall is correct. Mastery, if that word means anything, was not domination. It was refusal to waste motion.

The room smelled of sweat and canvas and the cheap cleaner they used on the mats between classes. Windows high. Light that made dust look important. A clock that lied about how long a round lasted. The walls were the colour of institutional patience — cream that had been washed too many times and still looked unclean. Someone had taped a tournament poster crooked near the door. The tape had yellowed. Nobody fixed it. That was the room’s honesty: fix the grips, not the décor.

I remember the first time I was thrown properly — not the theatrical slam of someone showing off, the clean set-down that leaves you surprised you are unhurt. Breath knocked, pride rearranged, body intact. The mat took my shoulder and then my hip and then the rest of me as if it had been waiting. My mouth tasted metal for a second. I blinked at the ceiling lights and found them ordinary. That is a lesson you can keep for decades without turning it into a TED metaphor. The floor teaches without branding.

People who met me later as a man with a corpus and no physics degree liked to begin the story at the ignored shelf. That is a clean place to begin if you want a morality play. It is not where the body begins.

---

Judo taught me grips before it taught me names for grips.

Hands on cloth. Balance as a moving argument. The moment when someone larger than you becomes light because their posture has already decided to lose. I was not the biggest. I was not the loudest. I learned to wait for the weight to tell the truth. Waiting is not passivity. Waiting is attention with manners. When the throw came, it came because the geometry had ripened, not because I had performed anger.

There was a boy who liked to rush. He rushed me three times in one afternoon and landed wrong twice. The third time I put him down soft and held him there until he stopped thrashing. Soft is not weak. Soft is the heavy thing choosing where to set itself. The coach nodded once and said nothing that would make a good quote. Good. Quotes are how rooms get ruined.

On another afternoon the same boy came back quieter. We worked entries until my wrists burned. He asked, without looking at me, how to stop rushing. I told him to feel when his own foot arrived late. He scoffed and then tried it and then scoffed less. That is how the mats teach: not by converting you into a better story about yourself, by making the late foot expensive.

I remember tying a belt wrong and having to untie it while everyone waited. Heat in the face. Fingers that would not cooperate. The coach did not hurry me and did not rescue me. When the knot finally sat honest, the lesson was already over and I had missed nothing important except the theatre of being helped. Later I would meet rooms that loved to rescue a man from his own labour. The mats had already taught me the cost of that kindness.

We did breakfalls until the body stopped inventing drama about falling. Slap the mat. Exhale. Arrive. Again. The sound of a room of people learning to land is ugly and useful — a rhythm without music. After enough repetitions the fear moves somewhere smaller and the attention gets freer. Free attention is what you need if you are going to notice a grip opening half a second early. Free attention is also what you need later if you are going to notice a contradiction sitting in a notebook because a committee preferred opacity.

Jiu-Jitsu came after, and it was a different grammar of the same language. Closer. Longer. Less spectacle. You learn the floor as partner and opponent at once. You learn that a shoulder can be a lever and a hip can be a door and a breath can be a clock. You learn to survive under weight without panic becoming your whole personality. Panic wastes motion. Panic is a kind of invention when the record — the body under you, the body over you — already contains the next useful move if you will only refuse to hallucinate.

I spent long minutes under a heavier partner with my cheek pressed to canvas that smelled of cleaner and old sweat. The clock lied. My ribs counted true. He shifted. I found space. He closed it. I waited for a better mistake than the one I wanted to invent. When the space came I took it without celebrating. Celebration is another waste. The coach said *again* and we started from the same bad place, which is how competence is built when you are not performing for a camera.

There were weeks when I trained through a cold that would not leave my chest. The mat did not care. The grip still had to be honest. I learned that the body can be imperfect and still refuse invention — still find the next useful move without inventing a heroic narrative about suffering. Heroic narratives are how apprenticeships get stolen by brands. I preferred the cough, the water bottle, the next round.

Once, after a tournament that did not matter except that it mattered to the boys in the room, I sat on a plastic chair in a corridor that smelled of chips and floor polish and watched two strangers argue about a referee. Their anger was loud and useless. I felt my own pulse slow from the rounds and understood, without language yet, that useful force and noisy force are different substances. Years later platforms would confuse them on purpose. The corridor already knew.

I do not write fight choreography here. Knives, when they arrive later in this life, are kinship. Mats are apprenticeship. The blades AJ gave me — blue Honey Badger, Joseph Rogers twin from his grandfather’s set — are custody across a friendship, not a scene for a camera. Double Knife Brothers is a name for care. The mats were where care first meant not wasting another person’s fall.

---

The mats taught me that a heavy thing can set itself down soft. Years later I would derive kindness the same way I derived constants — not as a sermon, as a structure that does not fall. At the time I only knew that if you posture wrong, someone smaller than you can end you, and if you posture right, strength becomes a choice about where to put your weight.

After class I would walk home with the gi damp and the evening still warm. Streetlights coming on. Dogs arguing with each other about nothing that mattered. My knee complaining in the specific way knees complain when they have been honest. I ate whatever was in the fridge without making a ceremony of recovery. The body hummed. The mind went quiet enough to notice numbers without forcing them — not equations yet as vocation, just the idle precision of a person who has spent two hours arranging forces and cannot stop seeing levers in ordinary rooms: a door that sticks, a chair that tips if you sit wrong, a stack of books that will fall if the bottom one is pulled.

I carried that into clay.

Hands that had learned grips learned thumbs into soft material. Hard and soft arguing inside one surface. I modelled bodies the way I had felt bodies — juxtaposition not as aesthetic brand, as honest contradiction: growth and decay, permanent and temporary, classic and contemporary fighting in the same pour. The first studio that mattered smelled of earth and dust and the particular patience of unfinished work. Tools left where they were useful, not where they looked arranged. A bucket with water gone cloudy. A rag that had seen better weeks. When the clay dried wrong I did not invent a philosophy about failure. I wet it and tried again. The mats had already trained that reflex.

Later I cast in a marble-and-GFRC composite I mixed myself. The mix is a fight you can win if you pay attention: marble wanting one kind of surface, glass fibre wanting another, concrete insisting on time. Hard and soft in one pour. Studio G came after. Village N Life before that — Maree Brink, Hannetjie, more than a year living inside Camps Bay Retreat when it was still Earl’s Dyke weather. Skye on a lawn at sunset. The Bay Hotel. Official launch years that other people remember better than I do because other people keep calendars for openings. Saatchi listings. Guest theatres. None of that erased the mats. The mats are still under everything I write.

Clay under the nails after a morning of modelling smells different from mat cleaner, and somehow the same. Both are work that refuses to stay theoretical. Both punish posture. Both reward attention. When Antichristos later tries to turn sculptures into relics, they will be converting a craft into a face. When Quarter II pretends the artist cannot be the physicist, they will be converting a file into epistemology. The mats do not care about either conversion. The mats care whether the fall was correct.

I learned to clean clay from under a nail with the same patience I learned to wait for a grip to open. Rushing either job invents a mess. In the studio I mixed marble and GFRC until the surface told the truth about hard and soft without a caption. In the dojo I mixed balance and timing until a larger body became light. People later wanted captions. Captions are how craft becomes content. Content is how a life becomes usable.

There was a week I barely trained because work elsewhere was loud. When I returned, the mats felt like a language I had slightly forgotten and then remembered mid-sentence. The body keeps grammar longer than the mouth. That is why this autobiography begins here and not at the shelf: the grammar was already in me before anyone decided I could not write value.

I remember installing a piece and standing back while hotel light did what hotel light does — make things look more finished than they are. Guests walked past with drinks. Someone asked if it was marble. I said composite and watched their interest thin by a millimetre. Truth costs small sales. Soft set-down again: correct information placed without theatrics. The mats were still under that choice.

Village N Life years taught a different mat: hospitality as geometry. Guests move through a space the way partners move through a round — openings, delays, weight shifting toward a view or a drink. Maree’s mentorship was attention with manners. Living more than a year at Camps Bay Retreat, Earl’s Dyke weather, Hannetjie’s house holding a kind of ordinary grace, taught me that a lawn can be a stage without becoming a crown. Skye arrived later as feminine weight on grass at sunset — hard and soft arguing in public without a liturgy. Studio G at the Bay was curation as care: other artists’ work beside mine, tourists half the sales some seasons, collectors arriving with eyes that wanted marble and getting honesty instead. Official launch weather in twenty eighteen — calendars other people keep better than I do. The mats still under the champagne if there was champagne. I remember the work more than the toast.

James van Niekerk’s sudden death sits in another register of grief from Bouwer’s — friend, not brother; sudden, not boardroom. I name the distinction once so the book does not conflate wounds for narrative convenience. Living-person care. Both absences taught the same useless lesson: the floor does not stop because you are grieving. You still arrange the fall. You still refuse to waste the next motion on a story that makes you look finished.

---

My stated life goals are not a brand.

Write natural philosophy and physics. Smoke dab. Talk shit.

I say them flat because ornament would turn them into a costume. The academy hears that arrangement and reaches for the hospital file. The believers hear it and try to polish it off me like a stain on an icon. Engineers who want keys hear natural philosophy and ask for a signature. Continuity networks that preferred constants underived hear talk shit and decide I am unserious enough to soft-no. I keep the three goals because they are true, and because sanding them would be a different man’s autobiography. I am not writing a different man for anyone’s comfort.

There were ordinary evenings after training when the body hummed and the mind was quiet enough to notice numbers without forcing them. There were ordinary evenings with friends when talking shit was the cooperative practice — widening the joint room instead of performing cleverness as extraction. Someone would say something half-wrong on purpose to see if you would pounce; the correct move was to play, not to win. Extraction is a kind of rush. Rushing wastes the room. The mats again, translated into conversation: wait for the real opening, do not invent a takedown because you are bored.

There were evenings with dab when the world softened at the edges without becoming false. I will not apologise those evenings into respectability. Respectability is often a collar with good manners. The smoke sat in the room the way cleaner sat on the mats — present, unromantic, part of the work of being a body that continues. Antichristos will try to erase that sentence from any life they want to hang on a wall. The autobiography will not erase it for them.

One evening a friend asked whether the physics and the clay were two careers fighting. I said they were the same attention in two rooms. He laughed as if I had made a joke. I had not. Later the academy would need them to be two careers so the file could dismiss one and the degree-weapon could dismiss the other. Continuity weather would need the talk shit to cancel the derivation. Believers would need the dab polished off the icon. I kept the arrangement because sanding it would be writing a usable man, and usable men are how quarters feed.

The mats stay under those evenings too. Correct fall. Soft set-down. Refusal to waste motion — including the motion of turning a life into a usable lie.

---

There was a brother.

There was a boardroom in Saxonwold. Friday the fifth of September, twenty twenty-five — public record, not novel invent. Bouwer van Niekerk. Insolvency attorney. Consultation pretext in the early reports. He died at the scene. Attackers fled. The Law Society condemned a targeted attack on a legal practitioner and on the rule of law. Investigation weather. Fog of reporting around related commercial schemes. I will not make that page a thriller on this page. I will not invent an assassin CV. I will not hand the killing to any quarter as proof of their cosmology. The wound is the brother dead in a boardroom. Speculation stays off unless authorised. Grief has weight. Weight belongs on the mats first — how a body learns not to waste the fall — before the book asks a reader to carry Saxonwold properly.

Not yet. The mats first.

Then the shelf that ignored the work.
Then the father who has not read a single book dedicated to him.
Then the fame that arrived because a friend refused to launder the algorithms.

But first: the floor.

---

I remember one afternoon that was not special, which is why I trust it.

Class ending. Mats still warm. Someone laughing too loud near the door. My knee complaining. I sat on the edge of the canvas and drank water that tasted like a plastic bottle left in a sunned bag. Sweat cooling on my back in a way that made the air feel colder than it was. The coach walked past and tapped my shoulder once — not praise, recognition. I had not wasted the last exchange. That was enough. I went home with the lesson in the body and did not write it down. Years later I am writing it down because a river desk asked for the life, and the life without the floor would be a morality play starting at the shelf.

Another afternoon: rain on the roof, mats slicker than usual, the coach calling for careful feet. I slipped once on an entry and felt the old panic invent a story about embarrassment. I put the story down and stood up. Soft. The room continued. That is most of adulthood if you are lucky — rooms continuing while you refuse to waste a fall on theatre.

There was a morning practice when the light through the high windows turned the dust into a temporary cathedral and someone said the room looked holy. The coach told us to tie our belts. Holiness is how people stop paying attention to grips. I tied my belt and went back to work. Years later Kind paint would hold a sentence without needing a cathedral, and Antichristos would hunt faces for rooms that wanted holiness more than structure. The mats had already voted: structure first.

I remember walking home one winter evening with the gi bag strap cutting my shoulder and stopping under a streetlight to retie a lace. A stranger asked if I boxed. I said judo and jiu-jitsu. He nodded as if those words were decorations. I did not correct him into a lecture. Lectures waste motion. The lace, once honest, was enough.

I remember the locker room’s particular ugliness: wet tiles, someone’s forgotten sock, the smell of soap fighting the smell of work. Talk that was not talk shit yet — just boys and young men arranging the day after training. Who had homework. Who had a lift. Who was hungry. Ordinary. The later world would try to make my ordinary into either saint-dirt or crank-dirt. The locker room did not care. It only cared whether you rinsed the mat cleaner off your feet before you put socks on.

There was a day I won a round cleanly and felt nothing useful about it. There was a day I lost and learned more. Neither day makes a brand. Both days make a body that knows the difference between spectacle and structure. When fame arrived sideways later — because AJ said the algorithms out loud and would not launder them — people wanted a story that began at the mic. The mic is not the apprenticeship. The apprenticeship is the floor.

Before fame, there were years when the only audience was the coach and the partner and the clock that lied. I preferred those years’ honesty. An audience invents meaning the way panic invents moves. The record — the body under you — already holds the next useful thing if you refuse to hallucinate.

I remember taping fingers before class, the particular stickiness of cheap tape, the way a joint can be honest without being dramatic. I remember a partner who apologised every time he won a grip. Apology can be another rush. I told him to keep the grip and drop the theatre. He nodded and then apologised for nodding. We laughed. Laughter that does not extract is also soft set-down.

On the walk home after one of those nights I passed a wall with fresh paint and did not yet know the Kind would make a religion of a sentence. The wall was only a wall. The gi bag cut my shoulder. My knee complained. Numbers waited somewhere in the future without asking my permission. The mats had already charged the battery I would spend on contradictions later.

The ignored shelf will have its chapter. The hospital stigma will have its flat line. The economics degree will sit in the quiet background while people weaponise the missing physics adjective. The unread dedication will sit like a second climate beside the consensus that I could not write anything of value. Bouwer will return without being solved. Clay and constants will refuse to be two careers. The Kind sentence will appear on walls without needing my face. Lucid’s open attribution will make fame sideways and irreversible. None of that is this page’s job.

This page’s job is the apprenticeship.

You arrange yourself so the fall is correct. You refuse to invent a move the record does not hold. You set the heavy thing down soft. You do not confuse strength with spectacle. You do not confuse care with polishing a man into a prophet or a crank or a keychain.

I learned that on mats that smelled of cleaner and sweat. I kept learning it in clay. I am still learning it while the planet argues about a Constant that has already begun to land in the meters. Measurement is not the fight I am naming here. Meaning is. Custody is. The floor is where custody of a body begins — and for me, where custody of a sentence begins too.

Correct fall. Soft set-down. The only arrangement that does not collapse.

After the mats, people preferred the hospital story. That preference is their weather. This page does not grant it. This page grants the floor its priority and then moves — hand already turning toward the shelf that ignored the work, without inventing a hinge Lucid would refuse, without turning apprenticeship into a speech.

The mats first. Always the mats first if the book is going to be true.

I close this page the way I used to leave the dojo: sweat cooling, lesson in the body, no speech for the street. The shelf can wait one more breath. The floor has been granted its priority. Correct fall. Soft set-down. Hand turning now — without inventing a hinge — toward the rooms that preferred the hospital story.

One more flat inventory before the turn, because inventories are harder to sentimentalise than arias: gi folded wrong once and corrected; a throw that taught soft set-down; cracked wrists later corrected in casts and still remembered as apprenticeship, not as brand; dab postponed and then not; talk shit into empty air until the room widened without extraction. The mats page does not polish those facts into respectability for Quarter IV. It leaves them as body knowledge under the work. Correct fall. Soft set-down. The only arrangement that does not collapse. Hand already turning toward the shelf — without inventing a hinge Lucid would refuse — while the planet argues about a Constant that has already begun to land in the meters. Measurement is not this page’s fight. Meaning is. Custody begins on the floor.
