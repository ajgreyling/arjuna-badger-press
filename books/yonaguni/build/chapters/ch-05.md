# Chapter 5 — Currents and Edges

Slack tide gives you maybe forty minutes. Tanaka said it the way you'd say the bus comes at nine — not a warning, a fact you arrange your life around. We dropped off the *Yaeyama-maru* in a line, four of us and her, into water the colour of bottle glass held to a window, and the first thing I felt through five millimetres of neoprene was the cold of the place arriving early, before the eyes caught up.

The Trench runs along the monument's southern flank like a corridor somebody forgot to roof. That's the believer's word for it, *corridor*. The skeptic's word is *channel*, and the geologist's word — Tanaka's word — is a Japanese term she gave us topside and then didn't translate, because, she said, the translation would do half my arguing for me and she wanted us to do our own arguing.

So we descended into the un-translated thing.

Here is the official story, and I want to give it to you straight, because it deserves better than the breathless version it usually gets. The Yonaguni Monument is sandstone and mudstone of the Yaeyama Group, laid down in the Lower Miocene — call it twenty million years ago, mud and sand pressed into rock long before there was anybody to wonder about it. Sandstone like this fractures along bedding planes, the flat seams where one season's deposit met the next, and along vertical joints where the stone cracked under stress and let go clean. Push those two habits together — horizontal seams, vertical breaks — and you get steps. You get terraces. You get right angles, because the joints often meet the bedding at ninety degrees, the way a brick wall does, for the same boring reason a brick wall does. Add earthquakes, which the Ryukyus have in abundance, add ten thousand years of marine erosion working the loosened blocks free, and you can build a staircase out of pure geology with nobody holding the chisel.

Robert Schoch dived here and came up with roughly that. Natural fracturing, jointing, the sea doing what the sea does. He pointed at formations on the islands *above* water — same stone, same right angles, same terraced look, dry and undisputed and made by nobody. If it's natural up there in the sun, why not down here in the dark.

That's the case. It's a good case. It is, by the count of credentialed geologists, the majority case, and I'd ask you to hold it in your hands like the heavy thing it is before anyone whispers the other word.

Tanaka took us along the Trench wall to show us the case in the rock itself.

She moved the way you move in a room you grew up in. No fin-wash, no clawing — she let a current we hadn't registered yet carry her elbow-first along the wall and put her bare fingertip — bare, she'd cut the tip off her glove years ago, she told us later, because the rock won't talk to you through rubber — on a horizontal seam at eye level.

I watched her trace it. The seam ran arrow-straight for the length of three of her hand-spans, then stepped down a hand's height to another seam, just as straight, and the corner where they met was a corner. Sharp. The kind of edge that makes the back of your neck go cold and your mouth want to say a word the rock hasn't earned.

She tapped it twice. Then she rolled her wrist and laid the same fingertip on the *underside* of the step, the bit you don't photograph, and held there, and looked at me through the mask until I came and put my own bare fingertip beside hers.

*The fish, our recorder's voice in my ear over the comms, dry as a dock at low water: "She's making you do your homework in front of her."*

I shut him out and felt.

This is the thing my wiring does, the thing that got me put in a quiet room a lot as a kid and gets me hired as an adult: I can feel where a surface was worked and where it broke. A worked surface has a *grammar* — the pecks march, the grind goes one direction, the chisel bites and skips with the hand that drove it. A fractured surface has weather instead of grammar; it conchoids, it steps along the grain, it follows the rock's own opinion about where to come apart. Most days that distinction sings to me clean as a struck glass.

Under the top edge of that step, the stone was rough in exactly the way fresh-broken sandstone is rough — granular, irregular, no march to it, the grain visible and going where the grain wanted. The top face above it, the part that read as a tread, that part was *flatter* than the underside had any right to predict, but it was flat the way a bedding plane is flat: it was flat because it was a seam, a buried sheet of denser sediment exposed when the looser sand above it sheared off.

I came up off the wall and gave Tanaka the flat-hand wobble. *Both. I'm getting both.*

She nodded once, like I'd passed something, and that's when the cold got worse, because the thing she was teaching me wasn't *it's natural*. The thing she was teaching me was *you cannot tell by touch*, and she was teaching it to the person whose entire value to this expedition was telling by touch.

She drew a line in the water with her finger — *follow* — and led us deeper along the Trench toward the place the locals call, when they call it anything, the corner of the steps.

Now the potato. You can't replicate a thing you don't understand, but you can try, and the trying tells you how hard the easy story is. The day before, in the shallow scatter off the harbour mouth, Tanaka had let us bring a chisel and a rock hammer down and have a go at a loose block of the same Yaeyama sandstone — her condition was that we work a block already broken free, nothing in situ, and she watched the whole time with her arms folded in the water like a teacher who has seen this lesson taught to fools before.

I'm decent with stone. Our recorder is patient and methodical and has dressed building blocks on land. Between us, with an hour and good tools and air on our backs and *intent* — that's the thing the sea doesn't have, intent — we tried to cut one clean ninety-degree corner into that block, the way the Trench wall does it for free.

We made a potato. The chisel bit and the sandstone didn't shear, it *crumbled*, the grain plucking out in little granular avalanches so that every edge came out furred and approximate, a child's idea of a corner. Underwater you can't swing for power, the water eats the blow; the stone fights you soft and wins. Forty minutes of two competent people with steel and lungs and a plan, and our corner was rounder and messier and more obviously *handled* than anything in the monument. The sea's corner was sharper than ours. The sea's corner was sharper than ours by a country mile, and the sea had used no hands at all.

Schoch, I thought, swimming after Tanaka's fins, would have enjoyed watching us fail. He'd have been right to.

The corner of the steps, when we reached it, was the cleanest thing I have ever put my eyes on underwater.

Two faces of rock met in a vertical line that fell from a tread above us down past two more treads to the silt floor, and the line was *plumb*. Not nearly plumb. Plumb. The light came down green-gold through twelve metres of water and lay along that edge and the edge held it like the spine of a book. To the left and right, the rock did rock things — encrusted, lumped, alive with coralline crust and the small commuting fish and a dark fan of soft coral working in the surge. But the corner itself stayed bare, swept, as though the current that ran the Trench kept it polished, and it ran true top to bottom with the sea filing it forever and nobody, nobody, ever once raising a tool to it.

I put my fingertip on it. I had to.

And here is where my gift ran out, and I want to be honest about the running-out because it's the truest thing I felt down there. The edge was sharp. The grammar I look for — the march of the worked tool — was not there; I felt no grind, no peck, no bite-and-skip. Just the grain, and the seam, and the joint, doing their twenty-million-year-old work. Everything my fingers said agreed with Schoch.

And everything my eyes said wanted to disagree. Because nature, the books all grant, fractures sandstone into steps and right angles *at small scale, here and there, often*. What my eyes were arguing with was the *and-then-it-kept-going*. The corner held plumb for two metres. The tread above ran flat and level for the length of a hallway. The repetition is the thing. One clean right angle is a Tuesday in sandstone. A staircase of them, stacked, at this scale, underwater — that's the part nobody, on either side of the credentialled split, can point to a second example of and say *see, nature does this too, all the time, here's another.* Above water, Schoch's similar formations are real and they are similar. They are also, the honest among the skeptics will tell you, *smaller*, and *fewer*, and not quite this.

That's the hole. It's not a tool mark — there's no confirmed tool mark, the believers haven't produced one in nearly forty years and they're owed the respect of saying so plainly. And it's not, on the other side, a matching natural twin of this size and this geometry that anyone can sail you out to. The hole is the shape of an absence on both shores. The case is split between two people with the same letters after their names, and the rock won't break the tie.

*"You feeling it?" the fish murmured. "That's the sound of a draw. Both heavyweights still standing. Crowd wants a knockout. Rock's not in the mood."*

Tanaka had been watching me feel the corner. She reached past me — that bare fingertip again — and laid it not on the sharp plumb line but two hand-spans to the right of it, on a face that looked, to my eyes, identical. Same crust, same green light. She pressed, and beckoned, and I came and felt where she'd felt.

This face stepped *wrong*. Where the corner ran plumb, this one had a hairline veer in it, a drift of maybe a finger's width over its run, the seam wandering off true the way a seam will when the bedding plane it follows has its own gentle dip. And once she'd put it under my hand I could feel that the surge worked this face differently — it caught here, eddied, where at the corner it had run clean. She held up two fingers. Pointed at the plumb corner. Pointed at the veering face. Turned her hand over, palm up, empty. *Same stone. Same sea. One ran true. One didn't. Why this one and not that one — that's the question, and I am not going to pretend I have closed it for you.*

She knew this Trench the way I know my own hands, and what her knowing produced was not an answer. It was a sharper question, handed over without being solved, which is a harder and more generous thing to give than a verdict, and I think I understood for the first time why she'd kept that one word in her own language.

Our LENS had been below us the whole time, hanging in the blue with the housing up, and I'd half-clocked the strobe not firing. When I looked he had the camera lowered, just holding it against his chest, watching the plumb corner with his actual eyes the way you'd watch a fire. He'd come here to make an image and the image was refusing to behave — was both threat and beauty, both made and broken, in the same frame, and you cannot caption that, and he knew it, and for once he wasn't trying.

Tanaka tapped her wrist. Slack was ending. We'd feel it in a minute or two, the Trench waking up, and when it woke it would not care that we had questions.

I went back to the corner one last time and put my hand flat against the cold of it. Worked or broken. Made or grown. My fingers, the best instrument I own, the thing that is how I see, said *I cannot tell you*, and for once that wasn't a failure of the instrument. It was the instrument reading the world exactly right.

The current came up the Trench then, soft and then less soft, a hand on the small of the back. Tanaka was already finning into it, easy, reading water none of us could see. Above her the survey unit's mooring buoy hung at the surface like a clean white verdict waiting to be issued.

She'd shown us the sea's case in full, edge by honest edge, and the sea's case was strong, and the sea's case had a corner two metres of true plumb wide that it couldn't quite account for.

We followed her up.
