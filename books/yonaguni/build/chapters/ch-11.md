# Chapter 11 — Pressure Drop

The barometer in the harbor office had a crack across its glass, an old one, the kind that gets pointed at and laughed about and never replaced. The needle sat just left of CHANGE. Mara photographed it anyway — close, the crack catching the morning, the dust on the brass case, the little chalk marks where somebody had tracked the pressure by hand for years before the digital one arrived and made the practice sentimental rather than useful. She did this. Lowered the lens to the dead instruments, the things nobody else thought to look at, because they were quieter than people and held still and didn't ask her what she'd seen.

Through the window the survey team's boat was loading. Two cases an hour, by her count. She'd been counting since six.

The National Maritime Survey Unit had three vessels now where last week there'd been one. Their crates were the modular kind, foam-cut interiors, stenciled with a serial logic she'd read off enough crime scenes and aid convoys to recognize as a system built by people who never expected to be questioned. Polite people. The unit's lead, a young man named Endo with a clipboard he didn't need and used anyway, had waved at her across the dock that morning. A real wave. There was nothing to hate in him, which was the part Mara found hardest to photograph.

"They moved the closure up," Saki said.

She'd come in without the door announcing her, the way she did everything — currents and conversations both, arriving where she already was. She had a wetsuit pushed down to her waist, the arms knotted at the front, and her hair was wet at the ends from a dawn dive nobody had cleared with anyone.

"How far up," Mara said.

"Tomorrow. Slack tide. After that the buoys go in and the permit becomes a thing you apply for in Naha and wait six months and get told no."

Mara let the camera hang. "Tomorrow's our window."

"Tomorrow's everyone's window." Saki looked at the cracked barometer like it owed her money. "There's one slack-water gap with light enough to work the Main Terrace. Eleven-forty. They want it too. Endo asked me this morning, very nicely, whether my charter would mind shifting to the afternoon."

"And you said."

"I said the afternoon current runs three knots through the Trench and I don't take guests into water that wants to fold them in half." She found the kettle, shook it, set it back. "He wrote it down. He writes everything down. It's how he makes a thing into a record and a record into a thing that already happened."

---

The official story, said plainly, is generous to everyone and unkind to certainty.

In 1986 a local diver named Kihachiro Aratake went looking for hammerhead schools off the south coast of Yonaguni and found instead a wall of stone that ran straighter than stone usually bothers to. Sandstone — fine to medium grained, mudstone in the seams, all of it Lower Miocene Yaeyama Group, which is to say the rock itself is around twenty million years old and was laid down flat and patient long before anyone wondered about it. Sandstone of this kind splits the way a stale cake splits: along its beds, and along the vertical joints that crack down through them, and where bed meets joint you get corners. Right angles, even. Steps, if the geometry feels generous. Tide and earthquake do the housekeeping.

That's the case Robert Schoch makes, and it is a good case, and the men who make it are not fools. They point at Yonaguni's own coastline, above the water, where the same beds make the same terraces in the open air with no one to carve them. They point at the absence — no confirmed tool mark in the peer-reviewed literature, no cultural layer, no midden, no abandoned chisel sulking in a crack. They say: when you have a hammer made of bedding planes, everything looks like a staircase.

And Masaaki Kimura, who has spent more hours over this rock than the rest of the argument combined, points at the Main Terrace. A hundred and fifty meters long. Forty wide. He maps the steps, the rooms with their right-angled corners, the Trench that runs parallel like a road that knew where it was going. He has photographs. He has a reading. He has never had the consensus, and he has never stopped diving.

Two credentialed geologists. Same stone. The line between them runs right down the middle of the water, and the water doesn't take sides.

Mara had read both papers on the boat over, in the order Olu the recorder had bound them, skeptic then believer then skeptic again, and she'd thought: *this is the rare honest fight.* Both men measuring. Neither lying. The hole sitting in the gap between them like something held in suspension, neither falling nor rising, just there.

---

She found Theo on the seawall, which was where the ND lead went when the day got too loud, and the day had been getting louder since the third survey boat arrived.

He had his hands flat on the concrete. Not gripping. Just reading it, the way he read everything, fingertips taking the temperature of a surface the rest of them only looked at. He didn't turn when she sat.

"They poured this in sections," he said. "You can feel the cold joints. Where one batch went off before the next came. Honest seams. Made things have honest seams." He pressed harder, as if to be sure. "That's the whole problem with down there."

"Tell me."

"Concrete tells you. Worked stone tells you. There's a — " he searched, and she watched him not find it, and let him not find it. "There's a confession in a made surface. Tool leaves itself behind. Even when you can't see the mark, the surface remembers being pushed. It's directional. It has a hand in it." He took his palm off the wall. "Down there the edges are too clean to be careless and too dumb to be careful. I keep waiting for the confession and the stone just — looks at me."

"Schoch would say that's because there's nothing to confess."

"I know what Schoch would say. I've felt it. The bedding plane thing — it's real. When I touched the long step on Tuesday it had that grain, that layered give, the way the rock split lazy along its own beds." Theo turned to her finally, and his eyes had the particular trouble in them, the trouble of a sense that had never failed him failing now not because it was weak but because the world was genuinely ambiguous and would not be bullied into a verdict. "But the corner above it. Where the wall meets the floor of the room. I put two fingers in it and it was square. Not square-ish. Square. And the rock doesn't owe me square."

The fish-voice in him, the part that heckled when he got too earnest, surfaced dry. "Or the rock owes the whole human race a square corner and we've been weeping over it for a million years like it's a miracle instead of just Tuesday's geology."

He almost smiled. It cost him something. "Tomorrow's the last touch. If I can't read it tomorrow I never read it. They close the door and I spend the rest of my life with a corner I can't name."

That was the weight. Not the mystery. The *missing* it. The specific grief of an instrument that knows it had one more chance to take the measurement and is about to lose the room.

---

They tried the potato again that afternoon, because Olu insisted, because the test deserved a fair last run and because all of them, Mara suspected, wanted something to do with their hands besides count crates.

The setup was honest. A block of the same sandstone — quarried, legally, from the island's own beds, papers and all, Olu had the receipt photographed — sunk in three meters of sheltered water inside the breakwater. Saki had picked the spot for current. Theo went down with a hammer and a flat chisel and the simple, stupid, beautiful intention to carve a right angle by hand into wet stone, the way a believer's ancient mason theoretically had.

Mara filmed it. She'd filmed a lot of things that didn't go the way the people doing them hoped.

The chisel skated. The wet rock didn't bite clean; it crumbled where it should have cut, and along the bedding it sheared in long lazy flakes that were *easier* than he wanted, that did half his work and the wrong half. He fought the grain. The grain won. After forty minutes underwater he surfaced with a block that had a thing on it you could call a corner the way you call a child's drawing of a horse a horse — because you love the child, not because you'd bet on it at the track.

It was a potato. It was always a potato.

"Rough," Theo said on the surface, spitting his reg, breathing hard. "Messy. Mine's got my whole arm in it. You can see where I slipped, where I dug in, where I lost my temper at minute thirty." He held the block up, sluicing. "It confesses. Look at it. It's *covered* in confession."

Saki took it from him. Turned it in her brown hands. Set her thumbnail along the so-called corner and looked at it the way she looked at everything, without hope or despair, just measurement.

"This is the thing nobody upstairs understands," she said. "You can't fake the clean one. We just proved you can't. Two thousand years ago, ten thousand, doesn't matter — a person with a tool makes *this*." She tapped the ruined corner. "Tired. Human. Full of slips." She looked south, toward where the Main Terrace lay under thirty feet of moving green. "And the sea makes the clean one. The sea has all the time and no temper and no arm. So when you go down there and find clean — " she handed the potato back, gently, like a thing that had earned respect by failing well " — the clean *is* the argument for natural. That's what Schoch is really saying. The cleaner it gets, the less human it looks."

"Then why does Kimura look at the same clean and see a hand," Mara said.

"Because at some point clean stops being lazy and starts being *intended*. Because nature is clean by accident, in patches, and gives up. And down there it doesn't give up." Saki was quiet a moment. "I've dived it three hundred times. I still don't know which I'm looking at. That's not a confession of failure. That's the most honest sentence I have."

She would not be drawn further. That was hers to withhold and she withheld it, and Mara, who had spent a career pointing a lens at people's worst and best moments and asking them to stay still for it, recognized the boundary and lowered the camera without being asked.

---

Endo came to the slip at dusk with two of his crew and a thermos and the clipboard, of course the clipboard.

"Dr. Tanaka." A small bow. To Mara, a nod, photographer to documentarian, a kind of professional courtesy that made her teeth ache. "I wanted to tell you directly. The closure is confirmed for the day after tomorrow at dawn. The buoys arrive tonight." He said it the way you'd report weather. "After installation, the site enters the archival protocol. Access by application."

"You're boxing it," Saki said.

"We're protecting it." And the worst part was he believed it, you could see it, a young man who'd been told the legend was hurting the science and the tourists were hurting the stone and that order was a form of care. "There are people who dive this who shouldn't. There are claims being made in the press — Atlantis, this and that. The monument deserves a clean record."

"The monument deserves the truth," Saki said. "And nobody has it. You'll archive a question and stamp it answered."

Endo wrote something down. Then he stopped writing and put the cap back on the pen, which Mara photographed without meaning to, the small surrender of a man caught between his orders and a thing he half-suspected was true. "The morning slack," he said. "Eleven-forty. We've scheduled our final mapping pass."

"So have I," said Saki.

"There's one window."

"There's one window."

They looked at each other across the slip, two professionals who respected each other and wanted opposite things from the same forty minutes of still water, and there was no villainy in it, which was somehow worse than villainy, which left nowhere clean to put the anger.

"I'll take the second half," Endo said at last. Quietly. "You take eleven-forty. We map after. The current will be turning by then but my people are good." He almost smiled. "And you said yourself you don't take guests into water that wants to fold them in half. I'm not a guest. It's our job to go where the water's bad."

He left the thermos on the bollard. A gift, or an apology, or a record of having been generous. Saki didn't touch it.

---

That night the buoy-boat ran its lights out past the breakwater, and Mara stood on the seawall where Theo had read the cold joints in the concrete and watched the survey vessel's wake unzip the black water and seal it again behind.

Theo came and stood beside her without his hands going to anything, which for him was a kind of stillness she'd learned to be glad of and afraid of in equal measure.

"Eleven-forty," he said. "How long's slack hold?"

"Saki says twenty minutes of real still. Maybe twenty-five." Mara watched the buoy lights diminish. "Then the Trench starts breathing."

"Twenty minutes to put my hand on a corner and find out whether the sea's a better mason than the whole of human history." He breathed out. The fish surfaced one last time, gentle now. "No pressure."

Below them, somewhere off the south coast, the Main Terrace lay in the dark holding its line straight as a held breath, a hundred and fifty meters of *maybe* under the moving sea, and it had held it for thousands of years and would hold it whether they read it or not, whether the buoys went in or not, whether the case was ever closed.

"One dive," Mara said.

"One dive," said Saki, from behind them, where she'd been the whole time, reading the water and the men with the lights and the pressure she could feel dropping behind the cracked glass in the office. "Get the gas sorted tonight. Sleep if you can." She turned to go, then stopped, her face half-lit. "And Theo. Tomorrow. Whatever your hands tell you down there — if they tell you nothing, that's not nothing. That's the answer most days. Most days the honest thing is the corner you can't name."

She went up the slip into the dark, and the barometer in the office, had anyone been watching, slid one more degree toward STORM, and the door to the last dive stood open exactly the width of twenty minutes of still water.
