# Chapter 8 — Above Water, Below Surface

The recorder — call him Pieter, because that is what the others called him when they wanted something logged, and there was always something to be logged — went up the cliff path at Sannin-dai with a clipboard he didn't need and a tape measure he did. He had stopped trusting his memory at nineteen, when a professor had laughed at a number he'd misremembered by a single decimal, and the laugh had cost him a grade and a small private dignity he had never quite got back. So now he wrote everything. He wrote the time. He wrote that the wind off the East China Sea came up the rock face in gusts that smelled of salt and diesel and, faintly, of someone's lunch from the harbour below. He wrote that the path was dry sandstone and that it rang slightly under the heel.

He wrote: *Above-water analog. Per Schoch. Compare.*

That was the assignment, more or less. If the monument under the water was just stone behaving like stone, then stone up here, in air and daylight, ought to be doing the same thing where nobody could call it a temple. Robert Schoch had said as much — the man had dived the site himself in 1997, surfaced, and pointed at the cliffs of Yonaguni and the neighbouring islands and said, in effect, *look, the rock does this on its own, you just can't usually walk on the version that's drowned.* It was a serious argument made by a serious geologist, and Pieter intended to give it the full courtesy of the tape measure.

"This way." Dr. Tanaka was already ahead of him, not waiting, the way locals never wait. She wore a faded rashguard under a fisherman's vest and moved on the rock like the rock owed her money. "The good outcrop is where the path turns. Mind the loose face. People fall here for cameras."

The LENS was behind them both, of course, with the camera, finding the place where the light fell off the cliff edge and made the wind visible in the spray. He had not lowered it once since the harbour. Pieter had stopped expecting him to.

---

Here is the official story, told straight, because it deserves to be.

Sandstone fractures. It is one of the most reliable things sandstone does. The Yaeyama Group that makes up these islands — laid down in the Lower Miocene, something on the order of twenty million years ago, when this was riverbed and shallow sea and not yet anything you could stand on — is bedded. It came down in layers, and layers part along their seams. That gives you the flat. Then the whole region got squeezed and shoved by the same tectonic temper that makes Japan Japan, and stone under that kind of stress cracks along planes, often at angles startlingly close to ninety degrees, because that is the geometry that relieves the stress most efficiently. Bedding plane horizontal. Joint set vertical. Where the two meet, you get a step. Where many meet, you get a staircase that nobody built.

You don't have to take it on faith. You can climb up to Sannin-dai with a tape measure and find it.

Which is what Pieter did.

He found a block the size of a small car that had parted clean off the cliff along a bedding plane and left behind a face flat enough to read a book on. He laid his hand on it the way the ND lead had taught him to, even though he didn't have the gift — flat, patient, listening with the skin. It was cool from the sea wind and very slightly gritty, like fine sugar pressed into mortar. He measured the parting plane. He measured the vertical break where the block had let go. He wrote down the angle the two made: *~88°.* Close enough to a right angle that, underwater, blurred by silt and a meter of marine growth and the human appetite for meaning, you would swear a mason had snapped a chalk line.

"You see it," Tanaka said. She wasn't asking.

"I see a step," Pieter said. "Made by nobody."

"Good. Now count them."

He looked up. The cliff above the flat block went up in three more like it, then four, then the count got away from him where the face leaned back into shadow. A flight of stairs for a giant who had never existed, going nowhere, holding nothing up, asking for nothing. He wrote: *Stacked terracing, natural, ≥7 risers visible, no tooling.* He ran his thumb along a riser edge. It was rounded, sea-rounded, weathered soft — the edge of something old that the weather had been at for a long time without any opinion about it.

"This is the case," he said, half to himself. "This is Schoch's case, just standing up in the air."

The fish-voice — the dry one, the recorder's opposite number, who had stayed at the foot of the cliff because climbing was for people with something to prove — drifted up on the wind from below.

"So we drove to an island, hired a boat, and learned that rocks have *stairs.* Riveting. Wake me when the rocks file a building permit."

Pieter wrote that down too. He logged everything.

---

Tanaka took them along the headland to where the path gave out and the rock ran straight down into the surf. She sat on a flat shelf as easily as another person sits in a chair and looked at the water for a while before she spoke, and Pieter had the sense, which he often had around people who actually belonged somewhere, that she was deciding how much to give.

"When I was a girl," she said, "the old men would not say the monument was anything. You understand? They would not say *made.* They would not say *natural.* If you asked, they got a face." She made the face — flat, polite, shut like a door. "Yonaguni is the end of Japan. You can see Taiwan from the lighthouse on a clear day; the children are taught that the island is the last step before the world stops being ours. There is pride in being last. There is also — " she searched for it " — caution. A small place learns that when outsiders come asking what something *is,* they have usually already decided what they want it to be, and they want the island to agree."

The LENS shifted, framing her against the drop-off, and then — Pieter watched it happen, the small private event he would not log — lowered the camera a hand's width, so that he was looking at her with his own eyes for a moment instead of through the glass.

"Aratake found it for the tourists," Tanaka went on. "1986. A diver looking for hammerheads, and he comes up over the terrace, and — " she opened her hand, let the wind take it. "After that, everyone wanted it to be something. The believers wanted Atlantis. The scientists wanted a clean answer. The town wanted the boats. My teacher, who taught me the stone, wanted it left *uncertain,* because uncertain is what it honestly is, and he thought certainty was the dishonest thing people did to a place to take it from it."

"And you?" Pieter asked. He kept his voice level, the way he kept his hand level on the stone.

"I want," Tanaka said, "for you to do your test first. Then I will tell you what I want." She almost smiled. "I have read about your crew. You break things to find out how they were made. So break something."

---

The breaking — the potato — they did the next morning, in shallow water off the harbour, on a slab of the same Yaeyama sandstone that Tanaka pointed them to with the resignation of a teacher handing out a problem she already knew the answer to.

The ND lead did it. This was their hour, and the rest of the crew got out of the light. They had a flat chisel and a wooden mallet and a slate to score along, and the simple, dead-straight goal of cutting one clean right-angled step into the stone — one riser, one tread, the corner sharp — to match the corners the believers swore were carved.

It is harder than it has any business being. Pieter logged it minute by minute. Sandstone is not granite; it doesn't fight you. It *crumbles,* which is worse. The chisel bit and the rock didn't part along a line, it parted along its own lines — the bedding it remembered from twenty million years ago, the grain it had been waiting to follow. The ND lead chased a straight edge and the stone gave them a curve. They scored deeper and the corner sheared off in a chunk that took a hand's-width of the tread with it. Underwater the work was slower, the mallet drag-heavy, the silt blooming up to hide the very cut they were trying to read.

When they finished — when the air ran low and the light went sideways — they had a thing. It was a step, technically. It was rough as a healed scar, the corner blunt and ragged, the tread dished where the rock had popped out wrong, the whole of it announcing in every grain that a clumsy primate with a stick had been here and tried.

The ND lead surfaced and pulled the regulator and floated on their back a moment, looking at the sky, breathing.

"It feels wrong," they said. "It feels — *worked.* You can feel my hand all over it. Every centimeter is a decision I made or a decision I lost. The terrace down there —" they tipped their head toward the deep water, toward the monument "— doesn't feel like decisions. It feels like *agreement.* Like the stone and the sea agreed on something and that was that."

Pieter logged it as: *Manual replication = rough; corner shear, tread dish; subject reports tactile signature of human work absent from monument.* Then, because he was honest, in the margin: *Subject is the best reader I have. This is not nothing.*

The fish-voice, from the boat: "So the lesson is the sea is a better stonemason than us. Comforting. I, too, would lose a chisel fight to the Pacific Ocean."

Nobody argued.

---

But here is the hole, and Pieter wrote it last, and slowest, because it was the thing he could not make sit down.

He had his above-water analog. He had Sannin-dai in his book, seven risers and an eighty-eight-degree corner and not a tool mark in sight — Schoch's argument standing up in the air for anyone to measure. The rock makes stairs. The rock makes flats. The rock makes right angles. All true. All logged. All real.

So that evening he sat on the harbour wall with his clipboard and the photographs the LENS had handed him, the dry prints still smelling of fixer, and he laid the above-water beside the below-water and did the one thing a recorder is for, which is to put the like things next to the like things and read the difference.

The above-water terraces were terraces. They were also small, and broken-edged, and rounded by weather, and they petered out — three risers, five, seven, then chaos, then a tumble of fallen block, the way natural fracturing does when you give it room and time. A staircase that started and lost its nerve.

The thing under the water did not lose its nerve.

The Main Terrace runs roughly a hundred and fifty meters long and forty wide. That is not a number you measure with a tape over lunch; that is a number you measure with a survey, and the surveys agree on the order of it. A hundred and fifty meters of flat, with steps that hold their line, with corners that keep being corners step after step after step — at a scale, and with a consistency, that the cliffs above the tide line simply do not produce. Pieter had walked the cliffs all day looking for the above-water match, the natural formation that would let him close the book and say *see, it's just bigger underwater, that's all.* He had found stone doing every individual thing the monument did. He had not found stone doing all of them at once, that clean, that vast, in one place.

That was the gap. Not a tool mark — there are no confirmed tool marks, and he was not about to invent one. Not an inscription — Kimura saw inscriptions; the peer-reviewed literature did not. The gap was *negative,* which is the hardest kind to hold: nobody, in decades, had produced the above-water natural formation that matched Yonaguni for geometry *and* scale at once. The believers couldn't show you the chisel. The skeptics couldn't show you the twin.

He wrote it down exactly, because it was real and because it was checkable and because it was not his to resolve: *Above-water analogs reproduce every feature individually. None reproduces all features at this scale in one formation. The match is absent. Cited: no equivalent published.*

Then he sat there with the photographs in the dark and did not write anything for a while, which for Pieter was a kind of vertigo.

---

Tanaka came and sat beside him on the wall. She had a flask of something hot and she poured him a cap of it without asking, which is how you know someone has decided you are all right.

"You found the cliffs," she said.

"I found everything," Pieter said. "Every piece. Separately."

"Yes."

"And not the one place where it all happens together. Above water."

Tanaka looked out at the dark sea where the monument was, twenty-five meters down, holding its line.

"My teacher used to say," she said, "that the island is honest. It does not lie to you. It only refuses to tell you the part you most want to know." She capped the flask. "You did your test. Tomorrow I will show you the currents, how the stone and the water work — and you will see things that could go either way, and I will not tell you which way. Not yet."

"Why not yet?"

"Because the survey people," Tanaka said, and now there was iron in it, the protective iron Pieter had heard under everything she'd said all day, "want to come and measure it once, fast, and write *natural* or write *artifact* on a form and lock the boats and call it documented. And then it is finished. Then it is a fact and not a place." She stood. "I will tell you what it is for when I am sure you can hold the not-knowing without dropping it. Most people can't. They reach for the answer and squeeze the wonder right out of it."

She went up the harbour toward the lights of the town.

Pieter looked at his clipboard. Under the last line he had written — *the match is absent* — he added the time, because he always added the time. Then he sat a while longer in the dark, holding the not-knowing, testing whether he could.

It was heavier than the tape measure. It did not ring under the heel. But he found, to his own surprise, that he had not dropped it yet.
