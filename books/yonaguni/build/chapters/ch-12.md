# Chapter 12 — Descent to the Trench

The water at slack tide goes still the way a held breath goes still — not empty, just waiting. Mara felt it on the back of her hands before she felt it anywhere else, that pause in the current the divers had been counting on all morning, the moment when Yonaguni stopped breathing long enough to let you in.

Tanaka rolled backward off the gunwale without a word. She didn't gear-check theatrically, didn't give the thumbs-up the way the survey divers did, all GoPro and procedure. She just went, and the sea took her, and by the time Mara's own mask cleared and the green-blue resolved into something she could read, Tanaka was already eight meters down and dropping like she had an appointment with the floor.

Mara followed. Behind her, the bubbles of the others — Devra with the slate strapped to her forearm, Hollis with the camera housing he treated like a sleeping animal, and somewhere up top, on the boat, Jakobus, who did not dive and had never pretended otherwise. *The road, not the lead*, he'd said that morning, handing her the spare reel like it was a baton. *I read what's on the table. Down there it's Saki's table.*

The light changed at twelve meters. It always did. The blue thickened and the edges came up out of it — and that was the thing, the thing the brochures and the cranks and Kimura and Schoch all agreed on before they agreed on nothing else: the edges. They came up *clean*. A terrace, broad and flat as a loading dock, and below it another, and below that the long fall of stacked steps the believers called a staircase and the skeptics called bedding planes parted along their natural seams.

*Could go either way*, the fish said in her ear, the dry little voice she'd given the doubt years ago so it would stop sounding like her own. *Loading dock or landslide. Both real popular underwater.*

She put her glove on the lip of the first terrace.

This was the gift, if you wanted to call it that — and Mara had spent enough years being told it was a deficit that she'd learned to call it nothing at all, just *how I touch things*. Worked stone and fractured stone do not feel the same. A fracture has a memory of violence in it, a directionality, the stone parting the way it always wanted to part. A worked surface fights its own grain; you can feel the argument in it, the place where a hand overrode the rock's preference.

The lip under her glove felt like neither and both. It ran straight — genuinely, stubbornly straight, the kind of straight that made the back of her neck go cold — but when she dragged her fingertips along it, she got the long clean break of a bedding plane, sandstone shearing the way Yaeyama sandstone has been shearing for twenty million years, since before there was anyone with a chisel to argue with it.

She lifted her hand. Put it back down a meter along. Same.

*See*, said the fish, *that's the trick of it. The hole's not in the stone. The hole's in your hand.*

Tanaka had paused below her, hovering with that horizontal stillness divers spend a decade learning, finning so small it didn't show. She watched Mara's gloved hand on the terrace. Then she pointed — not at the lip, but down, into the long dark cut that ran parallel to the whole formation. The Trench. The Road. The feature the maps drew as a corridor and the geologists drew as a joint widened by the sea pulling out a softer layer.

They descended into it together.

---

The Trench is the place where the argument gets honest, because the Trench is where the monument stops being a postcard and starts being a *space*. It runs maybe ten meters wide in places, walls rising sheer on either side, and the light comes down it in shafts that move when the surface moves. Marine life packs the edges — soft coral, the encrusting growth that makes every measurement a negotiation, the small fish that hang in the column and scatter when your bubbles reach them.

Devra worked the slate. She had a system: depth, heading, a quick sketch, a note on the encrustation because the encrustation was the thing everyone underplayed. *You can't read tool marks through forty years of coral*, she'd said on the boat, *and you can't prove there were never any, either. That's not a finding. That's a closed door someone painted to look like a wall.* She wrote it down now, the heading of the Trench, and beside it a single word Mara couldn't read upside-down but recognized by the shape: *straight*.

Hollis had stopped finning.

That was the tell with Hollis. The crew had learned it over four books — the moment he went still in the water and the camera came up slow, like he was lifting it against his own better judgment. He'd photographed worse than sunken rock. He'd photographed things that came back to find him at three in the morning, and the camera had been the only thing between him and them, the rectangle that turned a horror into a composition you could survive looking at. Down here there was no horror. Just a wall of sandstone too straight to be comfortable, going up into the green, and Hollis behind the housing, not pressing the shutter.

Tanaka touched his arm.

She didn't tell him to shoot. She put two fingers on the wall, just below where his lens was pointed, and held them there until he lowered the camera and looked at the wall instead of the picture of the wall. Then she moved on. It was the gentlest correction Mara had ever seen administered underwater, and Hollis followed her with the housing hanging at his side like a thing he'd forgotten he was carrying.

They came to the right-angled corner.

---

Here is the official story, told straight, the way it deserves to be told.

A man named Kihachiro Aratake — a local diver, looking for hammerhead grounds, not for Atlantis — found this in 1986, and the world did what the world does. Kimura of the University of the Ryukyus came and mapped it and read in it a ceremonial platform, a temple, maybe a city; he saw stairs and terraces and what he believed were carvings, and he published, and he stood by it. Schoch of Boston University came and read the same stone and saw the Yaeyama Group doing exactly what sandstone does when it's faulted and jointed and worked over by earthquake and tide — flat fractures along the bedding, vertical fractures along the joints, the two intersecting at the angles that so unsettle the human eye because the human eye is built to find rectangles meaningful. He pointed to formations above water on the nearby islands, same rock, same angles, no diver required. He pointed to the absence — no confirmed tool mark in the peer-reviewed literature, no cultural layer, no proven hand.

Two credentialed geologists. Same stone. No consensus. Decades on, none.

That is not a failure of the experts. That is the most honest thing in this whole book. Both of them are looking at something real and refusing to lie about it, and the gap between them is not ignorance — it's the actual shape of what we know, which is: *we don't*.

The corner Tanaka had brought them to was the cleanest on the site.

Two faces of stone meeting at very near ninety degrees, the vertical wall and the horizontal terrace, and the edge between them ran true for longer than any edge has a right to run. Mara put both hands on it, one on each face, the way you'd hold a doorframe.

The vertical face read like a joint. Cold, clean, the parting-memory of stone that had sheared along a plane it was always going to shear along. Fine. Natural. The fish was satisfied.

The horizontal face read the same.

But the *edge* — the line where the two met — that was the place her gift went quiet. Because a joint and a bedding plane meeting at a right angle is just two natural surfaces happening to intersect; the corner itself isn't worked, it's a coincidence of geometry, the rock being the rock in two directions at once. She knew that. She'd read that on the boat, in Schoch's own words. And her hand, running the edge, wanted to agree.

It was just too long.

A natural corner is a corner for as far as the two planes happen to align, and then the alignment wanders, because nothing in fracture is committed to a heading. This edge did not wander. It held its line through the encrustation, through a dip where the terrace stepped, through three meters of green water, and it was still holding it when Mara ran out of arm.

*Okay*, said the fish, and for once it didn't have a follow-up.

---

They had tried, two days before, to make one of these.

Down in the test patch Tanaka had cleared for them — a slab of the same Yaeyama sandstone, broken loose and lying flat — Devra and Mara had spent a whole tide-window trying to cut a straight edge by hand. Not to fake the monument. To *understand* it. To put a chisel against this rock and feel what it would take.

It took everything and gave back a potato.

The sandstone didn't cut; it crumbled. It came away in lumps where you wanted a line and held stubborn where you wanted it to release, and the marine growth fought every stroke, and the visibility went to soup with the first cloud of silt, so that you were carving blind inside your own mess. After ninety minutes Mara had produced an edge that wandered like a drunk and a face like a chewed thumbnail. Worked stone fights its grain — and her edge had the fight in it, all right, every ugly indecisive millimeter of it. It read, unmistakably, as *made*. Because that's what made looks like, down here, with hands. Rough. Reluctant. Human.

The monument's edge did not read as made. It read as *decided*.

Which was, of course, exactly what the natural-origin argument predicted. Fracture is decisive. Fracture commits. A bedding plane doesn't agonize over its line the way a diver with a chisel does — it just goes, clean, the whole length of the bed at once. The cleanness was the *evidence for nature*, not against it. Mara knew this. She'd written it in her own notes. The potato proved the skeptics' point: a hand makes a mess, and this was no mess, therefore no hand.

And yet.

She hung at the corner with both gloves on the impossible edge and could not make her body believe either thing, and that was the gift not failing but doing its actual job, which had never been to give answers. It was to feel the exact weight of not knowing, and to refuse to set it down early.

---

Tanaka was watching her again.

The Okinawan held position above the corner with the boat-time ticking somewhere in all their heads — slack wouldn't last, the current would come back like a closing hand, and up top the survey unit's RIB was already on the water, their schedule moved up *again*, their orders to document and archive and lock the legend in a box where it would stop embarrassing the maps. This was the crew's last window. Everyone in the water knew it.

Tanaka uncoiled and finned along the terrace, beckoning, and they went after her — past the corner, past the steps, to the long northern run of the Main Terrace where the formation's great straight margin lay.

A hundred and fifty meters. That was the published number, the Main Terrace, a hundred and fifty meters long and forty wide, and Tanaka took them along the edge of it the way you'd walk someone down a pier. She knew where the encrustation thinned. She knew which way the silt would drift if you breathed wrong. She'd been reading this stone since before any of them could spell Yaeyama, and she read it now without a single gesture toward proving anything, just *here, and here, and look at this*, a local showing visitors her own street.

She stopped at the far end and let them catch up and pointed down the length of the margin, back the way they'd come.

Mara turned and sighted along it.

A hundred and fifty meters of edge, running away from her into the green until the green ate it, and it did not bend. It did not wander. It held its line the whole impossible length, straighter than the terrace had any business being, straighter than fracture promised and straighter than hands could manage, and the two arguments she'd been carrying since the surface — *natural, decisive, clean as a joint* and *made, worked, fighting its grain* — both of them ran out of road at exactly the same place, somewhere out in the water where the line went on being straight after every reason she had for it had stopped.

Beside her, Hollis raised the camera.

This time he didn't lower it. He sighted down the long margin the way Mara was sighting down it, and the shutter went, once, and Mara understood that he wasn't taking a picture of the answer. There wasn't one. He was taking a picture of the question — the whole hundred and fifty meters of it — because the question was the most beautiful and most unbearable thing in the water, and the camera was finally, for once, the right tool for what was actually there.

Tanaka watched the line too. Her face behind the mask gave nothing. She had not yet said what it was for. She had not yet said whether it was *for* anything. She held that — the last word, the local's word — the way she held her trim, without apparent effort and without any intention of letting go before she chose to.

The current shifted in Mara's ear, the first faint pull of the sea waking up.

The straight edge ran on into the green, and did not stop, and did not explain itself, and the four of them hung above it in the failing slack, and out beyond the line, just past where the water turned everything to nothing, there was the place where the answer would have been, if there had been one.

Mara put her glove back on the margin.

It ran straight under her hand, all the way to the end of her reach, and then further, and she had no way at all to know which thing she was touching.
