A sunken stone city, or just a trick of the sea? The crew dives Yonaguni, the "Japanese Atlantis"—a massive underwater formation torn between two credentialed experts: Kimura, who reads a monument carved by ancient hands, and Schoch, who reads natural stone fractured by time and tide. The official story is split right down the middle, and both sides bring the receipts.

The crew comes in playing it straight: the skeptics’ case—natural jointing, no proven tool marks, similar shapes above water—gets full respect; the believers’ case—sharp angles, terraces, the sheer scale—gets its due. But when they try to replicate those geometric lines by hand, their best efforts always turn out rougher, messier—a potato. The sea, it seems, makes a better architect than any diver with a chisel.

But then comes the hole: after decades, no one can point to a single, definitive tool mark or cultural layer—or, on the flip side, show another underwater formation of this size, so geometric, made purely by nature. The two-credentialed-geologists split is real, unresolved, and the monument sits right in that gap.

The clean-handed adversary: a slick, government-funded survey team with orders to "document and archive"—read: lock down the site, box up the mystery, and keep the legend off the books. The chase is on to read the stones before the survey closes the site for good.

The local lead—an Okinawan marine geologist and veteran diver—out-reads the crew underwater, knows every current, every edge, and holds the key to how the monument is seen at home. The ND crew lead is the texture-reader, hypersensitive to the feel of worked versus broken stone—a gift that, at Yonaguni, keeps running into ambiguity. This is the book where even Jakobus’s made-thing reading hits its limit: the function, the “what is it for,” stays just out of reach, the answer the local alone can choose to give or withhold.

The wink: Is it a monument, or just a masterpiece of geology? The book leaves you in the maybe, standing at the edge of the last step, letting the water carry the question.

Back-matter promise: every real measurement, every debated claim, and every invented flourish (clearly flagged)—so the reader can chase the rabbit all the way down, and maybe, just maybe, see something everyone else missed.
