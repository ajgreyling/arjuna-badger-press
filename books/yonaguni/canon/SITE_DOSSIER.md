SITE: Yonaguni Monument (Japan)

REAL (all facts verifiable in published literature or first-hand investigation):
- LOCATION: Off the southern coast of Yonaguni, Ryukyu Islands, Japan; depth ranges from ~5 to 25 meters below sea level.
- DISCOVERY: First brought to wide attention in 1986 by local diver Kihachiro Aratake.
- GEOLOGY: The formation consists primarily of medium to fine-grained sandstone and mudstone of the Lower Miocene Yaeyama Group.
- STRUCTURE: Features include flat, terraced platforms; broad, straight steps; right-angled corners; a prominent "Main Terrace" ~150m long and ~40m wide; a "Trench" or "Road" feature running parallel to the monument.
- TOOL MARKS: No confirmed, universally-accepted human tool marks have been published in peer-reviewed literature.
- AGE: The rocks themselves date to the Lower Miocene (~20 million years ago). Any hypothetical carving would need to post-date last subaerial exposure; estimates for when the area was last above sea level range from 2,000 to 10,000 years ago.
- MARINE LIFE: The monument is heavily encrusted with marine organisms, complicating investigation.
- LOCAL CONTEXT: The Ryukyu Islands have a long prehistory but no known culture with the engineering capacity or motive to create such a structure at such a scale, according to mainstream archaeology.

CLAIMED (disputed, debated, or unresolved):
- MAN-MADE: Marine geologist Masaaki Kimura (University of the Ryukyus) claims the monument is a submerged, man-made structure—possibly a ceremonial platform, temple, or city—citing apparent stairs, terraces, and what he interprets as carvings and inscriptions. Kimura has published site maps and underwater photographs, but his claims are not accepted by the majority of geologists or archaeologists.
- NATURAL: Geologist Robert Schoch (Boston University) and others argue the monument is the result of natural fracturing of sandstone along bedding planes and vertical joints, plus the action of earthquakes and marine erosion. They point out similar formations on nearby islands above water, and the lack of unequivocal tool marks.
- SPLIT: There is no consensus among credentialed geologists; the "made or not" question is genuinely unresolved in the scientific literature.
- TOOL MARKS (CLAIMED): Kimura and some divers report possible tool marks and inscriptions; these have not been verified in independent peer-reviewed studies.
- CULTURAL CONNECTIONS: Kimura claims possible connections to Okinawan folklore and ancient Ryukyuan culture; these links are speculative and not recognized by mainstream historians.

UNRESOLVED (the "hole"):
- The site presents features (e.g., clean right angles, flat terraces, apparent steps) that are unusual for natural sandstone fracturing at this scale and underwater location.
- No definitive evidence of either human modification or exclusively natural origin has been produced, despite decades of investigation.
- The split between credentialed experts (Kimura vs. Schoch) remains, with neither side able to close the case.
