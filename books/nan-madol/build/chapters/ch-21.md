# Chapter 21 — The Spaces Remain

The tide was on its way out, and the canals knew it before anyone in the boat did.

Mira felt it first in the hull — that small shifting tug as the water decided which way it wanted to go, leaning the skiff a half-degree toward the open lagoon, then correcting. Keoki had cut the outboard back to a mutter. He let the ebb do the work. The reef did not hurry, and neither, this morning, did he.

She sat in the bow with her back to the prow, facing the others, facing the city. That was the only way she ever rode a boat — facing in, watching the faces, reading the wind off the water by the way it moved their hair instead of by the sound of it. Yusuf had learned this about her months ago and stopped offering her the comfortable seat at the stern. The good seat, the one she wanted, was always the one that let her see.

Behind her, Nandauwas slid back across the morning.

The walls did not get smaller so much as they got *quieter*. Up close, on the islet, in among the basalt, the thing had a roar to it — not sound but pressure, the way standing under a great old building presses on you. Now, from the water, with two hundred metres of ebbing channel opening between, the roar settled to a hum and then to a held note. Seven metres of stacked stone, header laid across stretcher, end-grain and long-face alternating the way Jakobus had walked his thumb along it on the first day — *this binds that, that binds this, log-cabin, nothing holding it but its own weight and somebody's enormous patience* — and the morning light came in low and raking and found every joint.

Nature pre-cut those columns. She kept having to remind herself of that, because the wall did not let you believe it. The basalt had cooled and cracked into its long hexagonal logs all on its own, thirty thousand years ago, on the Sokehs side of the island, twenty-five miles away across open water. The stone was a gift. The lifting was not.

Frik was filming the wall over her shoulder, the small camera held loose, no tripod, no plan. She watched his jaw. He had stopped narrating to himself somewhere back on the islet. That was new for him.

*"~750,000 tonnes,"* the fish had said yesterday, riding the gunwale of the dinghy in Yusuf's voice doing the fish's voice, because somebody had to, *"over four centuries, that's eighteen-fifty tonnes a year, every year, for four hundred years, on a reef, by hand, no crane, no ramp, and they left you NOTHING. No sledge. No rope. No diary. You absolute show-offs."* And then, quieter, because even the heckle had run out of road: *"do it again, then. Go on. We'll wait."*

They had tried to do it again. That was Chapter One, six weeks and a lifetime ago — the rig, the engineer, the single column on the reef flat, the careful modern attempt to raft and lift one basalt log the way the tidy story said it had been done ten thousand times over. It had come out a potato. The modern team, with the best kit and a plan and a budget, had made a graceless lumpen thing, and the city had stood behind them, flawless and silent, declining to explain.

Nobody had reproduced it. That was the fact, the cold one, the cited one. *Replication has failed* — not *it cannot be done*, never that, the honest word was *not yet*, and the gap between *not yet* and *cannot* was exactly the size of the wonder. Mira had spent her whole life in that gap. The space between what a face said and what it meant. The space between *we don't know* and *we will not say*.

The space between. *Nan Madol.* The water between the stone.

She looked away from the wall, for the first time in an hour, and found Edith.

Edith had the big camera in her lap. Not raised. Lying across her thighs like a sleeping animal, lens cap on, the strap looped twice around her wrist the way she carried it when she had decided, for the day, not to shoot. She was watching the canal mouth open behind the boat, the V of disturbed water closing itself smooth again as they passed. She had taken almost nothing here. Three frames, maybe, in six weeks — the coral fill at low water, a child's bare foot on a basalt step that the custodian had nodded permission for, the back of Jakobus's neck as he read a corner. Nothing of the dead. Nothing of the enclosure. Nothing she had not been told, in some language or other, that she could keep.

A photographer who does not take the picture. The whole book had been asking her to lower the thing, and here, today, on the way out, she had simply set it down in her lap and let the place keep its own face.

Mira caught her eye. Edith made the small flat motion with two fingers — *enough, that's enough* — that the crew had all started using because Mira's eyes were the channel that always worked. Mira returned it. *Enough.*

The custodian stood on the seaward edge of the islet, where the dressed stone gave way to the rubble apron and the rubble gave way to the reef. Pwurahdo. He had come down to see them off, which he had not had to do, which protocol did not require, which was therefore the whole message. He was not waving. He had his hands at his sides and his weight even and he was watching the boat go the way a man watches weather — not anxious, not wistful, just *seeing them out*, all the way, until the channel turned and the mangrove would take them.

He had told them *we don't know* on the fourth day. The official line, the same one the island had handed to every outsider who had ever come ashore wanting the answer for free. The archaeologists said it too, and meant it cleanly: the transport method, undocumented; the lift, unreconstructed; the choice of a foodless, waterless reef for a city, unexplained on any practical ledger. Real scholars, careful ones, leaving the gap open because the gap was honestly open.

And Pwurahdo had said the same three words, and Mira had watched his mouth say them, and had seen — three beats before anyone, the way she always saw, because she had never been given any other way to know a room — that his *we don't know* was not the scholars' *we don't know.*

His was a door. Held shut. By a hand.

She had not picked the lock. She had named it to the crew, once, low, on the second night, in the language they were all still bad at and getting better at, *fingers careful: he knows something, and it is his, and it is not for us* — and they had set their hands down. All of them. Yusuf, who could not let a funding trail lie. Frik, who narrated cathedrals to himself. Edith, who took the world's face for a living. They had each carried into this place the specific hunger of people the world had spent a lifetime calling *too much* — too watchful, too literal, too unable to stop reading the room — and the reef had asked them, gently, for the one thing none of them had ever been allowed to offer before.

To not need to know.

The boat came level with the last big islet and the channel began to bend.

"There was a buyer," Yusuf said, to the water, not to anyone. He was sitting low, arms on his knees. "Did I tell you what the brochure called it. The investment deck."

"You told us," Frik said.

"*'An unencumbered heritage asset.'*" Yusuf said it the way you'd hold up something dead by the tail. "Because the islanders say they don't know. You see the move. *They* say it's a mystery, so it's nobody's, so it's empty, so it's for sale." He shook his head, slow. "A vacancy is the easiest thing in the world to take. You don't even have to lie. You just point at the silence and call it an absence."

That was the part Mira had been built to read, and it was the part she would carry off this reef and not be able to put down. Two silences that looked from outside completely identical. The custodian's, and the buyer's. One a held door. One a wrecking ball with a smile. From the satellite they were the same blank. Only from inside the room, only watching the hand on the door, could you tell that one of them was *protecting* a thing and the other was *erasing* it so it could be sold as ground.

The buyer had not won. That was not a triumph the crew could claim, and they did not claim it — the deal had died of its own contradictions, of a state that knew exactly whose grave it was, of a *Nahnmwarki* order that did not need a foreign film crew to defend it and had defended itself for four hundred years. The crew had read the silence. They had not saved the place. The place had never been theirs to save, any more than the answer had been theirs to take.

*"You came all this way,"* the fish observed, from somewhere near the engine, *"to a stone city nobody can explain, sat with a man who can explain it, and DIDN'T ASK. You're broken. You're beautifully, beautifully broken."*

Nobody laughed out loud. Frik smiled at the water. Mira watched the smile reach his eyes and stay there, which was how she knew it was true and not performed.

She turned back to the city one more time.

The walls had gone to a single dark line now, low against the green of Temwen behind, the seven metres flattened by distance into a stroke of charcoal a child could have drawn. You could not see the joints anymore. You could not see the header and the stretcher, the patient binding, the four centuries at eighteen hundred tonnes a year. You could not see the fifty-tonne cornerstone that nobody had explained the lifting of and nobody, in her hearing, ever would.

What you could see was this: someone had loved their dead enough to build them a city across the sea, on a reef that grew no food and held no water, a place where the living could not stay — *would* not stay, perhaps, was perhaps the entire point, a metropolis for the ones who no longer needed to eat or drink, kept apart by a moat of ocean from everything that still did. She did not say that. The narrator of her own head did not even quite say it. It was not a fact. It was a shape the facts left, the way a body leaves a shape in a bed, and the shape was not hers to fill in with words and call it solved.

What is it for. Jakobus's question, the only one he ever brought, the one he had carried to the canal's edge on the third day and then — she had watched his hands do it — *handed across the water* to the custodian, openly, with both palms, *yours to give or to keep.*

Pwurahdo had kept it.

That was the answer. Not a riddle the crew had failed to crack. A thing a man held in his two hands and had every right in the world not to put down for them. The wonder did not shrink when he kept it. It got larger. It filled the whole channel and the whole sky and the twenty-five miles of open water the stone had crossed, and it would still be exactly that large tomorrow, when the crew were gone and the tide came back in and the canals filled and the city stood in its own water keeping its own counsel, the way it had since before her grandmother's grandmother, the way it would after.

The boat reached the bend.

Mira looked at the figure on the apron of stone — small now, a dark upright mark where the dressed wall met the reef — and she lifted one hand. Not a wave. Open palm, held a moment. *Seen. Thank you. We are leaving it as it was.*

Across the widening water, Pwurahdo lifted his hand the same way. Held it. Let it down.

Then the mangrove came in from the right, green and close and smelling of low tide and mud and salt, and took the line of charcoal, and took the small dark mark, and took the city, all at once, the way a turning closes a door — not slammed, just shut, just gently and completely not-for-you-anymore — and the canals were behind them and the open lagoon was ahead and the outboard found its voice again as Keoki opened the throttle for the run home.

Nobody spoke for a while.

The spaces between the islets stayed full of water and the spaces between what was known and what was kept stayed full of exactly what they had always held, which was not the crew's to drain, and Mira sat in the bow facing in, watching the faces of the people who had crossed an ocean to learn how to leave a thing alone, and found that for once in her life she did not need to read the room.

There was nothing in it being hidden from her.

There was only a thing being kept, by someone who had the right, and she had let him keep it, and the letting was the whole of what she had come for and had not known until now that she had.

The fish, for once, said nothing at all.
