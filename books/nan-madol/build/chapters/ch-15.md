# Chapter 15 — The Unheard

The tide came in the way it always did, without asking. Mira felt it before she saw it — the canal water shifting against the islet's stone foot, a low push she took up through the soles of her feet where she sat. She had her back to a wall that was older than every country she'd ever flown out of, and she was watching a man's mouth not move.

The custodian sat across the channel from her, on the Nandauwas side, on a column laid flat by hands no one could name. Between them ran maybe four metres of green water, a "space between," the kind the whole place was named for. Yusuf was talking. Yusuf was always talking when he was nervous, and he was nervous now because the question had finally been asked plainly and the answer had come back the same as every answer for a hundred years.

*We don't know.*

Mira read the three words off the custodian's lips, then read everything else.

The crew didn't see it yet. She gave them that — they were good, they were kind, they signed for her at the table at night with hands like men buttering bread in the dark, and Frik had learned the sign for *basalt* just to make her laugh. But they heard the words and stopped there, because that was what words were for, in their world. You said a thing and the thing was said.

She had never lived in that world. She had spent forty years in rooms where the words went over her head like gulls and the truth sat down on people's faces where they thought no one was looking. You learn to read the face when the mouth is a door you were never given the key to. You learn the difference between *I don't know* and *I won't say* the way a sailor learns weather — not as a fact but as a thing in the gut.

The custodian's *we don't know* was not the first kind.

---

The official story is clean, and it is true, and Mira had come to love it the way you love a thing that holds its shape under pressure.

A medieval dynasty — the Saudeleur — unified a drenched volcanic island and built, on a reef flat at its southeast edge, a city of the dead. Ninety-two artificial islets, give or take, laced with tidal canals you crossed by canoe on the rising water. The radiocarbon puts the monumental work at around AD 1180. Not antiquity. Not Atlantis. Medieval. There were cathedrals going up in Europe the same century, and here, on a reef in the western Pacific, a people were laying basalt logs into walls and stacking them seven metres high to house their kings in death.

The stone helps. Nature pre-cut it. Columnar basalt comes out of cooling lava in long hexagonal prisms — the Giant's Causeway physics, the Devils Tower physics — and the builders read that gift correctly and stacked the columns dry, header against stretcher, a long face then an end, log-cabin style, course on course on a bed of coral rubble. No mortar. Jakobus had spent an hour at it the first day, two fingers laid along a joint, reading the *how-it-stands* the way Mira read a face, and he had said almost nothing because the wall said it for him.

That part is solved. That part is craft, legible, magnificent, *human*.

Then you do the arithmetic, and the arithmetic does not stay quiet.

---

Naila had laid it out for them flat, on the second night, in a voice with no salesmanship in it. Mira had watched her mouth and her hands both and found no gap between them, which was its own kind of relief.

Three-quarters of a million tonnes of basalt. Spread over four centuries of building, that is roughly eighteen hundred tonnes a year, every year, sustained, for four hundred years. The columns run five to twenty-five tonnes apiece. A single cornerstone at Nandauwas comes in around fifty.

And the quarry is roughly twenty-five miles away, on the far side of the island.

So you take a fifty-tonne stone — a stone the weight of a loaded lorry, a stone you cannot push, cannot roll up a beach, cannot carry on shoulders no matter how many shoulders — and you get it across twenty-five miles of open salt water, and then you raise it, on a reef, by hand, into a wall already five metres tall, with no crane, no documented ramp, nothing dragged ashore and forgotten that would tell a careful modern eye *here is how.*

They had tried, of course. People always try. Experimental archaeology has gone at the quarry-to-wall feat with rafts and ropes and clever men, and it has fallen short. Not *impossible* — nobody honest says impossible. Just: not yet done again. The thing stands there flawless and the people who came to repeat it made, every time, a potato.

The columns on the seabed don't help the tidy story either. A University of Oregon dive in '88 and '89 found basalt columns on the lagoon floor, and the prosaic, honest reading is the right one: lost cargo. A raft that didn't make it. Stone that went over the side on a bad swell, twenty-five miles from where it was going. Which tells you the haul was real, and hard, and sometimes lethal — and tells you nothing at all about the method, because the method is exactly the thing that never came back to shore.

Mira had stood at the canal edge with that number — eighteen hundred tonnes a year — and felt the old instinct light up, the crew's shared disease: *solve it. Read it. Know it.* It was the instinct that made them unhirable everywhere else and indispensable to each other. It was the instinct she was sitting on now with both hands.

---

Yusuf had moved on to the worst part, the part that broke the *why* clean off the *how*.

"There's no water," he was saying, across the channel, gentle, almost pleading, the way you ask a thing you already know the shape of. "On the islets. No fresh water. Nothing grows. You couldn't keep a person alive out here a week without canoes coming in every day."

The custodian let that sit on the water.

This is the second hole, and it is the one that won't let go of you once it has you. They didn't just move three-quarters of a million tonnes of stone across the sea. They moved it to a place that could not feed or water a single living soul. A city for the dead, built where the living could only visit. Every drop of water, every yam, every mouthful that the priests and the workers and the keepers ever swallowed out here came by canoe, on the tide, forever. You don't do that by accident. You don't do that because it was convenient. You do it because the *where* was the whole point, and the point is not written down anywhere a stranger can read it.

*Why here,* the wall asks. *Why drag a metropolis across the ocean to a reef that keeps no one alive.*

The official answer to that, too, is *we don't know.* And it is true. And out here, on this ground, in this man's mouth, it was doing a second job.

---

Mira saw it the way she saw everything — not as a thought arriving but as a thing that had always been there, surfacing.

It was in the stillness around his eyes when Yusuf pushed. Not the flat blankness of a man who has nothing. The other thing. The held thing. She had worn it on her own face ten thousand times in rooms where she'd understood every word on every lip and chosen to let them think she hadn't, because the silence was the only thing in the room that was hers.

A man who doesn't know shrugs and his shoulders go loose. A man who *won't say* holds his shoulders exactly level, and keeps his hands quiet, and lets his eyes rest somewhere just past your shoulder so you can't fish in them. The custodian's hands were folded. His eyes were on the water between them, on the space between, which was the most honest place he could have put them.

He knew something. Mira would have bet her hearing aids on it, if they'd been worth betting. He knew, or his line knew, what this place was *for* — which king, which rite, which reason the dead were carried out onto a reef and walled in basalt where the wind and the tide had them to themselves. He knew, and it was not hers, and it was not Yusuf's, and it had been kept by men like him through the Saudeleur and through Isokelekel and through every missionary and surveyor and documentary crew and treasure-eyed outfit that had ever come across the water with a question and a hunger.

*We don't know* was the door. It had been the door for four hundred years. It was a good door. It had held.

Edith had lifted her camera, slow, the way she did now — asking with the lens before she took, the war still in the way she handled the thing, the habit of the frame that costs somebody. The custodian's eyes flicked to it. Mira watched him decide to allow it, and watched him decide what it would and wouldn't get. Edith lowered the camera anyway, a hair, and shot the water instead of the man. Mira loved her a little extra for it.

Across the channel, Frik shifted, and she felt him gathering breath to ask it a third way, a cleverer way, the way that had cracked open a dozen quiet men in a dozen quiet bars. The crew's whole reflex, leaning toward the gap, smelling the held thing, certain — as they always were, as they had every right to be, it was *who they were* — that one more good question would let the truth out.

Mira put her hand flat on the warm stone and stood up.

---

She crossed to where Yusuf sat and crouched by him, and she signed it small, just for the crew, her hands low and slow so even Frik could follow.

*He knows.*

Yusuf's face did the thing she'd known it would. The lift, the hunger, the lean. *He knows?* — already half-turned back to the channel, already framing the next question.

She caught his wrist. Held it. Shook her head, once.

*He knows,* she signed again. *And it isn't ours.*

She watched it travel through them. Watched Yusuf's lean settle back down into his hips. Watched Frik let the breath out unspent. Edith already had it — Edith, who had spent a career learning exactly what a frame steals, who had spent this whole trip asking the camera to come down. Naila, the only one of them with letters after her name and a stake in the answer, looked at the custodian and then at the water and then folded her notebook closed without writing anything in it, which Mira thought was the bravest thing she'd seen an academic do.

The hardest part was that they *could* have got it. Maybe. With the third question, or the tenth, or the bottle of sakau and the long evening and the rapport they were genuinely good at. They were the best readers of held things she had ever stood among, because every one of them had spent a life watching rooms they were never let into. That was the gift. That was the whole gift.

And the gift's twin, the one nobody had ever taught any of them, the one she was only now holding in her two hands at the edge of a sacred canal on the far side of the world: knowing a door is shut, and *not putting your shoulder to it.*

---

She turned back to the custodian. He was watching her — had been, she understood, watching her the whole time, the one across the water who'd stood up before the question came. He had read her reading him. Of course he had. He had spent his life keeping a thing the way she'd spent hers being kept out, and the two of them met now across four metres of green tidewater in a recognition that needed no mouth at all.

She didn't ask him what it was for.

She lifted her hand instead — not the question hand, not the reaching hand. The flat, open palm, held still, that in any language on earth means *I see you, and I'm not coming closer.* She held it where he could read it.

The custodian looked at the open hand. And the held stillness around his eyes eased — not into telling, never into telling, but into something Mira had wanted her whole life and never once been given by a hearing room: he let her see that he saw that she'd stopped.

He inclined his head, the smallest motion, toward the water between them. Toward the space. Then he stood, in his own time, on his own ground, and the tide carried his canoe a half-turn so the channel widened, and the door he had held for four centuries stayed exactly as shut as he had brought it.

Out on the reef the wall stood up against the sky, seven metres of basalt logs laid dry by hands no one could name, holding the dead of a dynasty on a stone where nothing drinks and nothing grows, for a reason that was his to give or keep — and he kept it.

Mira put her hand down. Behind her, very quietly, Frik signed *basalt*, just to make her smile, and it almost worked.

She didn't need to know. That was the new thing, the thing the narrator will not name for her because she would never name it herself. She had crossed the whole world to a place built on the spaces between, and found the one space she would not try to fill.

Some doors you read. Some doors you leave.

The water closed over the question and held it, the way it had always held everything else.
