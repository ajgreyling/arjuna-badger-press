# Chapter 11 — The Experimenters

The column was three and a half tonnes of basalt, and it had spent the whole morning refusing to behave.

Frik had it framed before anyone arrived. He liked to do that — set the tripod while the light was still grey and the reef flat was an inch deep in retreating tide, and let the place fill itself up. So the first shot of the day was the thing alone: a single hexagonal log of stone, dark and matte and longer than a man, lying on a sledge of fresh-cut mangrove the team had lashed together the night before. Behind it, two hundred metres off across the channel, the wall of Nandauwas stood the way it had stood for eight hundred years, doing the thing they were here to fail to do.

That was the joke, and nobody had to say it. The answer was already standing behind the question.

"Recording," Frik said, to no one. The word was a habit. He pressed the back of his hand to the camera body the way some men touch a doorframe leaving the house.

The experimenters came down to the flat at half six, in rash vests and reef shoes, carrying coffee in dented thermoses. They were not television people. That was the first thing Frik had clocked and the thing he kept clocking, frame after frame: nobody here was performing. There was an engineer named Dawson with a clipboard laminated against the salt, and two grad students whose names Frik kept losing, and the woman running it, Dr. Hale, a marine archaeologist out of Auckland by way of a dozen reefs, who had the specific calm of a person who had measured the world and found it heavier than the brochure.

She had been clear with them, twice, the day before. *We're not solving it. We're constraining it. There's a difference and it's the whole job.*

Frik had liked her immediately.

---

The official story, laid out flat, was not in dispute, and Hale told it again now to the camera the way you'd recite a hymn you actually believed — no flourish, every word load-bearing.

A medieval dynasty. The Saudeleur, who unified Pohnpei and ruled it from this reef. Radiocarbon put the monumental phase from around AD 1180 — *medieval*, she said, and let the word sit, because half the world wanted this place older than time and it simply wasn't. Ninety-odd artificial islets. Tidal canals laced between them — the spaces between, the name itself. A ceremonial city for the dead, and the great mortuary enclosure right there, Nandauwas, walls between five and a half and seven and a half metres, dry-stacked, no mortar, header-and-stretcher, basalt logs alternating long-face and end like a child's log cabin built by giants who had all gone home.

"And the stone is the part everyone gets backwards," she said. She crouched and laid a hand flat on the column on the sledge, the way you'd settle a horse. "Nature did the hard bit. This is columnar jointing — lava cools, it contracts, it cracks into these prisms. Same physics as the Giant's Causeway. Nobody carved this hexagon. The island handed it over pre-cut."

"So that's the easy part," one of the grad students said, grinning, because he knew what came next.

"That's the *only* easy part," said Hale.

Frik panned, slow, off her hand and across the channel to the wall, and held it there long enough that anyone watching would do the arithmetic without being asked.

Three-quarters of a million tonnes of this. Quarried twenty-five miles up the coast, the Sokehs side. Rafted — *somehow* — across open lagoon water. A single cornerstone in that far enclosure that ran to fifty tonnes. And then the part nobody could draw a picture of: lifted. Course on course. Onto a wall taller than the boat they'd come out in, on a reef, by hand, with no crane and no ramp surviving and nothing — *nothing* — left behind to say how.

Hale stood and brushed her palms.

"So," she said. "We're going to try to move one. Just one. Three and a half tonnes — a small one, an honest small one — about forty metres, onto a metre-high test course. And I want you filming when it goes wrong, because that's the data."

---

It went wrong with great dignity, which was somehow worse.

The plan was sound. The plan was, in fact, the consensus plan — the one in the literature, the one any sensible person reaches for. Rollers and a sledge for the flat haul. Then a timber A-frame, lever-and-lash, a shear-legs rig to walk the column up onto the course in stages, packing under it as you went. Coir rope, the real stuff, hand-laid, because that's what they'd have had. Eighteen people on the lines, which was already, Frik noted, more bodies than the brochure version ever pictured.

The haul across the flat went well enough that for twenty minutes Frik let himself believe. The rollers bit, the coir held, the column crept over the wet stone with a sound like a slow door, and the grad students chanted something to keep the pull together, and Hale walked beside it with one hand resting on the stone, reading it.

Then they reached the test course and tried to lift.

The A-frame was good. The lashings were good. The lever advantage was textbook. And the basalt — the dense, dark, naturally-prismatic basalt that the island had so kindly pre-cut — simply *was what it was*, which was three and a half tonnes of it, and the rope took the load and the rope sang, a high tight note Frik felt in his back teeth, and the column came up off the rollers maybe a hand's width and hung there, trembling, while eighteen people leaned into the lines and the A-frame legs dug little graves for themselves in the coral fill.

"Pack it," Hale said, very level. "Pack it, pack it—"

They got a baulk of timber under one end. They got the second end up. They got the column resting, finally, on a single course of packing, half a metre off the flat, and it had taken — Frik checked his own timecode later, and then checked it again because he didn't believe it — the better part of three hours.

Half a metre. One column. The small one.

Eighteen people, the best rope, the right rig, modern winches held in reserve and *not even used* yet, and an Auckland archaeologist who had done this on four other reefs, and they had moved one small stone up half a metre in an afternoon.

Frik thought about the wall across the channel. He didn't do the multiplication out loud. He didn't have to. The camera did it for him, sitting there with the half-metre lift in the bottom of the frame and the seven-metre wall standing flawless in the top of it, and the gap between them was the whole story and it was very quiet.

> *That's a potato,* said the fish, from the cooler where someone had put him to keep cool, which was Hecklefish's preferred vantage for the collapse of human endeavour. *That's a beautiful, expensive, peer-reviewed potato. They've got rope from the right tree and everything. Tell them about the cornerstone. Go on. Tell them it's fifty of those.*

Frik did not tell them. They knew.

---

He filmed the faces during the break, because the faces were the truth and the mouths were just doing logistics.

This was the thing he and Mira never had to discuss — they'd worked it out separately and arrived at the same place, the way you do. The mouth tells you the plan. The face tells you what the body already knows. Dawson, the engineer, sat on an upturned crate working numbers on his laminated sheet, and his mouth said *we shave the timber, drop the friction, we'd halve that lift time, easy.* But his pencil had stopped moving. He was looking at the wall. He was an engineer, and engineers do not enjoy looking at a thing they cannot reverse-build, and his face had the particular set of a man whose competence had just been handed a number it didn't like.

Hale sat apart, drinking water, watching her own students dismantle the A-frame. She was not frustrated. That was what got Frik. He'd expected frustration and found something quieter and harder to name — a kind of *respect*, aimed across the channel at people eight hundred years dead.

She caught him filming her and didn't flinch, which by now meant something. They had earned that, slowly, over three days.

"You want the line," she said. "The clean one. For your show."

"I'll take whatever you've got."

She thought about it.

"Here's the clean one. We didn't fail." She nodded at the column, propped on its half-metre of packing. "That's a successful experiment. We *constrained* it. We now know — to a decent confidence — that you *could* do this with rope and timber and people and patience. The physics permits it. There's no levitation required. Write that down, it's important: nothing here is impossible. Everyone wants me to say impossible. I won't."

"But."

The corner of her mouth went. "But it's never been reproduced. Not the haul-and-lift, quarry to wall, at scale. People have tried. The literature's honest about it — *not yet reproduced.* Which is not the same as *can't be done.* It's the gap between knowing a thing is *possible* and knowing how it was *actually done* — and at the volume it was done, and the *rate*."

"The rate," Frik said.

"Do the rate." She said it the way you'd dare someone. "Eighteen hundred tonnes a year. Sustained. For four centuries. We just spent an afternoon on three and a half." She let that breathe. "I'm not reaching past it. I want to be very clear, on your camera, that I am not reaching for anything. There's no mystery *mechanism* I'm hiding. The mechanism is rope and people. The mystery is the *organisation.* What island holds a workforce on a reef that grows no food and holds no water, season after season, generation after generation, and feeds them, and waters them, by canoe, the whole time, and *wants to* — for the dead. Not for the living. There's nobody living out here." She gestured at the dry, foodless, waterless stone all around them, the most expensive uninhabitable real estate in the Pacific. "Who does that. And keeps doing it. That's my hole. That's the one I can't close. And it's an *honest* hole — go ask anyone in the field, they'll give it to you straight."

Frik kept rolling. It was the best three minutes anyone had given him in months, and she'd handed it over plainly, the way you'd hand over a tool.

> *She's good,* the fish admitted, grudging, from the cooler. *She found the hole and she's not selling you a dragon through it. Where's the fun in that. Somebody bring me a man with a theory and a pamphlet.*

---

It was Hale who found the door, in the end, and Frik who filmed her not opening it — though he didn't understand that part until later, watching it back, with Mira beside him going very still.

The light was going amber. The team was loading the skiff. And a man had come down across the flat from the Madolenihmw side — older, unhurried, in a faded church shirt, who Frik recognised as one of the men who'd been with the custodian on the first day and whose name he had not been given and had not asked for. He stood at the edge of the test course and looked at the half-metre lift, and at the eighteen-person rig folded up beside it, and at the wall.

Hale went to him. Not as a scientist. She set her clipboard down first, deliberately, on the crate, which Frik's lens caught — the laminated sheet of numbers, the whole afternoon's data, *set down* before she crossed to him empty-handed.

They spoke too low for the mic. Frik didn't push in. He held the wide shot — the two of them at the channel's edge, the failed lift between them, the wall behind.

The man said something and gestured, once, up the coast toward Sokehs, toward where the quarry was, twenty-five miles of open water away. A small movement. Almost nothing.

And Hale — who had spent the whole day cheerfully telling Frik's camera every single thing she knew — went quiet. She nodded. She did not ask the follow-up. Frik watched her not ask it, watched the question form in the set of her shoulders and then watched her *put it down*, the same gesture as the clipboard, and just stand there in the gold light receiving whatever the old man had decided to give her and *not reaching for the rest.*

The man patted the top of the propped column, the way Hale had patted it that morning — settling a horse — and walked back up the flat.

Hale came back and picked up her clipboard and did not write anything on it.

"What'd he say," Frik asked. Gently. He already half knew it was the wrong question.

She looked at him, and there was that respect again, but turned now, aimed somewhere he couldn't follow.

"He said we were lifting it wrong." A pause. "And then he said something else, and it wasn't for the camera, and it wasn't for me to repeat. So I'm not going to." She almost smiled. "Funny. I came out here to find out if it was *possible*. I've got that. Possible, not reproduced, honest hole, you can air all of it." She zipped her dry bag. "And that man just told me, very kindly, that *possible* was never the interesting question, and I've spent eleven years on it."

She slung the bag and stepped down into the skiff.

"Get a shot of the wall before you lose the light," she said. "That's the only thing out here that knows the answer, and it's not telling either."

Frik turned the camera. Behind him the half-metre lift sat in its little failure on the reef. In front of him Nandauwas went from grey to gold, seven metres of dry-stacked stone that the best minds and the best rope had just spent a whole day getting nowhere near, standing exactly as it had stood, holding its dead and its method and whatever the old man hadn't said, all of it, between the stones.

> *Roll the tape,* said the fish, quietly, for once not heckling at all.

Frik rolled it. The wall didn't say anything. It never had.
