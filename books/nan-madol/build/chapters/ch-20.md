# Chapter 20 — What Is It For

The tide turned at the wrong time, the way it always does when you want one more hour.

The skiff sat low at the canal mouth, taking the crew on one at a time, the engineer's rig already barged off ahead of them and gone — winch, sling, the aluminium gantry they'd hauled out here in Act One to prove how easy it all was. The gantry had proved nothing except that aluminium rusts faster than you'd think and that a fifty-tonne stone does not care how clever your sling is. They'd left a divot in the coral fill where they'd dropped their one borrowed column. Edith had photographed the divot. Then she'd photographed the wall behind it, seven metres of dry-stacked basalt that had stood since before her camera was a glint in anyone's eye, and put the lens cap on.

Frik was last off the islet. He stood on the edge of Nandauwas with his hands in his pockets, looking up at the cornerstone like a man saying goodbye to a relative he never understood and now never would.

"Fifty tonnes," he said. "I keep doing the sum. It doesn't get smaller."

"Sums never do," said Mira. She was already in the boat, water-side, where the noise was least and she could watch the others' faces against the wall. "That's the thing about them."

Jakobus was the only one not looking at the stone. He was looking at the *joint* — the place where two columns met and held, header crossing stretcher, the long face of one block locked over the end of the next, the way a man builds a log cabin so the corners bind themselves. He had his thumb on the seam. Not pushing. Reading.

"There," he said, to nobody and to all of them. "You see how it's tied."

They saw. He'd shown them on the first day and he'd show them on the last, because it was the only part of this he could honestly claim to know. He knew the *how-it-stands*. Every course alternated. Every stone's weight pinned the stone beneath. No mortar, because mortar is a thing you add when you cannot trust the geometry, and whoever built this trusted the geometry completely. It would stand another eight centuries on the strength of a single idea, repeated.

"That part I can read," Jakobus said. He took his thumb off the seam. "I can read it because it's a made thing, and a made thing answers one question if you ask it right."

> *"Here it comes,"* said the fish, riding the gunwale, dripping. *"He's going to do the speech. He always does the speech."*

"Not who," Jakobus said. "We chased who. The dynasty. The dragon. The lost continent that the brochures love and the radiocarbon hates. Who is a parlour game."

"And not how," Yusuf said, because he'd been waiting all book to be allowed to say it. "We tried how. How is sitting on the bottom of the barge channel with a bent gantry."

"Not how." Jakobus almost smiled. On him it looked like weather changing. "How is the hole the experts kept honest about. They tried to lift it again with all our toys, and they made a potato, and they had the decency to write it down. *Not yet reproduced.* Good men. I'd drink with them." He looked, finally, at the water between the islets, the spaces that gave the place its name. "There's only one question a made thing has to answer. The rest is gossip."

He didn't say it. He never said it himself. He handed it across, the way the seed says he must, the way he'd handed the custodian's silence back to the custodian three days ago and not picked it up since.

It was Mira who put it in the boat with them.

"What is it for," she said.

---

The skiff pulled out into the lagoon and the city began to do the thing it does when you leave it: it stopped being walls and started being *the spaces between.* From the water you could see the logic of the canals, ninety-odd islets threaded by tide, a grid you could only read by going through it the way the builders went through it — by boat, on the water, on the water's terms. Land it on a map and it looks like a riddle. Float it on the lagoon at the turn of the tide and it looks like a machine that runs on the moon.

"It's for the dead," said Frik. "That's the official line. Mortuary capital. The royal enclosure, the tombs, the Saudeleur and their bones. It's a graveyard. A magnificent, three-quarters-of-a-million-tonne graveyard." He said it without a sneer, which had taken him the whole book to learn. "And that's true. The custodian said it was true. The archaeology says it's true. Built from about eleven-eighty, run for four centuries, the dynasty falls around the early sixteen-hundreds, and the whole time, they're stacking basalt onto a reef."

"True," Mira agreed. "And not the answer."

"Why not?"

She watched the wall recede. She always answered slowest, because she'd spent a life in rooms where the words went past too fast to catch, and she'd learned to say only the thing she was sure of.

"Because *graveyard* tells you what they did there," she said. "It doesn't tell you why *here.* Why a reef. Why a place that grows nothing and holds no water and can't keep one living soul alive without a canoe coming out every day with breadfruit and fresh water in a gourd. You don't drag fifty tonnes across twenty-five miles of open ocean to bury a king somewhere convenient. You do it somewhere *chosen.*" She let the next part sit a beat. "*For* is the why-here. Jakobus's question is the why-here."

> *"And the answer is —"* the fish prompted, leaning in, vibrating.

Nobody answered. The fish deflated visibly.

> *"You're all doing it on purpose now."*

"We are," said Edith. It was the most she'd said since the gantry went down. She had the camera in her lap, lens cap on, and her hand resting on it like you'd rest a hand on a dog you'd finally taught to stay. "We're doing it on purpose."

---

Yusuf had the numbers, because Yusuf always had the numbers, and out here on the water with the wall shrinking he ran them one more time — not to solve anything, he'd given that up, but because the size of a thing is a kind of prayer.

Seven hundred and fifty thousand tonnes. Spread it across four centuries of building and you get something like eighteen hundred tonnes a year, every year, sustained, generation on generation — basalt columns five tonnes, fifteen, twenty-five, quarried on the far side of the island and brought *here,* and the cornerstone alone fifty, a single stone the weight of a loaded lorry, levered somehow up onto a wall on a reef with no crane and no ramp and nothing — *nothing* — left in the ground to tell you how. No abandoned sledge. No worn road. No half-finished ramp of coral they got tired of. The columns on the seabed off Sokehs, found by the Oregon survey in the late eighties, read the honest way: cargo that didn't make it. A spill on the haul road, if the haul road is the sea.

"That's the part that gets me," Yusuf said. "Not that it's impossible. The experts don't say impossible — they say *not reproduced,* which is a different and better word. It's that they did it *casually.* Eighteen hundred tonnes a year is not a wonder of the world. It's a Tuesday. For four hundred Tuesdays' worth of years. They had a system so good it left no scar, and the system died with them, and we've been standing on the reef with a winch going *how, how, how* like a man knocking on a door that the people inside locked from the other side and then left."

> *"That's almost poetry,"* the fish allowed. *"Don't let it go to your head, you'll capsize us."*

"The point," said Mira, "is that we're not the ones who get to open it."

And there it was, the thing that had been sitting in the boat the whole way out and the whole way back, the thing the custodian had handed them on the first day wrapped in two soft words. *We don't know.* The crew had heard it the way the brochures hear it — a vacancy, a riddle, a *please decode me.* The well-funded outfit had heard it the way money hears anything empty — a thing you can buy and call yours, because who's to say it wasn't, if even the islanders say they don't know. A vacancy is the easiest thing in the world to steal.

But Mira had watched the custodian's face when the words came out, the way she'd watched ten thousand faces in ten thousand hearing rooms where the truth lived in the body because the mouth wasn't going to carry it. And the face had not said *I don't know.* The face had said *that's a door, and you're standing on the wrong side of it, and that's correct.*

She hadn't picked the lock. She'd named it to the crew, low, three days ago, and then she'd shut up about it, and the silence she kept was the only honest gift she had to give back.

"So what *is* it for," Frik said, and you could hear he half wanted the answer and half had already let it go. "If we're allowed to ask. Not to crack it. Just — to stand in it."

Jakobus, in the bow, off his home ground and out of his depth in every sense that mattered on water, did the thing he does. He gave it away.

"Ask the wall," he said. "The wall's straightforward. It's a function. Every made thing is a function, and this one's plain as a hinge." He tipped his chin back toward the receding stone. "It's a place to keep the dead where the living can't move in and turn it into a town. You want a grave that *stays* a grave, you put it where no one can stay. No water. No food. No farmer's going to fence it, no fisherman's going to build a hut on the king's bones, because the reef won't feed him. The very thing that makes the engineers go mad — *why here, you can't live here* — is the thing it's *for.* You don't site a tomb where you'd site a house. You site it where the dead get the whole island to themselves and the living have to *visit,* on the tide, with permission, every single time." He paused. "That's the function I can read. That's the hinge."

The boat rode the swell. Nobody spoke for a moment.

> *"...Okay, that one was actually good,"* the fish admitted, quietly.

"And the rest of it," Mira said. "The how. The full of the why. What's kept."

"The rest of it isn't a made thing," Jakobus said. "The rest of it is hers." He didn't look back at the islet, where no one stood now, where the custodian had not come down to the water to wave them off because that was not what this was. "She read us cleaner than we read the wall. She knew on day one we'd want to take the answer home in a notebook and put it in a film. She gave us the true thing — *mortuary city, medieval, ours* — and she kept the part that's not for the notebook. That's not a riddle. That's a *boundary.* You can tell the difference if you've ever been on the wrong side of one."

Mira had been on the wrong side of one her whole life. She didn't say so. The boat said it for her, the engine loud, her face turned to the water where the noise was least, the whole crew careful to keep half an eye on her mouth, signing badly and lovingly into the spray.

---

The reef dropped below the horizon line of mangrove the way reefs do, all at once, like a curtain rather than a fade. One moment Nandauwas was a grey tooth against the green ridge of Pohnpei, the cloud-forest piling up behind it dumping its endless rain on the wettest island on earth. The next moment it was gone behind a headland, and there was only the lagoon, and the smell of diesel and frangipani and low tide, and the long swell that had carried fifty-tonne stones to a place no one could live and would carry six unhirable strangers and one fixer back to a town with corrugated roofs and a Sunday church and *sakau* pounded at dusk.

"You want to know what's strange," Yusuf said, watching the water. "It's *more,* now. The wonder. Not less. We didn't solve it and it got *bigger.*"

"That's the trick of it," said Edith. She lifted the camera, sighted along the wake where the city used to be, and lowered it again without pressing anything. The frame had been hers to take. She'd chosen not to. "A people who loved their dead enough to build them a city out of the sea, in a place that could keep no one alive, by a method good enough to leave no mark and proud enough to keep no copy. That's the answer. And it's bigger than any answer we'd have hauled home."

> *"And the dragon?"* the fish tried, one last time, hopeful. *"The one that flew the stones? The lost continent? Just for me? Off the record?"*

The crew looked at the fish.

The crew looked at the water.

The crew did not answer, and the not-answering was the whole gift — the draught of the open gap, the *maybe* left standing where it belonged: not *did a dragon fly,* but the better one, the one the island had been holding the whole time. *Are some things nobody's to open?*

Mira watched the place where the wall had been until the headland took the last of it.

Then she faced front, into the wind, toward the town, and let the door stay shut.
