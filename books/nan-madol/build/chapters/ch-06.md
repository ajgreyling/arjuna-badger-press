# Chapter 6 — The Water Between

The skiff smelled of two-stroke and bait, and Frik loved it instantly, the way he loved any machine that told the truth about itself. Nothing on it pretended. The outboard had a hose-clamp where a hose-clamp had no business being. The bench he sat on was a plank gone soft at one end, the soft end carefully marked with a strip of red tape so you knew not to trust it. Somebody had thought about this boat. Somebody had read it and made a small repair and trusted the repair to hold, and the boat held.

It was the hour before the colour came back into the world. The lagoon lay flat and pewter and the mangroves were a black saw-edge against a sky that had not yet decided what it would be. Manny had the tiller. He was a wide, unhurried man with a cap that said a thing in faded letters Frik couldn't read and didn't ask about, and he ran the channel without looking at it, the way a man walks his own hallway in the dark.

"Tide's with us," Manny said, to no one, or to the water.

Frik had the dossier on his knees inside a freezer bag, and a pencil, and the small spiral pad he carried everywhere because numbers that lived only in his head got lonely and started lying. He had already, before the diesel-smell, written the thing he could not stop writing.

*25 miles. ~50 t. By hand. No crane. Nothing left behind.*

Across the boat Mira sat with her back to the bow, watching faces, which was where she lived. She watched Manny mostly. Edith had the camera down in her lap, lens-cap on, both hands around it like a mug of something hot, and she was watching the light not arrive yet, which was a photographer's particular patience and Frik admired it without understanding it.

"Sokehs," Manny said, and lifted his chin north and west, where the land humped up into a great square-shouldered rock with its head in the cloud. "Where the stone is."

That was the thing. That was the whole impossible sentence, said by a man at a tiller as if it were a grocery errand. *Where the stone is.* The basalt didn't come from Nan Madol. It came from over there — the quarries on the far side, the volcanic flanks where the lava had cooled slow and deep and cracked itself, without a single human hand, into hexagonal logs. Frik liked that part. He liked it enormously. Nature had done the quarrying. Nature had pre-cut the blocks into stackable prisms and stretched them out across the island like a delivery of timber.

And then somebody had to *fetch* them.

---

He had the official story straight, and he held it with respect, because it deserved respect and because the alternative — the floating-stone, the sky-masons — was an insult to people who had actually done a hard thing.

Here it was, the honest version, the one the archaeologists carried and the one Frik carried with them: a medieval people, a real dynasty with a real name, the Saudeleur, who from around AD 1180 had built a mortuary capital on the reef. Radiocarbon said so. The stone said so — the dry-stacked basalt, header and stretcher, log-cabin courses, a building grammar so legible Jakobus had read it off the wall like a label. Nobody serious doubted the *who*. Nobody serious doubted the *when*.

The doubt — the small clean honest doubt — was the bit in the middle. The bit with the boat in it.

Frik turned in his seat, careful of the soft plank, and looked back at Sokehs. Then forward, southeast, toward the reef they'd come from yesterday, the spaces between the stone. He laid a finger on the gunwale at the rock and dragged it slow across the open water to the place where Nandauwas stood with its seven-metre walls.

"How long's the crossing?" he asked. "Open water. This boat."

Manny considered it the way you consider a question that has a real answer. "Hour. Bit more. Depends the sea." He patted the outboard, which coughed agreement.

An hour, with thirty horsepower and petrol and a hull made for it.

Frik wrote in the pad. He did the thing he did, which Yusuf called *running the potato* and Mira called, with a flat fondness, *Frik being unbearable*. He took the easy version, the move-along version, the version where it was all just a question of effort and grit and many willing hands, and he made it stand up in the daylight and tried to walk it across the room.

*~750,000 tonnes total.*

He'd checked that figure three ways. It held to an order of magnitude, which was all anyone honest claimed for it. Three-quarters of a million tonnes of basalt, fetched and floated and stacked.

*Over ~4 centuries. ÷ that's ~1,850 t/yr.*

He underlined it twice and felt, as he always did, the strange double thing the number gave him: it was *reassuring*. Eighteen hundred and fifty tonnes a year was not a miracle. It was about five tonnes a day, on average, a working pace, a thing a determined community could plausibly sustain across generations the way cathedrals were sustained, fathers handing trowels to sons. The arithmetic, run cold, did not scream. It hummed. It said: *people did this, over a very long time, and it added up.*

That was the part the sneerers got right, and Frik would give it to them gladly. The total was *not* the hole. The rate was *not* the hole.

He clicked the pencil and turned the page.

The hole was the single block.

---

"Hecklefish wants to know," Yusuf said from the bow, deadpan, because somewhere on this boat there was a small waterproof speaker and somewhere in the world there was a fish, "whether you've personally lifted fifty tonnes today, or if you're just going to *write about* it."

"Tell him I'm doing reconnaissance," Frik said.

"He says reconnaissance is what people call it when they sit in the boat."

Mira's mouth did the thing that wasn't quite a smile. She wasn't watching the fish. She was watching Manny, who had heard the fish, and whose face had not moved at all, which was itself a kind of moving.

Frik went back to the page, because the fish was right, which was the unbearable thing about the fish.

He drew the cornerstone. He drew it the way he drew everything, badly and exactly, a fat rectangular prism with hatching for shadow, and beside it he wrote *~50 t* and beside *that* he wrote, in the small careful hand he used for things that frightened him, *one block.*

Fifty tonnes. The hardest single object to account for in the whole place. The cornerstone of the great enclosure, a column that weighed as much as a loaded articulated lorry — as much as ten of Manny's skiffs lashed together and stacked to the gunwales — and it had to be quarried out of the Sokehs flank, and brought down to the water, and put *onto* the water, and floated twenty-five miles across the open lagoon and reef on something, and brought to the islet, and then — and this was the part where Frik's pencil always stopped — then *lifted.* Up. Into a wall. Course on course, dry, no mortar, no crane, no ramp anyone has found, on a reef where there's no high ground to lever against and nothing solid to footing a sheerlegs into but the city you are in the middle of building.

"What floats fifty tonnes?" he said out loud, not really to the boat. "A raft floats fifty tonnes. Fine. Buoyancy doesn't care how heavy you are, it cares how much you displace. So you build a big enough raft." He was sketching it now, a platform, log bundles, the lashing he'd want, and he could feel the thing the sketch was doing, which was getting away from him. "But you load it on a falling tide and float it on a rising one, and the column's not a neat parcel, it's a five-metre log of rock, and the moment you're off the lee shore you've got swell, and a thing that displaces that much wants very badly to do one of two things. Sit on the bottom. Or roll."

"Did they do it on the tide," Edith asked. It wasn't really a question to him. She'd asked it to the water, to Manny, the way you ask the person who actually knows.

Manny had been quiet a while. He throttled down a touch, and the bow came off its plane, and the boat settled into the swell and started to talk to it, lift and fall, lift and fall, and Frik felt in his own gut the difference between *crossing water* and *being on it*.

"My grandfather," Manny said, "would not take this passage in the afternoon."

"Why not," said Frik.

Manny looked at him. Not unkindly. With the specific patience of a man who has watched a great many visitors arrive with a notebook and a number and a theory, and who has learned that the theory will leave on the same plane it came in on, and the passage will still be here.

"Because the wind comes up after midday," Manny said. "And the tide turns. You go out on the morning tide, with the morning wind, light. You come back on the evening tide. There are weeks you do not go at all." He nodded once at the channel ahead, a barely-there seam of darker water Frik would not have seen in a thousand years. "That is the road. The water tells you when. Not you."

Frik looked at the seam.

He looked at his sketch of the raft, and the swell lifting the bow, and the dark line of better water that Manny had been steering down the whole time without once appearing to steer, and he understood — not as a number, as a thing in the body, the boat rising under him — that he had been doing it wrong. He'd been treating the twenty-five miles like a *distance.* A scalar. A thing you divide by a speed. As if you could float fifty tonnes across it any day you chose, given enough rope.

But it wasn't a distance. It was a *door that opened and shut.* A few hours, on the right tide, with the right wind, in the right weeks of the right season — a narrow channel of *permission* through which a raft displacing fifty tonnes might be coaxed, slowly, by people who knew the water the way Manny knew it, the way his grandfather knew it, the way you only know a thing by being told no by it for a lifetime.

And then they did it again. And again. Five tonnes a day, on the days the water allowed. For four hundred years.

The arithmetic hadn't lied. The total was sane. The rate was sane. It was the *single heavy thing, on the single right day, lifted by hand into the single high wall* that wouldn't reduce — that sat in the middle of the sane arithmetic like a stone in a shoe.

---

Frik did the honest thing then, the cold-eyed thing, the thing the line asked of him, which was to write down not what he believed but what was *true*, measured flat, reached past toward nothing.

He wrote: *No documented transport-and-lift method.* That was a fact. Not a mystery dressed up as a fact. A genuine gap, the kind the experts themselves leave open and shrug at, honestly, the way you shrug at a thing you've decided is solvable but haven't solved. There's no surviving account of the rig. No ramp left in the mud. No abandoned half-floated column on the route to settle the argument — only the ones on the seabed off the Sokehs side, found by divers out of Oregon in the late eighties, and read, soberly and well, as exactly what they most plausibly are: cargo that went over the side. Stone that didn't make the crossing. The wreckage of the haul, not the proof of anything stranger.

And he wrote the other true thing, the one that had been sitting under all of it: *replication has failed.* People had tried. Real people, recent people, with rope and rafts and willing arms and the explicit goal of showing it could be done the routine way — and they had not reproduced it. Not "it's physically impossible." Nobody serious said that, and Frik wouldn't write it. The columnar basalt floats if you build the right raft; the levers work if you have the patience; the arithmetic, run cold, hums and doesn't scream. It is *not yet reproduced.* That was the whole of the honest sentence, and the whole of the honest sentence was enough.

The sun came up, finally, all at once the way it does in the tropics, and the lagoon went from pewter to a green so flagrant Frik laughed at it. Edith took the cap off the lens. Sokehs stood up out of the cloud behind them, the quarry-rock, the *where the stone is*, square-shouldered and complete and entirely unbothered by the fact that nobody alive could say how the stone had got from it to the walls.

"You have your number," Manny said. He'd seen the pad. He missed nothing on his own boat.

"I have my number." Frik turned the pad around and showed him, the way you'd show a man his own hallway. The total. The rate. The fifty-tonne block, drawn badly, drawn exactly. "It works, the average. It's the one block that doesn't. The one block, the one day, onto the wall."

Manny looked at the sketch for a while. The boat lifted and fell.

"My grandfather knew the water," he said again, slowly, and Frik had the clear and humbling sense that this was an *answer*, a whole one, and that he was being trusted with the shape of it but not the inside, the way you're handed a closed box and told, kindly, that the weight of it is the part you get to keep.

"And you know the water," Frik said.

"I know the water." Manny put the throttle back up. The bow came onto its plane and the seam of dark channel opened ahead of them, plain as a road now the light was on it, a road Frik would never have found and Manny could not *not* see. "There are things the water tells you," Manny said, over the engine, not turning round, "that you cannot write down."

Frik looked at the pad. At the number that hummed and didn't scream. At the one block that wouldn't reduce.

He closed the pad and put it back in the freezer bag, and watched the road on the water instead, the one he couldn't read, and for once didn't try to.
