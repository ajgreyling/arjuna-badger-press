# Chapter 13 — The Vacancy

The room had been a classroom once. You could still see where the world map had been taped to the wall — four pale rectangles in the paint where the corners had peeled, a continent-shaped ghost. Now it held a folding table, eight stacking chairs, a whiteboard wiped to a grey smear, and an air-conditioner that laboured against the wet like a man pushing a boat off a sandbar. Kolonia in the late morning: corrugated roofs ticking under the sun, a rooster somewhere being very sure of itself, the smell of diesel and frangipani coming in under the door with the heat.

Yusuf got there early because he always got there early. You learned the room before the room learned you.

The man from Meridian Pacific Heritage Group was already setting up. Tall, soft-spoken, a linen shirt that had been ironed by someone who took ironing seriously. He had a laptop and a portable projector and a bottle of water for every chair, condensation already beading, and he stood when Yusuf came in and offered a hand and a name — *Call me Devin* — with the open, unhurried warmth of a man who has never once in his life been asked to leave a room.

"You're early," Devin said, pleased. "I respect that."

"Habit," Yusuf said, and sat where he could see the door and the screen at once.

The others filtered in. Frik, who looked at the projector cable the way some men look at a snake. Edith, who took the chair in the corner, the camera bag in her lap zipped shut — she'd asked first, in the skiff, and Yusuf had said *not yet*, and she had nodded and zipped the bag and not touched it since. Mira last, choosing the seat at the end of the table with the angle on every face, the way she always did, not because she was suspicious but because the angle was the only fair share of the conversation she was ever handed.

Two men from Pohnpei State came too, polite, careful, a folder each. And a woman from the Madolenihmw side whose name Yusuf caught and held — Lihna — who sat very straight and did not take a water bottle.

"So," Devin said, when the air-conditioner had been turned up and turned down and left where it was. "Thank you all. I know everyone's busy, I'll be brief, I promise." He clicked. The screen filled with the reef at golden hour, the basalt walls of Nandauwas rising out of the canals like something the sea had decided to keep, the water between the islets gone to copper. It was a beautiful photograph. It was somebody else's photograph; Yusuf could see the watermark ghosted in the corner where they'd cropped it badly.

> *Stock photo. Even the slideshow's a tourist.*

"Nan Madol," Devin said, and let it sit there, the word and the picture. "The Venice of the Pacific. Ninety-two islets. A medieval city — and I want to be careful here, because the science is genuinely extraordinary — built around eleven-eighty, radiocarbon, the Saudeleur capital, overthrown in the seventeenth century. All true. All on the record." He clicked again: the cornerstone of Nandauwas, the fifty-tonne block, shot from below so it loomed. "Three-quarters of a million tonnes of basalt. A single cornerstone that weighs as much as a fully loaded semi-truck, raised onto a wall over twenty-three feet high. By hand. On a reef."

He paused, and looked around the table, and lowered his voice the precise amount.

"And here's the thing the experts will tell you, and I love them for their honesty: *they don't know how.* They've tried. Experimental archaeology — they've gone out and tried to move one column the way it would've been done. It doesn't work. Nobody has reproduced it." He spread his hands, palms up, an offering. "A hundred years of trying, and the most honest scholars on earth look at that reef and say — *we don't know.*"

It was, Yusuf thought, exactly right. Every word of it was exactly, infuriatingly right. The arithmetic was real. The failed lift was real. He had stood on the reef himself three days ago and watched a hired rig and a clever plan produce a potato — a single column slung wrong, swinging, the whole engineered dignity of the modern attempt reduced to four people and a winch shouting at a stone that would not go where the spreadsheet said it should, while behind them the wall that nobody could explain stood there being seven and a half metres tall and four hundred years patient and entirely silent about how.

So when Devin said *we don't know*, he was telling the truth.

It was what he did with it next that made the back of Yusuf's neck go cold.

"Which is the opportunity," Devin said, and clicked.

A rendering now. The reef, but cleaned — the mangrove pushed back, a boardwalk threading the canals on tasteful pilings, an interpretive centre with a roof shaped, vaguely, like a wave or a sail or a thing a designer in an air-conditioned office in a city eight thousand kilometres away had decided said *Pacific*. Little figures walking the boardwalks. A glass-bottomed something in the lagoon.

"A world-heritage-grade visitor experience," Devin said. "Done right. Done with reverence. Meridian doesn't build resorts on sacred ground — I want to be very clear about that, it's the first thing in our charter. We build *access.* We build *interpretation.*" Click. A revenue projection, the bars climbing year on year, green. "And the beauty of it — the genuine, marketable beauty — is the mystery itself. We don't have to claim anything. We don't have to say who or how. We sell the question. *The greatest unsolved engineering mystery in the Pacific.* People will fly across the world to stand in front of a question nobody can answer."

> *He's not wrong, is what kills me.*

"The story," Devin said, warming now, "is that there is no story. Or — no settled one. And that's perfect. A settled site is a museum. An *unsettled* one is a destination. The not-knowing is the asset."

He said it gently. He said it like a gift.

Yusuf watched the two men from Pohnpei State look at their folders. He watched the bars climb on the screen. He'd sat in rooms like this his whole working life, on three continents, the man at the side wall who counted the exits and read the contract that the principals hadn't read, and he knew the shape of this one the way you know weather. The money was clean. That was the thing nobody back home would ever believe. There was no cabal, no smoke-filled anything; Devin probably did love the archaeologists, probably did believe the charter, probably would write *reverence* in the brochure and mean it. The damage didn't need a villain. It needed a vacancy.

"Question," Yusuf said.

"Please." Devin turned to him with real attention.

"The not-knowing." Yusuf kept his voice flat, accountant's voice, the voice he used when he wanted a number to do the work. "Whose is it?"

Devin blinked. "Sorry?"

"The mystery. You said it's the asset. The fact that nobody knows how it was built, why it was built where nothing grows." Yusuf turned the water bottle a quarter-turn on the table, not drinking it. "Who doesn't know? When you put it on the brochure — *nobody knows* — who's the nobody?"

"The — well, science," Devin said, recovering, smooth. "The consensus is genuinely open. I'm not overstating it. The lift, the provisioning, the —"

"The lift's open," Yusuf agreed. "The dynasty's not. The date's not — eleven-eighty, you said it yourself. The *who* is on the record. It's the Saudeleur capital and there are people in this room whose families remember the fall of it." He didn't look at Lihna. He'd learned not to look at the person you were defending; it made it about you. "So when you say *nobody knows* — you've already cut out everybody who does."

The air-conditioner laboured. The rooster had given up.

"I'm not cutting anyone out," Devin said, and the warmth had gone careful. "We'd partner. Fully. Local boards, local guides, revenue share, the works. The interpretation would be theirs to —"

"What's it for?"

That was Frik, from down the table, gruff, not looking up from the projector cable he'd been worrying. Everyone looked at him.

"Sorry?" Devin said again.

"The walls. Nandauwas. What's it *for.*" Frik finally looked up. "You've got a rendering of a boardwalk through it. So somebody told you what it's for, well enough that your designer knew where to put the railing. Who told you?"

Devin's smile held but it had stopped reaching all the way. "It's a mortuary complex. Royal tombs. That's — that's standard, that's in every —"

"It's in every guidebook," Yusuf said. "Which is not the same as in the room. You've got a fifty-tonne stone you can't explain on a wall you can't reproduce on a reef that can't feed you, and your pitch is that the can't-explain is the *product.* You want to buy the question and resell it as a souvenir. But a question that belongs to somebody isn't for sale. You can only sell an empty one."

He hadn't meant to say that much. He felt it land long, felt the room go still around it in a way that wasn't his to have caused.

Down the table, Mira had gone very still in a different way. Yusuf knew the look — the look she got when the words in a room and the bodies in a room had stopped agreeing with each other and only she could see the seam. She wasn't watching Devin. She was watching Lihna, who had not said a single word, whose face had not moved, whose hands were folded on the table over no folder at all.

And then Mira looked at Yusuf, just a flick, and gave him the smallest shake of the head. *Not the content. Don't.* He understood it the way you understand a hand on your arm. He'd been about to ask Lihna directly — *isn't that right, doesn't your family* — to win the point, to make the silent woman a witness for the prosecution. Mira's chin moved a quarter-inch and he stopped, because the difference between the two of them had always been that she could see when defending someone and using them were the same gesture wearing different clothes.

He sat back.

Devin recovered, because men like Devin always recover; it is the one true thing about them. "Look," he said, easy again, opening his hands. "Nobody's selling anybody's question. I think we've gotten — I think the framing got away from us a little. Let me put it plainly. There's a site. It's degrading — the mangrove, the storms, the State doesn't have the budget, that's not a criticism, it's just true. Somebody is going to develop access to that site within ten years. I'd rather it be us, who'll do it carefully, than the next outfit, who won't. And the honest, beautiful fact at the centre of the whole thing is that nobody can tell you how it was built. That's not me erasing anyone. That's the brochure for the place writing itself."

He looked around, reasonable, warm, right.

"You're describing a grave," Mira said.

It was the first thing she'd said. She said it the way she said most things, level and dry and from the angle she'd chosen at the end of the table, and the room turned to her the way rooms did when the quiet one finally spent a word.

"A grave's got people who tend it," she said. "That's what makes it a grave and not a field with stones in it. You keep saying nobody knows. There's a woman at this table who hasn't told you what she knows, and you've built a whole boardwalk on the assumption that her quiet is the same as your spreadsheet's quiet." She didn't look at Lihna either. She'd learned it from the same place Yusuf had, or maybe she'd taught it to him. "It isn't. One of them's protecting something. One of them's selling that there's nothing to protect."

> *Oof.*

Devin opened his mouth and then, to his credit — and it was credit, Yusuf would give him that — closed it.

Lihna unfolded her hands. She did not stand. She picked up the water bottle she had refused at the start, turned it once, the way Yusuf had turned his, and set it back down without opening it.

"You can leave the slides," she said, to Devin, kindly enough. "The Nahnmwarki will want to see them." She let that sit. "We will tell you what can be told."

It was not a yes. It was not a no. It was a door, and on which side of it the answer lived was hers and not theirs and not, above all, the brochure's to decide. Devin heard *we will tell you what can be told* and his face did the small bright thing of a man who has heard *yes, in principle*; and Yusuf watched Mira watch him hear it wrong, and watched her decide, deliberately, with the whole quiet weight of her, not to correct him.

Because the correction was Lihna's to make or withhold. And she was withholding it. On purpose. The way you'd withhold the location of a grave from a man who'd just told you, sincerely, that its greatest value was that it might be empty.

Devin packed his projector. He shook every hand. He left the water bottles sweating on the table.

When he'd gone, the room exhaled. Frik coiled the cable. Edith's bag stayed zipped. The map's ghost glowed on the wall, four pale corners around a missing world.

"He's not buying the city," Yusuf said, to no one, working it out aloud the way he did. "The city's not for sale, the State knows that, the Nahnmwarki knows that. He's buying the *we-don't-know.* That's the parcel. That's the only empty lot on the reef."

Lihna was at the door already, the folder she'd never opened under her arm. She paused.

"We have always had visitors who needed it to be a mystery," she said. "It is easier to take a place that nobody owns." Then, with something that wasn't quite a smile: "We let them think that. It is one of the things the silence is for."

She went out into the heat. The rooster started up again.

Mira was looking at the four pale rectangles on the wall, the place where a world had been and wasn't. After a while she signed something low against the table edge, more to herself than to them, and Yusuf — who was getting better, who was still bad — caught only the shape of it and not the meaning, and let it be hers.
