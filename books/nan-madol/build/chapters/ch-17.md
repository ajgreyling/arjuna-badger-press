# Chapter 17 — The Checkable Thing

The fan in the crew house turned just fast enough to move the heat from one corner to another, which was its only achievement of the evening. Yusuf had the table to himself, more or less — Mira at the far end with a cold mug of tea and the angle of someone who'd decided to watch rather than join, Edith on the floor with her back against the wall and a lens cap she kept screwing and unscrewing without thinking about it, Frik somewhere out on the step where the signal lived, swearing at a phone.

Yusuf liked a table at night. He liked the way the fluorescent tube above it bleached everything down to fact. He had three notebooks, two of them his, and a printout that had gone soft at the folds from being carried in a damp pocket across the lagoon and back.

"Right," he said. "From the top. No flourishes. I want to know what we actually have."

"You always want that," Edith said, not unkindly.

"And I'm always disappointed." He uncapped a pen. "Tonnage first. It's the cleanest number we've got, so we use it as the floor." He wrote it large, the way you write a thing you intend to lean your whole weight on. *~750,000 t.* "Three-quarters of a million tonnes of basalt. That's the total mass of the complex. Not a guess pulled off a tour board — that's the order-of-magnitude figure the surveys land on. It could be off. It's not off by a factor of ten."

Mira's eyes moved from the number to his mouth and back. She wasn't reading him for a lie. There wasn't one. She was reading him for the place where he stopped believing his own summary, which was a different thing, and which she'd find before he did.

"Now," Yusuf went on, "divide it. Megalithic phase starts around AD 1180 — radiocarbon, that's solid, that's a date with a method behind it, not a story. Dynasty's gone by the early seventeenth century. Call the building span four centuries to be generous to everybody." He did the arithmetic in the margin, the long way, because he didn't trust the answer he already knew. "Seven-fifty thousand over four hundred years. Eighteen hundred and fifty tonnes a year. Every year. For four hundred years."

He set the pen down.

"That's not a heroic burst," he said. "That's not a king clapping his hands and a thousand men jumping. That's a *rate*. That's the same crews, the same canoes, the same reef, year on year on year, a tonnage a season that doesn't let up for sixteen generations. It's the most boring number in the file and it's the one that scares me."

*"Eighteen-fifty a year,"* came the voice from the doorway — Frik, back in off the step, phone dark, sliding onto the bench. He pitched it flat and bright, the way he pitched everything, which was how you knew he'd actually done the sum himself out there in the dark. "That's, what, five tonnes a day if you knock off Sundays. Light work. I move that before breakfast."

"You move five tonnes of basalt twenty-five miles over open water before breakfast."

"I said *light* work, I didn't say I'd enjoy it."

Yusuf almost smiled, which was its own kind of confession, and moved on.

"The haul. This is the one the room never wants to slow down for, so we slow down." He turned the printout around so the others could see it, though only Edith leaned in. "The quarries are on the other side of the island. Sokehs side. Call it twenty-five miles by the route a loaded canoe would actually take, hugging the coast, riding the tide. The stone is columnar basalt — and here's the part the fringe forgets and the textbook says plainly: *nature cut it.* The hexagonal logs aren't carved. Thick lava cooled, contracted, cracked itself into prisms. Same physics as the Giant's Causeway. Same as Devils Tower. The island handed them the blocks pre-shaped."

"So that part's easy," Edith said.

"That part's a gift," Yusuf agreed. "And it's the only easy part, and everyone who wants the whole thing to be easy points at it and pretends the rest comes free." He tapped the figure again. "Columns run five to twenty-five tonnes, common ones. There's a cornerstone at Nandauwas that comes in around fifty."

Mira lifted two fingers off her mug. *Say that one again.* He saw it.

"Fifty tonnes," he said, for her, slower. "One stone. Now — you've rafted it. Say you've solved the rafting, which nobody's shown you how to do, but say it. You're at the reef. The wall you're building it into is between five and a half and seven and a half metres high. Dry-stacked. No mortar. Header and stretcher, log-cabin style — long face, then end, long face, then end, so the courses bind." He drew it, badly, a child's idea of a wall. "Beautiful grammar. Jakobus read it off in about a minute and a half, the *how it stands.* That's not the hole. The hole is the verb in the middle. You've got fifty tonnes of basalt floating at the foot of a seven-metre wall, on a reef, on a tide, and now you *lift* it. By hand. No crane. No ramp anyone's ever found. Nothing left behind on the reef that says *this is how.* No worn timber, no posthole pattern, no spoil heap of failed attempts. The wall's just there. Flawless. Like it grew."

The fan moved the heat into the other corner.

"And before anyone says *ramps, levers, obviously*—" Yusuf held up the soft printout like evidence in a court that wasn't in session. "People have *tried.* That's the part I keep coming back to, because it's the part that's actually checkable and the part that's actually honest. Experimental archaeology. People with the right hands and the right questions and a genuine wish to show it could be done. They went out and tried to do the quarry-to-wall feat — move a column, raft it, set it — the way the settled story implies it was routine." He let it sit. "They didn't reproduce it. Not 'it's physically impossible' — I'm not saying that and the file doesn't say that. I'm saying the careful phrase, the true phrase, the one that doesn't reach: *not yet reproduced.* The brightest version of *us*, with kit those builders never had, tried the easy story and made a potato."

"A potato," Frik said, with relish, because it was the crew's word and he loved it the way you love a thing that's true.

"A potato," Yusuf said. "Stands there next to the real wall, half the size and twice the sweat, and the real wall doesn't even look at it."

Edith had stopped fiddling with the lens cap. "You're enjoying this too much."

"I'm enjoying it the right amount. It's the cleanest case I've worked. There's no trick to find because nobody's hiding one." He flipped a page. "The columns on the seabed. Nineteen eighty-eight, eighty-nine, University of Oregon, they're down there off the reef — basalt columns on the sea floor. Now: a certain kind of person wants that to be a drowned building. A sunken temple. The bottom storey of the lost city." He shook his head, slow. "And the prosaic read is the *better* story, because it's true and it's still astonishing. It's lost cargo. It's a canoe that went over, or a raft that came apart, on the haul. It's the freight that didn't make it. Which means—" he underlined nothing, just touched the page "—it's *physical evidence of the haul itself.* Proof the stone crossed the water. Not proof of anything stranger. The strange thing is already the true thing: somebody was moving fifty-tonne logs across open ocean often enough that some of them sank, and we found the ones that sank, and we *still* can't tell you how the ones that didn't sink got lifted onto a wall."

A moth had got in and was working the tube above them, a soft irregular tick against the glass.

"And then," Yusuf said, and this was the part where his voice changed, dropped a register, lost the courtroom and found something more like the inside of his own chest, "there's the question that the tonnage and the haul are only servants of. *Why here.* No fresh water on those islets. None. Grows no food. You can't keep a single soul alive out there for a week without a canoe coming the other way, loaded, every day, forever. It's not a city you *live* in. It's a city for the dead, on a reef that won't feed the living, twenty-five miles downstream of the only stone that'll build it." He spread his hands flat on the table. "So it's not just *how did they lift it.* It's *why drag three-quarters of a million tonnes across the sea to a place that could keep no one alive.* You answer the first one and you've still got the second one staring at you. They feed each other. Always have."

Quiet. The moth. The fan.

"That's the file," Yusuf said. "That's everything we actually have, with no story laid over the top of it. Every number on this table is real, and every one of them is a number the people who study this place put there themselves. The holes aren't ours. We didn't dig them to be clever. The archaeology *leaves them open.* It says, in print, in the careful words: transport-and-lift, not demonstrated; siting and provisioning, not fully explained. That's mainstream. That's the textbook leaving its own door ajar and being honest enough to say so." He capped the pen. "Which is the only kind of hole worth standing in front of. The kind nobody's lying about."

Frik laced his hands behind his head. "So we *don't* know."

"We don't know *how,* and we don't know *why-here,*" Yusuf said. "Cleanly. Provably. Those are checkable things. You can go and check them and they'll still be holes when you're done checking."

It was Mira who moved then. She set the cold tea down with a small click and pulled a notepad toward her — the crew signed, badly, lovingly, but the table was the table and she liked it black on white when it mattered. She wrote one line and turned it so Yusuf could read it.

*That's not the same kind of don't-know.*

He read it. Read it again. "Than what?"

She took the pad back. Wrote, and turned it.

*Than the one we heard on the canal.*

The fan turned. The moth gave up and sat.

Yusuf looked at the line a long time. He'd built the whole evening to land on *we don't know* like it was the floor — the honest, cold-eyed, mainstream-acknowledged floor, the one you could stand on without lying. And Mira had reached past the words on his table to the words they'd heard on the water, the custodian's *we don't know,* delivered with a face Yusuf had filed under *the same answer everyone gives* — and she was telling him, in five words on a notepad, that he'd filed it wrong. That one of those don't-knows had a hole behind it you could measure. And the other one had a door.

He didn't write it down. There was nothing to measure.

"You think they're different," he said.

She didn't nod. She didn't shrug. She held his eye the way she held a face when the mouth was busy saying one thing — and let the not-answering be the answer, which was a courtesy, and a line she wasn't going to cross for him, and the most honest thing said at that table all night.

*"Well,"* came Frik, into the silence, soft for once, *"now I want to know which one's which."*

Nobody told him.

Yusuf gathered the notebooks and squared the soft printout against the table edge, the numbers all where he'd left them, all still true, all still open. Three-quarters of a million tonnes. Eighteen-fifty a year. Fifty tonnes on a wall, by hand, not yet reproduced. A city for the dead that couldn't water the living. Real, every one. Checkable, every one. A draught you could stand in.

And one more that wasn't on the page, that he had no figure for, that he wasn't going to chase tonight — whether the silence out on the canal was a hole or a door, and whose it was to close.

He turned off the tube. The fan kept the heat moving from corner to corner, finding nowhere to put it.
