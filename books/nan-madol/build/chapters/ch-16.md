# Chapter 16 — The Hand Off

The tide had gone out far enough that you could hear the wall.

Frik didn't expect that. He'd expected the slap of water in the canals, the mangrove ticking, the outboard somebody was failing to start two islets over. What he hadn't expected was the wall itself making a sound — a low, dry, settling sigh as the basalt shed the day's heat into the cooling air, each column easing a hair against its neighbour the way old timber does in a barn at dusk. Seven metres of stacked stone, breathing.

Nandauwas. The big enclosure. The royal one.

He stood at the foot of it with his hands in his pockets because there was nothing useful to do with his hands here, and that was the whole problem with Frik on a good day. He watched the others arrange themselves the way the crew always arranged themselves: Mira a half-step back, reading the gaps between people; Edith with the camera *down*, hanging off her shoulder, lens to her hip, which was its own kind of statement on this ground; Yusuf doing arithmetic under his breath because Yusuf was incapable of standing in front of a large object without weighing it.

And Jakobus, who had come a very long way to look at someone else's wall.

The Beast was forty kilometres away and useless, shipped onto an island it couldn't help him cross. He'd come the last stretch by skiff like everyone else, on the tide, by permission. He looked, Frik thought, faintly absurd here — a weathered man built for dust and roads, standing in mangrove light with his trousers rolled — and not absurd at all the second he started to read.

He didn't touch it first. That was the tell Frik had learned to watch for. Lesser men touched it first.

Jakobus put his hands behind his back and walked the base course slowly, his head tipped, following something with his eyes that Frik couldn't see until Jakobus made him see it.

"There," he said. Not loud. "And there. See how he's laid it."

Frik looked.

"Long face. Then the end. Then the long face." Jakobus's chin moved along the course like a man counting boxcars. "Length of the stone running *with* the wall — that's your stretcher. Then he turns one ninety degrees, drives it *into* the wall — that's your header, the end-grain showing, tying the front face to the back. Stretcher, header, stretcher. Course on course, and he staggers the joints so no seam runs straight up." He stepped back. "It's a log cabin. Same binding a man in Norway builds in pine. Built in basalt logs that the planet split for him."

> *Oh good,* said the fish, from nowhere and everywhere, the way it did. *The planet pre-cut the LEGO. That's the part everyone leans on. "Nature made hexagons, mystery solved." Sure. And the bricks at the hardware store come pre-fired, so I assume your house assembled itself.*

Yusuf, still under his breath: "Five to twenty-five tonnes a column. The cornerstone's about fifty."

"Fifty," Jakobus agreed, like a man hearing a price he respects.

This is the official story, and it is true, and the crew had spent a fortnight learning to revere it: a medieval city. Not a lost continent, not skymasons — a known dynasty, the Saudeleur, who unified an island and ruled it from the water until a man named Isokelekel came and unmade them, somewhere around the early seventeenth century. The radiocarbon puts the great building phase from about AD 1180. While cathedrals were going up in stone across Europe, the people of this reef were going up in stone too, in their own grammar, for their own dead. Ninety-two islets. Canals threaded between them — *Nan Madol*, the spaces between. Three-quarters of a million tonnes of basalt, give or take, laid down at something like eighteen hundred tonnes a year for four hundred years.

Frik had seen Yusuf write those numbers in his notebook and then sit very still in front of them, which from Yusuf was a kind of reverence too.

"And the how-it-stands," Jakobus said, "I can give you. I'd stake the road on it. Dry-laid, header-and-stretcher, on a coral-rubble raft so the whole island floats its own weight on the reef. That's a builder's answer. I'd build it that way. I could *teach* a man to lay a course like this by Thursday."

He stopped.

"What I can't tell you," he said, and here his voice changed — got smaller, got careful, the way it always did right before the part Frik had come to think of as *the relay* — "is how the fifty-tonne one got onto the top of a seven-metre wall, on a reef, by hand, with no crane, no ramp, and nothing left lying about to say so. And I can't tell you why anyone dragged it twenty-five miles across open sea to a place with no water in it and not one thing growing you could eat."

> *Don't look at me,* said the fish. *I'm a fish. I'm extremely qualified to live on a reef with no fresh water and I STILL wouldn't build a city here.*

This was the potato, and it had been a real one. Frik had watched it happen with his own eyes back at the start — a modern team, a good archaeologist, a hired engineer and his rig, raft one column the easy way, the *settled* way, the way the move-along version implies it was routine. And the raft had sat low and sullen and the lift had been a misery and the thing they got up had been a lumpy, sweating, four-man approximation of a craft that had stood flawless behind them the whole time, not deigning to comment. Experimental archaeology has tried to reproduce the quarry-to-wall feat. It has not, so far, managed. *Not yet reproduced* — which is a smaller and more honest sentence than *impossible*, and a stranger one, because impossible you can file and forget. *Not yet* keeps you awake.

The columns on the seabed off Sokehs — the divers from Oregon found them back in '88, '89 — read, soberly, as lost cargo. A haul that didn't make it. Evidence of the carrying, not of anything that flew. Frik liked that. It was the right size of mystery: not a drowned temple, just the fact that someone was *carrying basalt across open water by the tonne* and sometimes dropped it.

So you had the how-it-stands, which Jakobus had just handed over clean. And you had the two holes, which he hadn't, because they weren't his to close.

What you had left was the third thing. The question Jakobus came across the world to *not* answer.

"What's it for," Frik said, before he could stop himself.

It came out into a silence and lay there.

Jakobus didn't take it. That was the thing Frik kept failing to be ready for, every single time, the move that made Jakobus *Jakobus* and not just a clever man with a good eye. He had the answer to so many things. He read this wall like other people read a paragraph. And the one question that mattered — *what is it for* — he turned, deliberately, like a man turning a header into a wall, and drove it sideways.

He looked at the custodian.

The custodian had been there the whole time. He'd come up from the skiff with them and stood off to the side, near the corner of the great enclosure, in the relaxed, rooted way of a man who is on his own ground and has nothing to prove to people who are not. A windbreaker over a faded shirt. He'd watched Jakobus walk the base course with an expression Frik couldn't fully read, which was unusual, because Frik could usually read *something*. He looked at the custodian and got: patience. Amusement, maybe, at the edges. And under it, shut like a good door, something Frik had no business reaching for.

Mira had gone still in the particular way she went still. Frik clocked that. She was watching the custodian's hands, his shoulders, the set of his jaw, the whole grammar of a body, and whatever she was reading there she was reading three beats ahead of the rest of them and she was not saying it out loud.

Jakobus spoke to the custodian directly. Not over his shoulder, not as a performance for the crew. To him.

"I can tell you how your ancestors stood it up," Jakobus said. "I'd be a fool to tell you what they stood it up *for*."

The custodian let that sit. He had the gift — Frik envied it the way he envied anyone unhurried — of letting a thing sit without filling it.

"People come," the custodian said at last. Easy. Friendly, even. "They ask how. We tell them what we know, which is plenty. They ask why." He smiled a little. "We tell them we don't know."

> *He just lied,* said the fish, very quietly, in the tone it used maybe twice a book. *No. Hang on. He didn't lie. Watch his face. That's not the face of a man who doesn't know. I'm a fish and even I can — okay. Okay. Not my reef. Carrying on.*

Frik glanced at Mira.

Mira had heard the same thing he had — except she hadn't *heard* it, she'd seen it, off the mouth, off the body, off the half-second between the question and the easy answer that was a beat too smooth to be ignorance. *We don't know* came out of the custodian the way a well-oiled gate swings: no resistance, no hunting for it. Frik watched her watch it. He watched her understand exactly what the custodian was doing — which was holding a door shut, politely, with both hands, in front of guests — and he watched her decide, in real time, not to put her shoulder to it.

She caught Frik looking. The smallest movement of her head. *Leave it.*

He left it.

It is, in fairness, the easiest thing in the world to leave a *we don't know* alone, until you've spent a fortnight learning that this one was load-bearing. That there was an outfit forty kilometres back in Kolonia in an air-conditioned office with renderings on the wall and a slide that said *site of undetermined historical significance*, and that the renderings looked very nice, and that the whole pitch — the thing that made the ground *takeable* — rested on the same three words. *Even the locals say they don't know.* A vacancy. A riddle free for the solving, which is to say free for the developing, because you can pour a foundation in a vacancy but you cannot pour one in a grave with keepers.

The custodian's *we don't know* and the developer's *we don't know* were, Frik thought, the exact same words. And they meant the precise opposite. One was a hand on a door. The other was a crowbar.

Mira could tell them apart. Frik had watched her do it. It was the only thing on this island anyone could do that the engineer's rig and the archaeologist's trowel and Jakobus's perfect eye could not, and she was doing it without saying a single word, and the not-saying was the whole point.

Jakobus inclined his head to the custodian. Not a bow. An acknowledgement. The kind one tradesman gives another across a finished joint.

"Then that's where it sits," he said.

"That's where it sits," the custodian agreed.

And that was the relay, complete. Frik felt it land — not as a solution, because nothing had been solved, but as a *handing over*. The how-it-stands: Jakobus's, given freely, a builder's pride in another builder's work. The how-it-got-here and the why-a-foodless-reef: open, honest, nobody's, hanging in the salt air for anyone to stand under. And the what-it-*for* — the heart of it, the meaning, the thing the whole hungry world wanted to take home in its pocket — that one had a custodian, and a protocol, and a right, and it stayed exactly where it had been for four hundred years.

Edith hadn't lifted the camera once.

Frik noticed that last, the way you notice the dog that didn't bark. She'd stood through the whole of it with the lens at her hip and her hand loose on the strap, and for a woman whose entire life was the choosing of what to frame, the choosing *not* to was the loudest thing she'd done all week.

The tide was on the turn. You could feel it before you could see it — the canals beginning to fill, the water finding its way back up the spaces between, the islets reconnecting their dark veins. They'd go out the way they came in, by water, by leave.

Jakobus took one more look up the wall, all seven metres of it, the staggered joints, the fifty-tonne stone sitting up there in the dimming light where no one had any business getting it.

"Beautiful work," he said, to the wall, or to the man who built it eight hundred years ago, or to the custodian standing at the corner of it now — Frik genuinely couldn't tell which, and decided he didn't need to.

The custodian heard it anyway. He looked up at the same stone.

"Yes," he said. And did not say what it was for.
