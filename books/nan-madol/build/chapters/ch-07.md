# Chapter 7 — Lost Cargo

The skiff smelled of two-stroke and bait, and Edith had her camera in a dry-bag on her knees like a thing that might bolt.

Soumas — that was the diver's name, or the part of it he'd offered, a man maybe sixty with forearms like mooring cleats and reading glasses pushed up into grey hair — cut the outboard, and the silence came in over the gunwale the way the tide does, all at once and from underneath. They were at the reef edge off the southeast face, where the lagoon shelves and then doesn't, where the colour under the hull went from the pale green of sand to the kind of blue that has a floor you can't see.

"Here," Soumas said. He didn't point. He just stopped.

Yusuf had the printout in a ziplock, soft at the folds from handling. The University of Oregon survey, 1988 and '89. Divers had gone down off this margin and found basalt columns on the seabed — the same prismatic logs that stood in seven-metre walls a thousand metres behind them, except these were lying in the sand and the silt, scattered, at depth. Hexagonal. Unmistakable. Quarried stone where no quarry is.

"You read it," Soumas said to Yusuf, the way you'd say *show me your hand.*

Yusuf read it. The dry, careful sentences. Submerged columnar basalt. Consistent with the construction stone. Distribution mapped along the channel approaches. He read it flat, the way the report wanted to be read, and Soumas listened with his glasses still up in his hair and his eyes on the water, and when Yusuf finished he made a small sound that wasn't agreement and wasn't argument.

Mira was watching the diver's hands. She'd told Edith once, on a different boat, that you learn more from a man's hands when he's listening than from his face when he's talking. Soumas's hands were loose on his thighs. Loose was a kind of answer.

---

Edith went down second, after Soumas, with the camera in its housing and the housing's weight pulling her trim wrong until she settled.

She had not wanted to bring the camera. She had wanted to and not wanted to, which was the same argument she'd been having with herself in one form or another since the war, the one where the want and the not-want were the same muscle. You see a thing. You frame it. The framing is a small theft and a small gift and you never get to choose which one it'll turn out to be. So she'd brought it, and she hated that she'd brought it, and she was already composing as she finned down the slope because that was the thing she could not stop doing, the watching she'd been doing since long before the war, since childhood, the only channel anyone had ever left open to her.

The light went long and blue. Soumas swam ahead of her with the economy of a man who'd been doing this since before she was kind to herself, barely moving his legs, and below them the slope steepened and then she saw the first one.

A column. Lying in the silt at maybe eighteen metres, half its length furred with growth, the hexagon of its cross-section clean and shocking and *machined-looking* against the soft chaos of the seabed. Then another, a body's length on. Then, off to the left where the slope fell away into the dark, the suggestion of more — angles that the eye wanted to assemble, the way the eye always wants to assemble.

That was the thing the report didn't have words for. The *wanting.*

Edith brought the camera up. Through the housing port the scene composed itself into the photograph it wanted to be: foreground column, the diagonal of it, Soumas's silhouette above with the surface a hammered sheet behind him, and in the mid-ground that blue suggestion of structure, of *more*, of a city that kept going down past where the light gave out. Frame it like that and you had the cover of a magazine. Frame it like that and you had *the sunken half of Nan Madol*, the drowned streets, the Pacific Atlantis, and every reader who ever saw it would carry that frame out into the world and it would be, in a way that the actual seabed was not, a lie.

She lowered the camera without taking it.

> *Coward,* said the fish, which had no business being underwater and was anyway only in her head. *That's the shot and you know it. Cthulhu's basement. Print it on a tote bag.*

She let it sink past, the way you let a current go by.

---

Soumas had stopped over the first column. He looked back to be sure she was watching — not the camera, *her*, the eyes behind the port — and then he did a thing she didn't understand at first.

He laid his palm flat on the seabed beside the column, in the silt, and made a slow line with the edge of his hand. A furrow. Then he pointed up the slope, toward shore, toward the shallows and the islets beyond. Then he made the furrow again, the same direction, and tapped the column, and shrugged the whole-body shrug a diver makes, hands out, that says: *it fell here.*

It fell. It was being moved, and it fell, or the raft swamped, or the lashing parted, and it went down the slope into the blue and stayed where it stopped, and there it was, eighteen metres down and seven hundred years on, lying exactly where a dropped thing lies.

She understood the gesture and she understood that he had chosen the gesture, that he'd brought her down here not to show her a sunken city but to *take the sunken city away from her*, gently, with his bare hand in the silt. The eye wants to assemble. He was un-assembling it for her, one furrow at a time.

She raised the camera again and this time she shot the furrow. His hand. The drag-line in the silt and the column at the end of it and the long empty slope going up toward the light where the work had been *going*. Not the drowned-city frame. The *dropped-cargo* frame. The same stones, the opposite photograph.

It was a worse magazine cover and a truer one. She took three exposures and her hands were steady and that, she'd tell no one, was the whole of why she'd come.

---

On the surface they hung off the line and pulled their masks down and the world came back loud — the slap of chop on the hull, a frigatebird working the thermals, the far diesel of someone's skiff in the lagoon.

"You see," Soumas said. Not a question.

"I saw it fell," Edith said. "You showed me it fell."

He nodded, slow, and pushed his glasses down out of his hair and onto his nose as if to read her, which after thirty years near these waters he could probably do better with the glasses than without. "Oregon people," he said. "Eighty-eight. Eighty-nine. Good people. They dive, they map, they write it down careful." He tapped his own chest. "Lost cargo. That is the honest one. Stone going to the wall, didn't make the wall. End of the story."

"But," said Yusuf, who had not been able to stop himself for the whole of his life and was not going to start now.

Soumas looked at him with something that was nearly affection. "But," he agreed.

---

Here is the honest one, the one Soumas had laid out with his hand: the columns on the seabed are the spillage of the haul. You move three-quarters of a million tonnes of basalt across open water over four centuries and you are going to drop some of it. The arithmetic Yusuf had been carrying since chapter and verse — the ~1,850 tonnes a year, sustained, for ~400 years — does not just describe a finished wall. It describes a *traffic.* A traffic that long and that heavy leaves wreckage the way a road leaves roadkill, and the wreckage was right there in the silt with Soumas's furrow drawn beside it. The columns underwater are not a drowned structure. They never said they were. The report never said so; Soumas would not say so; and the narrator of this will not say so either, because the honest read is the unglamorous one, and the unglamorous one is true.

That is the floor. That is the thing the dive establishes, cold-eyed, with a man's bare palm.

And now stand on the floor and look at what the floor doesn't cover.

Because the lost cargo is evidence — checkable, mapped, sitting at eighteen metres where anyone with a tank can go count it — of the *haul itself.* Not of a method. Of the *fact*. Stone left the quarry on the Sokehs side, around twenty-five miles off, and crossed open Pacific water, and some of it didn't arrive, and we can prove it didn't arrive because there it lies. What the cargo proves is that the impossible-sounding sentence is *literally true*: they were moving fifty-tonne logs of rock across the sea. The columns on the seabed close the question *did they really haul it that far* — yes — and they leave wide open, gaping, untouched, the question the haul was always about. *By what means.* Rafts that could carry a fifty-tonne cornerstone and mostly not sink. Some manner of lifting it off the raft and up onto a wall already five, six, seven metres high, on a reef, with the tide working against you twice a day. The lost cargo is a receipt for a delivery nobody can reconstruct.

> *So we proved the UPS truck was real,* the fish offered, surfacing for air it didn't need, *and we still got no idea how they got the couch up the stairs.*

For once nobody told it to shut up.

---

Mira had stayed in the boat. She didn't dive; the pressure and the ears were a conversation she'd lost too many times. She'd watched the surface instead, the bubbles tracking, and she'd watched Soumas come up first and pull his mask and *not* look pleased, and she'd watched the set of him as he talked to Yusuf and Edith with the chop knocking the hull.

When the others were back aboard and peeling out of their gear, she touched Soumas's wrist to get his eyes — she always asked for the eyes — and signed, slowly, the small fingerspelled-and-gesture pidgin the crew had built around her, *more down there?* and pointed at the dark water and then made the going-on, going-deeper sign they'd agreed on weeks ago, a hand sliding away and down.

Soumas understood her the way Jakobus understood her, by the body, without translation. He looked at her for a moment. Then he looked at the water. Then he did a thing she catalogued and did not interpret: he did not say *no*, and he did not say *yes.* He moved his head a fraction, side to side, the universal *who can say.* And his eyes, when they came back to her, were doing the careful, lidded thing she'd been watching the custodian's people do all week — the look of a man standing in front of a door he knows the shape of, with his hand flat against it, not opening it for you and not pretending the door isn't there.

She held his gaze and let him see that she'd seen it. *Door,* her own face said back, without words, because words were never the channel she'd been given. *I see the door. I'm not going to push it.*

Something in him let down half an inch.

"Lost cargo," he said again, out loud now, for Yusuf and Edith, for the report, for the record. "That is the true answer." He coiled the line in his cleat-forearms, neat loops, a man putting his boat away. "The Oregon people are right." He looked, once, back at the blue water where the slope went down past the light. "And there are stones down there past where they mapped. I have seen them." He shrugged, and started the outboard, and the two-stroke ate the rest of it, so that whether he'd said *and I have not gone to count them* or *and I will not tell you what they are* or nothing further at all, no one in the boat could have sworn.

Edith had the furrow-shot in the camera. The honest frame. She kept her hand over the dry-bag the whole way in, the way you keep your hand on a thing you're not yet sure you're allowed to have taken.

The reef slid by. The walls of Nandauwas stood up out of the mangrove ahead of them, dry and grey and silent, and somewhere off the southeast face, eighteen metres down and seven hundred years late, a dropped stone lay in the silt at the end of a furrow, pointing the way it had been going.
