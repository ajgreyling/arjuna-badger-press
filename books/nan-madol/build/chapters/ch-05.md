# Chapter 5 — The Adversary Surfaces

The Pohnpei State office in Kolonia kept its air-conditioner running like a grievance — a window unit, rust-streaked, dripping into a coffee can someone had set beneath it years ago and never moved. The can was full. Yusuf watched a drop hang off the lip, swell, let go. Across the table, a man in a pressed lavender shirt was sliding a folder toward the middle of the laminate like he was dealing a hand he already knew would win.

"We're calling it Madolenihmw Heritage Gardens," the man said. His name was Pruitt — Garrett Pruitt, *Director of Cultural Development*, a phrase that had been printed on a card and handed across with both hands, the way you'd give someone a gift. "Working title. Marketing'll murder it."

He laughed at his own joke. He had the easy warmth of a man who'd never once been the only person in a room who couldn't follow the conversation, and so didn't know to watch for it.

Yusuf opened the folder.

The renderings were good. That was the first thing — they were *good*, expensive, the kind of work that costs real money to make look this effortless. An aerial of the reef flat, the islets laid out in their soft greens and tidal silvers, and woven through them a network of teak boardwalks on slim pilings, lit from below so the canals glowed at dusk. A "discovery center" with a living roof. A "wellness pavilion." Little figures in linen, leaning on rails, gazing at the basalt with the precise expression of people who have paid to feel something.

"Elevated walkways," Pruitt was saying. "Zero-footprint. Nothing touches the stone. We had marine guys out, ecology guys. The whole point is preservation — you can't preserve a thing nobody visits, that's just decay with extra steps."

Beside Yusuf, Mira had her chin tipped slightly down, eyes up, the way she sat when the words were going to matter less than the man saying them. Edith had not taken her camera out of the bag. She'd taken her hands off it, which was a different thing, and Yusuf had learned to notice that too.

The lawyer had said nothing yet.

Her name was Loretta Henos. She'd come in with the folder already on the table, which told you whose office this actually was; she sat to the side, not across, a legal pad squared to the table edge and a pen she hadn't uncapped. Marine Conservation and Heritage Law, the NGO on her lanyard, the letters small. She'd shaken everyone's hand and then gone still in a way Yusuf recognized because it was his own way — the stillness of someone doing arithmetic while the other person does theatre.

> *Linen people,* said the fish, who had wedged himself into the coffee can's water and was regarding the renderings with naked contempt. *Look at 'em. Gazing. You don't gaze at a graveyard, pal, you take your hat off.*

Yusuf turned a page.

Here was the part he'd been waiting for. Past the renderings, past the ecology appendix with its turtle counts and its mangrove buffers, the language went flat and careful, and flat-and-careful was where the money lived. He read it the way he read everything — slowly, twice, the second time for what wasn't there.

*Whereas the site at Nan Madol is of indeterminate origin and uncertain custodial status —*

He read it again.

*— and whereas no continuous traditional ownership has been established to the satisfaction of —*

"This is beautiful work," Yusuf said, and meant the renderings, and Pruitt brightened, and Yusuf let him, because the next thing he said was going to cost the brightness. "Who holds the title now?"

"Well, that's the elegant part." Pruitt leaned in. "Nobody, really. It's State land — reef flat, foreshore. The traditional side, the Nahnmwarki side—" he made a small respectful gesture, the gesture of a man laying a flower he intends to invoice for "—that's deeply important, of course, deeply, and we'd want full consultation. But legally, custodially, it's…" He spread his hands. "It's open. Which is rare. Which is, frankly, a gift, because it means we can do this *right*, get the framework in place before anybody comes in and does it wrong."

*Indeterminate origin.* Yusuf set his finger on the line without pointing to it, just rested it there on the page where Pruitt couldn't see.

"You've done your homework," Yusuf said.

"We've been at this two years."

"Then you know the radiocarbon. Roughly eleven-eighty for the megalithic phase. You know it's the Saudeleur capital. You know about Isokelekel, the overthrow, early seventeenth century." Yusuf kept his voice mild, conversational, a man comparing notes. "That's not indeterminate. That's one of the better-dated medieval sites in the Pacific."

Something moved behind Pruitt's face. Not alarm. Patience. The patience of a man who has had this exact conversation before and has a paragraph ready.

"You're right," Pruitt said. "And isn't it remarkable? Honestly — it *is*. But here's the thing." He tapped the folder, friendly. "We know the *date*. We don't know the *how*. Nobody does. They quarried that basalt — what, twenty-five miles? Across open water. Seven-and-a-half-meter walls, dry-stacked, a cornerstone people put at fifty tonnes." He shook his head, and the head-shake was genuine, the wonder was *real*, that was the awful part. "They've tried to reproduce it. Smart people, modern equipment. They can't. Can't even get close. And if the experts can't tell you how it was built—" he opened his hands again, the gift gesture, "—then nobody can tell you, with any authority, what it *means*. Or whose it is to speak for. Can they."

There it was.

> *Ohh,* said the fish, very quietly, all the heckle gone out of him for a second. *That's a clean one.*

It was. Yusuf would give him that, alone, later, when he wrote it down. It was a clean one. The man had taken the most honest thing about Nan Madol — the genuine, mainstream, expert-acknowledged *we don't know how* — and he'd run it backward, like a current. *We don't know how* became *we don't know what it means*, and *we don't know what it means* became *so it doesn't, securely, belong to anyone*, and a thing that belongs to no one securely is a thing you can build on. The unsolved lift wasn't a problem for the resort. It was the foundation. The mystery wasn't in the way. The mystery was load-bearing.

Yusuf had spent a working life reading documents like this — the grant that funds the road that opens the forest, the conservation easement that turns out to convey the water rights. He knew the shape of a hustle that wore its best clothes to church. This one was tailored.

He glanced at Mira.

She was looking at Pruitt the way you look at a tide line, reading where the water had been. Her hands were quiet in her lap. Then she lifted them, small, just for the crew, and signed — *he believes it.* A beat. *That part is true.* Her face did a thing that wasn't quite a smile. *Makes the rest worse.*

Yusuf nodded once. That was the trap of it. A liar you can corner. A man genuinely moved by a thing he is also quietly stealing — there's no corner. He'll meet your facts and thank you for them and write them into the brochure.

"The construction question's open," Yusuf said. "I'll grant you that. Honestly open. The lift, the transport — they haven't reproduced it, you're right, and they're frank about it, the good ones. *Not yet reproduced.*" He let the *yet* sit. "But that's a question about an engineering method. It isn't a question about who buries their dead there."

"Buried," Pruitt said gently. "Past tense. With respect."

And the lawyer uncapped her pen.

She didn't write anything. She just uncapped it, set the cap beside the pad, and the small click of it stopped the room the way a tap on a glass stops a table. Pruitt turned to her with the reflex of a man who'd learned, over two years, exactly which sounds in this office to take seriously.

"Mr. Pruitt has shown you the gardens," Loretta Henos said. Her voice was unhurried and entirely without warmth toward him; toward the crew, neutral, a person establishing a record. "I'd like to show you the parts of the file the renderings sit on top of."

She turned the folder back toward herself, found a page near the bottom without searching, and read.

"'Indeterminate origin.' 'Uncertain custodial status.' 'No continuous traditional ownership established.'" She looked up. "Do you know what those phrases are?"

Nobody answered. Pruitt's smile had gone fixed, a thing left on a shelf.

"They're not findings," she said. "Nobody found those. They're *requests.* They're what you write in the recitals when you want a court, eventually, to treat a thing as empty because saying it's empty enough times in enough documents makes the emptiness load-bearing." She set the page down flat. "The site has custodians. It has had custodians without interruption for eight hundred years. The Nahnmwarki of Madolenihmw holds it. The protocol of who enters, who speaks, what is told and what is kept — that's not a gap in the record. That *is* the record. It's just not written where Mr. Pruitt's lawyers would like to find it filed."

"With respect, Ms. Henos," Pruitt said, and the *respect* was thinner now, "oral tradition isn't title. I wish it were. The State—"

"The State knows exactly what it's sitting on," she said, "which is why this has taken you two years and not two months." She turned to the crew, and now there was the faintest something in her face, dry, almost amused, almost an invitation. "You came here to film stones, I'm told. The lift. The *how.*"

"That's what we came for," Edith said. Her hands were still away from the bag.

"Good. Stay on the *how.*" Loretta tapped the recital line one more time, the line about indeterminate origin, and she said it without any heat at all, which was what made it land like cold water: "Because the moment you let him turn *we don't know how* into *we don't know whose*—the second you let those two silences sound like the same silence—you've helped him pour the slab. He's not buying a mystery. He's manufacturing a vacancy. There's a difference, and it's the whole difference, and the only people in this room being paid to confuse them are him."

The window unit dripped into the full can. Pruitt began to say something about partnership, about consultation, about how this was exactly the conversation he'd hoped to have.

Yusuf wasn't listening anymore. He was looking at the rendering — the boardwalks, the underlit canals, the linen people leaning on the rails with their paid-for awe — and he was seeing it the way the lawyer had just taught him to see it in nine seconds flat, faster and cleaner than he'd have gotten there alone. *Zero-footprint.* Nothing touches the stone. Of course nothing touches the stone. You don't have to touch a grave to take it. You only have to get everyone to agree it's a garden.

Mira's hand moved in his peripheral vision. He glanced over.

*Two kinds of "don't know,"* she signed, slow, so Edith could catch it too. *His and theirs.* She looked at Loretta — at the capped pen now recapped, at the woman who had read the whole hand in less time than it took Pruitt to clear his throat. *She knows which is which.* A pause, and then, dry as the rest of her: *We didn't.*

> *Speak for yourself,* said the fish, climbing out of the coffee can dripping, restored to insolence now that there was someone in the room worth respecting. *I clocked the linen.*

Pruitt slid his card across the laminate again, a second copy, in case the first had been lost — both hands, the gift gesture, immaculate to the end.

"Think it over," he said warmly. "There's room in this for everybody."

Loretta Henos stood, gathered her pad, and held the door. She didn't say *that's not true.* She didn't have to. She just held the door, and waited, the way you wait at the edge of a thing that is not yours to cross — and Yusuf understood, picking up the folder, that the silence she was keeping in that doorway and the silence Pruitt was selling looked, to anyone not watching closely, exactly alike.

He'd been watching closely. It wasn't enough. The drop hung off the lip of the can behind him, swelled, let go.
