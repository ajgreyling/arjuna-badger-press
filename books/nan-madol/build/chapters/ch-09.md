# Chapter 9 — The Dragon's Story

The rain had stopped an hour ago but the village still dripped, every leaf and gutter and corrugated eave letting go of the day's water one bead at a time, so the dark was full of small percussion. Yusuf sat where he'd been put — on a low bench at the edge of the cleared ground, under a roof of woven pandanus and tin, his back to a wall that smelled of woodsmoke and old kava. Somewhere behind the houses a generator coughed and held. The bulb overhead was the colour of weak tea.

The *sakau* was being pounded. Yusuf had watched it the whole way: the four men on their knees around the flat basalt stone, the rounded stones in their hands rising and falling, *tonk — tonk-tonk — tonk*, a rhythm that wasn't quite a rhythm, that kept shifting under you like a tide. The pepper root mashed to a wet grey pulp. Then the hibiscus bark, twisted into a rope, the pulp folded in and wrung, and the liquid coming off it the colour and consistency of something that should not be drunk and would be. The first cup went to the old man in the good chair. Everything in the right order, and the order was the point.

Mira sat to Yusuf's left, hands loose in her lap, eyes everywhere. She'd told him on the boat over — fingers and a little voice, the way she did when she wanted to be sure — *watch who they serve and when. That's the whole conversation, before anyone talks.* So he watched. Second cup to a man with a Honiara T-shirt and a face like carved wood. Third, fourth. The hierarchy poured itself out in coconut shells and nobody had to say a word.

Edith's camera stayed in the bag. She'd asked, once, with her eyebrows, and the old man's daughter had given the smallest shake of the head, and the bag had stayed zipped. Edith sat with her hands folded over it like she was keeping it warm.

"They call him Soumas," the daughter said quietly, leaning toward the crew. She was maybe fifty, with reading glasses pushed up into her hair and a phone in her shirt pocket that buzzed twice and was ignored. "It's not his name. It's — the one who holds the talk. Tonight he holds it. You should drink first, then he'll start."

The cup came to Yusuf in its turn, low down the order, which felt correct. He took it in both hands the way he'd been shown. It tasted of mud and aspirin and, underneath, something green and alive. His lips went numb in a slow expanding ring. He passed the shell back, said *kalahngan* the way the daughter had coached him, and the old man — Soumas — looked at him for the first time, and almost smiled.

Soumas talked low, in Pohnpeian, and his daughter laid the English over the top of it like a second voice on a second track, half a beat behind, so you heard the shape of the real thing under the translation the whole time.

There were two brothers, he said. Sorcerers. Olisihpa and Olosohpa. They came from somewhere to the west — *down the wind*, the daughter said, and then said it wasn't quite that, that the word held a direction and a long-ago both at once and English only had room for one. The brothers came to Pohnpei looking for a place to build an altar to Nahnsapwe, the lord of the thunder, so the people could honour him properly. They tried in the west. The reef was wrong. They tried again. Wrong again. They went all the way round the island, brothers and their followers, trying the stone in place after place, and each time the sea or the land said no, until they came to the lee of Temwen, to the reef flat where the water ran in channels, and there, finally, the stone would stand.

"And then," the daughter said, and Soumas paused, and the generator filled the gap, and a child somewhere was hushed.

"Then they sang."

The brothers sang, and the basalt came. The great stones lifted off the land where they lay — out at Sokehs, on the far side, the daughter said, the cliffs where the rock grows in columns like a stack of firewood God left out — they lifted and they *flew*, through the air, across the water, and laid themselves down course on course, long face and short face, the way you lace your fingers, until the walls stood. A flying dragon carried them, some tell it. The brothers' own power, others tell it. Soumas's grandmother told it as the dragon, the daughter said, and so Soumas told it as the dragon, because that was his grandmother's and now it was his.

Yusuf felt the whole thing settle into him, slow as the kava. He wanted it. That was the truth of his own body and he knew it for what it was — the same hunger that had got him fired from two jobs and onto this boat, the want that lit up like a struck match whenever a story was bigger than the room it was told in. *The stones flew.* He could see it. He'd have given a year of his life to see it.

He looked at the wall they could just make out past the houses, across the black water — Nandauwas, the great enclosure, the corner of it catching the village light, basalt logs stacked higher than two men, dry, no mortar, holding up four hundred years of nothing-has-moved. He'd stood under it that afternoon. He'd put his hand flat on a single column and felt the cold come off it and done the sum he'd promised himself he wouldn't do, because Mira's book or no, somebody on the crew always ran the numbers and lately it kept being him.

Five tonnes, the small ones. Twenty-five, the big ones. The cornerstone — fifty. Fifty tonnes is a loaded semi-trailer. Off the cliffs at Sokehs, twenty-five miles by sea around a reef, and then *up*, hand over hand, seven and a half metres, onto a wall, with the tide coming in over your ankles and nothing to push against. Three-quarters of a million tonnes of it. Eighteen hundred tonnes a year, every year, for four hundred years, while the same place grew not one yam and held not one mouthful of fresh water, so every drinking-gourd and every basket of taro that ever kept a builder alive came across that same water by canoe.

And nobody, in a hundred years of trying, in wetsuits, with cranes on barges and engineers with clipboards — nobody had managed to put one column back the way it had been taken down.

Of course you'd say a dragon. What else holds a weight like that? What other word is the right size?

---

The daughter had stopped translating. Soumas was watching Yusuf again, and this time it wasn't almost a smile.

"You're doing arithmetic," he said. In English. Flat and clear, no accent to hide behind, and the whole bench went still around it.

"I—" Yusuf started.

"It's all right." Soumas turned the empty shell over in his hands. "They all do it. The first ones did it. Men came off ships a hundred and fifty years ago — German, Spanish, before that the whalers — and they stood under the wall and their faces did exactly what your face is doing now. The sum. *It can't have been them.*" He set the shell down. "You know what they decided?"

Nobody answered. The smart move was to let him answer.

"They decided we couldn't have built it." The daughter had picked the translation back up, soft, even though her father was in English now, laying her own voice under his again like she couldn't stop, like the two-track was the proper way. "A lost race. White men, some of them said — a civilisation that drowned, that we were squatting in the ruins of. Atlantis come east. Sons of the sun." He let each one land. "Then later ones, with their own machines and their own arithmetic, they said: people from the stars. The dragon, you understand, became a *ship*. The brothers became — what is the word—"

"Astronauts," the daughter said.

"Astronauts." Soumas tasted it like the kava, found it wanting. "They took my grandmother's dragon and they cut its wings off and they put it in the sky and called it not-ours. Every one of them. The ones who said we were too simple, and the ones who said the truth was too big for us — they ended in the same place. *Somebody else did this. Somebody not these people.*" He spread his hands at the dark, at the village, at the wall across the water. "That is the only story outsiders have ever been able to tell about this place. They cannot say *we don't know how.* It costs them too much. So they say *it wasn't you,* which costs them nothing, and they go home."

---

Yusuf had been waiting his whole life to hear a flying dragon described by a man who believed it from his grandmother. He had got that. And he had got, in the same breath, the thing that turned his stomach over — the *aliens* riff, the one that always came dressed as wonder and was wearing, underneath, the oldest insult there was. He knew that theft when he heard it. He'd had it done to him, on a smaller scale, in committee rooms, every time someone took his good idea and put a credible face on it before they'd let it through the door. *Too strange to be yours. Let me hold that for you.*

"They wanted it to be true," he said, before he could stop himself, and then, because honesty was the only thing he had to offer that was worth anything: "I wanted it to be true. Just now. The stones flying. I wanted it so much."

"I know," said Soumas. Not unkindly. "It's a good story. It's mine, and it's good, and you wanted it, and that's right." He leaned forward and the chair creaked under him. "But you wanted the *dragon*. You didn't want the spaceship. You feel the difference?"

Yusuf felt it. It went down his back like cold water.

The dragon was theirs. The dragon came down the wind from the west with two sorcerer brothers who tried the whole island, reef by reef, before the stone would stand — a story about *belonging to the place,* about getting it wrong everywhere else and right *here,* about singing your dead a house. The spaceship was a story about the place being too good for the people in it. One was a gift his grandmother handed him in the dark. The other was a thing strangers had built out of the same materials to carry away.

Mira had turned her head. She was reading Soumas's face the way she read everything, and whatever she found there made her go very still, and then she did a thing Yusuf would think about for a long time afterward. She didn't ask the next question. The crew's whole instinct was sitting right there at the edge of the bench — *so how WAS it done, then, if not the dragon and not the ship, what's the real—* — and Mira looked at it, and set it down, and folded her hands back into her lap.

She signed something to Yusuf, small, just for him. *He's not telling us how.*

*No,* Yusuf signed back, clumsy.

*He's telling us it's not the question.*

Soumas watched the two of them pass the silent words and seemed to find it satisfactory, the way the *sakau* order had been satisfactory, things going where they belonged.

"You'll go and stand under the wall again tomorrow," he said. "You'll do the sum again. Everyone does. And the sum won't close — it never closes, that's the truth of it, even our people can't tell you the rope and the raft and the count of hands, it's a long time gone." He stood, slow, a hand on the chair-arm and the other on his daughter's shoulder. "When you can't close the sum, you have a choice. You can say *we don't know how.* That's the hard one — it leaves the wall standing and it leaves it *ours.* Or you can fill the gap with somebody from the sea or the sky, and then the wall is theirs, and there's nothing in it for us but to be the people who live in someone else's ruin."

He looked at Edith, at the bag she was keeping warm.

"Keep it closed tonight," he said. "Tomorrow you can ask my daughter. Some of the wall you can have. Some of it you can't." He started toward the houses, then stopped, and the last of it came over his shoulder, gentle as the rain starting up again on the tin.

"There's a man with money," he said, "been three times to the State office about the lease. He tells them the same thing you came here telling yourselves. *Nobody knows what it's for, nobody knows who.* " A pause. "An empty house, you can sell. He needs us not to know. You understand me?"

And then he was gone into the dark between the buildings, and the generator ran on, and Yusuf sat with the numb mouth and the dragon in his chest, holding both — the want and the theft, the gift and the lock — and not, for once in his life, trying to choose.
