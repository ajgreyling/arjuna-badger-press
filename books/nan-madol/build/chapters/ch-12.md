# Chapter 12 — The Spaces Between

The tide came in the way the custodian said it would, and not a minute before.

Mira felt it before she saw it — the canal under the skiff stopped being a thing they sat on top of and became a thing they were carried by. The hull lifted a hand's width. The pole the custodian held stopped pushing against mud and started pushing against water, and the difference travelled up through the wood and through the bench and into the base of her spine, where she kept most of what she knew about the world.

He had not turned on a light. He poled them in by the last of it, the sky gone the colour of a bruise behind the ridge, and the walls came up out of the dark on either side the way a cliff comes up out of fog — all at once, having been there the whole time.

Yusuf had his hand on the gunwale. Edith had her camera in her lap, capped, both hands flat on top of the cap, which was its own kind of statement and Mira had clocked it an hour ago. Frik sat at the bow with his knees up by his ears, too big for the boat, watching the water with the particular stillness he got when a thing was too large to make a joke about yet.

The custodian poled. He had not said three words since the launch ramp.

---

The official story is simple, and you should hold it in your head the way you'd hold a stone you mean to throw, because it is true and it is heavy and it will not save you.

Around the year 1180 — radiocarbon, medieval, the same century that was raising cathedrals a world away — a dynasty called the Saudeleur began stacking basalt on a reef flat off the southeast coast of Pohnpei. They built about ninety-two artificial islets. They laced them with tidal canals so the sea ran *through* the city instead of merely around it, and they called the place, in the end, the spaces between. Not the stones. The gaps. The water.

They did this for roughly four centuries, until a man named Isokelekel overthrew the last of the line, somewhere around the early seventeenth century, and the great work stopped.

That is the whole of it. A real dynasty. A known span. A purpose — a city for ceremony and for the dead, a place where rule and ritual sat together on the water. It is not a riddle. The archaeology is good and the people who do it are good at it and they will tell you, plainly and with love, what Nan Madol is.

> *"Medieval. Got it. So they were basically up to date." A pause. "On a reef. With no forklift. Carry on, professor."*

The professor — Marisol, who was at that moment in a guesthouse in Kolonia eating instant noodles and arguing with a spreadsheet — would have told the fish to carry on indeed, because the spreadsheet is the part nobody likes.

Seven hundred and fifty thousand tonnes of basalt. Run it flat: across four centuries, that's about eighteen hundred and fifty tonnes a year, every year, for four hundred years, hauled across roughly twenty-five miles of open water from a quarry on the far side of the island and stacked dry — no mortar, end-to-end and side-to-side like a man building a log cabin — into walls that in places stand more than seven metres high. One cornerstone runs to fifty tonnes.

Lift fifty tonnes. Onto a wall. On a reef. By hand. With nothing on the seabed afterward to tell you how, except some columns that fell off whatever was carrying them and lie there still — lost cargo, dropped in transit, evidence of the haul and nothing stranger than that.

People have tried to do it again. Properly, with permission and engineers and the best guesses anyone has. They have not managed it. *Not yet reproduced* is the phrase, and it is an honest phrase, and it does not mean what the man selling the boat tour wants it to mean. It means: we know it was done, and we cannot show you how.

That is the potato. The brightest people, in daylight, with the plan, fall short — and the thing they were trying to copy sits behind them in the dark, finished, mortarless, seven metres of it, not caring.

---

They turned into a narrower channel and the walls came in close enough to touch.

Mira put her hand out. Frik shot her a look — *don't* — and she didn't, she let it hover a finger's width off the basalt, feeling the cold come off it the way cold comes off a fridge door. The columns were stacked just the way Jakobus had read them three days ago at the canal's edge, before he'd handed the rest of it to the custodian and stepped back: a long face, then an end, then a long face, the joint of one course landing over the middle of the one below, so the whole wall locked itself together with nothing but its own weight and the patience of whoever set it.

She could see the building grammar. Anyone could, once it was pointed out. That was the cruel part — the *how it stands* was legible, plain, almost friendly. It was the *how it got up there* that opened under your feet.

The water carried them between two walls a canoe-length apart, and the title of the thing arrived in her body before it arrived in her head. The spaces between. They weren't moving past the city. They were moving *through* its absences — through the deliberate gaps the builders had left, the channels they'd kept open so the tide could come and go and the dead could be reached and the living could leave. The stone was load-bearing. So was the water. You could not have one without the other. Take the canals away and you didn't have a denser city; you had no city at all, just a quarry that had landed in the wrong ocean.

She watched the custodian pole. His face did almost nothing, and the almost-nothing was the whole conversation.

A lifetime of hearing rooms had given Mira this — the thing the world had handed her in place of the words it kept for itself. She read bodies because no one had ever let her stop. And what she read off the man in the stern was not the flat surface of *I have nothing to tell you.* It was the held tension of a man standing in a room he loves while strangers walk through it. The set of the shoulders that says *I am responsible for what happens here.* The way his eyes went, once, to a particular dark doorway in the wall to their left and then came deliberately back to the water, the way you make yourself not look at the thing you are protecting.

There was someone here. Not a ghost — she would not have given Frik the satisfaction. A *presence.* The reef was supposed to be uninhabitable, and was: no fresh water, no soil that grows food, a stone metropolis that could not keep a single soul alive without canoes bringing everything across the lagoon every single day for four hundred years. Nobody lived here. That was half the unanswerable — *why* drag three-quarters of a million tonnes to a place that could feed no one and water no one, and the honest answer the experts give is that they have not closed it.

And yet the place was not empty. Empty was a thing she knew. She had spent her whole life on the empty side of conversations. This was the opposite of that. This was a room full of people who had agreed to be quiet.

> *"It's very atmospheric," the fish allowed, from somewhere near Edith's lap. "I'd like the record to show I am not saying anything about anything. I'm a fish. I have no horse in the ghost race."*

Nobody laughed, which was how Mira knew the others felt it too.

---

Edith had the camera capped the whole way in, and Mira had been watching that cap like a tell.

In the half-second when the moon cleared the ridge and laid a long silver bar straight down the canal — straight down the *space between* — every instinct the woman had was to lift the thing and take it. Mira saw the hands tighten on the cap. Saw the breath go in. Saw the exact moment the war photographer in Edith woke up and reached, by thirty years of muscle, for the shot that would have explained the whole book in one frame, the moonlit canal and the seven-metre wall and the small dark figure poling them through.

And saw her not take it.

The breath went out. The hands stayed flat. Edith looked at the back of the custodian's head, and then she looked down at her own hands on the cap, and she left them there. Mira didn't know what it cost her. It wasn't the kind of thing you could read off a body — only that something had been paid.

The custodian, who had his back to both of them, said, without turning, "You can take it."

Edith's hands didn't move.

"It's all right," he said. "Many people photograph here. With permission." His voice was even, unhurried, a teacher's patience. "I am telling you it is permitted."

Edith looked at the moon-bar on the water. At the wall. At the doorway he had not let himself look at.

"I'll keep it," she said. Meaning the cap on. Meaning *no.*

He nodded once, still facing forward, and Mira felt the thing settle between them — a small thing, a courtesy returned that had not been asked for. The two of them had just had an entire exchange about consent in eleven words, and the only word that mattered was the one Edith hadn't taken when it was offered.

---

They came out into a wider basin, and the great wall of Nandauwas stood off to the right, the mortuary enclosure, the place where the rulers were laid. Five, six, seven metres of stacked basalt, the cornerstone in the dark somewhere at its foot weighing as much as a school bus, and not a grain of mortar anywhere in it.

Yusuf was the one who finally spoke, low, because Yusuf could not help reading the money of a thing even when the thing was four hundred years dead. "Somebody fed this," he said. "Every day. For centuries. The whole island, organised, just to keep stone moving to a place that grew nothing." He shook his head. "You don't build this for a king. You build this *as* a king. The building is the proof he can make the whole sea come when he calls."

The custodian's pole paused, mid-stroke. The smallest hitch. Mira caught it and said nothing.

He had not contradicted Yusuf. He had not agreed either. He had simply, for half a beat, stopped — the way you stop when a stranger gets *close,* close enough that the next thing you say either opens a door or shuts it, and you choose, and the choosing is yours.

He started poling again.

Mira had spent four days waiting for someone to say *we don't know* and mean it, and she had heard it from him on the first day, at the canal's edge, in exactly that flat true voice — *we don't know how they raised it* — and she had known then, before any of the others, that the flatness was not the flatness of ignorance. It was the flatness of a door held shut from the inside, by a hand, on purpose. She had said so to the crew, gently, that night. She had not said pick it.

Now, on the water, in the dark, surrounded by the spaces the builders had been careful to leave, she understood the second half of it, the half she could not have read from the shore.

The silence here was not a gap in the record. It was not a vacancy waiting for someone to come and fill it — not for the man with the spreadsheet, and certainly not for the other man, the one with the deniable funding and the development plans and the patient lawyers, who needed the island's *we don't know* to stay a we-don't-know forever because you cannot develop a grave that has keepers but you can develop an empty riddle.

The silence was full. It was kept. It was somebody's, by right — by the order of who may enter and who may speak and what may be told, an order older than the crew and older than the camera and older, God knew, than the resort.

She looked at the dark doorway the custodian had refused to look at, and she did not need to know what was behind it. That was the new thing, the thing she'd come four days and half an ocean to find out she could do. She did not need to know. She only needed to see that it was held, and by whom, and to leave it held.

> *The fish, very quietly, for once, did not say a word.*

The custodian poled them on through the spaces between, the water as load-bearing as the stone, carrying them past everything he had decided not to give. The moon laid its bar down the canal. Nobody lifted a camera.

"It is late," he said. "The tide turns soon. We should go back while the water still wants us."

He turned the skiff. And the dark closed over the doorway behind them, exactly as full as it had been before they came.
