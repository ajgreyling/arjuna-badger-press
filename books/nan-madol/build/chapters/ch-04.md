# Chapter 4 — The Door Held Shut

The tide came in the way it had been coming in for eight hundred years, without hurry, filling the canals between the islets a finger-width at a time, and the custodian waited for it the way you wait for a kettle — not watching, just present.

His name was Lehdesin. He had come down the path from the road on his own feet, in flip-flops and a faded shirt with a tuna company's logo on the pocket, and he had brought no one with him, which Mira logged and did not understand yet. He had a face that had spent a lot of years outdoors and a way of standing that put his weight on neither foot in particular, so that he could turn to the water or away from it with the same small effort. He stood at the lip of the canal where the coral fill gave way to the channel, and he looked at the wall of Nandauwas across the water, and he let them arrange themselves.

They were not good at being looked at. That was the thing about the crew, the load-bearing thing — they were the ones who looked, who had spent their whole lives reading rooms because no room had ever just handed them the words. Being on the receiving end of an unhurried gaze made Yusuf check the lashings on his bag twice and made Frik say something about the basalt that nobody answered.

Edith had the camera down. Not off — down, on its strap, against her sternum, the lens cap on, both hands visibly nowhere near it. She had asked Lehdesin before they crossed whether she could shoot, and he had said *some of it*, and she had not yet asked which, and Mira saw that she was not going to until he told her. That was new for Edith. That cost her something. Mira watched the cost cross her shoulders and settle.

"You can see the courses from here," Frik said, because he could not help it and because it was true. "Look — long face, then end-on, then long face. Header, stretcher. Same bind as a log cabin." He was right; Mira had seen Jakobus read exactly that wall the day before, his thumb tracing the alternation in the air, *how it stands, how it stands.* The hexagonal columns lay in the wall like cordwood, five courses, eight, the prisms nature had pre-cut in some old cooling lava field stacked dry, no mortar, the long basalt logs crossing each other to lock the wall against itself. It rose out of the canal to something like the height of a two-storey house and it had stood through every typhoon the Pacific had thrown at it.

"That's the easy part," Yusuf said. He said it gently. He had run the arithmetic out loud two nights ago and nobody had enjoyed it. Three-quarters of a million tonnes. Eighteen-fifty a year if you spread it flat across four centuries, which nobody did, which meant the real years were worse. A cornerstone that came in around fifty tonnes — one stone, the weight of a loaded articulated lorry, set onto a wall, on a reef, by hand. The quarry on the Sokehs side, twenty-five miles away across open water. No crane in the record. No ramp left behind. No documented method at all.

*The stacking's a craft,* Hecklefish observed from the gunwale of the skiff, where he had appointed himself supervisor. *The stacking I'll buy. It's the part where you float a lorry to the haunted Venice that I'd like minuted.*

Mira didn't look at the fish. She looked at Lehdesin, because Lehdesin had not looked at the wall the whole time Frik was reading it.

He was looking at the water.

---

Yusuf was the one who asked. He'd been chosen for it without anyone choosing, the way the crew did things — he had the warmest open face of any of them and he asked questions like he genuinely could not wait for the answer, which he could not. He crouched a little to bring himself level, which Mira clocked as the right instinct done a half-beat too obviously, and he said:

"Can I ask you something? The columns. The big ones. Twenty-five miles of open sea, and then up onto the wall." He nodded at Nandauwas, at the cornerstone they could not see from here, at the whole enormous fact of it. "How did they move them?"

The tide turned a small piece of weed in the canal. A skiff went by out past the breakwater islets, an outboard, a man and a boy, the boy's hand trailing in the wake.

"We don't know," Lehdesin said.

He said it kindly. He said it the way you'd tell a child the name of a star you didn't actually know the name of, no shame in it, no shutter coming down that you could hear. He even smiled a little.

"There are stories," he went on, and his voice did the thing a voice does when it is about to recite. "Some people say the brothers brought the stones by air. Olisihpa and Olosohpa. They sang the stones up out of the ground and they flew through the air to the reef and set themselves down. That is one story." He spread one hand at the wall, palm up, an offering, *take it or leave it.* "The scientists came. The university people, they found columns on the seabed out there." A small nod toward the lagoon. "They said: cargo. The canoes were carrying stone and some of it went over the side. Lost in the water." He shrugged, easy. "Maybe that. Rafts, maybe. Many men. We don't know how."

Frik leaned in, lit up. "But the seabed columns — if that's a wreck site, that's transport evidence, that's —"

"Lost cargo," Lehdesin agreed, before Frik could build the cathedral. "That is what they said. Stone that fell off the boats." He let it sit. He did not say whether he thought the boats were rafts or canoes or anything at all. He had handed them the official line, both halves of it, the science and the song, laid side by side on his open palm, and he had stepped back from both.

*Well, that was thorough,* the fish muttered. *He even brought the receipts. Why do I feel like I just got a tour of the gift shop?*

Yusuf nodded, and thanked him, and meant it, and turned to Frik to say something about the Oregon survey, and the conversation moved on the way water moves when it finds a lower place to go, and nobody noticed.

Mira noticed.

---

Here is what she had: a lifetime of hearing rooms. A lifetime spent two beats behind the words because the words came through air she couldn't fully use, so she'd learned to read the channel under the channel — the jaw, the hands, the place where a person's weight goes when they say a thing they mean versus a thing they've decided to say. She did not read minds. She read bodies. She read *that* a thing was held; she never once in her life had read *what.* The content was always somebody else's to give, and she had spent forty years on the wrong side of that, the one talked over, the one spoken for, the one handed the *we don't know* by every doctor and teacher and well-meaning stranger who'd ever decided her not-knowing was simpler than their explaining.

She knew the shape of a *we don't know* that meant *I don't know.*

This was not that shape.

When you don't know a thing, the not-knowing lives in your eyes — they go up and to the side, they go hunting, they cast about in the empty cupboard for the thing that isn't there. Yusuf's eyes did it constantly; it was half his charm. Frik's never stopped doing it. A true *we don't know* leans forward into the gap, because not-knowing is uncomfortable and a person tries to close it.

Lehdesin had leaned back.

His eyes had not gone hunting. They had gone to the water and stayed on the water, steady, the way you rest your eyes on something you already know the whole of. His weight had settled, both feet, the way Mira's father's weight used to settle when a salesman started talking, the way a man stands when he has already decided how much of himself the moment is getting and it is not all of it. His hands, when he spread them at the wall, had opened *toward* them — a gift — but the gift was the story, not the answer, and the difference between offering a story and offering a truth lives in a quarter-inch of the wrist, and Mira had watched the quarter-inch.

And the kindness. That was the tell that finished it. He had been kind. You are not kind to someone about a thing that hurts *you.* You are kind to them about a thing that is *theirs to be hurt by* and not yours. He had been kind to them the way you are kind to people standing outside a house in the rain — sorry you can't come in, here's the porch, here is some of the porch.

The not-knowing wasn't in him. The not-saying was. They were not the same animal at all, and only one of them lived in the eyes, and his eyes were out on the lagoon where the lost cargo lay, calm as a man who has known exactly where everything is his whole life.

It was a door. Shut. With a hand on the other side of it, holding it, and a face that had got very good at making the holding look like an open palm.

Mira's whole life had wanted to be on the inside of a door like that. Her whole life people had stood at a door and decided what she got of the room beyond it, and she had hated them for it with a clean cold hatred that had never once helped. The wanting came up in her now, fast and familiar, *what is it, what's behind it, I can read you, just stop holding it and let me —*

And she watched it come, and she watched what it was.

It was the salesman's hunger. It was every doctor's *it's simpler if I just.* It was the precise reflex of the person who decides they have a right to the room beyond someone else's door because they're so sure they'd be gentle in there.

She put it down.

She did not decide to put it down so much as recognise that she did not get to pick it up — that the door was his, and the holding was his, and that a man does not stand alone at the edge of his ancestors' grave-city and recite the song and the science both, carefully, kindly, to outsiders, by accident. He had built that little porch on purpose. He had built it many times. He had probably built it for the university people in 1988 and for every documentary crew since, and the building of it was itself the answer to a question they hadn't earned the right to ask: *who is this for, and who is it kept from?*

---

The others were ten feet down the canal edge now, Yusuf framing the long view for Edith, Edith with her hand finally moving toward the lens cap and then stopping, looking back at Lehdesin first, waiting. Lehdesin gave her a small nod — *that one, yes* — and she lifted the camera and shot the wall and not the man, and Mira saw him register that she'd understood which was which.

Mira came to stand beside him at the lip of the water. Close enough to be with him, not so close as to crowd the door. She didn't ask anything. She had run out of things she was allowed to ask, and the relief of it was enormous and strange.

The tide had come up another finger. The bottom course of the far wall was wet now, the basalt gone dark where the canal touched it.

"You already know," she said. Not a question. She kept it low and slow so he could see her mouth, and she kept her own face open, no triumph in it, nothing to win. "How they moved them. Whether the dragon's a way of saying something true. What this whole place is *for.*" She let her gaze go out to the same patch of lagoon his had been resting on. "You know all of it."

He didn't answer. His weight stayed on both feet.

"That's all right," she said. "I'm not asking."

And she felt — not saw, there was nothing to see, he gave away exactly nothing, which was its own kind of answer — she felt the air at the door change very slightly, the way it does when the person on the inside stops bracing because the knock has stopped coming.

*She's not going to make him say it,* the fish said, from very far off, and for once there was no heckle in it at all. *Huh.*

Lehdesin looked at her then, properly, the first time he had looked at any of them the way he'd been looking at the water. Whatever he read in her — and she had no doubt he was reading her, that he had been reading all of them since the path, that he out-read her the way the water out-waited her — he seemed to find what he was looking for.

"The tide's right in an hour," he said. "If you want to see the inside of Nandauwas." A pause. "Some of it."

Some of it. The porch, extended. But extended.

"We'd be honoured," Mira said, and meant the word, which she almost never did.

Behind them Yusuf was explaining columnar jointing to nobody, and Frik was arguing with Yusuf about it, and Edith was lowering the camera again because there was a thing happening at the water's edge that wanted not to be filmed, and the wall stood across the canal holding up three-quarters of a million tonnes of pre-cut stone and one entire unbroken silence, and not one of them — not the crew, not the experiments, not the divers, not a hundred years of asking — had laid a hand on it, because it was being held, deliberately, by a man in flip-flops who knew precisely how heavy it was.

The water came in another finger. Lehdesin watched it come.

So did Mira. And for once in her life, she let the thing she could not have stay where it was, on the far side of a door that wasn't hers, and found that the not-knowing, when you chose it instead of having it chosen for you, felt almost exactly like wonder.
