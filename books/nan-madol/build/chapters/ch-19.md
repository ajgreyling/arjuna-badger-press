# Chapter 19 — The Wink

The tide came in before the sun did.

Mira felt it through the hull of the skiff first — the canal filling, the boat lifting off the coral it had rested on all night, a slow rise that put the water level with the lowest course of the wall. She had her hand flat on the gunwale and read the lagoon by the tremor in the wood: the long pull of the open sea outside the reef, then the smaller, busier slap of water finding the gaps between the islets. Spaces between. The whole place was a machine for moving water, and at the turn of the tide it ran.

The custodian sat in the bow with his back to the dawn, so that for a long while he was only a shape, and the light behind him came up the colour of the inside of a shell. He had not spoken since they pushed off from the mangrove. He was waiting for something, and Mira had learned by now not to fill the waiting.

Yusuf could not help himself. He never could before coffee.

"You said," Yusuf began, and Mira watched his lips as much as she felt the boat carry the sound, "you said yesterday — when I asked who chose this reef — you said *they knew what they were doing.*"

The custodian's face turned a few degrees. The light caught the side of it.

"I did say that."

"So." Yusuf had his hands open, the way he held them when he was being careful and failing. "Then it wasn't random. Of all the reef flats on this coast — and we've flown over them, there are flatter ones, drier ones, ones nearer the springs — somebody picked *this* one. Threaded ninety-odd islets through it. That's a decision. That's a plan." He stopped. Started again, gentler. "What's it *for*?"

There it was. The question Jakobus had carried to the canal's edge three days ago and set down at this man's feet without trying to answer it, the way you'd lay down a tool that wasn't yours to use. *Not who, not is-it-real,* the old fixer had said, in that flat voice of his that came from being a long way from his own ground. *What is it for.* And then he had stepped back, and let the question belong to the man who lived here.

---

Mira would tell them the shape of the official story later, on the boat back, because somebody always asked her to, and because she liked to get it straight in her own hands.

The story was honest and it was hard. A medieval dynasty — the Saudeleur, the line that pulled a fractious island under one rule — had made this their seat for the dead and for the rites of power. The radiocarbon put the monumental building at around AD 1180, which was the thing the lost-continent crowd never wanted to hear: not a drowned antiquity older than memory, but *medieval*. Contemporary with Notre-Dame going up across the world. People who had grandparents and account-keepers and quarrels over fishing rights, who lifted the same impossible stone.

Ninety-two islets. A core a kilometre and a half by half a kilometre. Walls at the great mortuary enclosure, Nandauwas, that ran five and a half to seven and a half metres high — taller than a house — built of basalt columns nature had pre-cut into hexagonal logs and human hands had stacked dry, no mortar, header laid across stretcher, a long face then an end, course on course, the way a builder binds a log cabin so the wall holds itself together by its own weight and cleverness.

And the arithmetic. Mira ran it because somebody on the crew always had to, and the others were tired. Three-quarters of a million tonnes of stone. Spread across the four centuries of building, call it eighteen hundred and fifty tonnes a year, every year, generation on generation. A single cornerstone of around fifty tonnes — fifty — quarried somewhere on the far side of the island and brought *here*, across open water, and then lifted onto a wall, on a reef, by hand, with no crane and no ramp and nothing left behind to tell you how.

That was the part the official story said *we did this* and then went quiet on the *how.* Not because anyone was hiding it. Because nobody had found it. Experimental archaeology had gone out onto this very water with rafts and rope and good engineers and the best of intentions, and tried to do the easy version, the *obviously they just* version — and made, in the end, a potato. A short raft. A stone that wouldn't lift. A demonstration that demonstrated chiefly its own limits, while the real thing stood flawless and silent behind the cameras, seven metres of it, holding up four centuries of weather without a grain of mortar.

*And no one lives here.* That was the second hole, and it fed the first. No spring. No soil that grew food. A stone metropolis on a reef that could not keep a single soul alive without a canoe coming in on every tide with water and breadfruit and fish. So you had not only the *how* — how did they lift fifty tonnes onto a wall — but the *why here*: why drag the weight of a small mountain across the sea to a place no living person could stay.

Mira liked the holes. She had spent her life in the gaps where the words didn't reach, reading faces because the room never handed her the sound, and she had a fondness for a thing that stood there unexplained and refused to apologise for it.

---

The skiff slid into the main canal and the walls came up on both sides, dark and wet at the waterline, paling as the light climbed them. The custodian put his hand out — not a command, just a *here* — and Yusuf shipped the paddle and let them drift on the rising tide.

The custodian looked at the wall for a while. Then he answered.

"You stand on Nandauwas at the new moon," he said. "Where the high wall meets the low one — there, where the courses step. And you look out, between the islets, down the line of the canal." He lifted his chin at the channel ahead of them, the long straight run of black water with the sea-gate at the far end of it. "On certain nights the moon comes up the canal. Right up the middle of the water. Like it was poured down a trough to you."

He let that sit.

"The old people read the year off it. When to plant. When the *sakau* would be strong. When the reef fish would run thick at the passage and when they'd be thin. The walls are set so the moon and the stars walk down the gaps at the times that matter, and a man who knows the walls can stand here in the dark and tell you what month it is and what the sea will do." He patted the gunwale, once, the way you'd pat a working animal. "It is a calendar you can sail through. The water tells the tide; the gaps tell the moon. That is what it is for."

Mira watched his mouth and the muscles around his eyes the whole time, and her stomach did a small, careful thing, because the answer was *true* — she could feel the truth of it in the easy way it left him, no rehearsal, no glance to check how it landed — and it was also *not the whole of it.*

She had spent forty years learning the difference between a man who has run out of words and a man who has stopped on purpose. The first kind keeps reaching, the hands hunting for the thing they can't find, the eyes going up and to the side. The second kind goes *still.* And this man, when he finished — *that is what it is for* — went still in exactly the way her grandmother used to go still at the end of a sentence she had decided was the end, whatever anyone else thought.

He had given them a function. A real one. A grounded, physical, checkable thing: a lunar almanac you could stand inside, the moon poured down a canal at the new month, the walls a fixed instrument for reading the year off the sky and the tide. It re-opened everything — because now the fifty-tonne cornerstone wasn't just heavy, it was *aimed*; now the foodless reef wasn't a riddle but a *site chosen for what you could see from it*; now the spaces between the stone were not gaps but apertures, sights cut into the dark.

It was a beautiful answer. And it was a door, set politely ajar, with a hand on the other side of it.

"That's — " Yusuf was lit up. "That's the *why here.* That's it, isn't it? You don't build a city where people can live. You build an instrument where the sightlines are clean. The reef, the channel, the gate — it's *aligned.*" He turned to Mira, wanting the math, wanting her to confirm the moon, wanting to *solve* it.

Mira didn't give him the math.

She was looking at the custodian, who had gone on watching the canal as if Yusuf hadn't spoken, and she saw the thing she'd been hired by her own wiring to see since she was a child: the small set of the jaw that means *and there is more, and the more is mine.* He had told them what the walls *did.* He had not told them what was *kept* in the doing — who lay in the enclosure and why the moon was let in to find them, what was said at the new month and by whom, the part that was not a function but a covenant, the part the *Nahnmwarki* order held the way you hold a thing entrusted, not the way you hold a thing you've simply mislaid.

The official "we don't know" the whole island said to every outsider who ever came asking — Mira had understood, days ago, that it was two answers wearing one face. *We have not reconstructed the lift* was one of them, and that one was honest all the way down. *And there are things we do not say to you* was the other, and that one was not ignorance. It was a door held shut, by right, by people whose dead were on the other side of it.

He had just shown them, with great generosity, the part of the room he was willing to light.

---

Far off, on the mainland causeway, an outboard coughed and caught — the early boat from the resort survey, the clean-handed outfit that kept coming with its drone cases and its environmental-impact language and its careful, smiling people who would have *loved* this answer. Who would have written *lunar observatory* into a brochure by lunch. Who needed, more than anything, for the meaning of this place to stay a vacancy, an empty riddle free for the taking, because you can develop an empty riddle and you cannot develop a grave with keepers standing at the gate. The function the custodian had just handed them was, among everything else it was, a fence. *This is what it is for* meant *it is for something,* and a thing that is for something is not nobody's.

The sun cleared the wall. Light ran down the canal toward them, and for a moment it came straight up the middle of the channel, poured down the trough of black water exactly as he'd said the moon did, and Mira understood — felt, didn't reason — why a person might cut sightlines into the sea and carry a mountain across the water to set them true.

Edith hadn't raised the camera. Mira noticed that. The Lens sat with the body in her lap and her hand resting on it, watching the light come down the water with her own two eyes, taking nothing.

Yusuf was still leaning forward, the next question loading. *But the alignment — can we measure it — which stars — who's in the enclosure — * Mira could see all of them queuing behind his teeth.

She put two fingers on his forearm.

He looked at her. She moved her head, once, a small *no* — not *he's wrong,* not *there's nothing there,* but *that's as far as the road goes, and the rest of it isn't ours.*

Hecklefish, who had been pretending to be asleep against the bait cooler, opened one eye. *Oh, NOW she stops asking questions. The one time the man's actually talking. Forty years of reading lips and you pick THIS sentence to leave on the table.* He sniffed. *I'd have asked about the cornerstone. Just sayin'.*

Nobody answered the fish. Nobody ever did.

The custodian turned from the wall at last, and looked at Mira — only at Mira — and something moved across his face that was almost a smile, the particular recognition of one person who has read a great many silent rooms meeting another, the unheard meeting the unheard. He had watched her watch him. He had seen her see the door. And he had seen her hand close over Yusuf's arm, and stop the lock from being picked.

"You know," he said to her, "that I have not told you everything."

It was not a question, so she did not answer it. She held his eye and let her face do the only honest thing it could, which was to agree, and to be at peace with it.

He nodded, slow, satisfied — the nod of a man handing back a thing intact.

"Good," he said. "Then we understand each other."

The tide turned under the boat. The light came off the top of the moon-trough canal and the water went ordinary and grey-green and busy, and the custodian picked up the paddle and put them about for the mangrove, and did not say one word more about what the walls were for than the part he had chosen to give.

Behind them, seven metres of dry-stacked basalt held up four centuries of sky and kept the rest.
