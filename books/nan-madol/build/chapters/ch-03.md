# Chapter 3 — The Man Who Reads the Wall

The tide came in the way Pohnpei does everything, without hurry and without apology, and the skiff sat down on the reef flat as gently as a hand laid on a table. Frik climbed out into shin-deep water that was warmer than the air and stood there a second, holding the gunwale, feeling the coral fill grind under his soles — not sand, not stone, a rubble of broken reef packed down by people who'd been dead four hundred years.

He looked up at the wall and his chest did the thing it does.

He was an engineer. He had been an engineer since he was eight years old and took the back off his mother's radio to see why it talked, and the feeling that came over him at Nandauwas was the same one — the *who did this, and how, and may I please understand it* — multiplied by a number he didn't have a name for. The wall went up out of the canal like a thing that had decided to. Five and a half metres at the low end. Past seven where it cornered. Black basalt logs, hexagonal in cross-section, the long ones laid flat and the short ones laid end-on into the face, course on course, so the whole mass bound itself together by its own geometry. No mortar. None. He'd read that and not believed it and now he was looking at the gap between two columns where you couldn't have slid a coin.

"Okay," Frik said, to nobody. "Okay. That's not fair."

*"Took you long enough,"* said the fish, from the dry-bag clipped at his hip, in the voice he'd long ago stopped questioning. *"Wall's been standing eight centuries. You've been standing in it ninety seconds."*

"Give me a minute."

*"It's not going anywhere. That's sort of the whole problem with it."*

Mira had come ashore further along, picking her way up the islet's edge with that economy she had, the watching economy, reading the footing the way she read everything. Edith was already low and sideways behind the camera, finding the angle where the wall ate the sky. And then there was Jakobus, last out of the boat, the way he was always last, off his own ground and a long way from the Beast — which sat shipped and idle and entirely useless on a continent where there were roads, because there were no roads here, only water and the spaces between.

He didn't look up at the wall first. Frik noticed that. Everyone looked up at the wall first; it pulled your eye like a held breath. Jakobus looked at the *base*. At the coral, at the waterline, at the place where the bottom course of columns sat into the fill. He crouched there with his forearms on his knees and he read it the way Frik had once watched a structural man read a forty-year-old bridge: not impressed, not unimpressed, *attending*.

Frik went and crouched next to him because that was where he wanted to be.

"It's a raft," Jakobus said, after a while. Quiet. Not to the crew. More like a man saying a thing aloud to check whether it stayed true once it left his mouth. "The whole islet. Coral rubble piled on the reef to make ground that isn't ground. Then the wall sits on the raft." He put two fingers flat against the lowest column, the wet one, the one the canal had been licking since the Saudeleur were kings. "Soft thing carrying the hard thing. Usually it's the other way round."

"It hasn't subsided," Frik said. He couldn't keep the wonder out of it and didn't try. "Seven metres of stacked stone, no foundation but coral fill, on a *reef*, in the wettest place on earth, and it's *plumb*. That should have slumped. That should have walked itself into the canal three hundred years ago."

"Why hasn't it?"

"Because they built it as a—" Frik stopped, because he was about to caption the thing and Jakobus hadn't asked him to. "I don't know yet."

"Good," said Jakobus, and there was no sting in it. He stood, knees cracking, a man who'd crouched on a great many made things in a great many places that were not his home. "Now look at how it stands."

---

Here is the official story, and it is a good one, and it is true, and the people who reconstructed it did so with patience and trowels and radiocarbon and decades, and they deserve the love they don't usually get.

Nan Madol is a city for the dead. Ninety-odd artificial islets laid on the reef flat off the southeast coast of Pohnpei, threaded with tidal canals so the place reads from above like a drowned chessboard — the Venice of the Pacific, the brochures say, and for once the brochures undersell it. The name means *the spaces between*. The water between the islets is not a moat or an accident; it is the architecture. You move through this city by canoe, on the tide, the way you move through Venice by boat, except no one ever lived here. There is no fresh water on these islets. Nothing edible grows. Every cup, every fish, every breath of cooking smoke had to be paddled in across the lagoon. It was a capital with no citizens — the seat of the Saudeleur, the dynasty that bound Pohnpei under one rule, and above all a place to lay their dead, in the great enclosure called Nandauwas, behind walls a grown man cannot see over.

The stone is the joke that nature told first. It's columnar basalt — the same physics that built the Giant's Causeway, the same that stands Devils Tower up out of Wyoming. Thick lava cools, contracts, and cracks itself into long prismatic columns, hexagonal, regular, *log-shaped*. Nature pre-cut the blocks. You do not have to quarry a square edge out of these; the planet hands them to you ready to stack. That's the part the lost-continent crowd never sits with, because it deflates the magic in exactly the wrong direction: the miracle is not that the stones are strangely shaped. The miracle is what was *done* with them after nature was done being clever.

And the megalithic city is medieval. Radiocarbon puts the monumental phase at around AD 1180 — not antediluvian, not pre-flood, not a relic of sky-people, but contemporary with the building of the great cathedrals, raised by a known dynasty that fell to a man named Isokelekel around the early seventeenth century. It is hard, cited, astonishing, ordinary-human archaeology.

And yet.

---

Jakobus walked the face of the wall slow, with one hand trailing along it, and Frik walked with him, and the fish kept its peace for once, which it only did when it was watching too.

"Header," Jakobus said, and tapped a column laid end-on, its hexagon staring out of the wall like a stopped clock. He moved his hand a course up and along. "Stretcher." A long column, laid flat, running the length of the face. "Header. Stretcher." His hand kept moving and the pattern kept being there, course alternating against course, the long ones running one way and the short ones biting back into the depth of the wall behind them, knitting the outer face to the rubble core. "It's a log cabin. Exactly a log cabin. You lay your logs long, then you turn a course and lay them across so they grip the ones below. A child builds a fence this way with sticks. The grammar's the same whether the stick weighs a kilo or—"

"Five tonnes," Frik said. "These run five to twenty-five tonnes. The columns. Each."

Jakobus stopped walking.

He stood and looked at the wall the way you look at a sentence in a language you half-know, finding the verb. "So the binding's free," he said. "The geometry does the binding. You don't need mortar because every stone you lay across pins the two stones under it, and the weight does the rest. That's why it's plumb after eight hundred years. It isn't holding *together*. It's holding *down*. Every course presses the one below into the coral, and the coral takes it, and the canal takes the coral, and the reef takes the canal." He moved his flat hand down through the air, course by invisible course, all the way to the water. "It's a stack of weight finding the sea floor. That's how it stands."

Frik felt the back of his neck prickle. It was right. It was *obviously* right, and he hadn't had it, and a man who couldn't have told you a megapascal from a mango had read it off the stone in four minutes flat because he wasn't asking the engineer's question. He was asking the older one. *What is the made thing, and what holds it up.*

"You're good at this," Frik said, and meant it the whole way down.

"I look at things that don't fall down for a living," Jakobus said. "There's a grammar. You learn the grammar." He glanced at Frik, dry. "You learn it faster than I did. You just learned it pointing the wrong end."

*"He's not wrong,"* the fish offered. *"You did walk up and start admiring the part nature already did."*

"I'm allowed to admire the basalt."

*"Admire away. The basalt did nothing. The basalt cooled. Big day for the basalt."*

---

Here is the part where the easy story sits down in the mud and won't get up.

Say you accept it all — the dynasty, the date, the log-cabin grammar, nature's pre-cut blocks, the coral raft taking the weight down to the reef. Lovely. Now build it.

The basalt isn't here. The quarries are on the far side of the island, the Sokehs side, something like twenty-five miles off — across open water, on the tide. So before you lay a single header you have to get a five-tonne column, or a fifteen-tonne column, or in one particular case a cornerstone weighing somewhere around fifty tonnes, off a hillside, down to a shore, onto something that floats, *across the sea*, and up onto a reef. By hand. With rope and timber and people and the tide. No crane. No iron. No wheel that would do you any good on a reef anyway. And then you have to lift it — not roll it, *lift* it, course on course, up a wall that ends seven and a half metres in the air — and you have to do it about seven hundred and fifty thousand tonnes' worth of times, which works out, if you spread it flat across the four centuries the place was rising, to something on the order of eighteen hundred tonnes a year, every year, for longer than the United States has existed, onto islets that grew no food to feed the haulers and held no water to give them to drink.

And here is the cold-eyed part, the part the line will not let anyone shout past: people with degrees and budgets and good intentions have tried to reproduce it. Experimental archaeology. Smart, careful, modern, *get-the-column-onto-the-wall* tried. And it has not, to date, been done. Not "physically impossible." Not "therefore aliens" — set that down, it's not yours and it never was. Just: the brightest people, in daylight, with a plan, tried the easy version, and the easy version did not come.

Frik stood at the foot of the fifty-tonne corner and looked up the height of the wall and felt the number land in his body the way it's supposed to. He was an engineer. He could spec the lift. He could draw you the sheerlegs and the ramps and the gangs and the rollers, he could put it all on paper and the paper would be beautiful and the paper had been tried and the paper made a potato while the real thing stood flawless and silent at his shoulder, wet at the bottom, plumb to the top, not telling.

"How," he said. Just the one word.

Jakobus didn't answer it, because it wasn't his question and he knew the difference between his and not-his better than any man Frik had ever met.

---

They stood at the canal's edge, the four of them, where the islet ran out into the water and the water ran on into the city, between the stone, into the spaces between.

Frik waited for Jakobus to do the thing. To turn to the crew and say *now then*, to hand them the puzzle the way a man hands his apprentices the next bolt. He'd seen it coming the whole walk up the wall, the relay, the moment where the road steps back and lets the cleverer people take the lead. That was the shape Frik expected because that was the shape stories like this always had: the local mystery, the visiting minds, the crack and the solve.

And Jakobus read the wall one more time — the whole of it, the height and the haul and the held weight — and then he turned, and he did not turn to Frik.

He turned to the water. To the far side of the canal, where the custodian had come down quietly while they were lost in the stone and now stood with the patience of a man who had watched a great many outsiders fall in love with a wall, on a bit of ground that was theirs.

"I can read what it is," Jakobus said, across the spaces between, in the flat plain voice he kept for the truest things. "I can read how it stands. Header and stretcher, weight to the reef, the geometry does the binding." He let that sit on the water a second. "What I can't read off the stone is what it's *for*."

He didn't ask it. He set it down at the edge of the canal like an offering and he stepped back from it, and Mira — who had been reading the custodian's face the whole time, the way she read every face, watching for the truth the words might not carry — went very still, because she had already seen, three beats before the rest of them, that the answer was there behind the custodian's eyes, kept, whole, and that it was his to give across the water, or his to keep.

The fish, for once, said nothing at all.

The tide turned, and began, without hurry, to come back in.
