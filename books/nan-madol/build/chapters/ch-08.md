# Chapter 8 — No Water, No Food

By eleven the heat had a weight to it, and Mira had stopped sweating, which she knew was the wrong direction.

She felt it before she could have named it — the lightness behind the eyes, the way the basalt wall in front of her had begun to breathe very slightly at its edges. She put a hand flat on the stone. It was warm as a living thing. She held it there and waited for the world to settle, and watched the others, because watching was the channel she had.

Yusuf was on the islet's seaward wall, a phone held up at the columns, narrating something to nobody. Frik squatted by the canal with the cooler. Edith stood very still near the gap in the enclosure where the morning's tide had run out, the camera down at her hip, not raised, which Mira had learned to read as a kind of breathing.

They had packed for a day. That was the thing.

They had packed the way you pack for a long hot day on any island — water, a bag of rolls gone soft, oranges, the cooler with ice already turning to soup. They had carried it across on the skiff at the slack of the tide and set it on the coral fill of Nandauwas like people who would be fine. And it was, Mira saw now, very nearly the whole joke of the place, sitting in a blue plastic box on a reef.

Pwilin — the caretaker — sat in the shade the great wall threw, where the heat was a degree kinder, and watched them be people who had packed for a day. He had a way of sitting that used no effort at all. His machete lay across his knees. He had not touched the cooler.

> *"Lovely picnic spot. Seven-metre walls. No bar. One star."*

---

Frik came up the fill with two bottles, handed one to Mira without asking, which he had learned to do. She drank. The water was hot enough to be unpleasant.

"That's the last of the cold," he said, low, mouth angled so she could take it off his lips. "And about half the warm."

She nodded. She did the sum anyway, because the sum was the point. Four of them. Midday. The tide that had carried them in would not carry them out for hours yet, and going against it in the skiff over the reef flat was the kind of thing you did once and told stories about. She looked at the cooler — a day's water for a picnic, dwindling — and then she looked up, and let her eye go out across the islets, ninety-odd of them, the canals between drained silver in the low water, the whole stone city of the dead laid out in the sun.

And there was nothing on any of it to drink. Not a spring. Not a stream. Not a coconut palm doing its patient work over a freshwater lens, because the islets were laid stone on coral rubble and nothing grew on them you'd want to eat. The interior of Pohnpei, eight miles back, was one of the wettest places on the earth — cloud forest, waterfalls, rain you could lean on. Out here, on the reef, in the spaces between, there was the sea, and the sun, and the basalt holding the heat like a brick oven.

She felt the sum land in her body before she finished it in her head, which was the way it always went. Her tongue against the roof of her mouth. The dull pulse starting behind the right eye.

She caught Edith looking at her. Edith always knew first when one of them was paying for the room. The camera came up a fraction, then went back down. Not this. Mira gave her the smallest shake of the head — *I'm fine* — which was a lie they both let stand.

---

The official story was beautiful and it was true and you could hold it whole in your two hands, which made the next part worse.

This was the Saudeleur capital. The seat of the dynasty that bound Pohnpei into one rule, here, on these islets, from somewhere around 1180 by the radiocarbon — medieval, not mythic, a real line of real lords. The high ones lived here, in season, with their priests and their attendants and the people who served them. The dead lords were laid in Nandauwas, this enclosure, behind the wall Mira's hand was on, a wall that ran five and a half, seven and a half metres high in dry mortarless basalt, header and stretcher, log on log, the way Jakobus had read it at the canal's edge two days back and then handed the next question on.

So: a court. A capital. People — hundreds of them, the priests, the chiefs, the servants, the families that fed and dressed and guarded them. People who needed, every single day they were here, to *drink.*

And the islets gave them nothing.

Mira had read it in the dossier on the plane and it had been a line — *no fresh water, no food on site, uninhabitable without constant canoe resupply.* A clean little fact, the kind you nod at. Sitting on the warm coral fill of it with her own water down to half a warm bottle, the line stopped being a fact and became a logistics problem with a body's clock on it.

Every drop. Every mouthful of taro, every fish, every cup of fresh water for every soul on these stones — paddled out across the lagoon by canoe, on the tide, and paddled out again, and again, for as long as anyone lived here. Not once. Not for a festival. For generations. Four centuries of monumental building and the entire time it stood, the city could not keep a single person alive for a week on its own.

"Run it forward," Yusuf said. He'd come down off the seaward wall and was crouched by the cooler now, frowning at it like it had insulted him. He did this — turned dread into arithmetic, because arithmetic he could carry. "Say a few hundred people in residence, peak. A litre and a half a day just to not die, more in this. Call it five hundred litres a day, low. Of *water.* Carried. Then the food on top. Then the building crews on top of *that*, for four hundred years."

"And the dead don't eat," Frik said.

"The dead are the only ones who don't," Yusuf said. "Everyone alive out here is a delivery."

---

> *"So they built the fanciest address in the Pacific in the one spot you can't get takeout. Bold. Real bold."*

---

Mira watched Pwilin while Yusuf talked, because that was where the truth would be if it was anywhere, and it was not in the cooler.

He was listening. Not the polite empty listening of a man waiting for tourists to finish. He had heard all of it before — the sum, the appalled little laugh that came after the sum, the visitor doing the visitor's discovery out loud. He had heard it from every team that ever rafted out here with a blue cooler and worked out by noon what he had known since he was a boy: that this was not a place you lived. He let Yusuf get to the end of it.

Then he said, mild, to the wall as much as to them: "You brought enough water for one day."

"We did," Yusuf said.

"They brought it every day," Pwilin said. "For four hundred years."

He let that sit. He was not amazed by it. That was what Mira's eye snagged on — the absence of amazement. To the crew it was a marvel and a horror, the sheer mad cost of it. To him it was simply *what was done*, the way you'd say a thing your grandmother had told you in a voice that had stopped questioning it a long time ago. The wonder was theirs. The fact was his.

He shifted the machete and looked at Mira directly then, which men did not usually do once they'd worked out she couldn't hear them well — they tended to drift their faces away, toward someone who'd answer faster. He kept his face square to her. He spoke a little slower, not louder, which was the thing almost nobody got right, and she felt the small specific gratitude of it land in her chest.

"It is heavy," he said, "to carry water to the dead."

She read every part of it off him — the mouth, the set of the jaw, the way the word *dead* did not move his eyes the way it moved a stranger's. And under it, plain to her as a tide line, the thing he was *not* saying. Not hiding, exactly. Holding. There was a reason it was worth it, the carrying, the four centuries of it, and the reason sat behind his face the way the lords sat behind the wall, and he had no intention of bringing it out into the sun for them.

She did not reach for it.

She had spent her whole life in rooms reading what the mouths wouldn't carry, and the hardest thing she had ever learned was the difference between a wall that wants you to climb it and a wall that is doing its job. This was the second kind. She let it be the second kind.

"Yes," she said. Her own voice, the one she didn't fully hear, that she'd built by feel. "It is."

Pwilin looked at her a moment longer. Something in him registered that she'd taken the actual sentence and not the smaller one underneath it. Whatever he saw, he seemed content with it. He nodded once and went back to watching the canal.

---

The headache had become a fact of its own by then, a hot bar across the brow. Mira sat down on the fill in the wall's thin shade, beside the caretaker but a respectful arm's length off, and put the warm bottle to her mouth and made herself drink the rest of it slowly. The salt was already drying white on her arms. Her body was keeping its own honest ledger, the way bodies did, and the ledger said: *this place is trying, very gently, to make you a delivery problem too.*

That was the door the whole afternoon kept opening onto, and she made herself stand in the draught of it instead of stepping through.

Because the easy answer was right there, and it was wrong. The easy answer said: *defensive. They built it offshore to be safe, that's why it's miserable to live on.* But you don't haul three-quarters of a million tonnes of basalt twenty-five miles across open water to a place with no water for *defence.* A stockade is cheaper. A hilltop is cheaper. Anything is cheaper than this. The cost was the answer's enemy. Whatever this was for, it was worth more than the appalling daily price of keeping its people drinking — worth more for four centuries running.

Edith had drifted close. She crouched by Mira, the camera finally up, but pointed away, out across the drained canals where the heat shimmer made the far islets swim.

"You okay," Edith said — flat, not a question, the way she said it when she meant it.

"I stopped sweating," Mira said.

Edith went still. Then she reached into her own bag, not the cooler, her own bag, and put a sachet of electrolyte salts in Mira's hand and a half-litre she'd been hoarding, and she did it without a word and without looking at the others, the way she did everything that actually mattered, off-frame.

Mira tore the sachet with her teeth and watched the powder cloud the water.

"Drink it," Edith said. "All of it. Then we sit in the shade and we don't be clever for an hour."

---

> *"There it is. The whole island's a kava bar with no kava and the dead don't tip. Why HERE, you absolute legends. WHY HERE."*

---

She drank it. The hot bar across her brow loosened by degrees. Across the fill, Yusuf had given up on the cooler and was lying flat on the coral with his hat over his face, conserving, a man who had run the numbers and accepted them. Frik was rationing what was left into a single bottle, marking it, doing the small careful husbandry of someone who'd been hungry before and never forgot the arithmetic of it. The tide was a long way out yet. They were not in danger. They were only, for one afternoon, *living inside the question* — what it cost, every day, to keep the living alive out here, so the dead could lie behind this wall.

And the question stood up off the stone and would not lie back down.

You could explain the lift, maybe, given enough rope and ramps and people and time — Jakobus thought you could, in principle, even if no one had ever managed to prove it on the reef. You could explain the basalt; nature pre-cut it into logs. You could explain the canals and the courts and the dynasty and the radiocarbon. You could explain everything except the one thing the body kept asking from the inside of a dry mouth at noon.

Not *how.*

*Why here.* Why this reef, this foodless waterless flat where no living soul could stay a week — why carry the water and the taro and the fish and the whole machinery of being alive across the lagoon, in canoes, on the tide, every day, for four hundred years, to build the grandest thing your people ever made in the one place your people could not live.

Pwilin sat in the shade with his machete across his knees and watched the canal that would, in its own time, fill again and carry them home. He knew. Mira could see that he knew, the same way she could see the heat coming off the wall. It was behind his face, square and unhurried, and it was his.

She lay back on the warm coral beside her crew and looked up at seven metres of stacked black stone against a white sky, and let herself not know.
