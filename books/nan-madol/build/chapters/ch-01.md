# Chapter 1 — The Potato

The raft was not going to work, and everyone on it knew except the camera.

Yusuf had built rafts before — Sudan, a flooded road, jerrycans and a door — and he could feel the wrongness through his heels the way you feel a tooth before it aches. Too much above the waterline. The basalt column lay lashed athwart eight oil drums and a frame of green mangrove poles, and it sat there like a dropped sentence, going nowhere, while the tide that was supposed to lift it kept its own counsel out on the reef flat.

The column itself was a beautiful thing. Six-sided, near enough — nature's own carpentry, a lava log cooled and cracked into a prism a long time before anyone thought to want it. Three metres of it. Maybe four tonnes. A modest one, chosen on purpose; you did not start a demonstration with the fifty-tonne monster.

"Sea's coming," said Dione.

Dione Aldis was the engineer, and the engineer did not believe in any of this, which Yusuf liked about her immediately. She was Pohnpeian on her mother's side and a graduate of somewhere damp and serious on her father's, and she had agreed to run the lift the way you agree to drive a relative to the airport: out of duty, and faintly insulted that the duty existed. She wore a high-vis vest over a church shirt and she kept checking her phone, which had no signal out here, which is a thing people do when they would rather not be looking at you.

"Tide's at three-forty," she said. "We've got it for about an hour either side of high. After that the channel's too shallow and the flat's too deep, and you've got a four-tonne paperweight in the wrong place forever."

"Forever's a long time," said Frik, cheerfully, from the bow, where he had appointed himself ballast.

"Look around you," said Dione.

They looked around. Behind them — and this was the whole trouble with the demonstration, the thing nobody had quite costed — stood Nan Madol.

---

You have to come at it by water, on the tide, the way the order requires, and the first sight of it does something physical to the back of the neck. Walls of stacked black columns rise straight up out of the sea, mortarless, the long faces and the cut ends alternating course on course like the corner of a log cabin built by something the size of weather. Ninety-two islets, give or take, laced together by canals — the spaces between, which is what the name means, the gaps and the water and what runs in them. Nandauwas, the great royal mortuary, lifts its seaward wall five, six, seven metres clean, and on the corner of it sits a single stone that the books say weighs around fifty tonnes, and it has sat there, dry-laid, holding its line, since a few centuries before anyone alive was born.

The whole thing is medieval. That is the part that ambushes people. Radiocarbon puts the monumental building from about AD 1180 — younger than the cathedral at Chartres was getting started, give or take. This was the capital of the Saudeleur, the dynasty that pulled Pohnpei into one fist and held it until a man called Isokelekel took it back, around the early seventeenth century. A real dynasty. A real fall. Names that families on this island still carry the weight of.

A ceremonial city for the dead, built on a reef flat that grows no food and holds no fresh water, where no living soul could last a week without a canoe coming out to feed them.

Three-quarters of a million tonnes of basalt, hauled there.

Edith was up on the islet wall with the long lens, not pointed at the raft. She'd asked first — she always asked first now, that was the new Edith, the one who left a beat of silence before the shutter to see if anyone wanted to say no — and the elder who'd accompanied them, a slight man named Salihken who said almost nothing and missed nothing, had inclined his head a fraction toward the masonry and away from the graves, which was permission and instruction in one motion. So she shot the wall. The header-and-stretcher of it. The way the stone bound the stone with nothing but its own honest weight.

Mira stood at the canal edge, not on the raft, watching Dione's face.

---

*Hi, friends. Hecklefish here. The official story, straight, because we play it straight: medieval islanders, brilliant builders, stacked a mortuary city on a reef. True. Revered. Award the gold star. Now somebody explain to me why the engineer's already left the building behind her eyes, and the tide hasn't even — oh. Oh, here we go.*

---

"Drums," said Dione.

The tide came in the way it does on a reef flat, not as a wave but as a decision: a sudden general rising, the brown water going green going clear, the mangrove poles creaking, the drums knocking and lifting. The raft floated. There was a small involuntary cheer from somewhere — Frik — and Dione did not join it, because Dione was watching the freeboard, and the freeboard was nothing.

"Stop loading the front," she said. "Yusuf. Off."

He went off, into water to his chest, warm as a bath and full of small fish investigating his legs. The raft rose a hand's width. With the column on it, four tonnes of dead prism lashed across the beam, it answered the smallest swell like a tray carried by someone arguing. It wanted to roll. It would not stop wanting to roll.

"This is the textbook method," Yusuf said. Not arguing. Reading aloud. "Float it at high water, walk it down the channel, lever it up the islet face on rollers and ramps. They did it ninety times. A hundred. Over four centuries that's — what —"

"Eighteen hundred tonnes a year," said Mira, without looking up. She'd done the arithmetic the night before, flat, the way you'd read a tide table. Seven hundred and fifty thousand tonnes, four hundred years. A wall's worth a month, more or less, forever, for longer than the United States has existed. "Sustained."

"Sustained," Yusuf agreed.

"For four centuries," said Dione, "by people who left no rollers, no ramps, no sledges, no quarry road, no broken drag-tackle, nothing. Pull." This last to the men on the canal bank with the line. "Gently. *Gently.* It's not a tug-of-war, it's a baby."

They pulled the raft into the canal mouth. For a length, two lengths, it was almost grand — the black column riding the green water between the black walls, the past and the demonstration of the past sliding along together, and Edith came down off the wall to get it low and level, the lens just above the waterline, because that was the shot, that had always been the shot: *here is how easy it was.*

The raft touched the islet face.

---

The islet face is where the textbook quietly stops being a textbook.

Because now you have a four-tonne stone floating at your chest and a wall going straight up in front of you, and you have to get the stone from the one to the other and up, and there is no crane, there has never been a crane, the whole point of the exercise is that there was never a crane. Dione had brought a sheerlegs — three lengths of steel scaffold pole lashed into a tripod, a chain hoist hung from the apex, the most generous possible interpretation of *levers and human ingenuity.* It was, she'd said when they unloaded it, more help than the builders ever had. She'd said it the way you confess to cheating at solitaire.

They set the sheerlegs on the lowest course. Coral rubble underfoot, basalt at the shins, the tide at its absolute top now and beginning, you could feel it, to think about leaving. The chain hoist took up. The column came off the raft, swung free, four tonnes turning slowly on a chain over green water, and for one held breath it was going to work, the genre was going to win, the demonstration was going to demonstrate.

The lowest course shifted.

Not much. A column the long way is a header, its cut end out; the next course up lies the other way, a stretcher, long face out; that alternation is the whole grammar of the wall, that is what makes it stand without mortar, and it stands because every stone in it was placed dead and never asked to take a sideways load. The sheerlegs' foot put a sideways load on it. The header rolled a finger's width in its bed and the stretcher above it dropped its end with a sound like a door closing in another house, and Dione had the hoist's brake off and the column down onto the raft before Yusuf had finished saying her name.

The column landed badly. The raft took it badly. One drum folded its lashing and went, and the prism slid, and the green water came over the low edge in a sheet, and then the column was not on the raft.

It went in clean. No drama to it at all. A four-tonne stone enters the sea the way a coin enters a fountain — a chunk, a swallow, a column of bubbles, gone. Three metres of water on the flat now and the basalt sitting on the bottom of the canal it had been meant to climb, as level and as patient as it had been on the truck.

The tide began to leave.

---

*And there it is. The Potato.*

*Best kit. Best plan. A real engineer, a chain hoist the medievals would've wept for, a column they picked because it was* easy *— and one wall behind them, just the one, ten metres of it, that has stood flawless and silent through eighteen generations of weather and a couple of wars and now, apparently, through us. The brightest people in daylight made a potato. The thing on the reef did not.*

*Funny how nobody's laughing.*

---

Nobody was laughing. Frik was wet to the eyebrows and grinning the wide useless grin of a man whose adrenaline has nowhere to go, but his eyes weren't in it. Edith had not raised the camera. The column lay in the canal and would have to be got out before the lagoon edge dropped enough to strand it, and Dione was already roping the recovery, fast and unsurprised, like a woman who'd known the ending of this film before the credits.

That was the thing Yusuf clocked, standing in the falling water with the small fish gone and the mud coming back.

Dione had known.

She'd brought the sheerlegs anyway. She'd set the lift on the lowest course anyway, the worst possible course, the one any builder would tell you not to touch. She'd let the textbook fail in front of the lens cleanly, professionally, on the record.

"Who's paying for this," Yusuf said. Quietly. He'd come up beside her on the wall while the others worked the line.

Dione didn't stop coiling rope. "You are. Your production company. The grant."

"No. Who's paying *you.*" He'd seen the invoice that morning by accident, the header on it, a development consultancy out of somewhere with a glass lobby, *feasibility,* and her name in the cc line on an email she'd minimised too slow. "You don't believe the textbook can do it. Fine. Neither do I, after that. But you didn't run it to find out. You ran it to *show.* You ran it to put a film in the world that says *look how impossible this is, look how nobody can manage this place, look how it just sits here being a riddle nobody can answer.*"

The rope stopped.

For a second something honest moved across Dione's face — and Mira, on the bank, twelve metres off, in the noise of the recovery and with her back half-turned, caught it the way she caught everything, before the words, instead of the words. Not guilt. Closer to grief. The face of someone doing a job they understand the shape of better than the people who hired them.

"You should ask Salihken," Dione said, "what the wall's for. Not me. Not your engineer." She got the rope moving again. "And when he tells you he doesn't know — listen to *how* he doesn't know."

She went down to the line.

The water was nearly off the flat now. The reef came back grey and streaming, and the city stood up out of it the way it had stood up out of it every single day for eight hundred years, dry-laid, mortarless, the fifty-tonne corner holding its line against nothing but its own weight and the patience of whoever had set it there and then declined to leave a single note explaining how.

Behind them in the canal, their column waited to be hauled out of the only thing the day had actually proven.

Salihken stood at the edge of the islet with his hands folded, looking not at the failed lift and not at the wall, but at Mira — who had turned, now, fully, and was looking back at him. Neither of them said anything. There was nothing yet that needed saying, and both of them, in their different schools of it, were fluent in that.
