# Chapter 10 — A City for the Dead

The tide brought them in at the back of Nandauwas, where the seawall meets the channel, and Edith kept the lens cap on.

She had decided this on the water, watching the black walls rise out of the mangrove like something the island had grown rather than built. Lens cap on until told otherwise. It was a small discipline and it cost her, because the light was doing the thing the light does maybe twice a day in a place like this — coming in low and sideways off the lagoon, raking the basalt so every column stood out from its neighbour in relief, every dry-stacked log throwing a hard little shadow down the face of the wall. A photograph was sitting there fully composed, waiting, and she did not take it.

"You can put the cap back on your face," said Hecklefish, from the cooler where he was riding out the morning in a half-inch of lagoon water. "Nobody can hear it from there either."

"I'm being respectful," Edith said.

"You're being slow. The wall's not going anywhere. That's sort of the whole problem with the wall."

Mira touched Edith's wrist — *not yet* — though Edith already knew not yet. Ahead, on the islet, Soumas had stopped at the gap in the outer rampart and turned, and was waiting for them to catch up to a stillness he had been standing in for a while.

The custodian's name was Soumas and he did not hurry. He had taken them in on his cousin's skiff and he had not narrated the journey, which Edith had come to understand was itself a kind of speech. The men who narrate a place are selling it. Soumas let the place do its own talking and watched to see whether you could hear it.

They came up the islet through the outer enclosure into the inner, and the scale arrived the way scale always does — not as a number but as a feeling in the back of the neck. The walls of Nandauwas run five and a half metres, in places seven and a half, of stacked basalt prism laid header-and-stretcher, the long face then the blunt end, the long face then the blunt end, course on course, the way you'd bind the corner of a log cabin so it can't shear. Jakobus had read the grammar of it at the canal's edge days ago and handed the reading on. Standing inside it now, you didn't read it. You stood under it. The hexagonal ends of the columns faced out of the wall in their thousands, a honeycomb of cut-looking stone that nobody cut, and the morning came down them like water down a weir.

"This is the tomb," Yusuf said quietly. Not a question.

"This is the tomb," Soumas agreed.

In the centre of the inner court sat a low rectangle of stone, a vault built down rather than up, roofed once and open now. The royal mortuary. The Saudeleur lords had been laid here, the dynasty that unified the island and ruled it from this reef for four centuries until Isokelekel came over the water and ended them, somewhere around the early seventeenth century. Edith found she was breathing as you breathe in a church when you don't share the faith but you share the floor.

Frik had the survey tablet and was being very careful not to wave it around. "I keep doing the sum," he said, low, to nobody. "I can't stop doing the sum."

"Don't," said Mira, reading his face before his mouth finished.

"Three-quarters of a million tonnes," Frik said anyway, because the number had him by the throat and wouldn't let go. "Over four centuries that's eighteen hundred tonnes a year, every year, four hundred years running, and that's just the *delivery*. That's not the lifting. The cornerstone of this thing is fifty tonnes on its own." He looked up at the seven and a half metres of it. "Fifty tonnes. Onto a wall. By hand. On a reef."

"And then," said Hecklefish, from a respectful distance because even the fish had clocked the room, "nobody ever lived here. Just so we're all clear on the genius business plan. You build the hardest building on the planet, on a reef with no water and no soil, and the first thing you do when it's finished is make sure not one single person can stay the night."

Edith looked at the vault. The fish, as usual, had blundered into the true thing sideways.

Because that was it, wasn't it. Nan Madol grows no food. Nan Madol holds no fresh water. You cannot live here. The canals between the islets fill and empty with the tide and there is nothing to drink and nothing to eat and the whole stupendous labour of it — the haul of the columns twenty-five miles down the coast from the Sokehs quarries, the rafting of stone over open water that no modern crew has managed to repeat, the lifting of it into mortarless walls higher than a house — all of that effort went into building a place that could keep no living person alive. They didn't build a city and forget to feed it. They built it *for the people who don't eat*.

"It's not a city," Edith said. The words came out before she'd approved them. "It's the opposite of a city."

Soumas looked at her for the first time as if she'd said something. Not approval. Attention.

"Go on," he said.

She didn't want to. This was exactly the trap — the outsider warming to her own cleverness in front of the man who'd grown up inside the answer. She felt Mira's eyes on her and made herself stop at the edge.

"It's not my sentence to finish," Edith said.

Something moved across Soumas's face that she would not photograph if she'd had the camera up. He let a moment go by with the water sounding under the islet, and then he said, "The living went home. Every day. The chiefs, the priests, the men who lifted the stone — they came on the tide and they went on the tide, back to where the taro grew and the springs ran. They worked here. They worshipped here. They did not *stay*."

"Because you can't," Frik said.

"Because you *don't*," Soumas said, and the difference sat in the air between them like a third thing in the room.

---

Edith took the cap off the lens when he told her she could.

He told her at the outer court, not the inner. He walked her to the threshold of the inner enclosure and stopped there himself, and said the vault was not for the camera, and the wall facing it was not for the camera, but the outer rampart and the canal and the great honeycombed face of stone — those she could photograph, those the dead did not mind being seen. He gave her the line the way Jakobus gave readings, as a relay, a thing passed hand to hand and not seized.

So she shot the outer wall. And it was everything the low light had promised on the water — the columns standing proud of one another, the shadow running down each blunt hexagonal end, four centuries of patient impossible stacking holding its breath in front of her glass. She worked it the way she'd worked the things she was no longer allowed to think about, the way she'd worked rooms that had cost her something to be in, the old steadiness coming up through her hands.

And she found, in the viewfinder, that she kept tilting up. Past the wall. To the gap above it where the wall ended and the sky began.

"What's wrong," Mira signed, reading the hesitation in Edith's shoulders. Mira had been standing very still by the canal for a long time, watching Soumas the way she watched everyone, which was to say not for what he said.

"The shot's behind the wall," Edith signed back, badly, half voice. "The thing worth photographing is the thing he won't let me photograph. And he's right. That's the whole — " She stopped. "If I shoot the vault, I'm a thief. If I shoot the wall, I'm a tourist. There isn't a frame that's clean."

Mira watched her finish that. Then she did a thing she rarely did, which was to give an answer.

"Then don't shoot," Mira said, out loud, low and exact. "Witness it. That's allowed. The camera isn't the only way you're permitted to have seen something."

Edith lowered the lens.

Hecklefish, who had been carried up in his cooler to a spot in the shade and was watching the whole exchange with an unusual quiet, said, "Did the photographer just get out-photographed by the deaf woman who can't take a picture?"

"Yes," Edith said.

"I'm just checking. Carry on. I'll be over here, not saying anything, which is famously my whole brand."

---

Soumas brought them, at the end, to the threshold of the inner court, and let them stand at it. Not in it. At it.

The vault sat there in its low rectangle. The morning had climbed higher and the raking light was gone, and now the inner walls stood in their own shadow, the honeycomb darker, the air cooler by a degree the way the air cools near deep stone or deep water. Yusuf had stopped doing the engineer's arithmetic, which Edith counted as a kind of prayer in him. Frik had put the tablet away entirely. Nobody had told him to. He'd just stopped needing the sum to mean something here.

"You ask how it was lifted," Soumas said. He was looking at the vault, not at them. "Every one of you wants to ask. Your engineer has done the weight in his head three times since the skiff. I watched him do it."

"I have," Frik admitted.

"The men who built this could not tell you either," Soumas said, and there was no defeat in it. "Not because they did not know. Because the knowing was not the kind you write down. It went in their hands and their fathers' hands and it went into the ground with them. Some of it is here." He nodded at the vault. "You are standing at the edge of the people who knew how, and you are asking the people who keep them whether we'll explain it to you so you can put it in a film."

Edith felt the rebuke land and found it wasn't a rebuke. It was just true, stated cold, the way the weight was true.

"We're not — " Yusuf started.

"I know what you are not," Soumas said, not unkindly. "You're not the others. The others came with a chequebook and a story about how nobody here knows what this place is, so it must belong to whoever wants it. They will tell you they are protecting it. They put a hole in the world — *nobody knows* — and they pour money in the hole." He looked at them all, one at a time, and last and longest at Mira, who had not asked him a single question the entire morning. "You're standing in a grave, asking me to make it a riddle. I am telling you it is not a riddle. It is a grave. The men who carried the stone are *in the wall you want to photograph*."

The fish did not heckle. That, more than anything, told Edith how the room had changed.

"What's it for," Mira said.

It was the only question anyone had put to him directly, and she put it not in the place of *tell me the secret* but in the place of *I see there is a thing you are holding, and I will not take it, but I will let you give it weight in front of me if you choose.* Edith heard the difference and understood that Soumas heard it too, because he turned and looked at the small deaf woman who read bodies for a living, and the two of them stood inside a quiet that the rest of them were only guests in.

"It is for the dead to be kept *by* someone," Soumas said. "That is what it is for. Not so they are remembered. So they are *kept*. You leave a place like this empty of the living so it can be full of the other thing. And you do not leave it empty of *keepers*. The wall is not the city. The city is us. Standing here. Saying no to the people with the chequebook."

He let that go out across the water.

"That is why we do not tell," he said. "Not because we don't know. Because a grave that explains itself to strangers is one step from a grave that belongs to them."

Edith stood at the threshold with a thousand pounds of camera against her chest and did not raise it.

The dead were in the wall. The tide was coming back, and soon it would lift them off this islet and carry them home the way it had carried home everyone who ever worked here, four hundred years of living men going back to the springs and the taro at the end of the day, leaving the stone to its proper population. The light had moved. The shot was gone. She was glad the shot was gone.

"Tide's turning," Soumas said. "We go on the tide. Same as them."

And they went on the tide, and behind them the seven-metre walls held what they held, and not one of the crew asked him to say the thing he had just spent a morning, with great gentleness, declining to say.
