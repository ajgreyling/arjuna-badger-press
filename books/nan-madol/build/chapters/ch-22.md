# Chapter 22 — Notes on the Real, the Debated, and the Invented

Here is the part where the magician shows you the inside of the hat.

Most books do this quietly, in a smaller font, at the back, where nobody who matters will look. We are going to do it loud, because the whole point of this one was that the gap between *what is known* and *what is claimed* is not a footnote — it is the building. It is the spaces between. So: what was real, what is genuinely argued over by people with letters after their names, and what we made up. In that order. No cheating.

> *"Finally. The fish has been waiting twenty-one chapters to fact-check you."*

You have, and you can. Let's go.

---

**THE REAL.**

Nan Madol is a real place. You can go — by water, on the tide, by right and by protocol, the way the *Nahnmwarki* order requires, which is to say *not* the way you buy a ticket to a ruin. It sits on a reef flat off the southeast coast of Pohnpei, in the Federated States of Micronesia, fronting the district of Madolenihmw. Roughly ninety-two artificial islets, laced with tidal canals. A core about one and a half kilometres by half a kilometre. The name means *the spaces between* — the channels of water threading the stone. That is not a poetic flourish we added for the title. That is the translation. The island handed us the metaphor; we just refused to give it back.

The stone is real, and stranger for being explainable. It is columnar basalt — long hexagonal "logs" formed when thick lava cooled and contracted and split itself into prisms. Columnar jointing. The same physics that built the Giant's Causeway and Devils Tower. Nature pre-cut the blocks. We want to be very clear about this, because it is the single most important fact for *not* losing your mind: the columns were not carved into hexagons by anyone. They came out of the ground that shape. The wonder is not the shape. The wonder is what was done with it.

What was done with it is real, and it is a legible craft. The columns were stacked dry — no mortar — header-and-stretcher, log-cabin style, on a base of coral rubble fill. A block's long face, then a block's end, course on course, the way a log-cabin builder binds a wall so it locks against itself. This is the thing Jakobus reads in Chapter 3, and we did not invent the grammar of it. It is documented building technique. You can stand in front of Nandauwas — the great royal mortuary enclosure — and see it. Walls five and a half to seven and a half metres high. Columns typically five to twenty-five tonnes. A single cornerstone of around fifty.

The numbers are real, and we ran them straight. Total mass of the complex: on the order of seven hundred and fifty thousand tonnes. Built over roughly four centuries. Divide one by the other and you get something near one thousand eight hundred and fifty tonnes a year, sustained, for four hundred years. That is the calm, cold arithmetic Mira runs in Chapter 2, and it is an order-of-magnitude truth, not a false precision. We are not telling you it was 1,847.3 tonnes in a good year. We are telling you that whatever the real figure was, it was *that kind of number*, every year, for *that long*.

The dates are real and they are medieval. The megalithic phase — the stone city as you'd recognise it — began around AD 1180, by radiocarbon. This is not lost-civilisation antiquity. This is the same century as Notre-Dame's foundations. It was the capital of the Saudeleur dynasty, the line that unified and ruled Pohnpei, until the dynasty was overthrown by Isokelekel around the early seventeenth century.

> *"You'll notice he keeps saying 'around.' Watch him do it again."*

Around. Around the early seventeenth century. Some sources give a year — you'll see ~1628 — but we have written it as a range on purpose, and you should treat anyone who hands you a single confident date for the fall of a dynasty whose records were oral with the same gentle suspicion you'd give a man selling you a watch from the inside of his coat.

The quarry is real, and it is far. The basalt came from the Sokehs side of the island, on the order of twenty-five miles away. Across open water. And here is where the real becomes the *hole*, so let's stop and stand in it, because it is the whole reason the book exists.

---

**THE POTATO, AND WHY IT IS NOT A FAILURE OF YOURS.**

There is no documented transport-and-lift method. Nobody left a manual. And — this is the part we leaned on hardest, so it had better be solid — **experimental archaeology has tried to reproduce the feat and has not.** That is real. That is cited. Modern people, with modern understanding of the technique, with the best kit and a plan and daylight, have attempted the quarry-to-wall version of this and fallen short of the thing that is standing, flawless and silent, right behind them.

We need you to hear the exact shape of that sentence, because the shape is everything. It does **not** say *physically impossible.* It says *not yet reproduced.* Those are different countries, and the distance between them is precisely where the credulous fall off the map shouting about sky-cranes. The honest position — the one held by the people who have spent their lives on this reef in the rain — is a logistics-and-provisioning gap that the experts themselves leave open. It is not a claim against the textbook. It is a hole *in* the textbook that the textbook is honest enough to admit.

Two holes, really, and they feed each other.

The first is **how.** Rafting columns — some up to fifty tonnes — twenty-five miles over open ocean, then lifting them into seven-and-a-half-metre mortarless walls on a reef, by hand, with no crane, no surviving ramp, nothing left behind that says *this is how we did it.*

The second is **why here.** Nan Madol has no fresh water. It grows no food. It is uninhabitable without constant canoe resupply. Someone dragged three-quarters of a million tonnes of stone across the sea to build a city on a reef that could keep no living soul alive.

So the question is not only *how did they lift it.* The question is *why drag it all the way out there to a place that could feed and water no one.* And the honest, cold-eyed answer the book lands on — in Chapter 5, with Jakobus's question and the custodian's keeping of it — is the thing that re-opens the wonder without reaching past the evidence: **a city for the dead.** A craft and a love of one's dead great enough to put a stone metropolis where no one could live. That is not woo. That is human, and it is bigger than the dragon.

---

**THE DEBATED.**

Some things in this book are real disagreements between real scholars, and we have tried very hard never to settle them for you on the page, because they are not ours to settle.

The exact date of the Saudeleur fall is one, as above.

The columns found on the seabed are another, and this one matters because the fringe loves it. In 1988 and 1989, a University of Oregon survey documented basalt columns on the lagoon floor. Real. Cited. The prosaic, honest reading — the one we hold to — is **lost cargo.** Stone that went over the side during the haul. Evidence of *the journey*, not of a drowned super-structure, not of a sunken Mu. We have written it that way throughout, and where a character wants it to mean more —

> *"That'll be Yusuf, getting excited again."*

— that is a character over-reaching, which is a human thing to dramatise, and the narrator never once confirms it. If you came away from this book believing there is a sunken city under Nan Madol, you got that from a person inside the story who *wanted* it, not from us. We never said it. Go back and check. We'll wait.

---

**THE TRADITION.**

The Pohnpeians have their own account of how the stone moved. It involves the two sorcerer-brothers, Olisihpa and Olosohpa, and a dragon that flew the basalt into place. This is the island's oral history, and it is *theirs.* We render it with respect and we do not render it as fact, and — this is the firewall the whole book is built on — **we do not render it as a puzzle for the crew to decode.** It is not a cipher. It is not a clue the clever outsiders crack. It is a people's telling of their own dead and their own builders, and the most the book ever does is hold it up to the light and put it down again gently.

If the book worked, you noticed that the person who out-reads the crew at every turn that matters is the custodian. The crew own the *telling*. They never own the *place*. When the question of *what it is for* finally lands, it lands in a Pohnpeian hand, and it is given or withheld by a Pohnpeian right. Mira — who has spent a whole life being talked over, who reads the body because the words were never handed to her — sees that the official "we don't know" is a door held shut, not an empty room. And she does not pick the lock. That is the whole book. If you wanted her to open it, that want is the thing the book was about.

---

**THE INVENTED.**

So you know exactly where the made-up ends and the real begins:

The crew are invented. Mira, Yusuf, Edith, Frik — ours. The resort outfit and every named modern person attached to it — ours, built to dramatise a real and unglamorous danger, which is that *a place with no agreed meaning and no settled custodian is the easiest thing on earth to take.* The adversary is invented and is deliberately not a cabal — a fallible human, driven by money, whose best cover is the island's own honest "we don't know." That dynamic — that an admitted vacancy can be developed where a grave with keepers cannot — is the truest thing in the fiction.

The custodian, as a specific person with a specific name, is invented, and stands in for a real role and a real protocol — and is the part of this book most flagged as *provisional pending a Pohnpeian and FSM in-culture read.* We built on the facts. We did not present the invention as history. Where we have rendered consent customs, entry protocols, the *Nahnmwarki* order — those depictions are ours to get wrong and the island's to correct, and they bind.

Jakobus is invented — the weathered fixer who reads a made thing and asks *what is it for* and then, every time, hands the question on. He is the one literal thread through these books, and he is the road, never the lead. He defers. If you remember him deferring more than starring, the book did its job.

---

**THE WINK.**

We told you we'd name the question and decline to answer it, so here it is, named, and here it stays, unanswered:

*Who raised a stone city that could not feed or water a single soul — and how was three-quarters of a million tonnes of basalt carried across the sea and lifted into walls taller than a house, by hand, with nothing left behind to tell us how?*

We don't know. Neither do the people who've studied it longest, and they will tell you so to your face, and that telling is not ignorance — it is sometimes the most honest thing in the room, and it is sometimes a door held shut by right, and the deepest skill this book ever asked for was telling those two silences apart and honouring both.

Go and check the dossier. Every number in this chapter traces to a cited row, and we would rather you caught us than trusted us. Run the arithmetic yourself. Read the survey. Then stand on the causeway at low tide, with the diesel and the frangipani and the church bell carrying over the water, and look at the wall, and let yourself ask the question without needing the answer to arrive.

That draught you feel, standing in the gap — that's the door, left where it was.

You're allowed to wonder.

> *"And you're allowed to say maybe. Roll the credits."*
