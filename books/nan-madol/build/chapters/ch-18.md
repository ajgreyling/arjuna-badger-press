# Chapter 18 — The Offer

The bar at the Cliff Rainbow had a ceiling fan that turned just fast enough to push the heat around without moving it anywhere, and a television in the corner playing a rugby match nobody watched, the sound off, the score wrong, the whole thing buffering. Edith sat with her back to the wall out of habit she'd stopped apologising for years ago. From here she could see the door, the kitchen pass, and the man Yusuf had agreed to meet.

His name was Calloway. He had the kind of tan that costs money — even, maintained, the tan of a man who is outdoors on purpose and never by accident. Linen shirt, two buttons, a watch he didn't check because the watch was the point. He'd bought the first round before anyone sat down, which Edith filed the way she filed everything: a frame composed in advance.

"You've been out to the reef," he said. Not a question.

"We've been allowed out to the reef," Yusuf said. "There's a difference."

Calloway smiled at that like it was charming. He had a folder. People who carry folders into bars want you to see the folder before they open it, and he let it lie there on the wet ring his glass had left, untouched, breathing.

Edith had photographed men like this in three wars. The clean-handed ones were always the worst, because they never did the thing themselves. They funded it, they enabled it, they stood at the edge of the frame with their hands in their pockets while the actual taking happened just out of shot. You learned to read them by what they declined to look at.

Calloway hadn't once looked at Mira.

That was the tell, and it was a loud one. Mira sat to Edith's left, half-turned so she could see his mouth, her glass untouched, her hands quiet on the table. Calloway had clocked the hearing aid and the angle of her attention and had filed her, the way he filed everything, as not-a-decision-maker, and routed his face entirely to Yusuf and, occasionally, performatively, to Edith. *The man with the chequebook and the woman with the lens.* He thought he'd read the room.

He'd read the wrong half of it.

---

"Let me tell you what I love about this place," Calloway said, and turned his glass a quarter-turn on the table, lining it up with the folder. "What I genuinely love. It's the only unsolved one left."

"It's not unsolved," Yusuf said. "It's a Saudeleur mortuary capital. Radiocarbon puts the megalithic phase around eleven-eighty. There's a dynasty, there's a fall, there's an oral history four centuries deep. There are people in this town whose grandparents could name you the Nahnmwarki line."

"Sure." Calloway nodded along, agreeable as a brochure. "Sure. But ask anyone how they did it." He spread his hands. "Ask the engineers. Ask the archaeologists. Ask the *islanders*, God love them — what do they tell you?"

He waited. He wanted someone to say it. When nobody did, he said it himself, soft, almost tender:

"We don't know."

Edith felt the room change shape. Not loudly. The fan kept turning. But she'd heard those three words said the other way — said by an old man at the edge of a canal with his hand flat on a column of basalt, said the way you say *that is not for you* — and she'd heard them said now, by this man, and they were the same three words pointed in opposite directions, and she understood, sitting very still, that he was going to spend the next hour trying to make them mean the same thing.

"Three-quarters of a million tonnes," Calloway said. "Did you know that? Seven hundred and fifty thousand tonnes of rock, give or take. Rafted across — what is it, twenty-five miles of open ocean? A cornerstone that goes fifty tonnes on its own. Lifted into walls seven metres high. Dry-stacked. No mortar, no crane, no ramp, nothing left behind to tell you how." He let it hang. "And here's the part I really love. People have *tried* to do it again. Proper experimental archaeology, proper money, modern rigging. And they can't. They get a column off the quarry floor and onto a raft and they look at the four hundred years of it and they go quiet." He smiled. "Nobody's reproduced it. Not once."

"That's true," Yusuf said. Carefully. Because it was.

Edith had watched them try, in a way — not the historical attempts, but the spirit of them, in the failed lift back at the start, the modern team and the hired rig and the single basalt log that had come out a potato, lopsided and shameful in the shadow of the wall that had stood flawless behind it for eight hundred years. The thing on the reef didn't care that you had hydraulics. It stood there and said nothing and made you look small. That gap — that exact gap — was the only honest thing in this whole bar, and Calloway had just laid it on the table like a winning card.

"So." He finally touched the folder. Didn't open it. Just touched it. "You've got a site of global significance, genuinely mysterious, photographs like a dream — Edith, I've seen your work, the canals at low tide, that's a *cover*, that's a Netflix open. And you've got an island that, with the warmest possible respect, *doesn't know what it has.* That's not a criticism. That's just the situation. Nobody's developed it. Nobody's even properly *interpreted* it. There's no visitor centre worth the name, no narrative, no —" he searched for the word and found the one he liked best — "no *custody*."

"There's plenty of custody," Mira said.

It was the first thing she'd said. She said it flat and quiet and Calloway's head came round at last, surprised, recalibrating, and Edith watched him do the arithmetic he should have done forty minutes ago.

"Of course," he said, recovering. "Of course. I only mean — institutionally. Formally. The kind of custody that protects a place like this from, frankly, the wrong people getting to it first." He spread his hands again. The hands did a lot of work. "Which is where we come in."

---

The folder opened.

Edith didn't lean toward it. She'd learned long ago that the photograph you want is rarely the one being offered. She kept her eyes on his face and let Yusuf read the numbers, and she read Calloway reading them being read.

"Heritage management partnership," he was saying. "We're not buying anything — you can't buy it, it's sacred ground, we'd never — God, no. It's a *stewardship* model. We bring capital, infrastructure, conservation, a real scientific program. Dignified, controlled access. And the local stakeholders — the Nahnmwarki, the state, whoever's the right table — they're partners. Equity partners. There's a number on page three for the community that I'd ask you to look at before you make a face."

Yusuf looked at page three. He did not make a face, which was its own kind of face.

"The genius of it," Calloway went on, warming, and this was the part he'd come to say, "is that the mystery is the *asset*. You don't solve it. You'd be mad to solve it. The not-knowing is the whole product. People will pay to stand inside a question nobody can answer. We protect that. We frame it, we light it, we *preserve the not-knowing* — and we keep it out of the hands of, you know. The lost-continent crowd. The aliens-built-it people. The pseudo-archaeology grifters who'd turn it into a circus."

"You'd protect it," Mira said, "by keeping it empty of meaning."

"I'd protect it by keeping it *open* as a question."

"Same thing." She still hadn't raised her voice. "An empty question's easier to fence."

Calloway laughed, genuinely delighted, the way men like him laugh when an underling turns out to have teeth — pleased to have found a worthier opponent, not for one second considering he might lose. "Now *that's* a line. You should write the brochure copy." He turned back to Yusuf, the decision-maker, the man, the cheque. "Look. I'll be straight with you, because I think you'll respect straight. Three other groups are circling this. I know two of them and they're worse than me, considerably. The window where this gets done *well* is closing, and it's closing because the people who'd do it badly don't need anyone's permission to start asking, and the people who *should* have the say —" he gestured, vaguely, at the town, at the dark beyond the louvres, at the whole drenched island — "haven't organised themselves to say no fast enough. That's not their fault. But it's true."

And the terrible thing, Edith thought, was that some of it was. Not the conclusion. But the diagnosis. A place with no agreed meaning and no single throat to say *no* through was the easiest thing in the world to take, and he hadn't invented that, he'd just arrived to profit from it, the way the tide arrives, neutral and patient and certain.

---

Edith set her camera on the table.

She did it slowly, both hands, the way you set down something heavier than it looks. She hadn't shot a frame all evening. It sat there now between them, lens cap on, and Calloway glanced at it and back to her with a small congratulatory nod, like she was finally producing the asset.

"You said you've seen my work," she said.

"I have. It's extraordinary."

"You said the canals at low tide. The cover shot."

"It's a hell of an image."

"I took it on the third morning," she said. "Custodian walked us out on the falling tide. There's a wall at Nandauwas, seven metres, the cornerstone's the one that goes about fifty tonnes — you know your numbers, you'll know the one. He put his hand on it. Not for me. He didn't know I was there yet. He put his hand flat on the stone the way you'd put it on a coffin." She kept her voice level. The fan turned. "I didn't take that one. I had the shot and I let it go. You know why?"

Calloway, for the first time all night, didn't have a line ready.

"Because the photograph would have been mine," Edith said, "and the grief was his. And there are things you can take that don't belong to you, and the camera doesn't make it not stealing. It makes it worse. It makes it permanent." She turned the camera so the lens faced the wall, away from all of them. "You want us to frame the not-knowing. I've spent twenty years framing things that weren't mine to frame, in places where nobody could stop me, and I'll tell you what you actually get when you do that. You get a beautiful picture of an empty room. And the people who lived in it are somewhere outside the edge of it, and they stay there, because the frame's the fence now. You said it yourself. You'd preserve the not-knowing." She nodded, slow. "That's the part. That's the whole thing. You're not buying the mystery. You're buying the *silence.* And it's not for sale, because it's not a vacancy."

"Everything's for sale," Calloway said, but gently, and it came out less like a creed than a hope.

"That one isn't ours to sell," Yusuf said, and closed the folder. Slid it back across the wet ring, square, neat, the way it had come. "And it isn't yours to buy from us. You've got the wrong table. We're not even *a* table. We came out here on the tide because somebody let us. That's the whole of our standing. We don't have a 'no' to give you, Mr Calloway — and that's exactly why we won't give you a yes."

---

Calloway looked at the three of them for a moment, and Edith watched him decide it had been a wasted evening, and then watched him decide it hadn't — because now he knew where the crew stood, which was a thing worth the price of the drinks. He'd find his table. He'd said so. Three groups circling, two of them worse. He stood, buttoned the one button, left a note under his glass that was too much.

"You know the funny thing," he said, folder under his arm, almost wistful. "I do love it. I really do. I'm not the villain you've decided I am. I just got here first."

"You didn't get here first," Mira said.

He paused.

"People have been keeping that place for eight hundred years," she said. "You got here last. You just got here loud."

The fan turned. The rugby buffered. Calloway gave a small bow, half rueful, half something else, and went out into the warm and the diesel and the frangipani and the dark, and the door swung and stuck and swung.

Yusuf let out a long breath. "Three other groups."

"And he's the best of them," Edith said.

Mira was looking at the door he'd gone through. "He had it nearly right," she said. "All of it. The tonnage, the cornerstone, the failed lifts, the no-water-no-food. He could pass the exam." She picked up her glass at last, looked at it, set it down. "He just thinks a thing nobody will explain to him is the same as a thing nobody knows."

Edith reached over and put the lens cap in her pocket and left the camera where it was, facing the wall, switched off, an instrument with the integrity to refuse a shot. Somewhere across the dark water the reef sat under the falling tide, seven metres of dry-stacked stone holding up four centuries of held breath, and not one column of it had ever once asked to be understood.

Outside, the tide was going out. By morning you could walk to the wall, if you were allowed.
