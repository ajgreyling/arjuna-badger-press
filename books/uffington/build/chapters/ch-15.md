# Chapter 15 — The Consultancy Withdraws

The village green looked like the morning after a war it had won.

The Lens stood at the edge of it with the camera dead against her chest, not yet raised, taking the inventory she always took before she let herself shoot. A marquee had half-collapsed in the night, one pole down, the canvas slumped and pooled with rainwater that three teenagers were now sloshing off the roof with a broom handle. Bunting hung in wet ropes between the chestnuts. The cricket pavilion had a string of bulbs swinging from a single screw, blinking gamely, indifferent to the fact that nobody had told them the storm was over.

And it was over. That was the strange part. The light had that scrubbed quality you get after weather has thrown everything it owns at a place and run out — a low gold coming in sideways under the cloud-wrack, lighting the puddles so they looked deliberate, set out like coins. Steam was rising off the green where the sun touched it. Up the hill, above the village, you could see the chalk.

She could see the chalk.

The Horse ran clean across the slope, white against the deep storm-green, the lines fresh from the night's work — and the Lens, who had photographed things that had taught her not to use the word, found that the word she wanted was *alive*. The figure had not been there yesterday in this way. Yesterday it had been a patient, fading suggestion under turf, the way an old scar goes silver and almost-gone. Now it leapt. The crews had finished it in the dark, on their knees in the mud, packing crushed chalk into the trenches with the rain coming sideways into their hoods.

She had not raised the camera then either. She'd carried buckets.

"Tea," said Mdisplay — the kinetic one, the ADHD one, whose actual name nobody used anymore — appearing at her elbow with two paper cups and the wired brightness of a man who has not slept and has decided sleep is for cowards. "It's terrible. It's from the urn that's been on since four. It's basically tannin and regret. Drink it."

She took it. It was terrible. She drank it.

"He's here," Mdisplay said, nodding across the green.

---

Dr. Rowan Leigh did not look like a man who had lost. That was the thing the Lens noticed first and filed, the way she filed everything — the face before the words. He looked rested, which meant he hadn't been on the hill, which meant nothing and everything. He was in a good coat. He had a folder. He had, beside him, the young archaeologist who'd found the buried chalk fragment, and she was carrying the laptop with the model on it, the beautiful one, the rendered Horse as the consultancy proposed to render it: crisp, symmetrical, *correct*, every curve resolved into the shape some software had decided a Bronze Age intention must have been.

It was a good model. The Lens would give them that. It was the kind of good that made you sad without being able to say why, like a restored photograph of someone you loved, where the scratch was gone and so was the year.

Edie Vane was already standing on the green when Leigh reached it. She had not been there a moment ago. The Lens had learned not to be surprised by this — Edie arrived the way weather did, by having always somehow been on its way. She had chalk to the elbows and chalk in the deep lines of her hands and a thermos under one arm, and she watched Leigh come with the particular patience of a woman who has been to a great many meetings and knows that the people who call them are rarely the people who do the work.

Leigh stopped a respectful distance off. He didn't open the folder.

"It looks magnificent," he said, and meant it, and was looking up at the hill when he said it, not at Edie, not at the camera, and that was the first thing that told the Lens which way the morning was going to fall. "I've been up since dawn looking at it. I drove out to the valley road, where you see it whole." He shook his head slightly. "You finished it in *that*."

"We finished it in that," Edie agreed.

Frik had come up on the Lens's other side without sound, which was how Frik did everything, and his hands were already moving, low and quick, reading the scene to her in the shorthand they'd built between them over three weeks — *watch his shoulders, watch his hands, the young one's frightened.* The Lens looked at the young archaeologist. Frik was right. The girl was holding the laptop like it might be taken from her, or like she wished it would be.

"We came," Leigh said, "to save it. I want to be straight about that, because I think you've all decided we came to take it, and I'd like to leave with at least one of you believing otherwise." A small, tired smile. "We had the funding. We had the dating — Palmer and Miles, the OSL, the whole sequence, you've heard me on the OSL more than anyone should have to." He glanced at Mdisplay, who had quoted the dates back at him at a council meeting with the specific glee of a man who has memorised a thing purely to ruin someone's afternoon. "Fourteen hundred to five-fifty BC. Trenches a metre deep, packed with chalk, cut so the slope can't creep over it. That's not folklore. That's *engineering*. Somebody built this to last, and it has lasted, and my entire professional life is the conviction that what lasts deserves to be looked after properly."

"Nobody here disagrees with that," Edie said.

"No," Leigh said. "That's what I've finally understood. That's exactly the problem with me being here." He looked down at the folder, then handed it — not to Edie, but sideways, to the young archaeologist, as if he no longer wanted to be the one holding it. "I had a vision of looking after it *properly*. Cleaner lines. The geometry resolved. We did the survey — we could give you the Horse the way its makers must have first cut it, before three thousand years of human hands smeared it about."

"That's the bit," said Edie.

"That's the bit," Leigh said.

---

The Lens raised the camera. She did it slowly, the way she'd taught herself, the way the series kept asking her to do the opposite — to lower it, to be a person and not a lens — but this was a frame she would not get twice, and there is a difference, she had decided long ago, between stealing a moment and keeping one. She framed Leigh's hands, empty now, and behind them, soft and out of focus, the white shape on the green hill.

Here is the official story, and it is true, and it is the consultancy's to tell as well as anyone's: the Uffington White Horse is the oldest chalk hill-figure in Britain, and the only one we can date, and it does not survive by accident. Leave a chalk figure on a living hillside and the hill takes it back. Grass roots in. Soil creeps down. The white goes grey, then green, then gone — twenty years, give or take, and you'd never know it had been there. That is not a guess. There are hill-figures cut in living memory, by people who knew exactly what they were doing, who had budgets and committees and the best of intentions, and they have furred over and faded because the one ingredient is the one nobody can buy.

You have to keep coming back.

"The model's right," Leigh was saying, quiet now, almost to himself. "That's the maddening thing. If you wanted to know what the makers cut, three thousand years ago — the model's probably *right*." He looked up at the hill. "But the makers have been dead three thousand years. And every seven years or so, somebody who isn't them has come up that slope and put it back. Not *back to what it was*. Just — back. With their own hands, their own slightly-wrong eye for the curve of a leg." He breathed out. "I could give you the makers' Horse. I can't give you a hundred generations of the wrong hands, and that's — I've finally seen it — that's the only thing it actually is. The makers' lines have been gone for centuries. What's up there is the *scouring*. Not the Horse. The scouring."

Edie said nothing. The Lens, behind the camera, watched her not-say-it, which was its own kind of speech.

Because here was the hole, and the Lens had watched it open over three weeks, and it had not closed, and Leigh — to his enormous credit, to his ruin — had just stepped into it on his own two feet. The OSL dates the *chalk*. It dates the burial of quartz grains, last sunlight, all that hard clean science. It tells you when. It does not tell you *how it kept going.* How a maintenance act — not a meaning, not a myth, just the bodily fact of people climbing a hill with chalk — transmits itself unbroken across the Romans coming and the Romans leaving, across plague carts and enclosure and a world war when they *turfed the thing over on purpose* so the bombers couldn't navigate by it, and then dug it back out the moment the bombers were gone. Nobody wrote down a rule that said: keep doing this. By the Middle Ages they'd already lost the why so thoroughly they thought it was a dragon. They kept scouring a dragon.

*They kept scouring a thing they had the wrong name for.*

How do you keep a habit alive longer than you keep its reason?

The fish, if the fish were here, would point out that "unbroken" is doing a lot of work for a chain nobody can actually document all the links of, and the fish would be *right*, and that is exactly why the word in the back matter is "inferred." The chain is inferred from the simple, brutal arithmetic of survival: the Horse is here, the hill eats Horses in twenty years, ergo somebody never stopped. You can't prove a single year. You can only prove the result, standing white on the green slope, steaming in the after-storm light, while a funded and decent man stands on a village green and gives it back to the people who never let it go.

---

"So we're withdrawing," Leigh said. He said it to Edie, finally, properly, his eyes on hers. "The proposal. The trial re-chalking, the digital programme, all of it. I'll square it with the council — that's mine to do, I made the pitch, I'll unmake it." A wry flick of the mouth. "I'll keep the model. As a — cautionary thing. A picture of the Horse nobody's ever seen and nobody should."

The young archaeologist closed the laptop. The little click of it carried across the wet green.

"You found a real thing," Edie said to her — not to Leigh; to the girl. "Under the turf. That chalk fragment. That was a real thing and a good find and you should keep finding them."

The girl looked up like someone unhooked.

"Just," Edie said, "don't ever think the old chalk is the Horse." She turned and looked up the hill, and the Lens followed the look through the viewfinder, and for one moment all of them — the consultant, the archaeologist, the deaf face-reader, the kinetic one, the photographer who would not lower her camera — were looking at the same white shape for the same reason. "The old chalk's just the first lot of hands. We're the latest lot. That's all any of it ever is. Hands, and the next hands."

Frik signed something to the Lens, fast, and she lowered the camera to read it. *She didn't thank him,* Frik signed. *Watch. She won't.*

She didn't. Edie Vane did not thank Rowan Leigh for withdrawing, and the Lens understood, in the way she understood things faster than she could explain them, that this was not coldness. You do not thank someone for stepping off your foot. The reserve in Edie was not triumph and it was not forgiveness. It was the patience of a woman who had known all along that the Horse would still be here when the funding moved on, because it had outlasted every funding there had ever been, every empire and every committee and every man with a folder and a good coat and a true thing he believed.

Leigh seemed to know it too. He didn't wait to be thanked. He nodded — to Edie, to the crew, a small soldier's nod — and turned, and walked back across the green toward the car park, and the young archaeologist went after him carrying the closed laptop with the perfect Horse asleep inside it.

---

Mdisplay let out a breath. "Well," he said. "He was decent. I was all ready for him to be a *villain*. I'd worked up a whole thing."

"He believed it," the Lens said. "That was the trouble. He really wanted to save it."

"Everybody wants to save it," said the autistic one — Pell — who had been standing apart this whole time at the green's edge, looking not at the people but at the hill, at the way the new lines caught the light, the rhythm of curve and gap, reading the figure the way he read everything, as a pattern that wanted finishing. "Nobody wants to *do* it. That's the difference. Saving's a noun. The Horse is a verb."

Edie almost smiled at that. Almost. She unscrewed the thermos and poured something dark and steaming into the cup and handed it, without comment, to Pell, who was the one of them who'd worked the trenches longest in the night, who'd packed chalk with his bare hands for six hours and not spoken once.

Across the green, the broom-handle teenagers had got the marquee roof emptied and were hauling the fallen pole upright, three of them on a guy-rope, slipping in the mud, shrieking with laughter. Someone had got the urn going again. A woman was unboxing what looked like four hundred sausage rolls. A man with a fiddle was tuning it under the dripping chestnuts, badly, hopefully. The bunting still hung wet, but somebody had begun to wring it out, handful by handful, and rehang it.

The festival was coming back. Not the spectacle the consultancy had pitched — the lights, the heritage-trail signage, the visitor numbers, the Horse as a thing to come and *see*. That had walked off to the car park. What was coming back was smaller and older and harder to photograph: the cheese stall, the fiddle, the cricket teas, the children who would be brought up the hill later to put their own small wrong hands to the chalk so that in fifty years they could bring their own.

The Lens raised the camera one last time. She found the fiddler in the frame, and the wet bunting, and the upright marquee, and — past them, soft, up the hill — the white shape, fresh-cut, steaming, anonymous, three thousand years old and one night young.

She did not lower it this time. She let it run.

Up there, the Horse ran across the chalk, going nowhere, going everywhere, kept by hands that had long forgotten its name and never once forgotten its body. Nobody on the green knew what it had been for. Every one of them knew what they would do, the next time the grass came creeping in.

They would go up the hill. They would put it back.
