# Chapter 12 — Storm on the Downs

The first I knew of it was the sheep.

You learn things, hanging around a hill long enough, and one of them is that sheep are barometers with legs. All afternoon they'd been scattered easy across the lower slope, grazing the way sheep graze, like they've got nowhere to be and resent you for suggesting otherwise. Then — and I clocked the exact second, because I'd been filming them for B-roll nobody would ever use — they all looked up at once. Every woolly head swung southwest. Then they started moving uphill, fast, in a long pale river toward the lee of the rampart.

"Hey," I said, to no one, because the others were strung out along the trench-line forty metres off and Frik can't hear me anyway and the Lens had her back to me, doing the thing she does where she becomes furniture so the world forgets to perform for her. "Hey. The sheep know something."

Southwest, the sky had gone the colour of a bruise three days old. Not black. Worse than black — that yellow-green underbelly that means the weather's not coming, it's already decided, it's just choosing its moment. The wind that had been faffing about in the grass all day stopped faffing. It put its shoulder into the hill.

I should explain what we were standing in, because it matters for what happened next.

You hear "white horse" and you picture paint. A nice clean outline somebody touches up with a brush. The Uffington Horse is not paint. The Uffington Horse is *trenches* — proper ones, cut down about a metre into the chalk bedrock, packed solid with crushed white rubble, the whole 110-metre run of it. That depth isn't decoration. That's the engineering that keeps the thing alive. On a slope like this, the topsoil doesn't sit still; it creeps, it slides, it migrates downhill a few millimetres a year like it's got somewhere better to be. A shallow figure would've been smeared into a ghost inside a lifetime. A metre-deep trench holds its line against the whole patient violence of the hillside. Somebody three thousand years ago understood soil creep well enough to beat it. Sit with that one a second.

But "holds its line" doesn't mean "self-cleaning." The trenches catch grass. They catch washed-down silt. Leave it twenty years and the green just walks back in and takes the white, and the most-looked-at animal in Bronze Age Britain becomes a faint suggestion in the turf that ramblers argue about. The whole reason there's a festival, the whole reason any of us were up there, is that the white has to be *fed.* Crushed chalk, by the bucket, by the hand, packed back into the lines. That's the scouring. That's the doing.

And right now half the leg of the Horse was open.

That's how the scour works — you don't strip the whole figure at once and leave it naked to the sky. You work it in sections. Edie's lot had the near hindquarter and the long back leg unpacked, the old grass and tired chalk lifted out, the fresh chalk staged in trugs along the edge ready to go down the next morning when the light was good and the hands were fresh. An open trench is a hungry trench. An open trench in a storm is a drain.

The first drops hit like thrown gravel.

"EDIE." That was a scourer I didn't know, a big man with forearms like rolled brisket, and he wasn't asking a question, he was sounding an alarm. "Edie, the leg's open —"

"I know what's open." She came up the slope already moving, and I want to be honest about something, because the easy version of this scene is the calm wise local who's seen it all. That's not what I saw. I saw a teacher who'd done a hundred fire drills and was now in an actual fire, and the difference showed in her hands. They were going before her mouth caught up. "Tarps. The blue ones, by the gate, Dav's trailer. *Now.* Anyone not carrying chalk's carrying water away from that leg."

I did the thing I do. I'm not built for the slow read — that's Frik, that's our pattern-spotter who can stare at a hillside for an hour and tell you the drainage of three thousand years. I'm built for *now,* for the next ninety seconds, for what's loose and where it goes. My brain in a crisis isn't a calm river, it's a pinball machine, and the trick I've learned is to stop fighting that and just *aim the ball.*

Drainage. That was the ball. Water comes off this hill the way water has always come off this hill, and the open trench was sitting square in a line that wanted to become a stream.

"The trug — the chalk in the trugs —" I grabbed the nearest scourer's sleeve, a woman in a wax jacket already silvering with rain. "If that fills with water it's not chalk, it's soup, it'll wash out the bottom of the trench, we have to get it *uphill* of the line, not next to it —"

She looked at me for half a second the way locals look at the festival blow-ins who suddenly have opinions. Then she looked at the trug. Then she looked at the water already starting to find its path down the open leg, braiding into the loose chalk, lifting it, beginning — right in front of us — to *carry it away,* a thin white thread of three-thousand-year-old maintenance dissolving into the runoff.

"Boy's right," she said. "Up. Get it up."

We got it up. Trug after trug, hauled uphill of the open trench, while the rain went from gravel to needles to a solid grey weight that came at the hill almost sideways. The chalk dust that had been on everyone's hands all day turned to a paste, and the paste turned to a slurry, and the hill that had been firm green turf at four o'clock became, by full dark, a thing that grabbed your boots and didn't want them back.

Frik found me by the elbow. He doesn't shout, obviously, but he has a way of *arriving* in your peripheral vision that's louder than shouting. He pointed: down at the leg, then up at the rampart, then he made a shape with his two flat hands — a roof, a tilt, water running off — and then he jabbed back at the trench, urgent, *no.* Then he flipped his hands the other way: water running *in.*

It took me a beat. The Lens got there first; she'd been crouched in the muck with the camera, because of course she had, because that's the camera the whole thing keeps asking her to put down and she never will, and she lowered it just enough to follow Frik's hands.

"He's saying the tarp's wrong," she said. Flat, certain. "Look where they've pegged it."

They'd thrown the first blue tarp over the open leg like you'd cover a woodpile — pegged on the *downhill* side, sagging in the middle. Which meant every litre that hit it ran straight down into the open trench. They were funnelling the storm into the wound.

Frik wasn't reading the weather. None of us were, really, not the way he was. He'd spent the whole day watching *hands* — whose chalk went where, who deferred to whom, the silent grammar of a scour none of us could've written down — and now he was reading the tarp the same way, reading the *care* in it and finding it pointed the wrong direction. He didn't have the word for hydraulics. He had the eye for it. Same eye.

"EDIE." Now I was the one not asking a question. I waved her over and pointed and let Frik do it again with his hands, the roof, the tilt, the *wrong way,* and Edie — and this is the part I keep coming back to — Edie didn't argue and didn't explain. She watched Frik's hands for the length of one breath and then she was already at the tarp.

"Off it. *Off it.* We're pegging it high side, we're shedding it onto the grass, not into the line —" and the big brisket-forearmed man and the wax-jacket woman and a teenager I hadn't even seen arrive were all on it, ripping pegs, re-setting them on the uphill lip so the blue plastic became a ramp instead of a funnel, and the storm-water that had been pouring into the open leg now ran off the high side and away down the slope where it could do its three-thousand-year thing without taking the Horse with it.

I stood in the dark and the noise and watched a Bronze Age problem get solved by a Deaf man's hands and an Iron Age festival's tarpaulins and I had one of those moments where the pinball stops bouncing for a second and just hangs in the air.

Because here's the thing they don't tell you in the brochure. The official story — the one that's *true,* the one with OSL dates pinning the figure to somewhere between 1380 and 550 BC, the one with the metre-deep trenches you can measure with a tape today — the official story makes it sound *solved.* Bronze Age people made a clever thing, the cleverness keeps it sharp, the end. And it is clever. It's genuinely, soberingly clever.

But clever doesn't survive a Tuesday like this one. Engineering buys you the *structure.* It doesn't buy you the bodies on the hill at ten at night in horizontal rain deciding the Horse is worth getting cold for. The trench holds the line. *People* hold the trench. And somewhere out there in the dark, three thousand years of those people had made exactly this call, on exactly this slope, in exactly this kind of weather, and the figure was still here because every single time, somebody had said: not tonight, you don't.

Nobody up there could've told you what the Horse *means.* They'd lost that. Lost it so long ago that by the Middle Ages the locals had already decided it was a dragon and made up stories about it galloping off to Wayland's barrow at night. The meaning's been gone for the better part of a millennium and probably much longer. And every seven or ten years, for as long as anyone can reckon back and then well past where reckoning gives out, these people have hauled chalk up this hill and packed it into trenches for a horse they call a dragon for a reason nobody remembers.

You want the hole? That's the hole. Not how it was built — we know that, more or less. The hole is *why it was never let go.* Empire came and went. Plague came and went. There was a literal world war where they turfed the whole thing over so German bombers couldn't navigate by it, and then the second the war was done somebody dug it back out, because of course they did, because the alternative was unthinkable, even to people who couldn't tell you what they were saving or what it was for. You can chart the OSL dates. You can measure the trench. You cannot measure the part of a community that decides, generation after generation, with no living memory of the reason, that the white must not fade.

I'm not going to pretend I cracked it standing in that rain. I didn't. Nobody did. That's not the kind of thing that cracks.

We got the leg covered. We got the chalk staged uphill and dry under the second tarp. The storm did what storms do, which is rage like it'll never stop and then, around midnight, just — get bored. The wind dropped off the hill and went to bother somebody else. The rain thinned to that fine Berkshire drizzle that feels like the sky apologising. And we all stood there, twenty-odd of us, plastered in slurry, lit by the headlamps and the one work-light Dav had rigged off his trailer battery, looking at a half-scoured Horse that had not, in fact, washed off the hill tonight.

Edie did a slow count of her people. Touched a shoulder, said a name, the way you do. Then she looked at the open leg — the leg that still needed packing, the fresh chalk that still needed to go down by hand, line by line, the whole job that the storm had interrupted and not finished — and she looked at the sky, which had a thin bruise of paler grey low in the east that meant the night had a bottom to it after all.

"Light's in four hours," she said. To everyone. To no one. "Can't pack chalk in the wet. It'll set wrong, slump out the first frost. We wait for it to drain, then we go. Soon as we can see our hands."

"That's dawn," somebody said.

"That's dawn," she agreed.

And I looked at Frik, who was already looking at me, rain running off his nose, and he raised his eyebrows in that way that's a whole sentence, and I looked at the Lens, who had finally — *finally* — let the camera hang and was just standing in it, in the actual weather, a person and not a lens, and I looked at our pattern-spotter, who'd gone quiet the way he goes quiet when a thing's too big to narrate, and our ND lead's hands and the Lens's lowered camera and the cold and the chalk and the whole impossible inherited *doing* of it.

Nobody put it to a vote. That's not how it works with us. I just stepped forward, into the light, where Edie could see me, and I held up my two grey-white hands — Bronze Age coloured, festival coloured, the colour of every hand that's ever done this — and I made the shape Frik had made for the tarp, except I turned it the right way up. Chalk going *in.* The line being fed.

"We're staying," I said. "Tell us where the buckets go."
