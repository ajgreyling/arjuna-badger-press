# Chapter 9 — Hands in the Chalk

The hammers came out at seven, before the dew burned off, and nobody told us what to do with them.

That was the first lesson, though we didn't clock it as a lesson until later. Edie Vane handed Tem a long-handled wooden mallet — older than Tem, older than Tem's father, the head worn shiny on one face and splintered to felt on the other — and said, "You'll want the smooth side," and walked off up the slope to a knot of people who already knew which side they wanted.

So we stood on the flank of a 3,000-year-old horse holding tools like waiters who'd been handed instruments at a wedding band's interval, and we watched.

You have to understand what we were looking at. The official version is clean and it is correct, and I'll give it to you straight because it deserves that. Sometime between roughly 1380 and 550 BC — that's the OSL date, Palmer and Miles, buried quartz read for the last sunlight it saw, hard science, not a guess — Late Bronze Age people came to this hill and cut trenches a full metre deep. Not scratches. Trenches. Then they packed them with crushed white chalk, and they did it in a shape that makes no sense from where they were standing. You cannot see the Horse from above it. You stand on its back and you see a white scribble, a bend of line that could be anything. The figure only assembles itself from the valley, or from the air. They built a thing they could not see, for an eye that was not theirs.

And then somebody re-cut it. And somebody after that. And on, and on, every seven or ten years, through Rome arriving and Rome leaving, through the plague years, through a man called Alfred and the people who decided for centuries the Horse was his when it was a thousand years older than him, through the morning in 1940 they covered the whole thing with turf and brush so the Luftwaffe couldn't navigate by it, and through the afternoon they took the turf off again. Re-cut. Re-chalked. The shape held because hands held it.

*The fish, perched on Tem's mallet head, examined the splintered face. "Tradition," it said, "is just peer pressure with better PR."*

Frik figured out the work before any of us, because Frik wasn't waiting to be told. He never is. Words are weather to Frik — present, sometimes useful, mostly missed by the people making them — so he reads the room the way the rest of us read a sentence, and the room here was doing something specific. He watched a woman in her sixties kneel at the Horse's belly line, watched her run a flat palm along the chalk-packed trench, find a spot where grass had crept a thumb's width over the white, and not pull it. She tapped it. Two short taps with two fingers. A teenager three metres off looked up, came over, knelt, and pulled it — and the woman had already moved on.

Frik nudged me and signed it back: the tap, the look, the handover. *She doesn't do it. She shows where.* Then, after a beat, a gesture I didn't have a clean translation for — palm cupping something and passing it sideways. He did it twice. The second time I got it. *Handed down. Right now. In front of us.*

The autistic half of our pattern-spotting brain — call him Sol, everyone does — had gone quiet, which with Sol means loud. He was crouched at the Horse's eye, the strangest part of the figure, a separate ring of chalk that doesn't touch the head, and he was counting. Counting what, he wouldn't say yet. His lips moved. His hand went out flat over the chalk and hovered, mapping the slope, the fall of the land, the way the eye sat on a swell of hill so it caught light a half-beat before the rest. He'd been on the OSL paper for three days. He could recite the trench depth, the date range, the soil-creep mechanics — a metre down so the chalk wouldn't migrate downhill, so the line wouldn't blur, engineering and not decoration. He had the *what*. He was hunting the *where exactly*, which is a different animal.

Bex got us in. Bex always gets us in. ADHD runs through her like current and she can't sit through a briefing to save her life, but drop her among strangers doing a thing with their hands and within twenty minutes she's holding the thing, asking the loud honest questions everyone else is too polite to ask, and being adopted. By eight she'd learned the mallet was for tamping fresh chalk into the trench, that you didn't want it crisp, that the trick was to break the chalk lumps with the smooth face and then leave it rough — *leave it rough* — because the Horse wasn't supposed to look new.

"Why not?" she asked the man teaching her, a farmer named Gethin with chalk to his elbows.

Gethin thought about it the way you think about why you tie your shoes the way you tie them. "Looks wrong if it's too sharp," he said. "Looks like a logo."

We had, of course, seen exactly that. We'd watched the consultancy's trial patch from the field two days back — Dr. Rowan Leigh's "demonstration of best-practice conservation," a panel of the Horse's hind leg re-laid by their team with graded chalk and a string line and a level, every edge ruled, the white so even it looked printed. From the valley floor it had stood out from the rest of the figure like a crowned tooth. Crisp. Correct. Dead. A potato of the highest funding, a thing that had every fact right and the whole point wrong, and the worst of it was that you couldn't argue Rowan was *wrong*, exactly. The chalk was good chalk. The depth was to spec. It was preservation, by any sane definition of the word. It just wasn't the Horse.

Here's the potato proper, and I want you to actually feel it. The "easy" story is: just keep chalking it. Anyone could do that. Except: leave this figure alone and it's gone inside twenty years — grass takes it, soil creeps, the line softens and then it's a pale smudge and then it's a field. So somebody has to come back. Fine. Get somebody to come back. Except the somebody has to know that the eye doesn't touch the head, and which patches to leave rough, and that you tap rather than pull, and that the smooth face of the mallet goes down, and how much to do and — crucially — how much *not* to do, because a hill is alive and a too-keen scouring strips it as surely as neglect lets it fade. None of that is written anywhere with any authority. Hughes wrote a whole book about the festival in 1859 and it's a glorious document and it does not tell you which side of Gethin's grandfather's mallet to use. It's in the hands. It's only in the hands.

*"So it's a recipe," said the fish, "passed down by people who never wrote it, for a cake nobody remembers the occasion of."*

By mid-morning they'd stopped watching us watch and started correcting us, which is how you know you're in. Sol got moved off the eye — too much hovering, not enough scraping — and put on the belly with a hand-rake, and the woman who'd tapped earlier, whose name turned out to be Phyllida, came and stood over him and didn't say a word for a long minute, then took the rake, did four strokes, gave it back. Sol matched her four strokes exactly. She watched, nodded once, left. He told me afterward, low and precise, that her stroke fell with the slope and his had been fighting it, and that he could feel the difference in his shoulder but not see it, and that this annoyed him profoundly and he was going to think about it for some time.

Somewhere up near the head, someone started singing.

It wasn't a performance. It came up out of the work the way steam comes off a horse, two voices then four, an old tune with a lot of repeats in it and words half of them clearly didn't have all of, filling gaps with *la* and the line that did land, which was something about the horse and the hill and not letting go. Frik felt it before he could have heard a note of it — felt it in the way the bodies on the slope changed, the way the mallets found the beat, the tamping falling into the song's pulse without anybody deciding. He looked at me and put his hand flat on his own chest and then opened it toward the slope. *They're keeping time to it.* Then the cupped-palm sign again. *Handed down.*

The Lens hadn't shot a frame.

This is worth saying because the Lens always shoots. The Lens came to us out of three wars and a folder of pictures most of us will never see, and the camera is the wall between the Lens and the rooms the Lens has been in, and we have spent two books quietly asking the Lens to lower it. And here, on the chalk, in the singing, the camera was down. Hanging. The Lens was on knees with both hands in a trench, packing chalk, getting it under the fingernails, and there was an expression there I'd seen maybe twice — once at a kitchen table in the first book and once I won't write about — the expression of someone who has remembered, for an hour, that hands are also for this. I caught Edie watching the Lens watch nothing, and Edie's face did something it hadn't done in the four days we'd known her. It softened.

She came down the slope to us at the noon break, when the flasks came out and the foil came off the sandwiches and the whole hill sat down on its haunches in the sun. She sat by me with a mug and looked out over the valley, where the figure ran white under us, illegible from this angle, a stroke of nothing.

"You didn't fix the rough patches," she said. "I watched you. Bex left them."

"Bex was told to," I said.

"People get told to and fix them anyway," Edie said. "Can't help themselves. Want it nice." She drank. "You lot leave things alone. That's rarer than you'd think."

I asked her — because I'd been holding the question for four days and the chalk had earned me an inch of it — whether she knew what the Horse was for. The original thing. The why.

She didn't answer for a while. Down the slope the song had stopped and the eating had its own sound.

"My nan didn't know," Edie said finally. "Her nan didn't know. We've a long way back of not knowing." She turned the mug in her hands. "People come up here wanting me to *tell* them, and they think I'm being cagey. I'm not being cagey. There's a thing I can tell you and a thing I can't, and the thing I can't isn't a secret, it's just — gone. Burned off. Long before any of us." She looked at me sidelong, dry, the teacher in her surfacing. "What I can tell you is what it's *for now*. But you've not earned that yet. You've earned about half a morning of mallet."

*"She's better at this than you are," the fish observed, delighted. "She's holding the answer and making you scour for it. That's the whole gag, isn't it."*

And here's the hole, which I'll set down cold and leave open, the way the work taught me to leave the rough patches. Every part of the official story is documented. The date, the depth, the disappearance-in-twenty-years, the festival in Hughes, the turf over it in the war. All of it stands up. The single thing that does not stand up to documentation — that *cannot*, by its nature — is the chain itself. The unbroken handing-down. We say "essentially unbroken" because the figure survived and the OSL date is what it is, and a figure that vanishes in twenty years untended and is here after three thousand years was tended, again and again, by people who learned it from people who learned it from people. The survival is the proof. But there is no register. No ledger of every scouring back to the Bronze Age. The thing is true and unprovable at once, which is a deeply uncomfortable place for a fact to live, and it lives there, on the flank of the hill, in Phyllida's four strokes and the half-remembered song and the way Gethin couldn't say why not-too-sharp except that it'd look like a logo.

How does a *doing* survive when the *why* is dead? Not the story. The hands. How do you transmit four strokes that fall with the slope, across plague and empire and a war that buried the whole figure, when nobody ever wrote them down?

I'm not going to answer that. I don't think Edie was either, not fully, not yet. That's the wink, and you can sit in it as long as you like.

We were sitting in it when the sound came.

A small mechanical whine, rising, the sort of noise that doesn't belong on a hill. Bex heard it first — Bex hears everything, all at once, always — and her head went up. Then Sol's. The hill went quiet the way a field of sheep goes quiet, every face tilting.

The drone came over the shoulder of Uffington Castle and dropped to fifty feet, then forty, a four-rotor thing with a gimbaled camera underslung, and it began to fly the Horse. Methodically. Nose to tail along the back, then out along the legs, holding altitude, mapping. Reading the lines from the one angle the Bronze Age builders made them for, the eye they never had — the eye from above — and taking it, frame by frame, for a model on a hard drive in a consultancy's office, where the Horse could be made correct, and ruled, and kept forever exactly as it would never look on the living hill.

Up the slope, Phyllida set down her mug. Gethin stood. The song did not start again.

Edie watched the drone make its slow legible pass over the figure she could not, from here, see whole, and she said, very quietly, not to me:

"They're reading it from the wrong height."

Then she put down her mug and stood, and the whole hill stood with her.
