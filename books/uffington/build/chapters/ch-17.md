# Chapter 17 — What Is It For?

Dawn came up grey and then gold, and the Horse lay finished on the hill.

Tarryn felt it before she saw it — the way the work-noise had drained out of the morning and left only sheep and wind and the small clicks of people setting down their tools. She'd been moving for eighteen hours, fetching, hauling, packing chalk into the eye with the heel of her hand because someone said the eye always wanted doing last, and now there was nothing to fetch. Her legs didn't know it yet. They kept wanting to go somewhere. She stood at the top of the beak-shaped head and made herself be still, which was the hardest thing she'd done all night and she'd carried a barrow up a forty-degree slope in the dark.

Below her the lines ran white into the green. Not crisp. That was the thing she kept coming back to. You'd think after a whole community knelt on a hillside for two days the result would be sharp, and it wasn't — it was soft at the edges, a little uncertain, the long sweep of the back dipping where the turf rolled and the legs splayed in that way that looked wrong from up here and right from down there. From up here you couldn't read it at all. You had to know it was a horse. You had to take it on faith and the word of everyone who'd ever stood in the valley.

The consultancy's drone was packed away in its foam case in a van in the car park. She'd watched Rowan Leigh carry it down herself, last night, before the rain, and Rowan had stopped halfway and looked back up at the hill with an expression Tarryn hadn't been able to name and still couldn't.

"Hey," she said, to no one, to Frik, who was beside her. "Hey, it's done."

Frik wasn't watching the Horse. Frik was watching the people.

—

The official story, if you wanted it plain, was on a laminated board at the bottom of the hill, and it was a good board, and most of it was true. A stylised chalk figure, around a hundred and ten metres long, cut into the upper slope of White Horse Hill below the Iron Age ramparts of Uffington Castle. Dated by Optically Stimulated Luminescence — buried quartz, the last time the grains saw daylight, measured grain by grain by Palmer and Miles out of the Oxford Archaeological Unit after the dig that ran on from 1990 — to somewhere between roughly 1380 and 550 BC. Late Bronze Age into the earliest Iron Age. Which killed the old story about King Alfred cutting it to mark a battle, and killed the Saxon story too, and replaced both with a number so large it stopped meaning anything: three thousand years, give or take.

And it wasn't painted on. That was the part the board got across and most people skipped over on their way to the view. The makers had dug trenches a metre deep, near enough, and packed them with crushed white chalk, so the figure isn't a stain on the hill but a structure inside it — deep enough that the soil doesn't creep down and swallow it in a single wet winter. Deep enough to need digging out, every so often, by people who go down on their knees and lift the turf back and pound fresh chalk into the trench with a tool that looks like nothing so much as a flat-ended stick.

Every seven years, give or take. Sometimes ten. For three thousand years.

Skeptics will tell you, fairly, that the unbroken-chain part is inference. Nobody has a register. There's no continuous parish minute book running back to the Bronze Age, and you can't have one, and anyone who tells you the scouring has never once lapsed is selling something. What you can say is narrower and stranger: the Horse is still here. Leave a chalk figure on a living hillside and the grass takes it inside twenty years. There are figures on other hills that prove it — vanished ones, the white going green, a shape you can only find now in old aerial photographs. The Uffington Horse did not vanish. So somebody, in every generation, in every one, through plague and enclosure and two world wars, agreed to come up the hill and keep it.

That's the official story, played straight, and it's marvellous, and it has a hole in it the size of the hill.

Because the official story can tell you *who* and *how* and very nearly *when*. It cannot tell you *why*. By the Middle Ages the locals had already lost it — they were calling the thing a dragon by then, telling each other it galloped over to Wayland's Smithy in the dark. The meaning had drained out centuries before anyone thought to write it down. The act stayed. The reason left. And nobody, kneeling on the chalk with a flat stick at four in the morning, can tell you what the first knees were *for*.

—

Owen had stopped pacing, which Tarryn registered as a kind of alarm. Owen never stopped pacing. He'd spent the festival walking the line of the figure end to end, counting, muttering, working out by eye where last year's scour ran shallow and this year's would need to bite. He'd found the join, he said. The place where the chalk changed colour because it had been packed at a different time. He could read the hill in stripes nobody else saw, the way he read everything, the timeline laid out flat and legible while the rest of them lived inside the muddle of it.

Now he was sitting on the rampart with his knees up and his hands quiet and he was looking at the same soft, uncertain lines Tarryn was looking at, and he said, not to her, "It doesn't make sense from up here."

"No," she said.

"It's not supposed to. You build it to be seen from down there. From the valley. From the air, except there wasn't any air." He turned his head. "So they built a thing they could never see finished. None of them. The whole time they were making it they were working on a shape that was only ever right somewhere they weren't standing."

The LENS was a little apart from them, camera down. The camera had been down most of the night, which the crew had stopped remarking on and started understanding. There was a moment, near three, when the rain had come sideways and forty people had got down in the mud to shield the chalk with their backs, and the LENS had lifted the camera, framed it, and lowered it again, and just watched. Tarryn had seen that. She filed it where she filed the things she didn't have words for yet.

Edie Vane came up the slope toward them, last, the way she'd been first all weekend. There was chalk in the creases of her hands that wouldn't wash out till Tuesday. She'd taught half the people on this hill, in a classroom in the village, and she'd scoured this Horse since she was small enough to be lifted over the trench, and her mother had, and her mother's, back as far as the family went, which was the only chain anyone could honestly vouch for and it was long enough.

She stood with them and looked down at her Horse.

—

Frik watched Edie's face arrive at the view, and what Frik saw was not what a hearing person would have heard. Frik saw the small loosening at the jaw that wasn't relief. Saw the way Edie's eyes didn't travel along the lines as a stranger's would, tracing the famous shape, but went straight to three or four particular places — a hock, the curve under the tail, the gap in the rump where the chalk had thinned — and rested on each in turn, the way you'd check the faces of your children one by one without thinking you were doing it.

Frik signed to Tarryn, low and quick: *She's not looking at the Horse. She's looking at the work.*

Tarryn nodded. She turned to Edie, and she did the thing she was good at, the thing that had got them through doors in three countries, which was to ask the question that was actually a gift — to hand someone the chance to say the thing they'd been carrying.

"Can I ask you something," Tarryn said. "And it's the obvious one, so you've probably had it a thousand times, and you can tell me to get lost."

"Go on," said Edie.

"What's it for? Not — not what was it for, three thousand years ago, nobody knows that, I get it. I mean now. This." She opened her hands at the slope, the discarded barrows, the kids asleep against the rampart wrapped in a borrowed coat, the steam coming off the tea urn. "All this. You can't be doing it because of the dragon. You don't think it gallops to Wayland's. So what's the scouring *for*?"

Edie didn't answer straight away, and the not-answering was not a stall. Frik watched her decide whether to give it. That was the part they'd all come slowly to understand, over the weekend, the part the consultancy never would: it was hers to give or to keep. The meaning of the act lived in the people who did the act, and you could film the chalk all you liked and date the quartz to the grain and you would still not have the thing, because the thing was not in the ground. The thing was in whether Edie chose to tell you.

She crouched. Picked up a fist-sized lump of unpacked chalk from beside the trench and turned it over in her chalk-creased hand.

"You're asking it like there's an answer that's behind us," she said. "Everyone does. They come up and they want me to tell them what the Bronze Age folk meant by it. A god, a tribe, a marker, a map of the stars. I haven't got that. Nobody's got that. It went a long time ago." She looked at the lump in her palm. "But that's not what we're doing when we're on our knees. We're not remembering. You can't remember a thing you never knew."

Owen had gone very still in the particular way he went still when a pattern resolved.

"My gran used to say it different ways," Edie went on. "But it came to the same. She said the Horse isn't ours. We don't own it, we're not minding it for the council or for the history. We're minding it for the ones who'll do it next. She did it so I could do it. Her gran did it so she could. Not so we'd *know* anything. So there'd be a Horse for us to come up to." She set the chalk down, gently, in the trench, and pressed it level with her thumb. "It's not pointed backward at all. People always think it is, because it's old. It's pointed forward. Every time we scour it we're handing it on. That's the whole of it. The why went, and the handing-on didn't, and the handing-on turned out to be the why."

Nobody said anything. The wind came over the rampart and moved the long grass and didn't touch the white lines, which were below the wind, cut into the hill.

"The consultancy lot," Edie said, and there was no heat in it, "they wanted to fix it. Make it sharp. Make it right. Make a thing you could photograph and put on a tin." She straightened up, knees cracking. "But a right one would only need scouring once. Get it perfect, seal it, done. And then there'd be no next time. No reason for the children to come up. You'd have saved the Horse and killed the thing the Horse is *for*." She looked at the soft, wrong, perfect lines below. "It has to be a bit unfinished. So there's something left to do."

—

Tarryn had a notebook out. She'd had it out for the whole festival, full of arrows and underlinings, and she looked down at it now and found she didn't want to write any of this in it, and didn't know why not, and then did know.

Because writing it down was a way of taking it. Putting it in the file. Making it theirs, the clever thing the visiting crew found out on the hill, the answer they cracked. She closed the notebook.

Owen had got it before her, of course — he got the shape of things before the rest of them got the feeling. "We were going to put it in the cut," he said quietly, to her, not to Edie. "The 'what is it for.' Like every other time. Like it was a key we'd turned." He shook his head. "It's not a thing you find. It's a thing she does. We just got to watch her do it."

The LENS lifted the camera then — finally, once — and framed not the Horse but Edie's hands, the chalk in the lines of them, resting on the lip of the trench. Took the one photograph. Lowered it again.

Frik signed, slow now, for all of them, and Tarryn read it and her throat went tight in a way she'd have to deal with later.

*It was never ours to give. We only came to see if she would.*

Below them, in the valley where the lines made sense, the first car of the morning came along the lane, and a child somewhere down the slope woke up under the borrowed coat and asked, in the flat clear carry of a small voice on a still hill, whether they could come and do it again next time.

Edie smiled and didn't answer, which was its own kind of answer, and bent back to the chalk.
