# Chapter 3 — Potato on the Chalk

Tomas got to the gate first, because Tomas always got everywhere first, then forgot why he'd come.

The morning had that Berkshire trick of being four weathers at once — sun on the valley, a slate-coloured argument piling up over the Vale, and on the hill itself a wind that came at you sideways and smelled of sheep and bruised grass. Below, the Horse ran. You couldn't see it from up here, not properly. That was the whole joke of the thing. You stood right on top of one of the most famous shapes in Britain and saw nothing but white scribbles and turf, the way a beetle on a billboard sees only paint. You had to go down into the valley, or up into the air, to read it. Tomas found that personally insulting and deeply correct, both, and bounced a little on his heels thinking about it while the others caught up.

The consultancy had a marquee. Of course they had a marquee.

It was white, and pegged tight, and it had a banner along the side that said HERITAGE FUTURES in a serif so tasteful it practically apologised for existing. Underneath, in smaller letters: *Restoring the Past for Tomorrow.* Tomas read it twice and felt the *Restoring the Past for Tomorrow* do a small ugly thing in his chest he couldn't name yet, so he filed it under LATER and went to find the coffee, because there was always coffee at a thing with a marquee.

There was. It was good coffee. He hated that it was good coffee.

"They've done the maths," said Aïsha, beside him, not looking up from the ground. She'd been reading the slope since they parked — the long unhurried scan she did, the one that made strangers think she was rude and made Tomas think she was the only person actually present. "Drone survey. They've gridded it. There's a stake every two metres down the saddle of the neck." She nodded at a line of orange flags fluttering in the wind, neat as a runway. "Photogrammetry. They've got a model of the figure to the centimetre."

"To the centimetre," said Tomas. "That's good. That's thorough. I respect that." He meant it. He did respect it. He respected a centimetre with his whole heart and also wanted, suddenly and badly, to pull every one of those orange flags out and replant them in a rude shape.

He did not. Frik was watching him, and Frik knew his hands before he did.

*Calm,* Frik signed, flat-palmed, dry as ever. Then, because Frik missed nothing and forgave less, he tipped his chin at the marquee, at the woman stepping out of it, and signed: *Watch her hands, not her mouth.*

Dr. Rowan Leigh had good hands. That was the first thing — she used them like a conductor, open, including, drawing you in. She had a clipboard she never looked at and a high-vis vest she wore like a cardigan, and she crossed the wet grass toward the gaggle of locals and council faces with the easy spring of someone who genuinely could not wait to show you the thing she loved.

"So." She didn't shout. She let the wind do the work, let people lean in. "Three thousand years. Give or take. OSL puts the cut between thirteen-eighty and five-fifty BC — Bronze Age into the earliest Iron Age. Late Bronze, almost certainly. People stood here" — she pressed two fingers to the turf, light, reverent — "and they dug trenches a metre deep, and they packed them with crushed chalk, and they made a creature you can only see by leaving the ground. That's not graffiti. That's not a doodle. That's *intent.* That's engineering."

Tomas glanced at Aïsha. Aïsha gave the smallest nod. It was all true. Every word of it, gold-standard, dig it up and cite it. That was the thing about Rowan Leigh that made Tomas's teeth ache — she was *right.*

"And it should have died," Leigh went on. "By every rule of erosion it should have gone. Leave a chalk figure on a living hillside, untended, and you have maybe twenty years before the grass takes it. Twenty. Wessex is full of lost ones — figures we know existed, named in deeds, gone now, green now, nothing." She let that sit. "This one didn't go. Because people came back. Generation after generation, they came back and they scoured it — cut the turf, re-packed the chalk, kept the shape. And they did it" — and here she smiled, and it was a real smile, a good one — "almost certainly without ever knowing what it *meant.* By the Middle Ages they thought it was a dragon. They'd lost the why and kept the what. Isn't that extraordinary?"

It was. It was completely extraordinary. Tomas wanted to applaud and also to lie down on the grass and weep, and he did neither, which his old support worker would have called *progress.*

"What we want to do," said Leigh, gentler now, "is help. Not change it. *Honour* it. We have the model. We can true the lines — restore the proportions to the original cut, the one before three thousand years of slightly-wobbly hands moved a hoof here, a tail there. We can give people the Horse the Bronze Age made. The real one." She spread the good hands. "And then we keep it that way. Properly. Surveyed. Documented. So it never drifts again."

There was a silence. Into it, from the back of the gathering, somebody said, "Drifts."

Edie Vane had a voice that carried without trying, the schoolteacher in it, the bottom note of someone who'd ended a thousand arguments by simply waiting. She had a thermos in one hand and a look on her face Tomas was learning to read — not anger. Worse than anger. Patience.

"It's *meant* to drift," Edie said.

Leigh turned, all warmth. "Mrs. Vane—"

"It moves a hoof here, a tail there, you said." Edie unscrewed the thermos with the calm of a woman who had unscrewed it a thousand times in worse wind. "Yes. It does. Every scouring it moves a little, because it's cut by hands, and hands aren't drones. My grandmother cut that off hind leg. You can still see her in it, if you know where to look. You'd true that out of it, would you. Make it not-her. The real one." She poured. The steam went sideways instantly. "Whose real one."

"The original—"

"You don't have the original. You have a survey of where the chalk is *now.* The original's three thousand years of hands ago." Edie took a sip. Didn't even look ruffled. "But you crack on. Show us your trial."

And — give Leigh this — she did not flinch. She nodded, accepted it, even seemed to find Edie wonderful, which made Tomas like her against his will. "That's exactly why we do a trial," she said. "Small. Reversible. The lower flank, the belly line, a section nobody disputes. We try it. You look. We talk. Nobody owns anything today."

*Nobody owns anything today,* Frik signed to Tomas, deadpan, and the little fish-flick of irony in it nearly undid him.

---

They went down to the belly.

This was the part Tomas had been waiting for, the part his whole jittery nervous system had been leaning toward all morning — *the doing.* Because here was the thing the official story got so beautifully, dead-straight right, and here was the thing it could never quite make you *feel:* this shape is not paint. It is a wound in a hillside kept open on purpose. The white isn't drawn on. It's chalk packed into a trench a metre deep, the soil held back by sheer engineering so the grass can't creep in sideways and close the cut. You don't colour the Horse. You *dig* the Horse. You re-open it. Every time.

The consultancy did not dig.

They had a system. There were kneeling-mats, the foam kind, edged in hazard yellow. There were buckets of pre-mixed chalk slurry, factory-bright, a white so even it looked like dentist. There were trowels with rubberised grips and laser levels on little tripods and a man with a tablet who walked the flagged line calling out numbers — "Two-twenty, good, two-twenty-two, bring it left" — and two more on their knees laying the slurry into the cut with the smooth back-and-forth of people tiling a bathroom.

It was fast. Tomas would say that for it forever: it was *fast.* In the time it took the scourers to argue about lunch, the consultancy laid four metres of belly line straight as a die.

And straight as a die was the problem.

He felt it before he could say it — that was always how it went with him, the body knew first and the words came panting along after, late, breathless, holding their side. Something in the slurry line was *wrong* in a way that had nothing to do with archaeology and everything to do with the back of his neck. He moved to the valley side to see it from below, because that was the only place the Horse was ever honest, and the others drifted after him.

From down-slope, the new section glared.

The old belly line — the scoured line, the ancestral line, the line that had been a thousand grandmothers — was white, yes, but it was *alive* white. It had texture. The chalk in it caught the changing light and broke it up, so the line breathed, went grey in cloud-shadow, blazed when the sun broke, and the edge of it was not an edge so much as a negotiation, a fray where chalk met turf and neither quite won. It looked like a thing growing out of the hill. It looked, Tomas thought, like the hill had decided to be a horse and was still deciding.

The new four metres looked like a sticker.

It was too white. Too even. The edge was a *line,* ruled and final, the slurry filling the cut flush and smooth so there was no fray, no breath, no argument — just a clean bright stroke laid *on* the hill, refusing it. Where the old line was a galloping animal, the new bit was a diagram of one. It had the proportions right. Leigh's centimetres were perfect. And it had killed the thing dead.

Aïsha said it first, quiet, the way she said true things. "It's the wrong direction."

"What?"

"The light." She had her head tilted, doing the scan. "The old chalk's broken up — crushed lump packed loose, full of facets, faces every way. Scatters the light, so the line changes with the sky. The slurry's a smooth fill. Flat. One angle. It only does *one* light." She pointed, not at the new bit, at the old. "Theirs is right for one moment. Noon, sun out, flat on. The rest of the time it's wrong, and there are a lot of rest of the times on a hill." A beat. "It's a horse for a photograph."

*A horse for a photograph,* Frik signed, and Tomas watched him sign it to the LENS, who had been crouched ten feet back the whole time, low, patient, the camera not even up, *waiting,* and who now lifted it — once, a single frame, the new line and an old scourer's weathered hand resting on the live chalk beside it — and lowered it again. Tomas didn't see the shot. He didn't need to. He could feel that it was the one.

A schoolboy of maybe nine, dragged along by a scouring grandfather, summed up the entire commissioned report a year before it was written. He looked at the new four metres, and he looked at the rest of the Horse, and he said, with the brutal clarity of the very young, "It looks like a potato."

It did. It looked exactly like a potato. The whole gathered crowd, council and consultancy and scourers and crew, all of them looked at the four perfect, surveyed, centimetre-true metres of belly, and all of them, council included, saw a potato. You couldn't unsee it. The flat dead white, the ruled edge, the *correctness* of it sitting in the middle of three thousand years of living wobble like a road sign nailed to a tree.

Leigh saw it too. To her enormous credit, Tomas thought, she saw it and didn't pretend not to. Her good hands had gone still. She stood looking at her own perfect work with the face of a person who has spent a great deal of money proving something they cannot yet name to themselves.

"It'll weather," she said. Not to the crowd. Mostly to herself. "It'll dull down. Match in. A season—"

"It won't," said Edie. Not unkind, now. Just final. She'd come down to the valley side without anyone seeing, the way she did, the way the LENS did, the two of them the only people on the hill who knew you didn't watch a thing by standing near it. "It'll go grey-green and patchy because it's the wrong chalk laid the wrong way, and in three years you'll be back here digging it out, and in seven we'll be back here doing what we've always done." She screwed the thermos shut. "You truthed the shape and starved the life. You can do that. It's a free country and you've a permit." She looked at Leigh, and there was something in it almost like sympathy, which was worse than anything. "But you've not understood the one thing, love, and I'll only say it the once. We don't keep the Horse because it's right."

She left that there, on the wind, and started back up the slope to where the real chalk was waiting.

Tomas watched her go. He watched the schoolboy crouch by the old line and put one finger in the live, broken white, and the grandfather not stop him. He watched the LENS lower the camera and just *look,* for once, with his own two eyes. He watched Leigh stand over her potato in the changing light.

*We don't keep it because it's right,* Frik signed, slow, turning the shape of it over, the way he did with something he meant to carry a long way. *Then because of what?*

Nobody answered. Up the hill, somebody had started handing out the proper tools — the heavy ones, the old ones, the ones with no rubber grips. Tomas heard the first turf come up with a sound like the hill exhaling, and went to find out.
