# Chapter 4 — Reading the Ritual

The Fox and Hounds smelled of woodsmoke and wet wool, and Frik read it the way he read every room he walked into: from the door, fast, before anyone arranged their faces for him.

There was a lot to read.

The bar was a low-beamed space that had been a low-beamed space for four hundred years, and the men along it stood the way men stand in a place their grandfathers stood, with no need to look at the stools to find them. A fire that nobody tended kept itself going. Above the optics hung a horse — not the Horse, a brewery's brass plaque of a generic gallop — and nobody under that plaque had glanced at it in a decade.

Frik found a corner table where his back was to the wall and his eyes had the whole room. Old habit. You don't sit where you can't see hands.

Niamh dropped down beside him, kinetic as ever, already half a pint in and three conversations deep. She signed against the table edge where he could catch it without looking up: *Won the dominoes. Lost it on purpose at the end. They like you better if you lose the last one.*

He smiled. She was good at this — the ADHD made her a current, and currents find the cracks. People let her in because she moved too fast to be a threat and too warm to be a spy.

But the room wasn't in the dominoes.

The room was at the long table by the window, where six or seven of them sat with their elbows in beer-rings and their tools on the wood.

---

Here is the official story, and it is a good one, and it is true.

For roughly three thousand years, the people of this valley have walked up White Horse Hill and put the Horse back into the ground. They call it scouring. The figure is cut not as a painted line but as a trench — a metre deep in places — packed with crushed chalk, and chalk is not paint; chalk weathers, greys, slumps, feeds the grass that wants to eat it. Left to itself, the whole 110-metre animal would be gone inside twenty years. A man born under a sharp white horse could, untended, die under a green hillside with nothing on it.

So every seven years or so, they go up and they re-cut and they re-pack and they beat the chalk down with the flat of the tools, and the Horse runs again. It is documented as a festival back to the Middle Ages — Thomas Hughes wrote a whole book about the 1857 do, the cheese-rolling and the backsword and the ale — and it is inferred, by the simple miraculous fact of the animal still being *there*, all the way back to the Bronze Age people who first dug it. OSL dating of the buried quartz puts the cutting somewhere between 1380 and 550 BC. Older than Rome reaching Britain. Older than the word for the language Frik's hands were not speaking.

That's the story. Skeptics will tell you, correctly, that the folklore is patchwork — by the medieval period the locals had already forgotten it was a horse and decided it was a dragon, slain on the flat-topped hill below — and that the maintenance is "just tradition," the way you'd say a thing is *just* a heartbeat.

Hold that *just*. We'll come back for it.

---

The tools on the table were the read.

Frik leaned forward, very still, the stillness that lets the eye do the moving. He'd learned long ago that hearing people perform listening — they nod, they make the small sounds, they aim their faces. It's noise on the signal. He had no signal to lose. So he watched the hands, and hands don't lie the way mouths do.

There were rakes, but not garden rakes. The heads were narrow, the tines short and blunt and close-set, worn bright at the working edge and black with age up the shaft. There were wooden mallets the size of a forearm, the faces of them dished and silvered. There were trowels, and one long iron bar leaning in the corner like an old dog. None of it was new. None of it was from a shop.

And here was the thing Frik saw that nobody at the bar would have called a thing at all:

A young man — twenties, big-knuckled, nervous in the way of someone being looked at by people who matter — picked up one of the mallets to move it off the table so he could set down his glass. An older woman across from him didn't speak. She didn't even fully turn. Her hand came out flat, two inches above the wood, and *hovered* — not a grab, not a stop, just a held palm — and the young man put the mallet back down exactly where it had been, head pointing the way it had been pointing.

Then she nodded, once, and went back to her drink.

Frik felt the small electric thing in his chest he'd learned not to trust and not to ignore.

He signed to Niamh, low: *Did you see that?*

*See what?*

*The mallet. He moved it. She put it back without touching it. It has to point a certain way.*

Niamh looked. The table looked normal to her — six people, beer, old wood. *Maybe she's just particular.*

*No.* He didn't have the English for it. He rarely did; English came at him second-hand, through interpreters and captions and the strange flat country of lip-reading, and it never carried the part that mattered. The part that mattered was in the air above the table. *It's a place for the tool. Not a tidy place. A correct place. He knew he'd broken something and she knew and neither of them said.*

He watched the rest of the table catch up to it without being told. Within a minute every tool on the wood had its nose pointing the same way — downhill, he'd bet, toward the door, toward the valley where the Horse lived. He couldn't have proven it. You can't photograph a rule that exists only in the half-inch a hovering palm declines to cross.

---

The LENS came in from the cold, camera not raised, which for the LENS was its own kind of statement. The series kept asking that camera to come down and sometimes, lately, it did. The LENS clocked the corner, clocked Frik's stillness, and read the read off him the way Frik read the room — and did not lift the lens. Sat down instead. Some moments you take by not taking them.

Coll, the fourth of them, was at the bar's end with a pint going warm and a map of the parish unfolded across two beer towels, tracing the contour lines with one finger, lips moving over the numbers. The autistic hyperfocus had him by the back of the neck and wouldn't let go: he was matching the modern footpaths against an 1870s tithe map he'd photographed in the records office, and something in the overlay was lighting him up.

He signed across to Frik, fingers fast and precise: *The scouring road. There's a hollow way up the back of the hill that doesn't go anywhere now. Dead-ends at the slope. But it lines up with the cart track on the old map.* A pause, the careful one he did before he said the thing that mattered. *It's a road for bringing chalk up. It's only a road for that. Nothing else is at the top.*

A road that goes nowhere, except to feed the Horse.

Frik filed it next to the mallet.

---

Then the door's draught moved, and Edie Vane came in.

He felt the room re-organise. Not a hush — the bar didn't stop — but a settling, the way iron filings settle when the magnet's set down nearby. The woman who'd hovered her palm looked up and something passed between her and Edie that was older than greeting. Edie was carrying a canvas roll under one arm, the kind that holds tools, and she set it against the leg of the long table without comment, and it belonged there.

She was tired in the eyes — schoolteacher's term-time tired — and underneath the tired she was watching everything, which Frik respected, because it was what he did. She and the LENS recognised each other across the room like two snipers noting a third gun.

She came over to their corner. She knew sign — not fluent, but she'd learned some between Book One and now, which told him more about her than anything she could say. She kept her hands where he could see them and her face turned full to him, no half-profile, no shouting-at-the-deaf-man volume in the shape of her mouth.

"You've been watching the table," she signed-and-said, and her fingerspelling was slow and honest.

He didn't deny it. *The tools point downhill.*

Something in her face opened a centimetre. Not surprise. Recognition. The look of someone who has spent her life knowing a true thing nobody else bothers to see, and has just been told the true thing by a stranger.

"Most people," she said, slow, "see rakes."

*Most people hear words. I can't, so I watch what's underneath.* He shrugged. *The young one moved the mallet. Maggie put it back without touching it. He learned the rule by breaking it. Nobody taught him in a sentence.*

Edie was quiet a moment. Across the bar somebody laughed too loud at the dominoes.

"That's the whole of it, that," she said. "That's how it goes down. Not in a book." She glanced at the table, at the canvas roll, at Maggie. "My grandad never once told me how to hold the rake. He just stopped me holding it wrong. Hundred times, maybe. Then one day he didn't have to." A small, dry mouth. "Now I do it to the young ones and I couldn't write it down if you paid me. It's not in words. Words are the part that fell off."

*The meaning fell off.*

"Aye." She didn't flinch from it; she'd lived with it her whole life. "Nobody up this valley knows what it was *for*. Not really. The dragon's a tale somebody made up when the first lot of why-it-was got lost. We've forgotten so long we've forgotten we forgot." She looked at him steadily. "But the *doing* didn't fall off. You can lose the why. You can't lose the how, not if you keep doing it. The how lives in the hands."

Frik thought of the consultancy, two valleys over, with their drones and their digital model and their plan to "restore" the Horse to a *correct* shape — to fix it, to find the why and bolt it back on, to laminate the meaning. They wanted the words back. Edie was telling him the words were never the thing kept alive. The hands were.

---

Here's the potato, and it has teeth, so mind it.

It sounds like the easiest tradition in the world. *Just keep chalking it.* Walk up, dump chalk, go home. A child could do it.

So go and look at the other hill-figures. Half the white horses cut across these downs in the last three centuries — by squires, by jubilee committees, by enthusiastic Victorians who wanted a horse of their own — are gone now, grassed over, faint scars you'd miss from a moving car. They had money. They had Saturdays. They had photographs and committees and minutes of meetings. They had everything except the one thing Uffington has, which is a *thousand generations of hands that already know where the mallet points.*

Because it isn't dumping chalk. It's a metre-deep trench that wants to slump and a hillside that wants to creep and a chalk that wants to grey and a shape that only makes sense from a place you can't stand while you're cutting it. You're working blind on the animal's body, hauling chalk up a road that goes nowhere else, and the only way to know you've got the curve of the haunch right is that somebody beside you — somebody whose grandad stopped *them* a hundred times — hovers a palm over your work and you put it back.

Try doing that for twenty years with volunteers. People have. The horse goes green.

These people have done it, by inference, for three thousand. Through plague. Through enclosure. Through a war so total they *turfed the Horse over themselves* so the Luftwaffe couldn't navigate by it — buried their own animal to keep it safe, then dug it back out when the danger passed, as if the burying were just the longest, strangest scour of all.

That's the hole the *just* falls through. Not how do you cut a horse. How does a *how* survive when the *why* is three thousand years dead and the people carrying it can't put it in a sentence?

We're not going to answer that. Look at the palm hovering over the mallet and decide for yourself what's being passed down in that half-inch of air.

---

The light was going amber in the pub windows. Edie stood, and the canvas roll came up under her arm.

"We're going up," she said. "Walk the lines before dark. Mark where the worst of the slump is, so the crews know come Saturday." She looked round the four of them — Niamh half-up already, Coll folding his map with archival care, the LENS rising slow, Frik last and watching her hands. "You can come."

Niamh's face lit. She started to sign something about helping —

Edie's palm came up. Flat. Two inches off nothing. The exact gesture Maggie had made over the mallet.

"To *watch*," Edie said. Not unkind. Final. "Not to touch the chalk. Not yet." Her eyes found Frik's, because somehow she'd worked out he was the one who'd understand the size of what she was and wasn't offering. "You don't put a hand on the Horse till the Horse knows your hands. You walk it first. You watch how it's done. Then we'll see."

Frik signed: *Understood.*

And he did, more than the others, because he'd just spent an hour learning the whole grammar of this place was a held palm and a thing put back without a word — and he had no intention of being the man who moved the mallet.

They went out into the dusk, up the chalk road that went nowhere, toward the animal you cannot see while you stand on it. The grass had gone blue-grey. Below them the valley held its lights. And ahead, just over the brow, was the long white shape that no one alive could explain and no one alive would let die — waiting, as it had waited a hundred times before, for hands that knew where it pointed.

Edie walked ahead. She did not look back to see if they followed. She knew the road.
