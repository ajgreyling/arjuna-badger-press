# Chapter 19 — Chalk Dust and Footprints

The car park at the foot of White Horse Hill is a flat shelf of gravel scraped into the lower slope, and at half past six in the morning it holds three cars, a National Trust honesty box, and a wind that has come the whole way from somewhere with nothing to slow it down. The LENS stood by the open boot of the van with the camera up, and let the wind do what it wanted to her coat.

She had thirty-one frames left on the roll. She was rationing them like a woman who has learned, somewhere, that the last few are always the ones you wish you had back.

The Horse was up there. From here you could see the whole of it at once, which is the only place you can — the valley floor, or the air. From the hill above, standing right on top of the lines, you get a leg, a curve of chalk vanishing over the brow, a sense that you are inside something too big to read. You have to step back into the green of the valley before the animal assembles itself out of the white. Three thousand years of people climbing up to cut a shape they could not see from where they cut it. That had bothered her the whole week. It still bothered her. It was the kind of thing that did not stop being strange just because you understood the mechanism.

She found the Horse in the viewfinder. Last night's scouring had taken the grass-creep off the flanks and packed the trenches fresh, and the figure had that look it gets right after — not crisp, not the hard outline the consultancy's trial patch had worn for a week before the weather softened it. This was the other thing. The wild thing. The lines breathing.

*Click.* Thirty.

Behind her, the kinetic one — Tomas, who had not stopped moving in nine days and was not about to start — was repacking the kit in the wrong order and humming, and Saoirse, who had spent the festival with her sleeve up to the elbow in chalk and her notebook full of the angles of things, was crouched at the edge of the gravel scoring a line in the dust with one finger. Reproducing, the LENS thought, some geometry only she could see. Frik stood a little apart with his hands quiet at his sides, watching the hill the way he watched everything: completely.

Here is the official story, and it is a good one, and it survives every honest test you can throw at it.

A stylised horse, a hundred and ten metres of it, cut into the chalk of the upper slope below an Iron Age hillfort. Late Bronze Age, earliest Iron Age — somewhere between thirteen-eighty and five-fifty BC, pinned by optically stimulated luminescence on buried quartz, the work of Palmer and Miles and the Oxford unit after the dig in the early nineties. That date mattered. It put paid to the old stories — that it was Saxon, that King Alfred cut it to mark a battle. The hill is older than the kings who tried to claim it. The science is clean. The skeptics who say the dragon tales are medieval bolt-ons are right; they are. The folklorists who say the festival as we have it is a Victorian set-piece, Thomas Hughes and cheese-rolling and a cleaned-up pageant, are also right. None of it is fraud. None of it is alien. It is a very old shape kept very well.

The LENS lowered the camera and let her eyes do the work the lens had been doing.

*Try it.* That was the potato, and she had felt it in her own back. Take a shape on a living hillside — turf that grows, soil that creeps downslope under its own weight and the weight of rain, every winter trying to close the wound — and keep it sharp for a century. The Bronze Age answer was the trench: a metre deep, the line not painted on but cut into the hill and packed with crushed chalk, so the figure resists the creep, so the grass meets a wall when it tries to come back. Engineering, not decoration. But the trench buys you decades, not millennia. Leave it twenty years and it goes. Twenty years and the Horse is a green bruise in green grass and a story in somebody's grandmother's mouth.

So somebody comes back. Every seven years, ten at the outside, somebody climbs up with a bucket of chalk and a mell and gets down on their knees on the cold flank of the hill and does it again. For three thousand years. Through the Romans, through whoever the Romans were to the people already here. Through the plague years. Through a war so recent that Edie's own grandfather had been one of the men sent up to turf the Horse over, deliberately, so the German bombers could not use it to find their way — a hundred and ten metres of navigational gift you could not let the sky read. They put grass back over the chalk and let it sleep. And when it was done they came back and uncovered it and cut it sharp again, because of course they did.

That is the hole, and it does not close, and the LENS had spent the week trying to get it to close and failing.

Not *how* the Horse was made. We know how it was made. Not *what it means* — we have stopped pretending we know that, and the honesty of stopping is half the dignity of the place. The hole is the doing. How does an act — just the act, the climbing and the kneeling and the packing of the chalk — get handed from hand to hand for a hundred generations when the reason for it fell out of the chain somewhere back before anyone wrote anything down? By the Middle Ages they were already calling it a dragon. The meaning was already gone. And still they came. The memory of *why* failed and the *doing* did not.

She had no answer to that. The week had not given her one. She found she did not want one as badly as she had at the start.

The fish, if there is a fish, is the wind off the Ridgeway, and what it says is: *you came all this way to tell people you don't know why a hill is shaped like a horse. Marvellous. Could've stayed home and not known that.*

Fair. Take it on the chin.

*Click.* Twenty-nine.

Edie came down the chalk track on foot, the way she always came and went, with a thermos under one arm and yesterday's chalk dust still ghosting the creases of her knuckles. She had not changed her clothes. She had the look of a person who had slept three hours and would sleep three more and then go back up. She was not coming down to the car park to see them off, not really. She was coming down to fill the thermos and go back to the scourers, who were already up there, who had been up there since first light, working the last of the mane where the storm had nearly taken it.

"You're away, then," she said. Not a question.

"We're away," said Tomas, and put out his hand, and Edie shook it, and the chalk transferred itself from her skin to his and he looked at it on his palm like it was something he'd been given.

Saoirse stood up from her line in the dust. She had been working out something all week about the sightlines — the way the hillfort and the barrow at Wayland's Smithy and the long pale snake of the Ridgeway sat in relation to the figure, the way the whole landscape seemed to be addressing itself, the angles too clean to be accident, the dates not all the same dates. She had a theory. She had three. She had, the LENS suspected, talked herself most of the way out into something the evidence would not carry, because that was what Saoirse did when a pattern got its hooks in — she ran with it past the place the ground stopped being solid, and you loved her for it and you watched the drop.

"You think it lines up with the Smithy," Edie said to her. Mild. "On the solstice. You were going to ask."

Saoirse's mouth opened.

"People have measured it," Edie said. "Some of it lines up with things. Some of it lines up with whatever you stand where to make it line up. The hill's old enough that it's caught a few thousand years of people deciding it points at what they needed it to point at." She unscrewed the thermos lid. "I'm not saying it doesn't. I'm saying the hill doesn't owe you a reason. It never has. That's not it being mysterious. That's it being a hill that some people keep."

Saoirse looked at her line in the dust. Then she scuffed it out with her boot, not unhappily. The LENS had seen her hold a wrong pattern for days. She had never seen her put one down that fast. It took someone who had carried the chalk to make her do it, and that was the difference, and the difference was the whole point and nobody was going to say it out loud.

Frik had come up beside Edie. He raised his hands — the small economical sign-shapes he used, that Tomas had got passable at over the week — and asked her something, and Edie watched his hands and watched his face and answered with her own face first, before her mouth caught up: a flicker, a softening at the eyes, the particular almost-smile of someone agreeing to a true thing.

She'd told them, days ago, what Frik had seen on the second night. That the scouring was wordless where it mattered. That the old hands did not instruct — they did the thing slightly slower, slightly nearer, so the young ones beside them could match it, hand following hand, the way you teach a body and not a mind. That nobody had ever said *here is why we do this*, because nobody knew, and the not-knowing had been handed down as faithfully as the knowing-how. Frik had read it off the shoulders of the scourers in an evening. It had taken the rest of them most of the week to believe him.

What Frik asked her now, the LENS could not follow. What Edie answered, she could not follow either. But she watched Edie's hand come up and rest flat against her own sternum, and then turn outward toward the hill — *here, then there* — and watched Frik nod, slow, and that she understood completely, in the way you understand a thing you cannot put back into words afterward without spoiling it.

She did not lift the camera. She wanted to. The whole instinct of twenty years of standing in rooms where terrible and tender things happened, the instinct that had kept her sane by putting glass between her and the world, came up her arms and into her hands and she let it pass through and out the ends of her fingers and did not move.

There are moments the camera takes and gives you nothing back. She knew them now. She was learning, slowly, late, which ones to leave in the air where they happened.

Edie capped the thermos. "Right," she said. "They'll be wanting this."

"Will it last?" Tomas asked. "What you did last night. The mane."

Edie looked up at the hill, where you could just see them now if you knew to look — small dark figures moving along the white of the back, the slow bowing-and-rising of people working chalk into a hill. "It'll last till it doesn't," she said. "Seven years, give or take. Then someone'll be cold and complaining about their knees and they'll do it again." She said it without weight, the way you'd say the kettle would boil. "My nan did it. Her nan. Back as far as there's a back. We don't know who started. We've stopped expecting to." She glanced at the LENS, and there was something dry and kind in it. "You wanted the why. You're not the first. The consultancy wanted the why so bad they were going to build us one. Concrete it in. Make the lines that crisp nobody'd ever have to wonder again." She shrugged. "Wonder's the only thing we've got that's as old as the trenches. Why'd I let them take that?"

She was already turning. That was the thing about Edie — she gave you the door and she did not stand in it. She had a hill to get back to. The crew were the telling. They were not the keeping. The keeping went on with her up the chalk track whether the van was here or not, whether the camera was here or not, whether anyone ever told anyone anything about it at all.

"Edie," the LENS said.

Edie stopped.

"Thank you for letting us up."

"You came up and got on your knees," Edie said. "That's not nothing. Most don't." She lifted the thermos in something like a salute. "Mind the road."

And she went, up the track, into the wind, the chalk dust on her hands going back to the hill it came from.

Jakobus had the van running. He'd been ready twenty minutes and had said nothing about it, leaning on the wing with his cap pulled down, reading the slope the way he read everything — not for who, not for why, but for *what it does*. He'd said it once, two nights ago, in the rain, when the storm had nearly carried the outline off and they had all worked till their hands stopped feeling like hands: that the Horse was not a picture and not a message and not a god. That it was a thing that held. It held the hill against its own creep. It held the eye in the valley. It held a date in the dirt the science could read. And it held the people to the doing of it, year on year, knees on chalk, hand following hand — and that was the whole of its function, and the function had outlived every meaning anyone had ever hung on it. He'd said it and then he'd gone back to packing chalk, because that was Jakobus, the road and never the lead, handing the moment back the second he'd named it.

He held the passenger door now and the LENS climbed up.

She had one frame left. Twenty-eight on the back, but the counter said one, the way it always lied at the end, generous and wrong.

She rolled the window down and put the camera to her eye one last time and found the Horse, the whole of it, gleaming where the early sun was coming up the valley and laying itself along the fresh-cut chalk. The little figures moving on the white back. The wind taking the long grass in waves below the line, all of it pouring downhill, all of it trying, patiently, the way it had tried for three thousand years, to close the wound and take the Horse back into the green.

Up there they were on their knees again, packing it white. Refusing.

She did not know why. Neither did they. Nobody had for centuries.

*Click.*

The van pulled out onto the lane, and behind them on the hill the act went on, unbroken, the chalk going back into the trench, the shape kept sharp against the grass for no reason anyone could name and every reason that mattered — and the question came down the valley with the wind and rode along beside the window the whole way to the main road and did not get out:

what else do we keep, long after we have forgotten why.
