# Chapter 14 — Jakobus Reads the Hill

The storm had spent itself somewhere over the Vale in the small hours, and now the cloud was peeling back off the Downs in long grey sheets, the way wet paper lifts off a window. Soren had not slept. He'd stopped pretending to around three, when the wind changed its note and the canvas over the chalk-stack stopped drumming and started to hum, and he'd come up the slope to the lip of the hillfort to watch the light arrive.

It arrived the way it always did up here, which is to say from below. That was the first thing the hill taught you, if you let it. The sun did not crest the ridge and pour down. It came up out of the Vale, off the flooded fields and the silver thread of the brook, and it lit the underside of everything first — the bellies of the sheep, the underslung jaw of the chalk where the turf had been peeled back, the white running line of the Horse catching it before the green did.

Soren counted, because counting was how he held a thing steady. Eleven scourers still on the slope. Three asleep in the lee of the rampart under a tarp the colour of a traffic cone. Edie at the head of the trench with a kneeling-mat and a bucket, not working, just looking. And below her, lower down the figure than anyone, a man crouched on his heels at the very tail of the beast where it dissolved into a flourish, doing nothing at all.

Jakobus did nothing the way other men did heavy labour. With attention.

Soren watched him because Soren watched everything, and because there was a pattern here he hadn't named yet, and an unnamed pattern was a stone in his shoe. He'd been watching the fixer all week — the way Jakobus arrived at a place sideways, the way he never once stood where the photograph wanted him, the way he asked the question that nobody else thought was a question. *What does it do.* Not what is it, not who made it, not what does it mean. What does it *do.*

So here was the official story, and Soren ran it through again because the running-through was a comfort, like a rosary for a man who counted instead of prayed.

Late Bronze Age. Earliest Iron Age. Optically stimulated luminescence on the buried quartz, the Oxford dig in the nineties, Palmer and Miles, a date that opened like a hand: **1380 to 550 BC.** Not Alfred. Not the Saxons. Older than the hillfort behind him by a margin that made the hillfort feel like a recent addition, a conservatory bolted onto a house. They had not painted a horse on a hill. They had *dug* it — trenches a full metre deep, the chalk crushed and packed in tight, and that depth was not decoration, that depth was engineering. A metre of packed chalk doesn't slump when the hillside breathes. The slope creeps; the figure holds. Whoever did this understood that a living hill is always trying, very slowly, to swallow what you put on it, and they built against the swallowing.

That was the part the consultancy loved, and Soren could see why. It was *clean.* It was a fact you could put on a board. Rowan Leigh had said it three nights running at the village hall, and she'd said it well, because she believed it: *we have the date, we have the engineering, we have a defensible original form, and we have an obligation to it.* She wasn't wrong about any single piece. That was the trouble with her. You couldn't fault the bricks. Only the house.

Because here was the potato, and the storm had served it up fresh.

Soren had spent the night watching what it took to keep a shape alive on a hill that wanted it dead. Not metaphor — labour. Wet chalk weighs. Wet chalk slides off a shovel and slumps off a bank and you pack it with your hands, with the heel of your palm, and the rain finds the new-packed line and tries to take it back the moment you turn away. Leave this hill alone and the grass has the Horse inside twenty years. Twenty. Not three thousand — twenty. A man could plant it and live to see it gone. The whole monument was a bet against entropy that had to be re-placed, by living hands, again and again and again, a hundred times over, with no winnings except the right to make the bet once more.

And nobody up here, not one of the eleven, could tell you why.

That was the hole, and Soren turned it over and over because it would not lie flat. The *meaning* was gone. Long gone. Gone so thoroughly that by the Middle Ages they'd looked at the running line and called it a dragon, set it galloping to Wayland's Smithy at night, hung a wrong story on it the way you hang a coat on a peg in the dark and never notice it's not yours. The why had drained out of the thing centuries ago. And the doing had not so much as flickered.

How. That was all. Just — how. How does the *act* outlive the *reason*? How does a community hand down, unbroken, across empire and plague and a war so recent that men still living remembered turfing the Horse over so the bombers couldn't steer by it — hand down not a story, not a faith, not a meaning, but a *chore.* A maintenance schedule. Every seven years or so, somebody has to crush the chalk and pack the trench and keep the dragon from being eaten. And every seven years, for as long as there has been anyone to do it, somebody has.

Soren didn't have an answer. He was honest enough to know that the absence of an answer was the whole point, and disciplined enough not to fill it. The fringe filled it — that was what the fringe was *for,* filling holes with the nearest bright thing. He kept his hands off it. He just watched the man at the tail.

Jakobus had moved. Not up the slope, where the work was, but down — to the lowest curve of the figure, the place where the design stopped making sense if you stood inside it. He laid his palm flat on the packed chalk the way you'd lay a hand on the flank of a horse you didn't yet trust. Then he straightened, and walked out — not up, *out,* away from the hill and onto the apron of grass below the figure, twenty paces, forty, until he was standing where a man might have stood three thousand years ago to see if the thing he'd made looked right.

And Soren understood — felt the pattern click home with the small cold satisfaction of a tumbler falling — that this was the reading. This was Jakobus reading the made thing.

Because from the slope, from inside it, the Horse was nonsense. Soren had crawled the lines all week and from up close they were just trenches, abstract curves, a problem of chalk and gravity. You could not see the Horse from where you scoured the Horse. The makers had built a thing that could not be admired by the hands that made it. It was legible only from the valley floor, or from the air — from where nobody worked, from where you could only *receive* it. They had labour for the doers and meaning for the distant.

Jakobus stood at the receiving end and looked back up at the hill, and turned slowly, and looked at the Vale, and turned again, and looked at the long pale scar of the Ridgeway running off west toward the barrow, and Soren watched him doing the arithmetic that wasn't arithmetic, the thing the fixer did instead of theorising.

When Jakobus came back up the slope his boots were dark to the ankle and he stopped beside Soren without being asked and looked at the same Horse for a while, and said nothing, which was his way of inviting you to be the one to say it.

"You went down to the valley," Soren said.

"To where it's for." Jakobus had a voice like a gate that needed oil. "Built so you can't see it from the work. You ever build anything you couldn't see while you built it?"

Soren thought about it properly, because Jakobus asked questions you were meant to answer. "No."

"Hard." Jakobus said the word the way other men said *beautiful.* "Means they knew where it would be seen from before they cut a sod. Means somebody held the whole shape in his head while a dozen others dug blind." He nodded down the slope at the figure dissolving into its own flourish. "And it works *as a mark.* From the Vale. From the air. You're coming up the old road, the Ridgeway, you're coming for the Smithy or the fort or the water, and there's the Horse, and you know — without a word, without a soul to tell you — you know whose ground this is and that it's *kept.*"

"Kept," Soren said.

"That's the tell." Jakobus crouched, and pressed two fingers into the new-packed line, testing it the way you'd test bread. "A ruin says *was.* This says *is.* No ruin is this white. White means somebody was here last spring. The mark isn't the message. The *freshness* is the message. It says: there are still hands."

Soren felt the cold tumbler turn again, further this time. He had been so busy with the hole — how does the act survive the meaning — that he'd been treating the survival as the strange part. And Jakobus had walked down the hill and stood in the Vale and read the made thing the only honest way, from the function out, and the function was not *remembering.* The function had never been remembering. The function was *announcing the living.* The Horse didn't say *we were great.* It said *we are still here, and we still tend.* And a thing that says *we still tend* has to be re-tended to keep saying it, or it lapses into *we were here once,* which is a different and lonelier sentence.

The meaning could go. The meaning was always going to go — meanings are perishable, meanings are the soft tissue, the first thing the worms get. But the function didn't need the meaning. The function only needed hands. And so the hands kept coming, seven years on seven years, not to honour a story but because the story they were inside of *was the keeping,* and you cannot keep a thing by knowing why. You can only keep it by keeping it.

He didn't say any of that. Jakobus would have winced; the fixer hated a man who narrated his own footnotes. Soren just said, "It's a mark that has to be alive to mean anything."

"It's a mark that *is* the being-alive," Jakobus said, and stood, his knees telling the cold, and that was the closest he came to a flourish. "Not for me to say more. Ask her." He tipped his chin up the slope.

Edie had stopped looking at the Vale.

She was looking at them. At Soren, specifically — at the back of his head, the way you watch someone do a sum they don't know they're doing out loud. And when Soren turned and met her eye, something passed over her face that wasn't quite a smile, because Edie's affection ran too deep for anything so cheap as a smile at six in the morning after a night of wet chalk. It was recognition. The face you make when a stranger pronounces a word the way the locals do, and you realise they've stopped pretending and started belonging, a half-inch, just at the edge.

She'd seen it land. She'd been waiting all week to see if it would land in any of them, and she'd watched the consultancy fail to land it with all their drones and their date-boards and their defensible original form, and she'd watched the crew get closer than she expected and stall, and now she had seen the pattern-spotter go quiet next to the road-worn man and come up the other side of the silence holding the thing. Not holding *her* thing — she kept that. The naming was hers; she could feel them all knowing it was hers, and not reaching for it, and that was the half-inch.

Across the Vale the last of the storm-cloud tore free of the western ridge and the full light came up off the flood-water all at once, and the whole figure went incandescent — not white, *radiant,* throwing the morning back at the sky it had been cut to be seen from. The new-packed chalk of the night's work was indistinguishable now from the old. You could not tell which line was three thousand years and which was three hours. That was the joke and the glory of it; that was the fish, surfacing to heckle, *go on then, clever lad, which bit's ancient?* And the honest answer was: all of it, and none of it, and that question is a category error, and isn't it lovely that you can't tell.

Frik came up the slope toward them with chalk dust whitening the creases of his hands to the wrist, and he saw Soren's face, and he saw Jakobus, and he saw Edie watching, and he read the whole conversation that hadn't been spoken — read it off the set of three people's shoulders the way other men read a sentence — and he grinned, and made a sign Soren didn't know, a flat hand sweeping low across the hill and then opening upward, and didn't trouble to translate it.

The storm was gone east. The grass steamed. The Horse ran on the bright hill exactly as white as it had been the morning before the rain, which is to say exactly as white as it had been for three thousand wet, swallowing, hand-tended years, and the only people who knew what it was for were the dead, and the only people keeping it had long since stopped needing to know.

Edie picked up her bucket.

"Right," she said, to all of them and none of them, in the voice you'd use to a sheepdog or a kettle. "Tail's not done. It never is."

And she went back down to the chalk.
