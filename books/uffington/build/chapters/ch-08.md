# Chapter 8 — Scouring School

Six in the morning and the hill had its own weather. Down in the valley Uffington was still under mist, the church tower poking out like a thing waiting to be drawn, but up here on the white slope the sun had already come over the shoulder of the Down and laid the grass over in a long wet shine. Frik stood at the edge of the figure with his hands in his pockets and watched the light find the chalk.

You could not see the Horse from here. That was the first thing the books never quite made you feel. From the ridge above, where they'd parked, the figure was a senseless scatter of pale curves, a foot here, a beak-faced muzzle there, a stretched belly-line you could not connect to anything. You had to stand in the valley to see the animal run. Or, the books said, you had to fly. Three thousand years before anyone flew, somebody cut a shape into a hillside that only made sense from the air, and then the people who made it walked down to the valley to check their work and lived their whole lives never once seeing it whole the way a bird did.

Edie Vane came up the slope with a bucket in each hand and did not say good morning.

She set the buckets down by the Horse's eye — the eye was a separate ring of chalk, a hole within the head, which everyone found strange and nobody could explain — and she looked at the four of them lined up along the lip of the trench in their bright waterproofs, and her face did a thing Frik read instantly because reading faces was the whole of how he'd survived a hearing world: she was deciding whether this was worth her morning.

The signing interpreter, Mara, stood at Frik's shoulder and lifted her hands to render Edie. But Edie hadn't spoken. She was just looking. Mara waited.

Frik liked her for not talking. Most people, faced with the deaf one, performed. They over-mouthed, they pointed, they did the slow loud thing with their eyebrows up. Edie just looked at him as if she'd clocked exactly what he was — the one who watched — and had decided she'd watch back.

She crouched and put her bare hand flat on the chalk.

Then she looked at Frik and turned her palm up. *Come.*

He went and crouched beside her. The others stayed where they were, which was right; she'd called one.

The chalk under his hand was not what he'd expected. He'd expected something like pavement, packed and dead. This was cold and a little damp and faintly giving, a paste of crushed white stone gone slightly to mud at the surface, and under it, when he pressed, the hard. The trench ran down a full metre, the diggers in '90 had found, packed with chalk all the way, and that was the engineering that kept the shape from bleeding away under three thousand years of soil creeping downhill. A metre of white packed into a hill to hold a line. Nobody told the Bronze Age it would be easy.

Edie said something to Mara, who signed it: *Feel where it ends.*

Frik moved his hand sideways. The chalk went on, and on, and then — there. A lip. A softening. A place where the white slurred into green, where the grass had crept in over the season, a few centimetres of takeover. He could feel the edge of the Horse trying to dissolve.

He looked up.

Edie nodded. Then she did something with her own hand — laid it on the same edge, and with two fingers, gently, almost tenderly, she scraped the encroaching turf back, peeling green off white like skin off fruit, and underneath, the chalk was bright as a tooth.

*That's all it is,* her face said. *That's the whole secret.*

It was not, of course. That was the thing about the easy version. Just keep chalking it. The skeptics — and Frik had a real fondness for the skeptics, the men with clipboards who'd said for two hundred years that the Horse was nothing but maintenance and folklore and human stubbornness, no magic about it — the skeptics were *right*. There was no spell. There was no secret. A child could be shown what Edie had just shown him in eight seconds: scrape back the grass, pack new chalk, keep the line.

And the skeptics had never once managed to keep a hill-figure alive on their own.

---

The autistic one — Sanele, who'd been silent since the car, vibrating at a frequency the rest of them had learned to leave alone — had walked the entire outline already. Twice. He came back to them now and stood a little apart and said, to no one, to the air, in the flat clear way he had:

"Twenty years."

Edie turned.

"If you stopped," Sanele said. "If everyone here stopped, this year. The line holds maybe two seasons. Five years, the legs are guesswork. Twenty, it's a pale stain you'd need a drone to see. There are figures on the Downs that *did* stop. The Westbury horse had to be cemented. The cement one isn't alive. It's a photograph of a horse."

He wasn't being cruel. He was the most precise person Frik had ever met and precision in his mouth came out sounding like a verdict. Edie did not take it as one. She tilted her head at him the way she'd tilted it at Frik, the *I see what you are* tilt, and Frik watched something pass between the careful man and the chalk woman that did not need Mara at all.

"You've done your homework," Edie said. Mara signed it.

"It's not homework," Sanele said. "It's the landscape. It tells you if you look. The grass wants the hill back. The hill's been arguing with the grass for three thousand years and the only thing keeping the horse from losing is people coming up here with buckets."

Edie picked up one of the buckets. "Then let's argue with the grass."

---

She taught them the way you teach a thing that has no manual.

Not a lecture. There was almost nothing she would say in words, and Frik noticed — he always noticed — that the parts she *could* have put in words were the parts she put in her hands instead, because that was where they lived. She showed Thandi, the kinetic one, the ADHD one who could not hold still and had already befriended a man's dog three fields over, how to break the chalk. You didn't lay it on whole. You took the lumps and you crushed them, by hand or with the back of a mallet, into a coarse white grit, because crushed chalk packed and bonded and the rain wedded it; a slab just sat on the surface and slid off in the first storm. Thandi caught it in about four seconds and then made it better, found a rhythm, mallet-mallet-scoop, talking the whole time, and the chalk dust went up off her hands in a little ghost.

The LENS hung back with the camera, as the LENS always did, finding the edge of the frame and living there. Frik watched her watch. She'd been to places where the thing she pointed the lens at was about to die. He thought sometimes the camera was a way to stand at the edge of a thing without having to step into it. Edie clocked her too — clocked the not-stepping-in — and let her be. For now.

To Frik, Edie gave the edge.

She put him on the line itself, the boundary where white met green, and she did not tell him to be neat. He understood that within the first metre. The consultancy's trial patch, the one they'd rechalked with the laser-cut template and the bagged industrial chalk, the one the whole village had walked up to look at and then quietly walked away from — that patch was *neat*. Crisp as a logo. Frik had photographed it. It looked like the Horse had been replaced overnight by an insurance advert for the Horse.

Edie's line was not neat. She let the edge breathe. Where the old scourers before her had let a curve go slightly proud, she kept it proud. Where the muzzle ran thin she ran it thin. She was not restoring the Horse to a correct shape. She was renewing the shape that the *last* people had left, who had renewed the shape the people before *them* had left, and somewhere down at the bottom of that stack of hands was a hand from the Bronze Age, and nobody in the chain had ever drawn it from a plan because there was no plan, there was only the line you were handed and the line you handed on.

Frik scraped back a tongue of grass and packed white into the gap and smoothed it with his palm and felt, doing it, a thing he had no word for and didn't reach for one.

He looked up to find Edie already looking at him.

She'd seen it land. Of course she had. She'd been watching the room — this room, this hill, with its single ancient occupant — her entire life.

She made a sign. Not BSL; her own. A flat hand passed once over the chalk, slow, like calming an animal.

He copied it.

She nodded, and turned away, and the nod was worth more than anything in any of the books, because the nod meant *not wrong*.

---

By eight the mist had burned off the valley and you could see the whole green sweep down to the village and the Manger below, that strange dry combe the meltwater carved at the end of the last ice age, all of it soft and grazed and timeless-looking in the way that fooled people. None of it was timeless. Every inch of it was kept. The sheep kept the grass; the farmers kept the sheep; the scourers kept the Horse; and the Horse, Sanele had said somewhere in the early dark, the Horse kept the people coming back.

Frik sat back on his heels with chalk to the elbow and watched Edie inspect their morning's work. She walked the whole length of it. The eye, the ear, the long impossible muzzle that some said was a beak and the dragon-people said was the dragon's snout and the truth was nobody knew, nobody had known for so long that even the *misremembering* of it was medieval, the dragon story already old when Thomas Hughes wrote up the festival in 1859, the meaning gone so far back that the loss of the meaning was itself an antique.

That was the hole, and Frik felt it open under him the way he'd felt the metre of chalk go down under his palm.

Not how it was made. They knew how. Trenches, chalk, OSL dating, 1380 to 550 BC, the science clean and unsexy and certain. Not even *that* it was kept — the keeping was the most documented thing about it. The hole was the *transmission*. How does the doing pass down a hundred generations through plague and conquest and a world war when the army literally turfed the whole thing over so the Luftwaffe couldn't navigate by it — turfed it over, killed the line on purpose — and then, after, somebody came back up the hill and dug it out again? Why come back? The meaning was already gone by then by a thousand years. They didn't come back for the meaning.

He looked at his own white hands.

He didn't reach past it. Edie wouldn't have let him, and he didn't want to. The wonder wasn't in solving it. The wonder was that it was sitting right there, scrubbed clean as the chalk under the grass, and no one could say why the hands kept coming, only that they did, that here were four more pairs of them this morning, and a fifth behind a camera, and a sixth on the road somewhere bringing the van round.

---

Edie came back to where Frik sat. The others gathered without being called.

She crouched again, where she'd crouched at the start, and put her hand flat on the part Frik had done, and left it there a moment, reading it through her palm the way he'd read the edge through his.

Then she stood, and wiped her hand on her trousers, and looked at the four of them — and at the LENS, who lowered the camera a fraction, which was as far as the LENS ever came.

"Saturday," Edie said. Mara signed it. "The scouring."

She paused. Frik watched her choose the next words, and watched her decide not to dress them up.

"There'll be sixty, seventy people up here. Schoolkids. The society lot. My nan's friends, the ones who can still get up the slope. The consultancy will be down in the valley with their drone and their good coffee, telling the council how we're doing it wrong." Something that was almost a smile. "We've been doing it wrong, by their lights, for three thousand years."

She picked up a bucket and held it out — not to Frik, to all of them, an offer with a handle on it.

"You can stand at the edge with the camera and film the quaint old custom. Plenty have. Or" — and here she looked at Frik, and then at Sanele, and then at Thandi, and she let the look take its time — "you can carry chalk."

Mara's hands stilled.

For a second nobody moved. The grass hissed in the wind that always ran over the Down. Down in the valley a dog was barking — Thandi's new friend, probably. The Horse lay all around them, half-renewed, breathing white through the green, going nowhere, the way it had gone nowhere for longer than there'd been an England to go nowhere in.

Thandi reached out and took the bucket by the handle.

"Hands," she said. "Not cameras."

Edie didn't smile this time. She just let go of the bucket, and let it be theirs, and turned to look down the long pale shape of the thing she'd never been able to see whole, the way none of her family ever had, the way you'd have to leave the ground to manage — and she said, to the Horse more than to them:

"Then you'd best learn to crush chalk faster."
