# Chapter 1 — Arrival on the Hill

The thing about a hill that isn't much of a hill is that it sneaks up on you sideways.

Frik knew the geometry from the maps — they all did, the autistic one had narrated the contour lines from the back seat for forty minutes in a voice like a metronome, the Ridgeway crossing here, the hillfort ramparts there, the dry valley called the Manger folded in below — but maps lie about the body of a place. They tell you elevation and forget weather. They give you a figure carved at 110 metres long and forget that you can't see it. That was the first joke the hill played, and Frik felt it land in his chest before he understood it: they had driven all this way to look at the most famous chalk drawing in Britain, and from the car park you could not see the horse at all.

He got out into wind. The wind here was a presence, a hand laid flat against the side of the head, and being Deaf he took it as pressure rather than sound, felt it shove the car door and tug his jacket and comb the grass into long silver runs down the slope. The grass was the green of something that had never once been thirsty. Sheep stood in it like punctuation. And above them, where the slope steepened toward the crown of the down, the turf was scarred open in lines of raw white that, this close and this high, refused to add up to anything.

A leg. A long swimming neck, maybe. A staring round eye, divorced from any head.

Frik turned in a slow circle on the gravel and felt the others arranging themselves around him — he tracked people the way other people tracked sound, by where they put their weight. The LENS had already drifted ten feet off, camera not yet up, only watching, the way he always watched first, reading a place for its exits before he'd read it for its picture. The ADHD one — Devan — was bouncing toward a knot of people by a marquee half-erected at the field edge, drawn to the human warmth of them like a moth to a kettle. And the pattern-spotter, Wren, stood with her back to the horse and her face to the valley, because Wren had read the same thing Frik had: you don't look at the horse from the horse.

Frik tapped the LENS on the shoulder and signed, hands cutting the wind: *Where's the picture?* He pointed up the slope at the white scars. *Can't see it from here.*

The LENS made the small flat gesture they'd worked out between them over two books now — *wait* — and tipped his head down the hill, toward the Manger, toward the long green emptiness of the valley floor.

Right. You had to go away from it to see it. You had to stand in the valley, or be a bird, or be God. The people who cut this thing into the chalk somewhere between thirteen-eighty and five-fifty before Christ — and the dating was good, the dating was OSL, optically stimulated luminescence run on the quartz grains pulled up from under the chalk packing by the Oxford team, the kind of number that had quietly murdered the old story about King Alfred carving it after a battle — those people had made a creature that no one standing on its own back could ever read. They had made it for the valley. Or for the sky. Frik felt the size of that decision the way you feel a cold coming on, low and slow and certain: somebody, three thousand years gone, had aimed a picture at a place no living eye could comfortably stand.

He looked back up at the white lines and tried, the way you try to see the duck-rabbit, to make them resolve. They wouldn't. Up close it was just trenches in a hill, filled with crushed chalk, blinding where the cloud-light caught them.

Just trenches in a hill.

Frik crouched and put his hand flat on the nearest scour-line where it ran near the path, and the chalk was not soft. That surprised him. He'd expected something like cliff-face, friable, a thing you could erase with a boot-heel. Instead it was packed — rammed — a paste of broken white driven down into a cut he could see was deep, deeper than the surface suggested, the edges of the trench standing crisp where turf met chalk. A metre down, the records said. They'd dug it a metre down, three thousand years ago, and packed it, because chalk on a slope wants to creep, wants to slump and smear and soften, and a metre-deep trench packed hard holds an edge against the long patient pull of the hillside.

Engineering. Somebody's grandmother's grandmother's people, a hundred generations back, had solved the problem of soil creep on a chalk down, and the solution was still working under his palm.

He stood, brushed white dust off his hand, and the dust did not all come off. It clung in the lines of his skin like the negative of a fingerprint.

*That's the catch, then,* he thought, and the thought had the shape Frik's thoughts always took — not words, exactly, but a felt diagram, a thing he'd have to translate later for the others. The catch was right here in the dust on his hand. The easy story, the story you'd tell a child, was: it's a drawing, it's three thousand years old, isn't that marvellous. And it was marvellous, and it was a drawing, and it was three thousand years old. But a drawing on paper is a thing that happened once. This was a thing that had to keep happening. Leave this slope alone — leave the wind and the rain and the patient green grass to themselves — and in twenty years there is no horse. The figure is not old the way a fossil is old. It is old the way a fire is old when somebody has kept feeding it for three thousand years.

He found he was grinning, and didn't entirely know why.

—

Down by the half-built marquee, Devan had already made three friends and lost his hat to the wind. Frik came up in time to read the conversation off the faces, which was how he took most conversations: a stout man in a body-warmer doing the patient explaining-to-tourists face; a younger woman with a clipboard doing the polite-but-busy face; Devan doing his open, kinetic, *tell-me-everything* face that won people over right up until it didn't.

The marquee, the trestle tables stacked flat, the hand-painted sign leaning against a fence post — SCOURING — Sat 9am — VOLUNTEERS & FAMILIES WELCOME — all of it had the feel of a village fete assembling itself out of habit. There were cheese-rolling rumours (Devan signed them across to Frik, gleeful, *they roll cheese down the hill, the steep bit*), there was a sound system being argued over, there was a woman setting out a tea urn the size of a water heater. People moved in the easy unbothered way of people who have done this before and will do it again, who learned it from someone who learned it from someone.

Frik watched a grey-haired man hand a younger one a tool off the back of a trailer — not a modern tool, a worn thing, a sort of heavy mallet-and-tamper — and watched the younger man take it without a word, without a glance, the transfer so practised that neither of them looked at the object passing between their hands. They were looking at each other's faces. Something went across in that look that Frik felt go through him too, a small electric recognition: *that* was the thing. Not the mallet. The handing.

He filed it. The LENS had caught it too — the camera was up now, the soft motorless lift of it, and Frik knew without checking that the frame would hold both faces and the tool a blur between them, and that nobody in this field would notice the photograph being taken, which was the whole point of the LENS.

Then Devan turned, hat recaptured, face fallen a notch, and signed: *That lot. The clipboard people. They're not the festival.*

He angled his chin across the field.

There was a second marquee. Frik had taken it for part of the same gathering, but now he read it properly, and it was a different species of tent. White, taut, branded — a green logo, a line of clean type — and under it a folding display rack with renderings on it, and a slim man in a good coat gesturing at the renderings to two people from the village who stood with their arms folded and their feet planted, listening hard. The renderings, even at this distance, even half-furled in the wind, were the giveaway: a horse, the horse, but rendered crisp, complete, every line resolved and unbroken, glowing white against a green so saturated it looked stencilled. A horse that made sense from every angle. A horse you could put on a mug.

*Heritage Consultancy,* Devan signed, finger-spelling the long second word with a sour twist. *That bloke gave me a card.* He produced it. Frik read it. ROWAN LEIGH, it said, with letters after the name, and below: *Restoring Britain's Story.*

Frik looked from the card to the renderings to the actual horse on the actual hill, the one made of disconnected white scars that refused to be a horse at all, and back to the rendering, and felt the gap between them open like a trapdoor.

*Restore it to what,* he signed slowly. *It's not broken.*

—

Wren found the answer to the question nobody had asked yet, which was Wren's particular gift — she came back from her circuit of the valley rim with her phone out and her thumb already moving, and she stood square in front of Frik so he could see her hands and her mouth together, the way she always did, no fuss, just access.

*WWII,* she spelled. *They turfed it over. Forty-something to forty-five. Covered the whole thing with hedge cuttings and earth so the Luftwaffe couldn't use it as a landmark on the bombing runs to the factories.* She let that sit. *Then they uncovered it after. Dug it back out. Re-cut it.* Her face did the thing it did when a pattern closed, a small fierce satisfaction. *So even the gap got minded. Even when they hid it, they hid it on purpose, to bring it back.*

Frik turned this over. Three thousand years, give or take, and somewhere in the middle a generation had deliberately erased the horse to save the village underneath it from being bombed flat — had treated the figure as a thing precious enough to bury, and a thing certain enough to come back to that they kept the burial reversible. You don't bury a corpse the way you put away the good china.

The official story was still ironclad, and Frik liked it for being ironclad — the date, the trenches, the engineering, no aliens, no ley lines, no need for any of that. The marvel didn't require a mystery to prop it up. The marvel was the maintenance. The marvel was that a thing which vanishes in twenty years untended had not, in three thousand, vanished. Empires had vanished faster. Plague had come and gone and come again. The Romans had walked the Ridgeway right past it and left, and the horse had outlasted Rome the way it had outlasted everyone, not by being remembered for what it was — because nobody knew what it was, hadn't for centuries, the medieval folk thought it was a dragon and gave it a hill to gallop to at night — but by being *kept*. By somebody every seven years or so deciding the lines must not fade.

And nobody, not Wren's phone, not the Oxford team, not the slim man under the white tent with his glowing rendering, could say *why.*

Frik liked that too. He liked it the way you like a held breath.

—

The historical society came at them as a delegation, which is how you know you've stopped being a curiosity and become a problem.

Three of them, the stout body-warmer man among them, and a woman in front who did all the talking and most of the looking. She had the local committee face down cold — courteous, immovable, a face that had said no to better-dressed people than these. Devan tried first, all warmth and quick hands, the camera explained, the project explained, *we just want to document, to listen, not to interfere* — and Frik watched the woman's politeness set like cooling tallow.

He couldn't hear the words. He didn't need them. He read the lift of her chin and the small backward set of her shoulders and the way her eyes went, just once, to the LENS's camera and stayed cold, and the way she said something short that made Devan's whole body go still.

*She says,* Devan signed, careful now, the kinetic gone out of him, *the festival's not for filming. She says everyone wants a piece of the horse this year.* He glanced toward the white tent. *She thinks we're with them. Or like them. She says the village will scour the horse the way it's always scoured the horse, and they don't need outsiders to tell them what it means.* A beat. *She says: you can watch from the valley. Like everyone else.*

Like everyone else. From the valley. Where the horse is legible and you are not invited up onto its back.

The delegation turned and went, and that was that, and the wind took the woman's words apart before they reached anyone who could hear them.

But Frik was watching past her, the way he always watched past the thing in front of him, and so he was the one who saw the other woman.

She stood apart from all of it — apart from the society, apart from the white tent, apart from the tea urn and the cheese-rumour and the stacked trestles. Older, weathered the way the hill was weathered, a coat that had been rained on many times. She had a tool in her hand — that same worn mallet-and-tamper, or its twin — and she was not using it, only holding it, the way you hold something you've held so often it's stopped being a separate object from your hand.

She wasn't looking at the renderings. She wasn't looking at the delegation, or the slim man, or the camera. She was looking up the slope, at the disconnected white scars that weren't a horse, with the absolute attention of someone reading a sentence the rest of them couldn't see was even written.

Then, for one second, she looked down off the slope and across the field, and her eyes went over the crew — over Devan's stillness, over the LENS, over Frik himself — and stopped on the white dust still clinging in the lines of Frik's open palm.

She saw it. He saw her see it.

Her face did something almost too small to read. Not a welcome. Not anything so warm. Just a flicker of — *noted.* A door not opened. A door noticed to exist.

Then she turned and went up the hill, toward the scars, where she was allowed and they were not, and the wind closed over the place where she'd stood.
