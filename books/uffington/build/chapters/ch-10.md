# Chapter 10 — Lines and Loss

The tent had walls of stretched white nylon and the light inside it was the light of a hospital, of a place where something is going to be measured and then improved. Saul felt it before he saw the screens — that particular hum of a generator behind canvas, the wrongness of cold blue glow on a hillside that had been doing fine for three thousand years on weather and chalk dust and the angle of the sun.

Outside, the down was green going gold at the edges, and the Horse lay where it always lay, its long body legible only if you walked down into the valley and turned and looked back up. Inside the tent, the Horse had been brought indoors and made to behave.

There were four screens. On the largest, the figure floated in a grey void, scrubbed clean of grass and hill, rendered as pure outline — a vector ghost, every curve plotted to a tolerance. Saul read the screen the way he read everything, which was all at once and then in pieces: the registration marks in the corners, the small green numbers ticking the coordinate of his own breath as someone moved a cursor, the legend down the left side. *Survey mesh. Drone capture, 1,200 images, photogrammetric model.* And beneath that, the thing his eye had snagged on from the doorway, the thing that had pulled the whole rest of the room into focus:

Two outlines. One white. One amber.

The white was the Horse as the drone had found it last Tuesday. The amber sat over it like a corrected sentence, a teacher's pen through a child's spelling — *here, and here, and here* — and where the two diverged, the software had shaded the gaps a soft accusing pink.

Dr. Rowan Leigh was talking, and talking well. Saul made himself attend to the words and not just the shape of them, because the words were the official story said cleanly, with love, and you had to grant a thing its dignity before you could find the hole in it.

"What we're looking at," Leigh said, "is drift."

She had a way of standing slightly to the side of her own slides, so she never blocked them, the way a good museum guide never gets between you and the case. The locals had filed in — Saul counted nine, then eleven, the back of the tent thickening — and they stood with the particular stillness of people on land they know watching a stranger explain it to them.

"The Horse you see today is not the Horse of 1990," Leigh went on. "It isn't the Horse of 1850, either. Every scouring shifts the line a little. A boot scuffs the trench edge. New chalk packs a centimetre wide of the old. Over a century, over many centuries —" the amber outline pulsed once, helpfully — "you get migration. The beak of the head has crept. The disconnected legs have wandered. The body has, frankly, fattened."

Somebody near the door laughed, short, then thought better of it.

"None of this is anyone's fault," Leigh said, and she meant it; Saul watched her mean it, watched the small openness in her face that wasn't a sales tactic. "It's the price of being loved by hand. Every act of care leaves a fingerprint. Multiply the fingerprints across a hundred generations and the original draughtsman's intent is buried under all that affection. What our model does —" she let the amber line settle, lock, brighten — "is recover it. We took the OSL-dated trench profile, the deepest packed chalk, the bits that haven't been disturbed since the figure was first cut. We worked back to the line they actually dug. We can give you the Horse the Bronze Age made. Not the Horse three thousand years of guessing has made of it."

It was, Saul thought, a beautiful argument. That was the trouble with it.

He looked at the OSL figure on the lower screen and the science was real and he loved it. He had read the Palmer and Miles paper twice on the drive down, the careful business of buried quartz holding the memory of the last sunlight it ever saw, the lab teasing that date back out — thirteen-eighty to five-fifty BC, the number that had taken the Horse off King Alfred and the Saxons and handed it to people who left no name. The trenches a full metre deep, packed with crushed chalk, engineered — *engineered*, that was the word that got him — so the hill's slow downhill creep wouldn't smear the shape. Whoever cut it had understood that a line on a living slope is not a line you draw once. It is a line you commit to maintaining or it is not a line at all.

That was where the amber outline lied, and it lied so gently that Saul could feel the room half-believing it.

He found Frik across the tent and made the small flat gesture they used for *watch — them, not it.* Frik's eyes were already going around the locals' faces, that ceaseless reading, and Frik signed back, low against his chest where only Saul would catch it: a closed fist, then opened slow. *Holding still. They're holding still.*

Eleven people on their own hill, holding very still.

And Edie Vane, by the tent pole, saying nothing at all.

---

Here is the potato, and you have to handle it honestly or it bites you.

Say you agree with Leigh. Say the drift is real — and it is; the survey is real, the migration is measurable, you can overlay the 1990 dig photos on a modern capture and watch the beak walk. Say you decide, reasonably, lovingly, that the right thing is to fix it. To stop the wandering. To lay down the true line and from now on scour to *that*, to a model, to a number, so the next three thousand years don't blur it further.

Now go and do it.

You will need the community to scour to your line and not their own. You will need the man whose grandfather packed the off-fore hoof to pack it eight centimetres to the east of where his grandfather packed it, because the model says so, because the model is right. You will need the children — and there are always children, with their fistfuls of chalk and their faces white to the elbow — to stop filling the trench by feel, by the shape their hands already know, and to start filling it by station and offset and a tablet held by a man from Oxford.

And the moment you do that, the act changes from a thing the hill's people do into a thing that is done *to* the hill, by a plan, from outside. You have not saved the Horse. You have made the Horse a print of itself, very accurate, and stopped the living thing that was making it.

That is the potato. The easy fix — *just chalk it correctly* — is correct, and it kills the only thing that ever kept the figure alive, which is people doing it wrong, together, by hand, the same wrong way their grandmother showed them, year on year, until the wrongness is the Horse.

Leigh wasn't wrong about the drift. She was wrong about what the drift *was for.* But nobody in the tent had said that yet, and it wasn't going to be the crew who said it. Saul could feel the others knowing this too — Petra near the entrance flap with her phone already winning over two scourers, charming, kinetic, asking about the meal they cooked the night of the scouring, not the line at all; the Lens off in the corner with the camera up but not, yet, taking. Just framing. Just holding the room the way the Lens held everything, the way you hold a thing you might have to lower.

---

"Can I ask," said a woman near the front, "what happens to the bits you call wrong."

Leigh turned to her with real warmth. "Nothing happens to them. We document them. They're part of the figure's story — the whole accreted history of care, all that drift, it's a record, it's precious. We'd archive every layer. Then we'd cut the recovered line."

"So you'd cut a new trench."

"We'd cut *the* trench. The original. The one underneath."

"Underneath," the woman said. "Through ours."

There it was, the small cold seam in the warm argument, and the woman had her thumb in it without raising her voice. *Through ours.* To get to the Bronze Age line you go down through everything laid over it since. Through the chalk packed in 1857, the year Hughes wrote the festival down, the games and the cheese-rolling and the whole down black with people. Through the turfing-over of the war, when they hid the Horse from German bombers who'd have used it as a road-sign to Swindon, and the men who turfed it knowing exactly where every line was because they had it in their hands. Through the unrecorded thousand scourings nobody wrote down because why would you write down breathing.

You cannot recover the first line without removing all the love stacked on top of it. The model could show both. The trowel could only choose.

Leigh felt the seam too. She was good. "We wouldn't do anything," she said, "the community didn't want. I want to be very clear. This is a proposal. We think it's the most respectful thing — to give you back the figure your ancestors actually made, instead of the slow accident of —"

She stopped, because she'd been about to say *of your care* and she heard it.

The generator hummed. On the screen the amber Horse and the white Horse hung over each other, the pink shame-shading in the gaps, and Saul thought: *that pink is the part where the people are. They've shaded the people pink and labelled them error.*

---

Edie still hadn't spoken.

Saul had learned, in Book One, on the other hill, that Edie Vane's silences were not empty. They were the silence of someone who has the answer and is deciding whether the room has earned it. She stood by the pole with her arms folded and her weathered teacher's face giving nothing, and Frik was watching her the way you watch the one person in a room who actually knows, and Frik signed to Saul, very small: a hand laid flat on the back of the other hand. *She's holding it. Still holding it.*

The whole tent was waiting for Edie to say the thing. And Edie was not going to say the thing, not here, not in the blue light, not to the screens. That was its own answer and Saul loved her for it. The "what is it for" was hers. She would not spend it in a tent.

It was Petra who broke the held air, kinetic, sideways, the way she always came at a locked room — not at the lock. She'd drifted up beside one of the scourers, a big quiet man in a fleece, and she said, not quietly, to him and not to Leigh: "How do *you* know where the line goes? When you scour. Do you measure?"

The man almost smiled. "Measure," he said, like the word was a small joke. "You stand in it." He looked at the amber ghost on the screen as if it were a photograph of a relative who'd been heavily retouched. "You stand in the trench your dad stood in and you fill it where your hands say. You don't look at the whole horse. You can't, from up here. You just do your bit and trust the next one's doing theirs." He shrugged. "It comes out a horse."

*It comes out a horse.* Saul wrote nothing down. He had it. Nobody up on the hill ever sees the Horse they're making. The form is only legible from the valley, from the air — from where the hands aren't. Every scourer works blind to the whole, fills their metre of trench by feel and by faith that everyone else is filling theirs, and the figure assembles itself out of all that trust, out of nobody being able to check. The drift Leigh wanted to correct wasn't error. It was the sound of a thing made by people who can't see what they're making and do it anyway. For three thousand years. On faith that the next hand knows.

You could not put that on a screen. You could put its absence on a screen, shaded pink.

---

And then the flap opened and the cold day came in, and one of Leigh's people — young, mud to the knee, breathless in the way of someone who has run uphill with news — said, "Rowan. You need to come and see. The east trench."

Leigh went still. "See what."

"We were taking the profile core. By the off-hind." The young man held up something, and the blue light caught it, and it was not white. It was the wrong white — older, harder, a packed grey-white lump the size of a fist, threaded with something dark, and it had the density of a thing that had been crushed and tamped and then buried under everything that came after. "It's chalk packing," he said. "But it's *under* the layer we modelled. It's deeper than the bottom of the trench we surveyed. It doesn't match the curve." He turned it in his hand and the room leaned. "The line down there goes somewhere else. The original — it's not where the model put it. It's not where the *Horse* is."

Leigh took it from him. Held it the way you hold something that has just rewritten an afternoon.

On the big screen, the amber Horse hung there, certain, recovered, finished — the true line, the line they'd worked back to from the trench. And in Rowan Leigh's two hands was a fistful of chalk that some person had packed, by feel, in the dark of the ground, along a line that ran out from under the figure and off the edge of every survey in the tent — to where, nobody in that nylon room could say.

Petra had stopped moving. The Lens, at last, took the shot.

And Edie Vane unfolded her arms. She looked at the lump in Leigh's hands, and then — only then, for one quarter of a second — she looked at Frik, and something passed between them that had no words because Frik did not need words and Edie, just now, did not have any she was willing to spend.

She still said nothing.

But she'd seen where the line went. Saul would have bet the van on it. She'd looked at that fragment and read it the way none of them could yet, the way only someone whose family had packed those trenches by hand could read a thing packed by hand — and whatever she knew, she was keeping, in the blue light, with the screens going on certainly behind her, the amber Horse insisting it had the answer.

The generator hummed.

The pink gaps glowed.

And under the whole tent, under the model and the mesh and the recovered line, a piece of older chalk said: *not here. Somewhere else. Somebody knew. Ask her.*
