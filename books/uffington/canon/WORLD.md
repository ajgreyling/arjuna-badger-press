The world is the living landscape of the Berkshire Downs, centering on White Horse Hill below Uffington Castle. The Horse itself is a stylised, 110-metre chalk figure, visible only from the valley or sky, cut deep into the hillside and kept alive by the hands and feet of generations. The site is ringed by other ancient monuments: Uffington Castle (Iron Age hillfort), Wayland's Smithy (Neolithic barrow), the Ridgeway path. The chase geometry: the crew arrives as the biennial scouring looms, drawn into the rhythms of the local festival and the urgent push by the heritage consultancy to "restore" the figure to a fixed (and marketable) vision of ancient Britain. The land is alive with weather, sheep, and the quiet presence of people whose families have scoured the Horse for centuries. The texture of the wonder is tactile: chalk dust on hands, the deep green of the hillside, the white lines vanishing under grass if left untended. The wonder is not in a secret chamber but in the unbroken act itself—a living tradition, made sharper by the fact that its original meaning is lost but its care persists. The world is real, mapped in detail, and the people—farmers, festival-goers, schoolchildren, the local historical society, Edie Vane—are the living custodians of the Horse.
