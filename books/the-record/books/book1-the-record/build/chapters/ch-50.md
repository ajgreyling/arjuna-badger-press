# Chapter 50

The judge asked the question without a lawyer's help.

“Can this method prove murder rather than accident?”

Kiki sat in the witness box with the sparse reconstruction frozen on a court monitor. It showed the
chair's path, the descending field, the unresolved contact and the later transfer to the vehicle.
No face occupied the room. The public gallery had waited through three hours of derivation to see
less than the video had shown in twelve seconds.

“No,” she said.

The answer moved through the gallery.

Counsel for the former security figure stood before the judge could continue. “Then the consortium's
claim that my client caused a death is speculation.”

“No,” Kiki replied.

“You have just said the system cannot distinguish accident.”

“The physical sequence supports causation. It does not establish criminal intention.”

The judge asked her to separate those statements.

Kiki identified each registered product. The security path supplied force to the chair. The chair
rotated while the missing man's mass remained partly supported. His foot lost or could not recover
its base. The fall followed. Those relations survived both independent material paths.

Whether the security figure intended to kill, injure, frighten, restrain or move furniture did not
survive.

“Could it have been an accident?” the judge asked.

“Yes.”

“Could it have been murder?”

“The physical result does not exclude it.”

“That is not proof.”

“No.”

The family attorney directed the court to the post-impact sequence.

Here the record widened. The vehicle left and returned. Cleaning materials arrived with it. Three
people moved the fallen mass. A heavy object held the door open. Water, solvent and repeated wiping
altered the room for ninety-four minutes. Paper burned in a metal container outside. The body left
without a living gait or supported biological activity.

“Does the method establish why they did those things?” she asked.

“No.”

“Does it establish that they did them?”

“Within the certified uncertainty, yes.”

“Could ninety-four minutes of cleaning be an accident?”

The opposing lawyer objected. The judge sustained the objection before Kiki answered.

He put the question differently. “What can this court infer without asking your instrument to infer
intention?”

“That the fall was followed by coordinated work which moved the man, altered the site and removed
him. The work began after the fatal sequence and used materials not present before it.”

“Concealment.”

“That is the supported physical classification.”

The smooth video had concentrated moral certainty inside one second. The physical record placed its
strongest evidence in the hour and a half afterwards, when people fetched supplies, returned, lifted,
washed and carried. Each act touched more matter than the fall. Each reduced the chance that a later
person would know what had happened.

Kiki could not tell the court what any man meant.

She could show what they had done to prevent the room from telling it.

The judge admitted the sequence, uncertainty map, raw fragments and competing contact histories for
the limited purpose of the custody hearing. He made no finding of criminal guilt. He ordered that the
family receive the findings before any public release and that the engineering-yard cores be
returned to their owner after independent imaging.

The sheriff would still inventory the rented office that afternoon.

As Kiki left the witness box, a reporter called out whether the machine had failed to solve the
murder.

She did not answer in the corridor.

The machine had refused to invent the crime the question required.
