# Chapter 32

Everyone in Technopark thought Naledi was protecting somebody.

It would have been easier if she were.

The name on her secure screen belonged to a dead man whose political life had not ended with him.
His face still opened party conferences. His phrases appeared in judgments and budget speeches.
Three candidates in the coming succession contest claimed his confidence, and two sitting ministers
owed their careers to the belief that they had inherited it.

Presence at an apartheid security installation did not establish guilt. It did not even establish
knowledge of the fall.

That qualification would survive for perhaps an hour after release.

The name would survive alone.

Naledi closed her office door and read the technical finding again. The source chain was cleaner
than most intelligence she received. The limitations were explicit. The method remained intelligible
to so few people that an independent appeal would require one of the institutions already party to
the result.

No statute defined a historical reconstruction as evidence. No court had ruled on the standing of
a dead subject, the consent of descendants, or the rights of living people inferred from matter they
did not own. There was no discovery procedure for uncertainty fields and no offence for using a
valid query to manufacture a politically timed truth.

Release it this month and the consortium would enter a succession contest without standing for
office. Hold it, and the government would repeat the method by which inconvenient history became an
archive box nobody was authorised to open.

Early in her career, Naledi had signed a temporary restriction on security files while threatened
witnesses were moved. The reason had been real. The restriction lasted eleven years. By the time it
lifted, two witnesses were dead and one file had lost a page nobody admitted removing.

Good reasons did not remain good merely because they had once been true.

She opened the family's consent instrument.

Present?

Did he leave alive?

Was anyone else accountable?

She read the questions twice. They were the only part of the file that did not ask what the result
would do to a minister, a party or a country.

Her legal adviser recommended classification under inherited national-security powers. Naledi
refused. The old statute could hide evidence this sensitive and provide an official reason
afterwards.

Her political adviser recommended immediate publication through an independent university. Naledi
refused that too. Moving the disclosure to a respectable institution would not create appeal,
standing or law. It would only give the intervention a cleaner letterhead.

She drafted a seventy-two-hour suspension.

No further identity queries. No distribution beyond the authorised quorum and the family's attorney.
The family could be told that the third answer was supported and that government had stayed the
name. The order would enter the public administrative register immediately, including its expiry.
Extension would require a court.

It was narrower than secrecy and wider than justice.

She called Technopark.

Lwazi spoke first. “The family gave permission.”

“The family cannot confer legitimacy on an instrument affecting people outside the family.”

“The state had no objection while the result concerned a dead man without power.”

Naledi let the sentence stand.

Dries asked what would change in seventy-two hours.

“A court can appoint independent technical advisers and determine interim custody.”

“A court cannot learn the machine in three days.”

“Neither can Parliament. That is the problem.”

Kiki asked whether the suspension included the refusal receipt.

“No. The family's answers and the refusal remain theirs.”

The distinction did not repair the call.

“You have built the first honest witness in human history,” Naledi said, “and handed it to a
continent with no law about witnesses.”

Ama answered from Ghana. “Then write the law. Do not disappear the witness.”

“I am putting the disappearance on a clock.”

Naledi signed the order while they watched. The public register accepted its hash. The consortium
queue stopped. Four nodes released compute. Antarctica abandoned an alignment already under way.

She knew who would accuse her of protecting the old state and who would accuse her of betraying the
liberation movement. She knew Lwazi would see cowardice, Kiki a custody breach, Ama an institution
claiming a family boundary when power became visible.

Naledi knew the exact shape of the fracture before she signed.

She signed anyway.
