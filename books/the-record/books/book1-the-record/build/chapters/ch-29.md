# Chapter 29

The statements had disagreed for thirty-seven years.

One witness said the missing man came out of the building on his feet. The other said two people
carried him to the vehicle.

Each statement had been taken within weeks of the disappearance. Each bore a signature, a date and
the old administrative marks of the office that filed it. Later copies had accumulated annotations:
`RELIABLE`, `POLITICALLY MOTIVATED`, `RECANTED`, `COACHED`. None of the annotations contained new
evidence. They recorded which historian had trusted which man.

Kiki verified the earliest surviving copies before she let the words near the reconstruction.
Different paper. Different typewriters. Matching filing damage. No common correction fluid, copied
tear or substituted page. The statements were independent and materially authentic.

Their disagreement was authentic too.

The first witness had placed himself east of the yard entrance. The second had waited near a fence
to the south. Lucid found human-compatible ground loads at both positions during the post-impact
interval. The result did not identify either man, but it put observers where both had always said
they stood.

“So one of them lied from the right place,” Dries said.

Kiki displayed the old building plan and removed its walls.

“Or neither saw what he described.”

The vehicle door opened across the eastern witness's view. When they restored the measured panel
angle, it covered the threshold from his knees to above his head. He could have seen people approach
the vehicle. He could not have seen whether the missing man's feet touched the ground.

The southern witness had an open line through the yard.

He had almost no light.

The archive plan claimed an exterior lamp above the door. Procurement records showed that a lamp
had been installed. The physical field found no operating ballast, no current draw and no warm
fixture during the relevant interval. Light from the vehicle reached the witness at an angle that
separated moving upper bodies but merged their lower shapes against the building.

He could have seen two figures close together. He could not have distinguished carrying from
supporting, restraining or walking.

Kiki placed the statements beside the sightline products.

The first man had written: `I saw him walk.`

The second: `I saw them carry him.`

Both claims exceeded the available view.

“They might still have been right,” Lwazi said.

“Yes.”

“We just can't use either one.”

“Not for the disputed fact.”

He read the statements again. “They spent their lives being called liars.”

Kiki did not answer. Her work had taught her to find the broken seal, the altered timestamp, the
page inserted after signature. Those failures had remedies. You exposed the change and returned to
the earlier record.

Here, nothing had been changed.

The witnesses had converted partial perception into complete memory before anyone wrote it down.
The paper preserved that conversion faithfully.

The family's attorney joined the review from a dark office. She asked whether the two men could be
shown the sightline result.

“Only with their consent,” Ama said.

“One is too ill to give it. The other no longer speaks to researchers.”

“Then no.”

The attorney nodded. “Put that in the disclosure. People will say you protected them.”

Kiki added a custody note explaining why neither witness had been re-interviewed. It sat oddly below
the computational result: a human limit beneath an optical one.

Her certified finding took six revisions.

The statements were authentic. The witnesses occupied positions consistent with their accounts.
Neither position permitted reliable observation of the detail on which the statements conflicted.
The reconstruction did not establish deceit.

Dries read the final sentence. “Both honest?”

“Unsupported, not exonerated.”

“That's colder.”

“It is smaller.”

She signed it.

For thirty-seven years, two camps had treated contradiction as proof that one witness had corrupted
the record. Kiki's hashes could show that nobody had.

The record had arrived incomplete.
