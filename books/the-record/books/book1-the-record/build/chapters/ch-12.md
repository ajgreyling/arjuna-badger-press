# Chapter 12

Technopark called them noise events for six days.

On the seventh, Ama changed the field name.

The alteration was not supposed to matter. It touched no data and changed no transform. The internal
schema label `noise_events` became `rain_fragments`, with the old name retained as an alias so
Dries's code would not break before breakfast.

At 07:12, the first complaint arrived.

`DRIES: what is rain`

Ama answered from the control desk in Accra.

`AMA: what you called noise`

`DRIES: noise is a technical category`

`AMA: no. noise is what you call data`  
`before you know why you need it`

The Rain arrived from Karoo in overlapping sheets. Electromagnetic products, instrument-state
transitions, geological integrations, vacuum-noise windows, environmental monitors and the isolated
clock—each carried its own shape of forgetting. No individual stream contained a room, an object or
an action. Together they constrained a narrow transition volume that appeared nowhere else.

To keep that relationship, Ghana had to retain fragments the current solver could not use.

Ninety-four per cent of the incoming products fell below the active threshold.

The storage forecast lasted eleven weeks.

Ama took the problem to the procurement call. Technopark joined with a proposal already prepared:
drop unsupported fragments after thirty days, preserve aggregate statistics and retain full detail
only for products admitted into a query.

It was a sensible design for almost every data system ever built.

“No,” Ama said.

The procurement officer looked at the capacity chart. “Then which budget funds indefinite raw
retention?”

“It is not raw.”

“The quote says raw stream storage.”

“The quote is wrong.”

Dries came onto the call from his car, stationary in R44 traffic. “We don't need every unsupported
fragment at full rank. Keep the transform receipts and summary bins. If a future query needs one,
reconstruct from source.”

“The source does not retain it.”

“Karoo has local buffers.”

“For seventy-two hours.”

“Then extend the buffers.”

“With which budget?”

The procurement officer raised one hand. “That is the question presently under discussion.”

Ama shared two cost models.

The first implemented deletion. Every expired fragment would be selected, verified against
retention policy, removed from replicated stores, recorded in the audit chain and reconciled across
backups. The operation consumed compute, network, staff time and energy. It was irreversible by
design.

The second changed a rank value.

Fragments below the active threshold moved into colder storage, disappeared from ordinary query
results and consumed almost no processing until another constraint raised them. Nothing was erased.
Nothing had to be reconstructed. The operation could be reversed by changing the rank again.

The second model cost less.

Dries read it in silence.

“You are keeping everything by making almost all of it invisible,” he said.

“I am demoting unsupported fragments.”

“Forever.”

“Until storage fails physically or a governed deletion occurs.”

“That is forever in a budget meeting.”

“Deletion is an operation. It has a cost, a direction and a signature. Once paid, it cannot be
unpaid.”

The procurement officer said, “All storage eventually costs.”

“Yes. So does forgetting.”

Ama placed the two totals side by side.

“You are asking me to pay to make something smaller. I can pay less to make it quieter.”

The call paused while everyone performed the private arithmetic of having expected philosophy and
received a cheaper invoice.

The no-delete model passed provisionally.

It created a second problem immediately.

If Ghana retained everything, the query system could not give every fragment equal attention. The
compute queue became the actual instrument. A high-ranked request could raise dormant products,
consume days of reconstruction capacity and delay every other experiment. Somebody had to decide
which questions mattered first.

Ama designed the initial queue to resist whoever could pay the most. Registered replication tests
first. Instrument failures second. Scientific queries by pre-committed allocation. Commercial and
prestige requests last, regardless of offered compute.

On the same afternoon, the European laboratory requested priority for an independent reproduction
and attached enough cloud capacity to run it.

The queue placed them ninth.

They called the decision anti-collaborative.

Ama called it Tuesday.

At 18:40, the revised solver raised a fragment from the first sealed-room run that had spent six
days below threshold. On its own it was meaningless: a low-confidence transition in a geological
product, too broad in time and too weak in amplitude.

Joined by event order rather than timestamp, it narrowed the shared transition volume.

The machine still could not find the table.

But it had found one more way in which the room had changed.

Ama watched the fragment move from cold rank into the live query. Nothing had been recovered. It had
never been lost.

Outside the racks, evening rain began against the roof, loud enough to enter every microphone in the
room.

The name remained.
