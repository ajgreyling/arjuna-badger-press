# Chapter 31

The fourth figure had been addressed by name.

Lwazi found the vocative in a pressure sequence too poor to render as speech. The middle syllable
held across the floor vibration and electromagnetic products; the opening varied between three
consonants, and the ending could have belonged to either a name or the next word.

Lucid produced forty-one lexical candidates.

None appeared on the display as sound. They occupied rows with confidence intervals, language
sources and conflicts. Lwazi reduced them against the documented languages of the site, then stopped
before adding any historical person.

Kiki fixed the phonetic result to the public anchor.

Only after its hash appeared outside the consortium did they search the permitted archives.

Seven names survived period, geography and age. Five belonged to people with no documented reason
to be within five hundred kilometres of the site. One belonged to a security employee whose shift
record placed him at another installation, though the record came from the same incomplete archive
they were testing.

The last name belonged to a revered figure in the liberation movement.

Lwazi knew it before Lucid completed the citation list. It had been painted above the assembly hall
at his school. His mother had kept a newspaper portrait in the passage at home. The figure's speeches
appeared in university courses, party congresses and the openings of public buildings. Present
leaders still borrowed authority from having stood near him in photographs.

At school, the name had not belonged entirely to history. Teachers used it when assemblies became
too loud and politicians used it when crowds became inconvenient. A line about discipline appeared
on examination papers without the sentence that had followed it. At his mother's union hall, the
same portrait hung above a cabinet of strike notices because the figure had once arrived at a factory
gate when arrival still carried risk.

People who agreed on almost nothing agreed that he had been necessary. That agreement had polished
away the years in which he had been difficult to his own movement, useful to rivals and wrong in
public. Death completed the editing. By the time Lwazi entered university, the figure could endorse
any present leader because he could no longer object.

Lwazi opened the school website. The assembly hall had been rebuilt, but the name remained across
the lintel in the same block letters. Beneath it, this year's pupils smiled in three rows. He closed
the page before it entered the case workspace.

Official biographies placed him in another province that week.

A local organiser's private calendar placed a meeting with an unnamed visitor near the district.
The calendar had entered a public archive years before and had never been indexed by location. A
newspaper photograph from the following morning showed the revered figure wearing shoes whose
measured sole geometry was compatible with the fourth load path.

Compatible did not mean unique.

The phonetic address, local calendar and load geometry were independent. Together they crossed the
identity threshold.

Lwazi removed the name and sent the seven candidate packets to Ama with neutral identifiers. Ghana
reran the source-independence test without biographies, portraits or political rank. Candidate Seven
still crossed. Candidate Six remained below threshold because its shift record and the archive that
stored it were not independent enough to support each other.

He had wanted the blind repeat to make the name disappear.

It made the method harder to accuse.

Ama read the proposed finding. “Presence only.”

“Presence and speech,” Dries said.

“We do not know what he said.”

“We know that path vocalised.”

“Then presence and vocal activity. No words.”

Lwazi barely heard them. On his screen, the name sat above a conflict map. The official itinerary
remained red. Two biographies repeated it from the same source. Three later histories cited the
biographies as independent support.

One administrative entry had become five respectable footnotes.

The entry came from an office whose filing system the liberation movement had inherited after the
transition. Later historians had treated the inherited stamp as neutral because the institution
above it had changed. The paper had not become more independent when the flag over the building did.

Lwazi almost called his mother. He could ask why she had kept the portrait without revealing the
finding. The answer would tell him what the name meant to one house and nothing about what the man
had done in the room.

He put the phone down.

He opened the family question.

`WAS THERE SUPPORTED PHYSICAL EVIDENCE THAT ANOTHER PERSON WAS ACCOUNTABLE FOR HIS DEATH OR
CONCEALMENT?`

The name did not answer it. The name made an answer possible.

It did not establish intervention, concealment or knowledge of the fall. A person could be at a site
without causing what happened there. A revered person could too.

Lwazi added that limit to the finding.

Dries objected. “We don't add innocence clauses to ordinary identities.”

“This isn't an ordinary identity.”

“The method cannot change because you had his picture in your house.”

The remark found its mark because it was true.

Lwazi removed the special sentence. In its place he used the same language applied to every other
result: identity did not establish action beyond the certified physical events.

Kiki checked the chain. Ama checked the source independence. Sanna signed the temporal interval.
Dries signed the inversion.

Lwazi signed last.

The result displayed the name in plain type. No portrait appeared beside it. No biography opened.
Nothing in the interface indicated what the name had meant to a country or to a boy beneath an
assembly-hall wall.

The secure call from Minister Naledi Maseko arrived before they could notify the family.

She asked them to suspend the query.
