# Chapter 11

Samuels found the pattern because Dries printed the failure.

The comparative decay plots were too wide for ordinary paper. Dries tiled them across twelve A4
sheets, taped the sheets together and fixed the result to the corridor wall outside the technical
room. On a monitor the lines could be zoomed, isolated and recoloured until any hypothesis looked
temporarily persuasive. On paper they had to coexist.

People stopped in front of them on the way to the kitchen.

Most asked whether the spikes were important.

Riaan Samuels asked why the clocks were dirty.

He worked two buildings away for a company that maintained fleet telemetry systems whose oldest
active unit had been installed before several members of the consortium finished school. Dries knew
him from a failed startup, a successful migration and one December weekend when a banking switch
had begun rejecting vehicle events from the future.

Samuels had come upstairs because the coffee shop was closed for plumbing.

He stood before the paper carrying tea in a glass cup and read the plots from right to left.

“The source clocks are signed,” Dries said.

“I didn't say they were wrong.”

“You said dirty.”

“Dirty is not wrong.”

He pointed to an electromagnetic stream, then to a vibration product beneath it. The scales were
different. The sampling rates were different. One measured amplitude; the other represented a
derived state change. Across both, almost hidden inside the incompatible units, was the same small
hook: a rise, a reversal and a short flat interval.

“There,” Samuels said.

“Those are unrelated sensors.”

“Yes.”

“They don't share hardware.”

“Good.”

“Or a network path.”

“Better.”

Dries called up the original products. Samuels refused the chair and leaned over the desk while the
plots loaded.

Years earlier, he explained, cheap vehicle units had reported impossible state changes during power
transitions. A truck appeared to move backward by six metres while stationary. An ignition event
preceded the voltage rise that powered the unit. Doors opened twice. The data had been classified as
corruption and dropped until a theft investigation depended on one of the discarded intervals.

“Different clocks recovered in a different order,” Samuels said. “GPS came back. Local oscillator
drifted. Network time arrived late. The packet wasn't damaged. The state changed while the unit had
three opinions about when.”

“We preserved our source clocks.”

“You preserved their numbers.”

He overlaid two Karoo streams using transition order rather than timestamp. The hooks aligned.

Not perfectly. Perfect agreement would have suggested a shared processing artefact. They aligned in
shape while retaining the timing behaviour of their own instruments.

Dries added a geological stream. The hook appeared stretched almost beyond recognition.

Then vacuum noise. Inverted, delayed, present.

“That isn't damage,” Samuels said. “That's where it changed its mind.”

Dries called Ama.

She answered on audio while walking between Accra's racks. “If this is another smoothing request—”

“I have a transition signature across four independent families.”

“Amplitude?”

“Order.”

She stopped walking. The cooling noise around her steadied.

Dries shared the alignment. Ama asked for the raw transformation histories, not the plots. Samuels
nodded at the question.

“Who is with you?” she asked.

“Riaan Samuels. Fleet telemetry.”

“Consortium access?”

“None.”

“Then remove him from the room.”

Samuels lifted his tea. “Coffee shop should be open.”

He left before Dries could object.

Ama waited until the door closed. “Now send the registered products to a clean workspace. No labels
for sensor family. No common time projection. I want event-order transforms and each instrument's
own decay model.”

“You think it is real.”

“I think your visitor found a question.”

“Same thing at this stage.”

“No. That is how pretty failures happen.”

For the next six hours, Ghana rebuilt the ingestion path around transitions rather than common
amplitude. Karoo supplied instrument-specific decay functions. Technopark changed the solver so it
could admit several clocks without forcing them into one timeline first.

The room did not appear.

Neither did the block, the chair or the bent copper.

But the haze changed.

Across four physically unrelated sensor families, one narrow transition volume survived every
wrong-coordinate test and vanished when the sealed-room interval was excluded.

It was not an object. It was not an image. It was a place where several systems had changed state
together and remembered the change differently.

Dries found Samuels downstairs after closing. The coffee shop gate was half lowered. He was helping
the owner move a refrigerator away from a leaking pipe.

“You were right,” Dries said.

Samuels kept pushing. “About which expensive thing?”

“The clocks.”

“They were dirty.”

“We found a shared transition.”

The refrigerator cleared the water. Samuels straightened and rubbed his hands on his trousers.

“Then stop looking for the signal,” he said. “Look for where the systems disagree about losing it.”
