# Chapter 6

The History Machine was rejected in nine minutes.

Nobody called it a History Machine in the application. The title was `Cross-Domain Persistence of
Correlated State Transitions in Heterogeneous Sensor Environments`, which had taken three days and
fourteen people to make less alarming. The executive summary did not contain *history*, *memory*,
*past* or *machine*. It proposed a controlled test of whether a documented physical state produced
measurable correlations that survived the removal of its ordinary evidence.

The committee chair read the summary, turned one page and asked what new detector they intended to
build.

“None,” Lwazi said.

The chair looked disappointed.

The meeting took place over a university video bridge at eleven on a Thursday. Dries sat beside
Lwazi in the same dead-company boardroom where Jacobs had asked what came next. Ama and Kiki joined
from their own nodes. Karoo had audio only because a lightning warning had moved non-essential
systems off the main link. Antarctica was represented by a written note from Sanna that began
`TEMPORAL REFERENCE NOT YET JUSTIFIED` and became less encouraging from there.

The committee had eleven members. Five cameras were off.

“If there is no new detector,” said a materials scientist in Pretoria, “what exactly are we
funding?”

Dries answered. “Integration time, ingestion, compute and independent verification.”

“Using existing instruments?”

“Yes.”

“Then why can this not be done under your existing allocations?”

“Because the instruments belong to different programmes, the data clocks do not share a reference,
the raw streams are not retained at the required resolution, and the current compute queue assumes
each sensor family is analysed separately.”

The scientist nodded as though Dries had confirmed that there was no new detector.

Another member, an economist, leaned toward her camera. “The application refers to removal of
ordinary evidence. What does removal mean in this context?”

Kiki said, “A registered physical state will be altered inside a controlled volume. Conventional
records of the alteration will be withheld from the reconstruction team. They receive only the
coordinates, the time bound and the pre-registered sensor streams.”

“Destroyed?”

“Where ethically and legally permissible, yes.”

“Why?”

“Because otherwise we would be testing whether scientists can read a file.”

One of the hidden cameras unmuted long enough for a cough.

The chair said, “Your budget includes GPU capacity.”

“Record inversion is computationally expensive,” Lwazi said.

“You have not demonstrated record inversion.”

“Correct. The capacity is needed to determine whether it fails.”

“Could you determine that with less capacity?”

“Less quickly.”

“Then the answer is yes.”

Dries watched the budget shrink in real time.

They had asked for a sealed experimental volume, dedicated storage at two nodes, one month of Karoo
instrument time, a clean GPU allocation, an isolated timing system and enough travel for the five
technical leads to occupy the same room once before the run.

The committee removed the room first.

Technopark already had offices.

It removed the dedicated storage next. Ghana already operated storage.

Ama explained that a storage system designed to keep interpreted products was not equivalent to one
designed to preserve unsupported fragments from raw streams.

The committee restored one quarter of the request.

Travel disappeared without discussion.

The GPU allocation survived until a member from the national compute facility asked whether the
work had been classified as physics, computer science or digital humanities. Nobody could answer in
a way that put it inside an open queue.

“We can run a reduced solver on CPUs,” Dries said quietly.

Lwazi covered the table microphone with his hand. “In how many years?”

“If the volume is small, days.”

“You said the GPU path was essential.”

“I said it was expensive.”

“Those are not synonyms.”

“Not to engineers.”

Lwazi removed his hand. “We can accept deferred GPU access if the committee funds a commodity CPU
fallback for method validation.”

The chair looked relieved to encounter the word *commodity*.

“Cost?”

Dries gave it.

The sum was less than the meeting itself had probably cost.

It passed.

By the seventh minute, the grand experiment had become a borrowed office after hours, existing
sensors on best-effort schedules, one quarter of the storage, no travel, no new detector and a stack
of ordinary processors Technopark procurement could buy from three approved suppliers.

At the eighth minute, the economist asked what commercial product would result if they succeeded.

“None,” Lwazi said.

At the ninth, the chair rejected the application.

Then she invited them to resubmit under the university's seed-instrument programme with the reduced
budget, the phrase *fundamental persistence test* substituted for *record inversion*, and an
explicit undertaking that no human subject or historical material would be involved.

“That programme closes at noon,” Ama said.

The chair checked the time. “You have forty-one minutes.”

The call ended.

For two seconds nobody spoke.

Then Kiki said, “The undertaking changes the protocol.”

“Write it,” Dries said.

“It requires institutional execution.”

Lwazi was already replacing the title.

“Find the dean,” he said.

Ama opened the budget sheet. “Your storage number is fiction.”

“It was fiction before,” Dries said. “Now it is smaller.”

“Ghana will not delete unsupported fragments because Stellenbosch saved money in a committee.”

“Then we need another tier.”

“With what disks?”

“The ones you haven't told procurement you still have.”

Ama stared at him through the camera.

“That was a joke,” he said.

“No, it was not.”

Karoo's audio returned. “We can give you midnight to four on the low-noise array for six nights.”

“The application says a month,” Lwazi said.

“The application has thirty-seven minutes left to become honest.”

Antarctica's note remained open on the shared screen. Dries cut the temporal-reference work package
entirely. If the cheap test produced nothing, they would not need a clock for it. If it produced
something, Sanna could reject them in person.

The dean arrived with twelve minutes remaining and objected to signing an undertaking he had not
read.

Kiki read it to him.

He signed at 11:57.

The resubmission completed at 11:59:41. Its budget was eight per cent of the first application. Its
claims fit on one page. Its equipment list contained nothing a journalist would photograph.

At 12:06 the committee approved it by circulation.

Dries looked at the approval letter.

`FUNDAMENTAL PERSISTENCE TEST`, it said.

Below that, in the conditions:

`NO EXOTIC DETECTOR DEVELOPMENT AUTHORISED.`

Lwazi read over his shoulder. “Nobody gets the machine they want.”

“Good,” Ama said from Accra. “Then nobody gets to pretend they built it alone.”
