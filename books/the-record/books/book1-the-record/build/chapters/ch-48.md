# Chapter 48

Naledi could not stop the seizure.

The order named the commodity servers, engineering-yard samples and every copy of the historical
products in the rented office. A sheriff would execute it at eight on Friday morning. The payroll
company had already received notice that its neighbouring suite might be inaccessible during the
inventory.

The order was broad, careful and likely to survive appeal.

She found one defect.

It treated the substitute quorum's signed products as assets of the closed consortium, though four
signatures belonged to independent institutions and one path had been produced under Ghanaian
authority. The defect would not defeat seizure of the physical equipment. It could require a court
to separate custody before execution.

Her legal adviser gave the objection one chance in five.

“How long?” Naledi asked.

“If the duty judge hears it, four days. Perhaps less.”

“File it.”

“Cabinet instructed cooperation.”

“Cabinet instructed lawful cooperation.”

He looked at her until the distinction had become the decision.

Three months earlier, the machine's lack of legal legitimacy had justified containment. It still
lacked a statute, an appeal body and settled rules for people drawn into queries they had not
authorised. Naledi had not changed her view.

The facts beneath it had changed.

The first historical finding now had a family boundary, two independent physical paths, a public
failure audit and six institutions capable of disagreeing in their own names. Seize the system before
its root reached the public mirror and every later copy would descend from something government had
taken. The result would survive, if it survived, as a tolerated leak.

Legitimacy no longer required containment. It required enough daylight that authority could not
become authorship.

Naledi filed the objection under her ministerial signature.

The duty judge stayed execution until Monday afternoon.

Cabinet called before the sealed order reached the sheriff. Naledi let the first call ring. She
answered the second and declined to withdraw the filing. By the third, the president's office had
removed her from responsibility for the historical review and requested a written explanation of
her conduct.

She sent one sentence: `THE OBJECTION PRESERVES THIRD-PARTY CUSTODY`  
`PENDING JUDICIAL SEPARATION.`

It was accurate and convinced nobody.

She called the rented office from a personal device. The daughter's face occupied one small window.
Dries stood behind the server rack with a fan in his hand. Kiki had quorum receipts open. Lwazi was
reading the latest admissible-history count. Ama joined without video from an office she was no
longer authorised to enter.

“You have four days,” Naledi said.

“Why?” Lwazi asked.

“Because the order confuses ownership.”

“Why did you object?”

She gave the answer once.

“I am not delaying it because you are right. I am delaying it because in eleven years nobody will
be able to say you were only allowed to be right.”

The daughter asked what happened after four days.

“The sheriff inventories the room.”

“And you?”

“Someone else will take my calls.”

There was no thanks. Naledi had not expected any.

She ended the connection and entered a meeting about a road whose completion date had slipped again.
For forty minutes, officials argued over drainage, procurement and a bridge joint. Naledi took notes,
assigned two follow-ups and did not check the historical queue under the table.

Afterwards, her access to the review portal had been revoked.

The court stay remained.

Four days were the whole of her remaining power.

They were enough to make the next act belong to someone else.
