# Chapter 19

The building had existed for thirty-eight years and appeared on no map under the same name twice.

Dries began with the current lease plan. Before Technopark, the parcel belonged to a light-industrial
developer. Before that, to a holding company dissolved during the transition years. Earlier municipal
records described the district by farm portion, not street address. Electron Road did not yet exist.
Neither did the building around him.

He aligned the plans by survey markers rather than names.

The first overlay placed the sealed room above an access lane.

The second placed it inside a storage yard.

The third showed nothing but a boundary line and a rectangular omission where the draughtsman had
rendered adjacent structures in careful detail.

Dries requested the omitted sheet from the municipal archive.

It was unavailable.

He requested the predecessor reference listed in the index.

That file had been transferred to a provincial repository under a retention schedule no longer in
use.

The repository returned a scan stamped `DUPLICATE — WORKING COPY`. It showed drainage, temporary
power, a vehicle entrance and four unlabelled structures behind an internal fence. One occupied the
coordinates beneath the present office.

No purpose appeared on the plan.

At the lower right, a distribution list contained three initials, a municipal engineering office
and a state-security property unit that had ceased to exist before the first Technopark tenant moved
in.

Dries printed the page.

He walked downstairs and stood in the car park with the plan folded against the wind. The old vehicle
entrance ran beneath a row of parked hatchbacks. The internal fence crossed reception, continued
through a payroll-software company and ended near the coffee shop's outside tables. The temporary
structure occupied part of his building and part of the one next door.

History had not survived as ruins. It had survived as ordinary rent.

The facilities manager joined him carrying keys.

“What are we looking for?”

“A building before the building.”

“There were sheds here.”

“What kind?”

“Construction. Storage. This whole side was redeveloped.”

“Before redevelopment.”

The manager studied the plan. “Where did you get this?”

“Municipal archive.”

“That fence isn't on the transfer drawings.”

“No.”

“Neither is that power feed.”

“No.”

They followed the old coordinates through the building. A wall had moved. A passage had become two
offices. The sealed-room floor covered the edge of the temporary structure; the unresolved layer lay
inside it.

In a service cupboard behind reception, the manager found a blank steel plate covering a conduit no
current diagram used. He removed two screws. Behind it, old cable sleeves descended into the slab,
cut flush and sealed with resin.

Dries did not touch them.

He photographed the cupboard, registered its coordinates and called Kiki before calling Lwazi.

By the time he returned upstairs, Jacobs was waiting in his office.

The liaison had not signed in at reception.

“You have a result linked to historical material,” Jacobs said.

“We have an unresolved correlation layer.”

“Dated to the late apartheid period.”

Dries stopped at the door. That detail had not been published.

“Who told you?”

“I was asked to bring you into a briefing.”

“By whom?”

Jacobs gave him a secure meeting code.

The call contained Minister Naledi Maseko, two officials whose cameras remained off, Lwazi in Karoo,
Kiki in Nairobi and Ama in Accra. Sanna's signed finding appeared as a document because the Antarctic
radio window had closed.

Naledi did not waste time on congratulations.

“What can you establish?” she asked.

Lwazi answered. “A structured correlation layer at the site, independently bounded to the late
apartheid period.”

Ama added, “A stable phonetic segment compatible with the name of a missing person.”

“Can you establish that person was present?”

“No.”

“Can you establish what occurred?”

“No.”

“Can you reproduce the result?”

Kiki said, “The laboratory capability, yes. The historical query has not been authorised or
repeated.”

Naledi looked down, reading something outside the camera frame.

“The site was a temporary security installation,” she said. “Interviews, processing and short-term
holding. Its surviving files are incomplete and partly restricted.”

Dries unfolded the municipal plan on his desk.

“The missing man's file says he was never here.”

“Many files say many things.”

“The floor disagrees.”

“The floor has not been admitted as a witness.”

The phrase was careful enough to have been prepared before the call.

Naledi ordered the historical products contained pending independent verification, legal review and
contact with the surviving family. No public statement. No expanded query. No archive search seeded
with the missing man's identity. The sealed-room method could remain public; the historical finding
could not.

“For how long?” Lwazi asked.

“Until we know whether you have found evidence or manufactured an accusation with excellent
provenance.”

Nobody had a clean answer to that.

After the call, Dries returned to the corridor wall where the sealed-room reconstruction still hung
on paper. The supported table edge floated above the darker, older layer they had removed from the
published claim.

The experiment had not merely found a residue.

It had found a witness beneath its own office.
