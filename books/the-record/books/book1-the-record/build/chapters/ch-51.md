# Chapter 51

The family received one page and twelve appendices.

Ama placed the page on the attorney's table. Kiki placed the refusal receipt beneath it. The
derivation, uncertainty maps and competing contact histories remained in a sealed folder unless the
family asked to open them.

The mother read the summary first. She passed it to the uncle, who read it standing. The daughter
waited until both had finished.

“The security man moved the chair,” she said.

“Yes,” Ama replied.

“And that made my father fall.”

“That is the supported physical sequence.”

“You cannot say he meant to kill him.”

“No.”

The daughter moved her finger to the next paragraph.

“The other man came in.”

“During the fall.”

“He tried to help.”

Ama did not improve the finding for the room. “He physically intervened. We cannot recover his
purpose.”

“Then he helped them take my father away.”

“Yes.”

“And clean.”

“Yes.”

The mother asked whether the revered figure had called for medical help. No recoverable call left the
site during the interval. Whether he had asked someone inside to do so could not be established from
the permitted products.

The uncle opened the appendix containing the unresolved contact. Two sparse diagrams placed the same
hand-sized field against the falling body. One history accelerated it. The other opposed it.

“Which one do you believe?” he asked Lwazi.

Lwazi looked at the diagrams. “I do not know.”

The answer stayed in the room without defence.

The attorney explained the court's limited ruling and the planned public release. Lawyers for both
historical camps had filed objections. Neither objection changed the physical root. Criminal
proceedings, if any, would belong to another court and another standard of proof.

Then she placed a second document beside the finding.

The original instrument required written family agreement before publication. The consortium no
longer existed to request it, so the replacement quorum had supplied a release schedule naming every
object that would become public: the one-page finding, twelve appendices, refusal receipt, protocol
failure, raw fragments, solver trace and external objections.

The daughter read the list.

“His photograph?”

“No,” Kiki said.

“The generated face?”

“Excluded.”

“The fear question?”

“The question and refusal, if you permit both.”

The mother asked what happened if they refused publication.

“The court-held products remain under seal,” the attorney said. “The Ghanaian and public technical
roots may still establish parts of the sequence. The false video remains public.”

It was not a free choice. The person who had published a face without permission had helped create
the reason to publish the faceless correction.

The daughter added one condition: the refusal had to appear beside the finding, not in an ethics
appendix. Her mother added another: no outlet would receive the result before the public mirrors.
The uncle asked that the competing push and catch histories remain adjacent wherever either diagram
appeared.

Kiki wrote all three into the execution root.

The family signed the release schedule separately. Permission to publish did not amend permission to
ask.

Nobody asked whether this was closure.

The daughter returned the summary to the top of the folder. It gave her father's presence, his
departure, the chair, the fall, the vehicle and the work of concealment. It did not give a body or a
grave. It did not choose between push and catch. It did not answer the question preserved in the
refusal receipt.

She closed the appendices.

“With the scans you already have,” she said, “can somebody ask again after we take it back?”

“Our copies retain your boundary,” Ama replied. “The external query proved another operator can ask
the site without the token. Returning the object does not return the whole capability to you.”

The daughter looked at the release schedule she had just signed.

“Then put that in the public explanation too.”

Kiki added it.

“I want the token back.”

The attorney said the evidence custodian still held it under the old transfer order. The new court
direction permitted an application for return once its existing scans were fixed and no further
physical analysis was sought.

“Are you seeking any?” the daughter asked.

Ama said no.

Kiki said no.

Lwazi said no.

The attorney wrote the application before anyone left the table.

Two days later, a court officer returned the sealed tray. The daughter checked the custody number,
opened it and compared the object with the intake photographs before lifting the damaged brass into
the tobacco tin herself. The blackened edge, protruding gear and crescent of glass remained where
they had been. Nobody had cleaned away the years.

Her mother held the lid while the daughter lowered the token. The officer offered the family a new
evidence bag for storage. They declined it. The tin had never been part of the machine's custody and
would not become official now.

The watch still read 21:14.

She closed the lid.
