# Chapter 43

The facilities manager remembered where the old concrete went.

“Not all of it,” he told Dries. “The clean spoil went to fill. The cut stone went back to the
contractor because his father believed dressed material should never be crushed.”

“Where is the contractor?”

“Dead.”

“And the stone?”

“Probably exactly where he left it.”

The invoice was thirty-eight years old. It listed demolition, transport and storage without an
address. A later tax record tied the contractor to an engineering yard beyond the industrial edge
of town, where the road narrowed, lost its markings and passed through vineyards before returning to
warehouses.

Dries drove there with Lwazi and a Nairobi custody form open on his phone.

The present owner met them beside a lathe older than all three men. He was the contractor's son and
had inherited the yard, the invoices and his father's refusal to discard anything that retained a
straight edge.

“People pay me to remove old work,” he said. “Then they pay somebody else for new old-looking stone.”

He led them behind two sheds. Under several tarpaulins lay window frames, broken lintels, granite
offcuts, lengths of rail and stacks of concrete cut into blocks too irregular to sell. Every pile
carried a metal tag wired through a drilled hole. Rust had eaten most of the writing.

The Technopark job occupied three pallets beneath a blue cover turned grey by weather.

Dries checked the transport docket against the archive invoice. The quantities disagreed. The truck
registration matched. So did the date and the contractor's handwritten site abbreviation.

Kiki joined by video before anyone moved a block.

“You are not under the current site order,” she told the owner. “The material belongs to you. You may
refuse examination, sampling or transfer.”

“Will you take it away if I say yes?”

“Only the registered sample.”

“Then no.”

Lwazi stared at him.

The owner waited until discomfort had done its work. “You may sample it here.”

He produced a hammer.

Kiki refused the hammer because it had not been cleaned or registered. The owner found that
entertaining. Dries had brought a sealed masonry bit, sample bags and a portable scanner from the
ordinary building-maintenance kit. They photographed the pallet, fixed its coordinates and chose
three blocks by a random sequence generated before the cover came off.

The first sample contained modern sealant.

The second came from poured concrete with aggregate inconsistent with the old foundation.

The third carried a narrow mineral inclusion: quartz, iron-rich mica and a trace distribution found
in one microscopic cavity on the family's token.

Lwazi compared the spectra twice.

“Same source?” Dries asked.

“Compatible mineral environment.”

“How compatible?”

“Enough to justify independent analysis. Not enough to tell a story in this yard.”

They took three more samples under the same sequence. Two repeated the inclusion. One contained a
resin fragment matching the sealed cable sleeves behind Technopark reception.

The stone had occupied the temporary installation before redevelopment, left during construction
and spent decades beneath a tarpaulin. It lay outside the seized coordinates, outside the Anchor's
broken custody and outside the consortium's suspended cloud.

It did not lie outside history.

Kiki issued a new custody root naming the owner, the yard and every contamination visible in the
scan. The owner signed only after adding a condition in his own handwriting: the drilled blocks
would be returned and nobody would clean them.

“Cleaning removes the value,” Dries said.

“Cleaning removes the age,” the owner replied.

They left with six sealed cores and no stone.

In the car, Lwazi opened Mercer's governance schedule, looked at it and closed it again.

The most advanced experiment they had attempted now depended on a mismatched server cabinet, a
maintenance scanner and material preserved by one family's habit of keeping what other people paid
to forget.
