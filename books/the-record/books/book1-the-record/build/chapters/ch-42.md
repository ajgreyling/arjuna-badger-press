# Chapter 42

The statement was true when Naledi signed it.

`THE CIRCULATING AUDIOVISUAL FILE`  
`IS A SYNTHETIC RECONSTRUCTION`  
`AND DOES NOT CONSTITUTE`  
`A CERTIFIED OUTPUT`  
`OF THE AFRICAN RECORD CONSORTIUM.`

She had fought for every limiting word. *Circulating.* *Audiovisual.* *Certified output.* Cabinet
lawyers accepted them because the leaked faces, speech and camera were generated. The government
could rebut a destabilising forgery without taking a position on the sealed historical findings.

At noon, the statement went public.

The first broadcaster shortened it to `GOVERNMENT DECLARES`  
`APARTHEID VIDEO SYNTHETIC`.

The second used `HISTORICAL RECONSTRUCTION`  
`IS DISINFORMATION`.

By the afternoon, an official party account said the entire claim had been manufactured by foreign-
funded scientists. An opposition account said the government had admitted suppressing genuine
footage. Both linked to the smooth video.

Nobody linked to Kiki's audit.

The same cabinet decision closed the consortium project pending legislation and criminal review of
the unauthorised query. Historical work lost its operating authority. The court kept the Anchor.
Cloud access remained suspended. Staff could preserve existing records and conduct no further
inversion at the site.

Naledi called the family's attorney before the closure notice arrived through ordinary service.

“Is it the government's position that he was not there?” the attorney asked.

“No. The statement concerns the fabricated video.”

“Will you release the certified presence finding?”

“It remains under court seal.”

“Then the government has published the denial and sealed the distinction.”

“The statement is not a denial.”

“You still think the nouns will save you.”

The attorney ended the call.

Naledi read the statement once more. It remained accurate. Accuracy had not survived distribution
any better than the witnesses' sightlines.

That evening, she joined the five nodes from a committee room emptied for recess. Dries reported
that the commodity servers were off. Ama had archived the Rain. Kiki had copied the ledger into
three university repositories before the closure took effect. Sanna's minority finding remained
public in Antarctica and uncertified by the consortium.

Lwazi told them about Mercer.

He displayed the governance schedule, restored-compute guarantee and ninety-day disclosure pause.
Nobody accused Mercer of lying. That made the discussion worse.

“It preserves the work,” Lwazi said.

“Under one African vote,” Ama replied.

“Under twelve votes.”

“Eleven of which can decide Africa is the interested party in its own history.”

Kiki asked whether Mercer's trust would recognise the family's consent instrument.

“Contractually.”

“The current outsider did not.”

“The trust would have courts behind it.”

Dries asked whether the CPU path formed part of the transfer.

“All operational code.”

“Then their first act of stewardship is disarmament here.”

Lwazi did not deny it.

Naledi listened as the project divided along the lines she had predicted and helped create. Waiting
for national law left the leak uncontested. Accepting Mercer saved the record by surrendering the
jurisdiction that had made the project possible. Running Dries's fallback would violate the closure
and confirm every accusation of covert capability.

The original Anchor was gone from them. Central compute was gone. Full consortium authority no
longer existed.

Ama asked whether the site's present materials could serve as an independent Anchor.

Dries shook his head. Redevelopment had replaced too much, and anything still inside the old
coordinates fell under the order.

“Then we need material that left before the current building,” she said.

Lwazi closed Mercer's schedule.

The project had been declared finished.

For the first time, they began discussing how to continue without it.
