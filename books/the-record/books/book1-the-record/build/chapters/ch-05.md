# Chapter 5

The national-security liaison wanted to know what came next.

Dries told him another measurement.

The answer was true, which made it useful for almost three minutes.

They met in a ground-floor boardroom named after a company that no longer existed. Its logo had
been removed from the frosted glass, leaving a clean shape where years of fingerprints had not
reached. The liaison introduced himself as Mr Jacobs, offered no rank and placed a closed notebook
on the table between them.

He was younger than Dries had expected and less theatrical. No dark suit. No warnings. He had driven
from Cape Town in ordinary traffic and apologised for being late.

“When you say another measurement,” Jacobs said, “you mean another physical constant?”

“That is what the project is funded to test.”

“And after that?”

“If it fails, we publish the failure.”

“If it succeeds?”

“We publish the survival.”

“And after that?”

Dries looked at the closed notebook. “Do you have a required number of afters?”

Jacobs smiled. “I have been asked to understand whether this morning's result creates a capability.”

“It doesn't.”

“Could it?”

“Everything creates a capability if you write the briefing broadly enough.”

“Mr Venter.”

“Mr Jacobs.”

The liaison opened the notebook at last. The page contained three handwritten lines. Dries could not
read them upside down.

“Does the work imply that information about a physical event remains recoverable after ordinary
evidence has been destroyed?” Jacobs asked.

The fixed-line caller had taken forty minutes to reach government.

Or government had reached them both.

“No,” Dries said. “It implies that G uses the word *Record* in a formal way that makes physicists
angry when engineers think it means storage.”

“Does it mean storage?”

“No.”

“What does it mean?”

“Ask a physicist.”

“I did. They sent me to you.”

That was how Dries knew Jacobs was telling the truth.

He stood. “Come upstairs.”

The consortium had been scheduled to spend the afternoon selecting the next vulnerable expression.
By the time Dries brought Jacobs into the technical room, the meeting had already divided into the
people who wanted the most precise available measurement and the people who wanted the shortest
paper.

Lwazi stood at the whiteboard beside a list of candidate tests. Nobody acknowledged Jacobs directly.
His visitor badge did it for him.

“No,” Lwazi said when Dries explained the question.

Jacobs took a chair against the wall. “No, the information is not recoverable?”

“No, the framework does not say what you said.”

“What does it say?”

Lwazi pointed to the top of the board, where somebody had written the sentence from the corpus:

`WHAT HAS HAPPENED CANNOT UNHAPPEN.`

“Actualisation is directional,” he said. “An event that occurred is part of the realised structure.
That does not mean the universe keeps a video.”

“I didn't say video.”

“Not yet.”

“Could the realised structure be measured?”

“The present can be measured.”

“Could the present contain consequences of the event?”

“Obviously.”

“Then could those consequences be used to infer the event?”

The room became quieter.

Dries went to the board and underlined *cannot unhappen*.

“This,” he said, “is the part everyone reads as philosophy because it has no units.”

“It is philosophy,” Lwazi said.

“It is in a physics corpus.”

“Physicists are permitted philosophy. We are simply punished when engineers read it.”

Dries ignored him. “If an event creates a record, what physical operation releases that record when
the normal traces disappear?”

“Nothing releases it. That is the wrong model.”

“Fine. What physical operation removes the traces?”

“Many operations.”

“Name one.”

“Thermalisation. Scattering. Decoherence. Chemical change. Mechanical destruction. Information
disperses into degrees of freedom you cannot reconstruct.”

“Disperses,” Dries said. “Not vanishes.”

“For every practical purpose, those are the same.”

“Storage engineers built an industry inside that sentence.”

Somebody at the back laughed. Lwazi did not.

“You are imagining a recoverable log,” he said. “There is no log. There is no address. There is no
read head. There is no guarantee that the present constraints are sufficient to distinguish one
past microstate from another.”

“Good. Now we have a test.”

“We have several reasons not to waste instrument time.”

Dries wrote beneath the sentence:

`WHAT DOES DELETION COST?`

Lwazi put the marker cap down very carefully. “Deletion of what?”

“A bit. A state. Anything. If the event cannot unhappen, and its ordinary record is erased, where is
the physical bill?”

“You are mixing semantic information with thermodynamic information.”

“I am asking for the invoice.”

“Landauer,” said a voice from the far end of the table.

The correction came from a physicist named Marietjie Smit, who had spent most of the meeting
annotating an uncertainty table and had not previously looked up.

“Ralph?” Dries asked.

“Rolf,” she said.

“Rolf Landauer. Not Ralph.”

“I said Ralph?”

“You did not say either. I am preventing the next ten years.”

She stood, took the marker from Lwazi and wrote:

`E >= kBT ln 2`

“IBM, 1961,” she said. “Logically irreversible erasure has a minimum thermodynamic cost. At room
temperature, roughly two point eight seven zeptojoules per bit, dissipated as heat.”

Jacobs opened his notebook to a fresh page.

“This has been measured?” he asked.

“The bound has been experimentally approached in single-particle systems. Bérut and collaborators,
2012. Later experiments in other substrates.”

Dries looked at the equation. “So deletion leaves heat.”

“Erasure requires dissipation. Do not turn one laboratory principle into your cosmic filing
cabinet.”

“Information is physical.”

“Yes.”

“Forgetting is an operation.”

“At the level Landauer describes, yes.”

“And the operation has a price.”

“A minimum price. This does not imply the price contains an invertible description of what was
erased.”

Dries wrote `2.87 zJ` beneath the equation.

He had expected a metaphor and been given a unit.

“Landauer worked out the price of forgetting in 1961,” he said. “Nobody thought to ask for the
receipt.”

Marietjie capped the marker. “Many people asked. Most of them understood the paper.”

“Can the heat be measured?” Jacobs asked.

“Not as a historical label saying *this bit died here*,” she said. “Real systems dissipate vastly
more than the minimum. Heat diffuses. Environments interact. You are not going to point a thermal
camera at an archive and read the pages that were burned.”

The liaison underlined something in his notebook.

Lwazi noticed. “That was not an operational suggestion.”

“Understood.”

“Was it?” Dries asked.

Jacobs closed the notebook again. “I am here to understand implications.”

“Then understand this one,” Lwazi said. “Landauer is established physics. Recoverable residue from
arbitrary historical events is not. We have not detected it. We have not shown it persists. We have
not shown the inverse problem has a unique solution. If your briefing joins those statements into a
capability, it will be fiction written by government.”

Jacobs received the rebuke without changing expression. “And if the experiment were narrow?”

“Which experiment?”

Dries turned to the candidate list on the board. The constants tests were elegant, expensive and
already inhabited by better-equipped laboratories. Another surviving interval would improve the
framework's reputation. Another failed one would improve its honesty. Neither would answer the
stranger question.

“We create a documented physical state,” he said. “We observe it with instruments we already have.
Then we remove the ordinary evidence and look for correlated changes across the residual streams.”

“You break something,” Marietjie said.

“If that helps.”

“It always helps engineers.”

“No new detector,” Lwazi said. He sounded as though he were setting bail conditions. “No claim about
history. No claim about memory. A persistence experiment in a controlled volume, nothing more.”

“Available sensors,” Dries said. “Borrowed time. Commodity compute.”

“And a pre-registered failure condition.”

“Nairobi will demand three.”

“Nairobi will be right.”

Jacobs stood. “Would such an experiment have national-security implications?”

Everyone looked at the board.

The equation was small. The sentence above it was not.

“No,” Lwazi said.

At the same moment, Dries said, “Not yet.”

Jacobs looked from one to the other, thanked them for the orientation and left with his notebook.

The room listened until the outer door closed.

Then Lwazi erased `WHAT DOES DELETION COST?`

Dries wrote it back.
