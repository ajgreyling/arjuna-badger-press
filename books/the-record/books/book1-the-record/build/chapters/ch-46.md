# Chapter 46

Kiki published the failure before she asked anyone to trust her replacement.

The paper was fourteen pages long. Its title contained no apology: `VALID AUTHORITY, UNSUPPORTED
INTERPRETATION: FAILURE MODES IN THE WITNESS PROTOCOL`. It reproduced the signed smooth-query
instructions, the ninety-one-centimetre selection, the verified hashes and the sentence she had
spoken during the original room design.

Integrity could preserve a lie honestly.

She signed the paper alone.

The standards committee suspended her chair pending review. A university withdrew her from a
conference panel on trustworthy evidence. Two colleagues asked privately why she had not described
the leak as an implementation error instead of a protocol failure.

Because the implementation had followed the protocol, she replied.

Then she began calling institutions with no reason to rescue her reputation.

The first university held the original sealed-room design minutes. The second had publicly criticised
the historical project and could verify the offline derivation without being accused of protecting
it. A medical council understood consent that survived institutional convenience. A church archive
held politically dangerous records from opposing movements. A trade union had members on both sides
of the present dispute. A municipal library ran the public mirror no ministry had managed to close.

None controlled the machine.

Kiki asked each to hold one quorum key.

The church archivist wanted the right to stop any query touching sacred or pastoral material. The
medical council refused a category-wide veto that could conceal abuse. The trade union demanded that
technical objections be published in ordinary language. One university wanted a conventional peer-
review delay. The library would join only if every refusal receipt reached the public mirror with the
findings.

They spent nine days failing to agree.

On the tenth, Kiki removed the requirement that the institutions endorse one governance policy.
They needed to attest separate facts: custody, executable consent, source independence, derivation,
clock continuity and public availability. Four of six keys could open a checkpoint. No combination
could alter a question after the daughter approved it. Any institution could publish its objection
without invalidating the other signatures.

“That is not consensus,” the university lawyer said.

“No,” Kiki replied. “It is contestability.”

The medical council signed the consent role. The critical university signed derivation. The first
university signed source custody. The library signed public mirroring. The trade union accepted the
plain-language disclosure role after rewriting half the form. The church archive joined last and
recorded its unresolved objection to the lack of a sacred-material veto.

The objection travelled with its key.

Antarctica supplied clock continuity. Sanna could not ask an institution to certify a station none
of its representatives could reach during winter, so she opened the custody log to the crew who had
maintained the oscillator, power and safety routes around ARIADNE. The cook had signed generator
transfers. A mechanic had witnessed timing swaps. The station doctor held duty records showing who
could have entered the instrument space.

Sanna put their names beside hers.

Kiki checked the attestations and found three small disagreements in time notation. Sanna did not
correct the crew into uniformity; she attached the mappings and let the differences remain visible.

The substitute quorum met for the first time on an audio call so poor that the church archivist
mistook the payroll company's hold music for an interruption from government. Nobody laughed until
the second time.

Kiki displayed the execution root. Six institutions, the family witness and the engineering-yard
custodian could stop or challenge different parts. None could certify the entire result alone.

The critical university representative asked the question everyone else had approached politely.

“Why should this quorum trust the person who designed the failed one?”

“It should not,” Kiki said. “That is why I do not hold a key.”

The answer cost her less than she expected and more than she showed.

Four signatures arrived.

The first historical checkpoint opened.
