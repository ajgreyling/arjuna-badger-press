# Chapter 8

Kiki rejected the room because it had a smart light switch.

“We can disconnect it,” Dries said.

“Then it becomes a disconnected smart light switch whose memory we have not examined.”

“We can remove it.”

“Who records the removal?”

“A camera.”

“No cameras.”

They had been inside the candidate room for four minutes.

It was an unused training space on the second floor of a Technopark office, windowless, recently
painted and almost empty. A folding table stood in the centre. Two plastic chairs leaned against the
wall. The ceiling contained six recessed lights, one smoke detector, one motion sensor, two air vents
and a square panel nobody could identify without a ladder.

Kiki looked up at the panel.

“Wi-Fi access point,” Dries said.

“Remove it.”

“It hasn't been powered for three years.”

“Then removing it should not inconvenience anyone.”

Lwazi stood in the doorway with the expression of a man watching grant money evaporate into screws.
“The experiment needs a room, not an archaeological clearance.”

“The experiment needs a boundary,” Kiki said. “This room has at least nine ways of remembering what
happens in it.”

“The smoke detector does not remember.”

Dries looked at it. “Depends on the model.”

Lwazi closed his eyes.

The Nairobi design team had travelled through the night because the consortium's travel budget had
been removed and Kiki had found a university exchange line that could pay only if she delivered a
seminar while in Stellenbosch. She had lectured for two hours that morning on threshold signatures,
answered twenty-seven student questions, and arrived at the room carrying tamper seals, paper forms
and no patience for improvisation presented as efficiency.

She opened her clipboard.

“The operator enters after the room is sealed,” she said. “No camera, microphone or networked sensor
inside. The action is selected only after sealing. The reconstruction team receives coordinates and
time bounds, not the action key. Every object is weighed, photographed and registered before entry.”

“Photographed by the camera we aren't allowed to use,” Dries said.

“Outside the room.”

“And if an object changes inside?”

“That is the purpose of the test.”

“If I cannot see the system state, I cannot debug it.”

“If you can see the action, there is nothing to reconstruct.”

“Observability is not the same as knowing the answer.”

“It is when the answer is an observable event.”

Dries walked to the table. “Suppose the run fails. We get nothing. Was there no residue? Did Karoo
miss it? Did Ghana demote it? Did the clock drift? Did the solver fail? Did the operator perform the
action incorrectly? Your design gives us no way to distinguish an empty result from a broken
pipeline.”

“Correct.”

“That is not experimental design.”

“It is blind experimental design.”

“Blindness is not a virtue when everyone is blind to a different thing.”

Kiki wrote the sentence down.

“What are you doing?”

“Keeping the useful part.”

They left the room and convened in the corridor because Kiki would not allow design discussion
inside the future test boundary once the inspection had begun. Dries sat on the floor against the
wall. Lwazi remained standing. Two technicians arrived with a ladder and began removing devices
from the ceiling under a paper chain-of-custody form Kiki had produced before anyone asked.

“Separate diagnostic envelopes,” she said. “Each node receives enough information to verify its own
layer and no more. Karoo knows the sensor schedule, not the selected action. Ghana knows stream
identity and transformation rules, not the room inventory. Technopark gets the registered sensor
products, coordinates and time bound. Nairobi holds the action key.”

“Who verifies the operator?” Lwazi asked.

“A witness who does not see the reconstruction.”

“That witness creates a conventional record.”

“Yes.”

“Which must be destroyed.”

“After independent verification of what occurred.”

Dries looked up. “Destroyed how?”

“Secure deletion for digital records. Physical destruction for paper.”

“Logged?”

“Every operation is logged.”

“So the deletion record survives.”

“The fact of deletion survives. The content does not.”

“And if the content survives somewhere else?”

“Then the run is invalid.”

“How do you prove it doesn't?”

Kiki did not answer immediately. That was one reason Dries trusted her despite finding her almost
impossible to work with: she did not use speed to disguise a missing answer.

“We cannot prove a negative across systems we do not control,” she said. “We can register the
approved record paths, attest their destruction and expose the procedure to challenge.”

“So the ledger proves the room event is true.”

“No.”

“The operator and witness sign it.”

“They sign that an action key was executed and witnessed.”

“The objects are registered.”

“They sign that those objects entered under the stated custody.”

“The sensor roots are anchored.”

“They establish which data the instruments emitted.”

Dries stood. “Then what does your ledger prove?”

Kiki shifted the clipboard into one hand.

“I can prove what we recorded. I cannot prove our interpretation is correct.”

The technicians stopped working above them.

It was not a dramatic sentence in her voice. It was a limitation, stated before anybody could build
a reputation on forgetting it.

Lwazi said, “Then scientific truth comes from independent repetition.”

“And contestable interpretation,” Kiki said. “Integrity gets the evidence into the room. It does not
decide what the evidence means.”

Dries looked through the open doorway at the table. His instinct was to instrument every failure
path until the experiment became observable enough to repair. Kiki's design made repair harder on
purpose. It treated his access as contamination.

The irritating part was that she was right.

“Mechanical selection for half the actions,” he said. “Human choice for the rest. If the solver sees
only one class, we learn something about the operator channel.”

Kiki considered it. “The mechanical selector must be registered before sealing and actuated after.”

“By the operator.”

“Without the operator knowing the resulting action.”

Lwazi said, “We can use sealed instruction capsules selected by a physical randomiser.”

“No network,” Kiki said.

“A physical randomiser.”

“I am repeating it for Dries.”

The access point came down from the ceiling. Behind it, the technician found an active cable.

Dries traced the label back to a switch in the adjacent office. The port had transmitted fourteen
bytes during their inspection.

“What were they?” Lwazi asked.

“Keepalive from the old controller, probably.”

Kiki held out an evidence bag.

Dries disconnected the cable and placed its labelled end inside.

“We need another room,” he said.

“We need to understand this one first,” she replied.

The design meeting lasted three days.

The first run would take eleven minutes.
