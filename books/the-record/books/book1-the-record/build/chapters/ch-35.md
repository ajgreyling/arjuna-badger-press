# Chapter 35

The television producer wanted the past to weigh something.

“Three point one nine times ten to the minus thirty-eight kilograms per bit,” she said, reading from
her tablet. “A full drive is heavier than an empty drive. That is the published claim.”

Lwazi watched G push the studio makeup cloth away from his face.

“Published where?” G asked.

The producer named the paper and its author.

“Proposed, then. Not measured.”

“The distinction will be in the interview.”

“Before or after the animation of tiny silver bits falling onto a scale?”

She did not answer.

The consortium had agreed to the interview because the smooth reconstruction had supplied its own
explanation. According to the leak's promoters, physical information persisted as a retrievable
substance; Lucid found that substance and converted it back into pictures. A substance could be
complete. Missing faces became a software failure or, worse, a political choice.

The claim was easier to say than constrained inversion across dispersed consequences. It also had a
number.

Lwazi had spent the morning briefing G on the damage. He wanted a clean distinction the public could
carry away: Landauer described the thermodynamic price of erasure; the consortium measured present
physical consequences and solved backward; Vopson's information-mass proposal was separate,
contested and unnecessary to either.

G listened until Lwazi finished.

“What kills it?” he asked.

“Which part?”

“The mass claim.”

“A sufficiently sensitive comparison between encoded states.”

“Do we have one?”

“Not cleanly.”

“Does anybody?”

“There have been proposed experiments.”

“That was not my question.”

Lwazi admitted there was no decisive measurement available to them.

The studio counted down. The host introduced G as the theorist behind the equation that had made it
possible to see the past. G objected before the introduction ended.

“No equation does that.”

The host smiled with professional patience. “But your work says information is physical.”

“Landauer showed that erasure has a thermodynamic cost. Physical implementation matters. That is
not the same as saying information is a material stored in the walls for us to collect.”

On a monitor behind them, the silver bits began falling onto a scale.

The host turned to it. “A physicist has calculated the mass of one bit.”

“A physicist has proposed a relation.”

“Could it be true?”

This was the moment the press office had hoped to use. G could say possible. The host could say
scientifically plausible. The consortium would gain a sentence strong enough to compete with the
leak.

G looked at Lwazi beyond the cameras.

“It might even be true,” he said. “I have no way to make it wrong yet, so I have no use for it.”

The host tried again. “Surely an untested theory can still guide research.”

“A testable one can.”

“Are you saying the information-mass theory is pseudoscience?”

“I am saying I cannot kill it. I do not adopt claims because they are difficult to kill.”

By the end of the segment, the host had asked three times whether the consortium's own founder
rejected its science. G corrected the premise each time and made it sound less friendly.

Outside the studio, Lwazi's phone filled with messages from the press office. One university partner
paused its public endorsement. A sponsor requested an urgent explanation of whether the theoretical
basis remained valid.

“You could have said it was separate and stopped,” Lwazi told him.

“I did.”

“You also made it sound worthless.”

“To me.”

“That qualification will not survive.”

G folded the rejected makeup cloth and placed it on the producer's table. “Then do not build the work
on a sentence that requires qualifications to be hidden.”

Lwazi wanted to tell him that public trust did not behave like a derivation. Instead he replayed the
answer and heard the refusal the project needed, delivered in the least useful way available.

For a week, the consortium lost the argument.

The claim remained outside the machine.
