# Chapter 22

The daughter returned two days later with a tobacco tin.

She placed it where the photograph had been and did not open it.

This meeting contained only Ama, Kiki and the attorney. No other family members. No scientists on a
screen. Kiki had registered the table as a temporary custody surface without knowing what would be
placed on it.

The daughter had chosen the smaller meeting. Her mother had lived with the returned object in the
house for twenty-six years and did not want to watch another institution put a number on it. Her
uncle wanted to come and had been asked not to. The family had agreed that one person would carry
their questions into the room and nobody would mistake attendance for consent.

“This came back to us eleven years after he disappeared,” the daughter said.

“From whom?” Ama asked.

“No name. Padded envelope. Posted locally.”

“Was there a letter?”

“One sentence. *This should be with you.* We gave the envelope and note to the inquiry. They kept
them. This was returned.”

She opened the tin.

Inside lay a damaged brass identity token fused along one edge to part of a watch mechanism. Heat or
force had deformed the token enough to obscure some stamped characters. A gear protruded from the
blackened metal. The watch face was gone, but two hands remained trapped beneath a crescent of
cracked glass.

Ama did not touch it.

The object was ugly, small and immediately more persuasive than it had any right to be.

“How do you know it was his?”

“The number.”

The daughter indicated the surviving stamp. It matched an employment identifier in the family's
records and a photograph of a token he had worn on a work lanyard years before his disappearance.

“That supports identity,” Kiki said. “It does not establish where the object was during the missing
years.”

“We know.”

“The anonymous return breaks legal custody.”

“We know that too.”

The attorney slid a folder across the table. It contained the postal receipt recorded by the family,
the inquiry's intake note, the later return record and photographs taken before anyone cleaned the
object. Emotionally, the provenance formed a straight line. Legally, most of the line was assertion.

The last page was not evidence. It was a list in the daughter's handwriting.

No cutting. No polishing. No chemical cleaning. No separation of the fused parts. No transfer to a
state office. The family would receive every scan made from the object and could ask for its return
without explaining why. If an instrument required destructive sampling, the request would come back
to this table before anyone opened a tool.

Kiki read the conditions into the temporary custody record.

“The university normally retains an evidentiary object until analysis is complete,” she said.

“It retained my father until analysis was impossible,” the daughter replied. “This is not going into
another building on a normal rule.”

Ama looked at Kiki.

“Then the object remains family property under a revocable loan,” Kiki said. “The custodian can hold
it. The consortium cannot own it.”

The attorney added a return date and made them initial it.

“Your machine needs something from the event,” the daughter said.

“It needs an Anchor that can constrain the query,” Ama replied. “This may have participated in the
event. It may also have been damaged elsewhere, attached later or returned to make you believe a
particular story.”

“Can you tell which?”

“Potentially. Not by assuming the story.”

Kiki set a clean evidence tray beside the tin. The daughter lifted the object herself and placed it
inside. Custody began where ignorance ended; the earlier gap remained visible in the record.

The tobacco tin did not enter custody. The daughter kept it beside her elbow. It had held the token
in a kitchen cupboard, then a bank box, then the bottom drawer of her mother's wardrobe. None of
those locations made the metal evidence of anything except the family's refusal to throw it away.

Ama photographed only the object, not the family documents. A portable scanner mapped its surface
without removing corrosion. The first instrument pass identified brass, steel, degraded lubricant,
glass and mineral inclusions too small to classify in the room.

The watch hands had stopped at 21:14.

Ama sent that fact alone to Sanna with no context beyond the registered Anchor scan and historical
interval.

The Antarctic response arrived during the next radio window.

`21:14 IS NOT COMPATIBLE`  
`WITH THE CURRENT TEMPORAL WINDOW.`

The daughter read the printed message.

“Then one of you is wrong.”

“Yes,” Ama said.

“Which one?”

“We do not know.”

The object could be false. Antarctica could be wrong. The watch might have stopped for a reason
unrelated to the disappearance. Or one piece of matter could carry consequences from more than one
event, layered across years nobody had yet separated.

The daughter closed the empty tobacco tin.

“If you decide it is false,” she said, “you return it.”

“Yes.”

“If you decide it proves nothing.”

“Yes.”

“If the government says it must stay with the case.”

Kiki did not answer for the government. “We record the objection and notify you before any transfer
we can see.”

The daughter looked at the sealed tray. “That is less than a promise.”

“It is less.”

She left the tin open after that, an empty place the object was meant to come back to.

“You may examine it,” she said. “You may not ask whatever you like.”

The attorney placed a blank consent schedule on the table.

“Then,” Kiki said, “we write down the questions before the machine learns how to answer them.”
