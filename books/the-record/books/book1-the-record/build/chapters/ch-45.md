# Chapter 45

The new laboratory shared a wall with a payroll company.

Dries rented the office under his own name because the consortium no longer existed as a legal
tenant. It contained six desks, a kitchenette, a window facing another building and a carpet whose
pattern made spilled coffee difficult to prove. The payroll company occupied the other half of the
floor and complained on the first day about fan noise.

There was no historical significance in the coordinates.

That was the point.

They covered the window, removed the wireless access point and traced every cable entering the room.
Power remained the only external service. Dries ran it through a registered meter, batteries and the
isolated timing oscillator installed after the Technopark generator rehearsal. The building clock
could step, the network could lie and the municipal supply could fail; the oscillator would hold one
local sequence long enough for a checkpoint.

The four commodity servers filled a metal rack borrowed from a closed call centre. One case was too
wide, so Dries removed its side panel and registered the change. The reduced corpus shard occupied
local storage. Certified sensor fragments arrived on sealed drives from each node and entered only
after their hashes were checked against copies published before the shutdown.

No cloud account appeared anywhere in the build.

The daughter sat at the sixth desk while Kiki tested the stop control. Dries submitted a synthetic
room query, waited until the first CPU block began and told her to press it whenever she chose.

She pressed immediately.

The current block ran for another forty-three seconds. Its completion hash entered the local ledger.
The result remained encrypted and unavailable. The next block never started.

“Why doesn't it stop at once?” she asked.

“Interrupting mid-block can leave an output whose custody cannot be established. The control stops
the result from opening and prevents continuation.”

“So the machine finishes a thought I am not allowed to read.”

“It finishes a computation.”

She looked at him.

“Yes,” Dries said. “That distinction matters to me more than it may matter to you.”

They repeated the test with the network cable connected but physically cut inside a transparent
box, then with municipal power removed. The office went dark. The servers dropped to battery. The
payroll company lost its router and somebody swore through the wall.

The oscillator held.

On the fourth day, Dries loaded the first engineering-yard sample. The interface showed mineral
constraints, contamination fields and an admissible-history count. It offered no image pane. The
rendering module could draw load paths, measured surfaces and uncertainty volumes; it contained no
model capable of completing faces, speech or missing walls.

The missing detail was not an ethical setting. The software to invent it was absent.

On the seventh day, a foreign delegation arrived at the old Technopark reception asking to see the
History Machine. The facilities manager sent them to the rented floor. They walked past a company
selling payroll compliance, found four unmatched servers behind a folding screen and asked when the
visualisation suite would be installed.

Dries said it would not.

They stayed twelve minutes.

On day eleven, the reduced solver completed its calibration against the sealed-room public test. It
recovered less spatial detail than the cloud path and preserved every feature the test had certified.
Where resolution failed, the display ended.

No line softened. No surface guessed its continuation. The sparse version looked worse and carried
fewer ways to lie.

Dries printed the calibration receipt. The daughter signed as witness. The engineering-yard owner
confirmed the sample custody from his phone and reminded them that the cores had to come back.

The machine was ready to ask the three questions.

It still lacked a quorum authorised to believe the answers.
