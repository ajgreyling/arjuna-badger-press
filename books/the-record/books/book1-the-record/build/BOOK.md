# Chapter 1

The number arrived at 06:17 and did not announce itself.

It appeared halfway down a release from the International Constants Working Group, between a note
on the revised electron magnetic moment and a paragraph correcting the uncertainty budget of a
measurement Lwazi had never used. No red type. No congratulations. No sentence beginning *For the
first time*. Just a value in a table, typeset to eleven significant figures, with a bracketed
citation and a superscript pointing to three pages of systematic error.

Lwazi Ndlovu read it once while the coffee machine considered whether to give him coffee.

He read it again while the machine made a sound like a cupboard closing in another room.

Then he put the paper cup down without drinking and opened the PDF on the large monitor.

Technopark before seven belonged to cleaners, security guards and people whose computers had done
something expensive overnight. The corridor lights came on in sections. Beyond the window, the
mountain was a darker absence against the first grey of morning. Electron Road had not yet filled
with school traffic and delivery vans. Somewhere below, a generator started, caught badly, and
settled into an uneven idle.

The project dashboard had already found the release. A green tile in the upper right said
`COMPATIBLE`.

Lwazi closed it.

He downloaded the release again from the working group's public mirror, not the consortium cache.
The checksum matched the notice. That established only that he had downloaded what they had
published. It did not establish that the number in the table meant what the dashboard said it
meant, or that the expression against which it had been compared was the expression G had frozen
fourteen months ago, or that somebody had not corrected a unit in the intervening year and forgotten
to tell the triumphant green square.

The coffee machine clicked behind him. He left the cup where it was.

He began at the wrong end.

The paper's headline value was the end of an apparatus, not the beginning of a fact. He opened the
methods supplement and found the measured frequency ratio. Then the calibration table. Then the
environmental corrections: field drift, cavity deformation, temperature, gravitational potential,
the systematic allowance for a component replaced during the second run. He copied each published
input into a blank notebook and recomputed the value without the collaboration's code.

His first result disagreed in the ninth digit.

The feeling that went through him was not disappointment. The relief was sharp enough to make him
laugh. A discrepancy was familiar. A discrepancy had handles.

He checked his transcription. Correct. Checked the unit conversion. Correct. Checked the erratum
linked from the footnote and found that he had used the old reference temperature for the second
run.

He changed it.

The digits aligned.

Lwazi leaned back. The chair complained. On the small monitor to his left, the consortium chat had
begun to wake.

`MARA: anyone seen ICG release?`

`DEV: dashboard says green`

`MARA: dashboard has said green about invoices`

Then a bottle emoji from somebody in Karoo, followed by three more.

Lwazi muted the channel.

He had been here before—not with this number, and not with stakes anybody outside a narrow field
would have recognised, but with the sensation of a result becoming socially true before it had
become technically defensible. Years earlier, he had stood in front of a room in Geneva with a
slide whose error bars were too small because an instrument correction had been applied twice. He
had not written the pipeline. He had not approved the slide. He had, however, been the person whose
face was beside it when a postdoc in the third row asked the question.

The correction took twelve minutes. The photograph lasted for years.

He opened the frozen expression.

Not the convenient copy in the active repository. The release artifact.

The 420 corpus called it the proton–electron limb, though *limb* made it sound more organic than the
thing looked. It was an expression assembled from ratios and constraints, one branch of a larger
framework G insisted was either vulnerable to measurement or useless. The expression had been
sealed before the previous constants release. Inputs fixed. Transform fixed. Tolerances fixed. A
future measurement could enter the interval or miss it; nobody would be permitted to move the
interval around the answer afterwards.

That was the claim.

Claims were cheaper than timestamps.

Lwazi found the signed artifact identifier and followed it outward. Consortium repository. Public
mirror in Accra. University archive in Nairobi. A software heritage store in Europe that had copied
the release without caring what it contained. Four hashes, four dates, four systems administered by
people who did not share a payroll or a reason to lie in the same direction.

The artifact was old enough.

He still did not run the comparison.

He inspected the frozen inputs one by one. Two came from values superseded in the new release. One
had a covariance term represented differently in the current tables. He rebuilt the calculation
using the old published basis, propagated the revised uncertainties, and wrote the interval by hand
on the glass wall beside his desk.

Only then did he write the new value beneath it.

The number sat inside.

Not at the centre. Not elegantly. Inside.

He stared at the two lines until the marker fumes reached him.

The framework had not predicted eleven digits. It had not explained the apparatus. It had not
turned a speculative architecture into a law of nature. The new measurement had moved, and the
frozen expression had remained compatible with it. That was all.

It was also considerably more than nothing.

His phone vibrated across the desk. The muted group had found other routes to him.

`LWAZI ANSWER YOUR PHONE`

Then:

`Please tell me the green is real.`

Then a photograph of three bottles standing in an ice bucket in a Karoo control room, one bottle
labelled PROTON, one ELECTRON, and the third KILL SWITCH in black tape.

Lwazi smiled despite himself. He saved the image and did not reply.

On the dashboard, below the green proton–electron tile, another tile remained red.

The neutron–proton limb had failed eleven months earlier. Not weakened. Not become controversial.
Failed. A cleaner measurement had landed outside the frozen interval, and the project had left the
result on the front page where every visitor could see it.

There had been arguments about that. Not about the mathematics; the mathematics was dead. About
presentation. Whether a retired branch belonged on an active dashboard. Whether red implied a
software fault. Whether donors understood falsification. Whether the public needed the entire
history of an idea every time it encountered the surviving part.

G had settled the argument by refusing to attend the meeting in which removal was proposed. In his
absence he sent one line: `A theory without its graveyard is advertising.`

Lwazi looked from the red tile to the number on the glass.

The survival mattered because the failure remained visible.

He opened a local terminal and ran the consortium comparison from a clean environment. The machine
downloaded the frozen artifact, verified its signature, fetched the newly published constants and
stopped.

`REFERENCE COVARIANCE SCHEMA HAS CHANGED.`

Below that:

`AUTOMATIC COMPARISON REFUSED.`

Lwazi let out the breath he had been holding.

The dashboard's green tile had come from a compatibility shim in the monitoring service. Someone
had mapped the new covariance field to the old one so the nightly job would finish. The mapping
looked sensible. It was probably correct. It was also not part of the frozen calculation.

He took a screenshot, then disliked the ease of it and exported the run receipt with the environment
manifest. He sent both to Dries with no subject line.

Dries answered before the files finished uploading.

`You ran clean?`

`Yes.`

`Dashboard?`

`Lied politely.`

The typing indicator appeared, vanished, appeared again.

`Don't touch it.`

Lwazi looked at the green square.

`I wasn't going to.`

`You were looking at it in a corrective manner.`

`Come upstairs.`

He forwarded the schema change to Nairobi and Accra, asking for independent treatment of the
covariance rather than his own mapping. To Karoo he sent only: `Do not open anything yet.`

The reply was immediate.

`Too late for proton. Electron still sealed.`

He poured the untouched coffee into the sink. It had developed a skin.

By 07:04 the building had begun pretending to be an ordinary workplace. Doors opened. Somebody
laughed near reception. A cleaner moved her cart past the glass wall, read the two rows of digits,
and continued without asking whether physics had changed before breakfast.

The answers returned seven minutes apart.

Nairobi supplied a signed notebook and a paragraph distinguishing what it established from what it
did not. Accra supplied two calculations, one using the working group's covariance and one rebuilt
from the underlying measurements, along with a complaint that Technopark had named a field
`legacy_covariance_final2`.

Both placed the published value inside the frozen interval.

Lwazi checked their hashes. Checked that the notebooks had executed from fresh inputs. Checked the
direction of the movement relative to the previous release.

Still inside.

His phone showed twenty-three missed calls.

He unmuted the consortium channel. The messages rushed past: jokes, caveats, people tagging G,
somebody asking whether communications had prepared a statement, somebody else saying they had been
preparing one for fourteen months.

Lwazi typed:

`The revised value remains compatible with the frozen proton–electron expression under the new
published uncertainty. Independent recalculation: Nairobi and Ghana. This does not confirm the 420
framework. It does not prove the expression. Do not use either word.`

He attached the receipts.

Before sending, he scrolled up to the first bottle emoji and felt the old Geneva room gather at the
edge of his vision: bright projector, too-small bars, the postdoc lifting one hand.

He deleted the sentence he had almost added—*But it held.*

Then he sent the rest.

For six seconds nothing happened.

The channel marked his message read in Accra, Nairobi, the Karoo and three offices in Stellenbosch.

Then Dries came through the door carrying a printer tray's worth of paper and said, “Show me the
date.”

# Chapter 2

Dries put the paper on Lwazi's desk in four untidy stacks.

“That is not a date,” Lwazi said.

“Correct.”

The first stack was repository history. The second was certificate metadata. The third was a set of
archive receipts with the useful fields circled in blue pen. The fourth appeared to be the source
code of the compatibility service that had turned green overnight.

Lwazi looked at the printer paper, then at him. “You printed the software.”

“Only the lying part.”

“The dashboard lied politely.”

“That is the worst kind. Everyone thanks it.”

Dries pulled the spare chair around and sat without taking off his jacket. He had dressed for the
temperature outside and then climbed two flights of stairs too quickly. His glasses had fogged at
the edges. On Lwazi's wall, the surviving interval and the new value remained in black marker, one
above the other, guarded by nothing more sophisticated than handwriting.

He did not look at the number first.

He looked at the notebook receipts from Nairobi and Ghana. Two independent runs. Different base
images. Different dependency graphs. Same frozen artifact. Same new source release. Agreement past
the precision that mattered.

“Good,” he said.

“I know.”

“You know the calculations agree. That is not the same as knowing why.”

“Ama rebuilt from the underlying measurements.”

“I saw.”

“Kiki verified the source package.”

“I saw that too.”

Lwazi folded his arms. “Did you come upstairs to inspect my email in person?”

Dries tapped the first stack. “I came upstairs because the number is interesting. The date is the
experiment.”

He had been saying versions of this for twenty-three years, mostly about systems nobody would ever
put in a paper. A vehicle could claim it had crossed a boundary. A bank could claim it had received
an instruction. A field unit could claim its copy was current. The useful question was never whether
one system possessed a timestamp. Systems possessed whatever timestamp they had been told to
possess. The useful question was how many independent things would have to be wrong in compatible
ways for the timestamp to be false.

Lwazi moved the coffee cup out of the way. “Show me.”

Dries turned the first stack around.

The frozen expression had been committed on a Tuesday afternoon fourteen months earlier. That fact
alone was nearly worthless. Repository administrators could rewrite history. Signing keys could be
misused. Clocks could drift. People could generate an old date by typing one.

The commit had produced a release artifact. The artifact had been signed by three consortium keys
under Nairobi's old two-of-three rule. Better, but still not enough. Two keys could be compromised;
the whole purpose of a threshold was to quantify how much betrayal a system could survive, not to
declare betrayal impossible.

The release artifact had then been mirrored.

Accra had copied it six minutes later as part of the ingestion run. A public university archive had
harvested it that night. The European software heritage store had taken it three days later. A
student in Kumasi had cited the artifact hash in a thesis deposited eight months ago. An automated
legal-discovery crawler had placed the release index in a commercial evidentiary bundle whose own
root was published weekly in three jurisdictions.

Dries drew lines between the receipts with Lwazi's marker.

“Any one of these can be forged,” he said. “This one is ours, so assume it can be forged by us. This
one is theirs, so assume it can be forged by them. The thesis can be replaced at the university.
The crawler can be persuaded it saw something it did not.”

“You are making a poor case for your case.”

“If I wanted a good case I would show you one timestamp and speak confidently.”

He circled the hash repeated across all four systems.

“To move the prediction after today's measurement, you have to alter our signed release, Ghana's
ingestion history, Nairobi's archive, the heritage store and a student's deposited thesis. Then you
have to repair every later root that contains one of those objects. You have to do it without
changing the hash, or replace every place the hash escaped to. The question is not whether any one
record is sacred. None of them is. The question is whether they can all be made to tell the same new
lie.”

Lwazi studied the lines. “Could they?”

“Of course.”

He waited.

“That was not the answer you wanted.”

“It was the answer I expected.”

“Given enough access and enough time, yes. But not between 06:17 and now. Not without leaving a
larger event than the one you are trying to fake.”

The cleaner returned along the corridor, pushing the cart toward reception. She glanced through the
glass at the paper covering the desk. Dries lowered his voice, though nothing they were discussing
was secret yet.

“The expression predates the measurement,” he said. “That part holds.”

Lwazi finally let his arms fall. “And the calculation?”

“You have three of those. I am the plumbing.”

“You say that when you want permission to be rude.”

“No. When I want physicists to stop asking me whether a database proves nature.”

On the small monitor the group channel continued to climb. The communications director had arrived
and was asking for a five-minute holding line. A dean wanted to know whether *vindication* was
technically safe. Somebody from the private fund had sent a congratulations message before anybody
had told them there was something to congratulate.

Dries pointed at that message.

“How do they know?”

Lwazi opened the thread. “The release is public.”

“The release is public. Our compatibility result was not.”

“Anybody with the frozen expression can run it.”

“Yes.”

“So they ran it.”

“Maybe.”

The word sat there between them, too small to support suspicion and too large to discard.

Dries opened the monitoring service on Lwazi's terminal. The compatibility shim had been merged
nine weeks earlier by a contractor working on schema migration. Reviewed by two consortium
engineers. Tested against the previous constants package. Deployed automatically. Nothing in its
history suggested it had been planted to manufacture today's result.

It was merely helpful beyond its authority.

The old schema stored covariance as a dense matrix. The new release supplied components with source
labels and a construction rule. The shim flattened them into the old matrix so the dashboard could
continue comparing values. Mathematically defensible. Operationally convenient. Epistemically
wrong, because it silently inserted a transformation that had not existed when G froze the
expression.

Dries changed the tile from green to grey.

`REQUIRES INDEPENDENT RECALCULATION`, it said.

The group channel reacted as if he had switched off a life-support machine.

`DEV: WHO KILLED GREEN`

`MARA: Dries is in building`

`DEV: understood`

Lwazi said, “We have independently recalculated.”

“Then the tile can go green when Nairobi's receipt is attached and Ghana's path is reproducible from
the public release.”

“It is reproducible.”

“You know that because you read Ama's message. The dashboard does not know it.”

“You are punishing the display for not being a scientist.”

“I am punishing the scientists for treating the display as one.”

Lwazi made a quiet sound that might have been a laugh. Dries returned to the receipts.

The repository page was mutable. The public archive was less mutable, not immutable. Certificate
status could change. Mirrors could vanish. Even the student thesis was subject to a retention
policy written by somebody who believed twenty years meant forever.

Dries selected the smallest useful set of evidence: artifact hash, signature bundle, mirror
receipts, archive roots, the old expression's input manifest, and the identifiers of the two clean
recalculations. He assembled them into a plain report with no live links required to understand it.
At the bottom he added a sentence in twelve-point type.

`This receipt establishes public precedence`  
`of the frozen expression.`  
`It does not establish the physical truth`  
`of that expression.`

Lwazi read over his shoulder. “Kiki will like that.”

“Kiki will tell me *establishes* is too strong.”

“Will she be right?”

“Probably.”

He sent the draft to Nairobi anyway. Her response came in under a minute.

`Replace ESTABLISHES with SUPPORTS.`  
`Define PUBLIC. Receipt is not self-verifying.`  
`Otherwise useful.`

Dries made the changes.

Then he printed it.

The office printer woke at the far end of the corridor. Its rollers engaged with a sequence of
small mechanical decisions. A sheet emerged, warm and faintly curled, followed by four pages of
appendices.

Lwazi watched him collect them.

“You have just spent an hour proving paper is the least trustworthy object in this building.”

“No. I spent an hour proving trust is an agreement among objects.”

“And now?”

Dries held up the first page.

“Now I want one that cannot silently update while everybody argues.”

He signed the margin and wrote the time from the wall clock. Not because his signature made the
receipt true. Not because the wall clock was privileged. Because from that moment any later change
would have to explain the disagreement with the paper in his hand.

He gave Lwazi one copy and kept the other.

The chat chimed. Nairobi's receipt had been attached to the dashboard. Ghana's public calculation
path had passed from a clean machine. The grey tile changed to green again, this time with two small
links beneath it and a line describing what had been transformed.

No champagne emoji appeared.

For almost ten seconds, the channel was quiet enough to resemble thought.

Then G joined.

`Why am I being congratulated?`

# Chapter 3

G joined without video and spent the first eleven seconds listening to people congratulate him.

Lwazi watched the black square at the end of the conference grid. It contained a white letter G,
the microphone symbol, and nothing that could be mistaken for pleasure.

Around it, the consortium had assembled itself into rectangles.

Ama's team occupied a meeting room in Accra where the air-conditioning had lost an argument with
the racks. Kiki sat alone in Nairobi with a window behind her and a printed copy of the recalculation
on the desk. Karoo had crowded six people into the control room and placed the three champagne
bottles where the camera could see them. Technopark filled two adjacent windows because the larger
boardroom's conferencing system had failed to recognise its own certificate.

The call was secure. The audio was terrible.

“—historical moment,” the dean was saying through an echo. “For the institution, certainly, but
more importantly for the framework and for African—”

“What moved?” G asked.

The dean stopped.

His voice came cleanly through the black square. No echo. No room around it. He could have been next
door or on another continent.

Lwazi leaned toward the table microphone. “The revised proton–electron value moved upward relative
to the previous release.”

“By how much?”

Lwazi gave him the value and the combined uncertainty.

“No,” G said. “Direction I heard. How much toward the centre of the interval?”

Lwazi opened his notebook. He had calculated it, because he knew the man.

“Zero point two-one standard deviations on the frozen basis. Depending on covariance treatment,
zero point one-nine.”

“Why depending?”

Dries said, “Schema changed. Automatic comparison refused after we removed a compatibility shim.
Ghana and Nairobi rebuilt independently.”

“Removed or disabled?”

“Disabled. It is still in history.”

“Good.”

The word produced more relief in the room than the measurement had.

On the Karoo feed, somebody reached for the bottle marked PROTON. Lwazi shook his head. The hand
withdrew.

The dean tried again. “The important point, as I understand it, is that a prediction made fourteen
months before the new experimental value has now been confirmed within—”

“No,” G said.

He did not raise his voice. He did not need to.

“Perhaps *confirmed* is not the precise term,” the dean said. “Corroborated.”

“No.”

“Supported?”

Kiki looked down at her copy. Ama turned her face away from the camera, which meant she was smiling.

G said, “A frozen expression remains compatible with a revised measurement. That is the sentence.
If it is too disappointing, improve the theory.”

The communications director spoke from the second Technopark window. “The press will ask whether
the Code predicted the value.”

“Then tell them what it predicted.”

“They will ask for yes or no.”

“They are allowed to ask badly.”

“We still have to answer.”

“Why?”

The director glanced offscreen, perhaps toward somebody whose job was to explain why. Nobody did.

Lwazi had met G in person only three times. Each occasion had included a moment when ordinary social
cooperation should have required him to soften a technically correct statement and he had declined.
It was not bravery. Bravery implied temptation. G seemed genuinely unable to understand why warmth
should be purchased with an inaccurate noun.

“What is the new uncertainty?” G asked.

Lwazi gave it.

“And the old?”

He gave that too.

“So the interval narrowed.”

“Yes.”

“Which kill switch is closest now?”

The champagne hand disappeared completely.

Lwazi shared his screen. The framework's live limbs appeared as a plain table: expression,
measurement family, frozen date, present distance from exclusion, status. One row green. Several
grey. One red.

G said, “Sort by distance.”

Lwazi sorted it.

The surviving proton–electron expression moved down the page. Two untested limbs rose above it. The
red neutron–proton row remained pinned at the bottom, visible under a heading that read `KILLED`.

“The clock-ratio limb,” Lwazi said. “If the next optical-network result moves in the expected
direction and halves uncertainty, it can exclude us.”

“Expected by whom?”

“By the experimental collaboration.”

“Not by us.”

“No.”

“Good. Put that in the announcement.”

The communications director blinked. “Put our next likely failure in the announcement?”

“It is not our likely failure. It is our nearest informative measurement.”

“That distinction will not survive a headline.”

“Then the headline is not an instrument.”

The call fractured briefly. Accra froze with Ama's mouth open. Karoo's audio arrived twice. The
secure bridge declared one participant's network unstable and reduced everybody else's resolution
in sympathy.

When the sound returned, G was asking why the neutron–proton limb remained at the bottom.

“Pinned failure,” Dries said.

“By whom?”

“Me.”

“Why?”

“Because people kept proposing a retired tab.”

“A retired tab is where theories go to become legends. Leave it.”

The dean placed both hands flat on the table. “Nobody is proposing that we conceal a negative
result.”

“You proposed moving it where nobody looks.”

“For clarity.”

“Yes.”

The dean waited for more. None came.

Lwazi watched the red row. Eleven months earlier it had belonged to him more personally than any
equation should belong to a physicist. He had designed the comparison, defended the uncertainty
treatment and expected the result to survive. When the new measurement excluded the interval, G
had not consoled him. He had asked whether the instrument team had published its covariance and
whether any honest transformation kept the limb alive.

The answer had been no.

G had said, *Then it has done the work we asked it to do.*

Lwazi had hated him for nearly a week.

Now the failed row made the green one legible. Without it, today's survival would have been another
system reporting its own success.

The communications director shared a draft statement. Its title was `AFRICAN THEORY VINDICATED BY
GLOBAL PRECISION MEASUREMENT`.

Ama laughed aloud before remembering her microphone.

“That is a working title,” the director said.

“It is working very hard,” Ama said.

G asked, “Who wrote *global*?”

“The measurement collaboration is international.”

“The planet is not a credential.”

“It signals scale.”

“It signals appetite.”

Lwazi opened the champagne photograph on his phone. He enlarged the label KILL SWITCH until it
filled the screen and sent it privately to G.

There was no response.

The meeting moved into the slower misery of collective editing. *Vindicated* became *supported*,
then *consistent with*, then finally the sentence G had supplied at the beginning. *Prediction*
became *frozen expression*. *Proof* vanished from paragraph two and reappeared in a quotation from a
deputy vice-chancellor who was not on the call. Kiki marked the quotation unsupported. The dean
asked whether a quotation could be unsupported if the person had truly said it.

“Easily,” Kiki said.

At 08:12, Karoo opened the champagne by accident. A cork crossed the camera frame and struck
something metallic. Everyone looked toward the sound.

G said, “What was that?”

“Calibration event,” somebody answered.

“With what uncertainty?”

There was a pause.

“Brut.”

Even Kiki smiled.

The tension left the call in a single uneven breath. Cups appeared. Somebody in Accra found a bottle
of water and raised it with exaggerated solemnity. The dean surrendered and accepted a glass from
an assistant. Lwazi let Karoo's laughter reach him without muting it.

“G,” he said, “we are going to mark the result.”

“I can see that.”

“You could allow yourself one minute before asking how it dies.”

The black square remained black.

When G answered, his voice slowed without softening.

“It survived a measurement. That is good.”

Around the grid, people waited.

“Now find the measurement it cannot survive.”

The private-message indicator changed. G had replied to Lwazi's photograph.

`Champagne has error bars. Ask the French.`

Then he left the call.

# Chapter 4

By nine o'clock, everybody who had always supported the project had found Dries's email address.

The first message came from the office of the rector. Its subject was `COORDINATION`, capitalised as
if coordination were an emergency service.

The second came from a laboratory in Europe whose previous contribution to the 420 work had been a
reviewer's comment describing it as numerically imaginative. They congratulated the African Record
Consortium on *our shared progress* and offered to host an independent verification environment.
The environment included eighteen thousand GPU-hours, a communications team and a governance board
on which the African nodes would hold two of eleven seats.

The third came from a private science fund. It offered enough compute to remove the consortium's
queue for a year.

In exchange, the fund wanted first access to “non-public derived insights.”

On the side monitor, the consortium's Ethical Casino traded fake chips against delayed real market
candles. Its header carried the disclaimer `NO CLAIMED EDGE`. The teaching tool accepted prices and
public news, never consortium sensor data. It lost money before breakfast and displayed the loss
without apology.

Dries read that phrase twice. Then he created a folder called `LONG-STANDING INTEREST` and moved all
three messages into it.

His office looked over a car park and the blank side of a building renamed so often that nobody
under forty knew what its letters had originally meant. Beyond it, traffic had thickened along the
R44. The mountain had emerged into full daylight, indecently clear, while his inbox reproduced
itself faster than he could classify it.

Stellenbosch wanted a controlled announcement.

The Department of Science wanted a briefing before the controlled announcement.

Another department wanted to know whether the measurement had national-security implications.

The university's insurer wanted confirmation that the work involved no radioactive material,
biological agent or prototype propulsion system.

A documentary producer wanted an exclusive interview with G and had attached a proposed title:
`THE MAN WHO FOUND THE EQUATION OF EVERYTHING`.

Dries forwarded that one to G without comment.

The reply arrived seven seconds later.

`No.`

He filed it under answered correspondence.

At 09:14, Ama called.

She did not greet him. “Why is the public mirror still pointing at yesterday's calculation
package?”

Dries checked. “Because the new package is in the release candidate bucket.”

“The receipts are public. The inputs are public. The calculation that joins them is not.”

“It passed fifteen minutes ago.”

“Then why is it not mirrored?”

He opened the deployment queue. A hold had been placed on the release by university communications,
pending alignment of the technical package with the announcement.

“Coordination,” he said.

“What?”

“Nothing. I found the hold.”

“Whose?”

“Stellenbosch.”

“The number is already public.”

“They are worried the explanation will escape before the statement.”

“The explanation is the part that should escape.”

Dries removed the hold. He had the authority because the release pipeline still treated
communications as advisory. That would change by lunch.

The package propagated to Accra's mirror, then Nairobi's. Ama watched the same status page from her
side.

“Good,” she said. “Now the result belongs to whoever can kill it.”

“That sounds like G.”

“G does not own all the useful sentences.”

She ended the call.

At 09:22, Kiki sent a question in the consortium channel.

`Who is authorised to call this a prediction?`

The responses arrived immediately.

`LWAZI: nobody yet`

`COMMS: consortium spokespeople`  
`once wording agreed`

`DEAN: principal investigators`

`G: the date is authorised`

Kiki ignored all of them and attached a one-page notice. It distinguished the frozen expression,
the later measurement and the present claim of compatibility. Under `AUTHORITY`, she had written:

`The Consortium may attest that the expression was fixed before the measurement and that the stated
comparison was executed. It has no authority to bind external interpretation of the result.`

Dries downloaded it before somebody improved the sentence.

The phone on his desk rang. Not his mobile—the fixed phone supplied with the office, used mostly by
reception when a delivery driver could not find Meson Street.

“Venter.”

“Doctor Venter?”

“No.”

There was a pause. “Mister Venter, then. I am calling on behalf of the Office for Scientific and
Cultural Collections.”

Dries had never heard of it. The caller's accent gave him nowhere useful to place her, and her
formal English sounded prepared rather than foreign.

“Which office?”

She repeated the name without adding a country or institution.

“How can I help?”

“We are trying to understand the scope of the persistence claim discussed in this morning's
release.”

“There is no persistence claim in this morning's release. A constants expression survived a revised
measurement.”

“Of course. We are asking about the broader research programme.”

“That programme is public.”

“The corpus is extensive.”

“Yes.”
