# Chapter 22 — Appendix: Real, Debated, Invented

The last thing Naila did before they left Newark was sit on the cold causeway between the Octagon and Observatory Circle with a notebook on her knees and write down everything she was *sure* of. Frost on the grass. A jogger going by on the path with earbuds in, no idea she was crossing a lunar instrument the size of fifty football fields. The highway breathing somewhere past the wall.

She made three columns. Tessa had suggested it, weeks back, in the OHC archive when the Foundation rep was still circling and everyone was arguing about who got to *say* things. *Keep your columns clean,* Tessa had said, not unkindly. *Real. Debated. Invented. Most fights are people mixing the three on purpose.*

So here it is. The columns. Check them yourself.

---

## REAL

These are not in dispute. Not by us, not by the archaeologists, not by the descendant nations whose ancestors stacked the baskets. Stand on the floor; it holds.

The earthworks at Newark are the **largest set of geometric earthen enclosures on Earth.** Built by the people we call the **Hopewell** — a name they did not give themselves; it belongs to a nineteenth-century farmer whose land sat over another site — between roughly **100 BCE and 400 CE.** The complex once covered around **3,000 acres.** Less than **ten percent** survives. The rest is under Newark's streets and lawns and the parking lots of ordinary errands.

What's left, mostly: the **Great Circle** and the **Octagon with its attached Observatory Circle.**

The **Great Circle** is about **366 metres** across, with walls standing **8 feet** and a ditch *inside* them. Write that down twice. The ditch is on the inside. A fort puts its ditch outside, so the enemy has to climb up out of a hole into your spears. This ditch faces the wrong way to kill anyone. It was never a fort.

The **Octagon** is eight walls, each about **550 feet** long, enclosing **50 acres**, joined by a causeway to the **20-acre Observatory Circle.** Every cubic foot of it was carried by hand, in baskets, by people with no draft animals, no metal tools, and no writing. Basket. By basket. By basket.

In **1982**, two scholars from Earlham College — **Ray Hively**, a physicist, and **Robert Horn**, a philosopher — came to the Octagon to *disprove* the claim that it was aligned to anything. They were skeptics. They were doing the right thing. They wanted to put the astronomy nonsense to bed.

They found that the Octagon encodes the **full 18.6-year lunar standstill cycle.** All **eight** of the Moon's standstill rise-and-set points are built into the walls and the gateways, to **sub-degree precision.** At the major standstill, the Moon rises and clears the far gateway within about **half a degree** of the figure's exact centre.

The Ohio History Connection's own phrase for the surveying: **"twice as precise as Stonehenge."** Their words, not ours. We just kept reading them.

And the part Naila underlined three times: **a single human being cannot find this cycle.** Not in a year. Not in ten. The 18.6-year wobble only resolves through observation kept, written into something — earth, cord, memory, relay — and handed forward across more than a lifetime. Somebody watched the eastern horizon and held the record before the shape could even *be* a shape. Then they laid it into the ground at half a degree.

There is a **standard unit** at Newark — call it the **Observatory Circle Diameter**, the **OCD**, about **1,054 feet.** The circles sit six of those apart. The geometry nests a circle inside a square inside the octagon — the **squared circle** the math people get quiet about.

And the same OCD lays out the **High Bank Works**, 53 miles south near Chillicothe — the **only other octagon the Hopewell built** — which encodes the **same lunar alignment** with the **same ruler.**

The earthworks were inscribed on the **UNESCO World Heritage List in 2023**, as part of the Hopewell Ceremonial Earthworks. The United States' twenty-fifth such property.

For most of a century, the Octagon lay inside a private golf course lease. People played the back nine across a lunar observatory and didn't always know it.

That is the REAL column. Every line of it is checkable, footnoted in the people we cite at the back, and astonishing without a single visitor from anywhere off this planet.

"That's the thing that gets me," Frik said, leaning over her shoulder, reading upside down because he reads everything upside down. "We came in ready to fight someone, and the truth just — *sat* there. Being weirder than anything we'd have made up."

---

## DEBATED

Here's where you keep a cold eye. These are not lies. They are *maybes* that haven't been nailed down, and the honest thing is to label them as maybes and let you stand in the gap.

The big one is the **apparent-size proportion.**

The ratio of the Great Circle to the Observatory Circle comes out around **1.131.** The ratio of the Moon's apparent size at its nearest, perigee, to its farthest, apogee, is about **1.127.** That's a **0.35 percent** difference. Tantalisingly close. It would mean the builders had not only mapped where the Moon rises but *how big it looks* across its orbit, and built that ratio into the ground.

Frik wanted it so badly. You could see it on him at High Bank, the day the geometry lined up — he had the look of a kid who's found the secret panel. "It's *right there*," he'd said. "Point-three-five percent. That's not luck. That's somebody clocking that the Moon swells and shrinks—"

And Tessa, walking the inner curve with Naila, hadn't shut him down. She'd just looked at him the way you look at someone whose enthusiasm you respect too much to flatter. "It's pretty," she said. "It might even be true. But you can't stand on a maybe and call it a floor."

Naila said it more bluntly, the way only she could, because she'd run the numbers and they made her itch: "Two ratios being close isn't intention. I can find you a 0.35 percent match to something in any pile of measurements if I'm allowed to choose what I'm matching. The alignment is a fact. This is a *coincidence that might be a fact.* Those are different shelves."

Frik took it well. He usually does, when he's the one over-reaching — he's faster than anyone at catching the reach when it's his own. "Fine," he said. "DEBATED column. But I'm allowed to like it."

"You're allowed to like it," Tessa said. "Just don't sell it."

So: **apparent-size ratio — debated. Possibly designed. Not a thing to build a book on.** We didn't.

---

## INVENTED

This column is short, and it's the one the genre fills first, so we'll be careful.

There were no ancient astronauts at Newark. Nobody from elsewhere taught the Hopewell their geometry. There is no lost master race, no Atlantean survey crew, no cabal handing down secret cubits.

We want to be exact about *why* this matters, because the polite version of the lie wears a lab coat. The lie is: *they couldn't have done this themselves.* That somebody — wiser, paler, off-world, whatever — must have shown them. That lie is older than the earthworks' UNESCO plaque and it has done real damage, and the whole reason this book exists is to refuse it.

Because the actual evidence points the other way, and it points somewhere *better.*

A shared ruler across two octagons, 53 miles apart, generations apart, doesn't mean an outside hand. It means a **people.** A network. A practice of sky-watching with a standard length passed teacher to student, river town to river town, the way you'd pass any craft worth keeping. Somebody learned the OCD and taught the OCD and went and laid the OCD into the ground at Chillicothe, and the only thing stranger than a master race is *that* — that this was schooled, social, ordinary genius, kept by hand for longer than anyone alive could verify.

The wonder doesn't need rescuing from the Hopewell. The Hopewell are the wonder.

The fish, who had been quiet through the math because the math bored him, surfaced for this one. *Oh good. The aliens column is empty. Tell the History Channel. They've got a marathon to fill.*

## A STATEMENT ABOUT WHO THIS BELONGS TO

We need to say a thing plainly, and then stop talking.

We came to Newark to read something. We're a crew that reads things — it's what our wiring is *for*, the watching nobody ever let us switch off. Naila reads geometry the way you read a friend's face. Frik reads the seam where a claim's been stretched too thin. The Lens reads the room, and the cost of seeing it. We're good at this. We're proud of it.

And the single most important thing that happened on this book is that we *didn't finish the puzzle.* Because it was never ours to finish.

There's a moment in the field, near the end, that Naila still chews on. The dawn alignment had just happened — the Moon clearing the far gateway, dead centre, exactly as Hively and Horn said it would forty years ago, exactly as somebody intended two thousand years ago. Everyone there had gone silent. And Jakobus, who'd driven in from wherever Jakobus drives in from, asked the question he always asks. The one that re-opens the wonder instead of closing it.

*What is it for?*

He didn't ask Naila. He didn't ask us, the clever outsiders with the notebooks and the camera. He turned to **Dr. Tessa Redwing** — archaeologist, Tribal Historic Preservation Officer, descendant of the people who carried the baskets — and he handed her the question and got out of the way.

She let it sit a long time. The frost was burning off.

"It's a record," she said. "And it's ours."

That's the answer. That's the whole answer, and it is complete, and it does not belong to us to expand, footnote, monetise, or improve.

The earthworks are returning to public stewardship under the Ohio History Connection, in consultation with the descendant nations — Eastern Shawnee, Shawnee, Absentee-Shawnee, Miami, and others whose ancestors and relations are tied to this ground. The lease that fenced an observatory inside a golf course for a century is ending. A clean-handed outfit would have liked to read it and frame it and *say what it means* first. They don't get to. The people whose record it is get the last word on what it's for, including the word *no.*

We held the camera. We did not hold the meaning. If anywhere in these pages it reads like we cracked somebody's mystery, we failed, and Tessa would tell you so, and she'd be right.

## GAZETTEER — GO STAND IN IT YOURSELF

You should. The scale doesn't survive a photograph; it lives in your shins when you walk a 550-foot wall.

**Octagon Earthworks & Observatory Circle** — Newark, Ohio, off North 33rd Street. As the golf lease winds down, public access is expanding; **check the Ohio History Connection** for current hours, open days, and the scheduled alignment viewings (the major standstill is rare — worth planning a life around). Go at dawn if you can. Bring a coat. Stand in the Observatory Circle and try to feel the figure you can't see from the ground. You can't. That's the point — they built a shape only the sky could read whole.

**Great Circle** — a few miles south, on Cooper Avenue. Open, walkable, free as of writing. Walk into the ditch. Note which side of the wall it's on. Argue with anyone who says "fort."

**High Bank Works** — near Chillicothe, 53 miles south. Access is limited and partly conservation-managed; **confirm before you go.** This is the second octagon. The same ruler. The shared measure. If you stand here and at Newark in the same week, you will feel the network in your feet.

**FURTHER READING:** Ray Hively & Robert Horn's papers on the Octagon alignments (the 1982 work and after) — the primary source, and a model of skeptics following the evidence past their own intentions. Ohio History Connection publications and the UNESCO inscription dossier for the Hopewell Ceremonial Earthworks. And, before anything else, the **statements of the descendant nations themselves.** Read them first. Read them last. Let them be the ones to tell you what it's for.

---

Naila closed the notebook. Three columns, clean.

The Moon had set hours ago; it was just sky now, ordinary blue over an ordinary Ohio town that happened to be holding a calendar somebody started knowing they'd never see it close.

"You okay with not knowing?" Frik asked. "The whole *who taught them the ruler* thing. We never got the bottom of it."

"There's no bottom to get to," Naila said. "There's a shared measure. Somebody keeps leaving the same length in the ground." She looked south, toward Chillicothe, toward fifty-three miles of nothing-in-particular that wasn't nothing at all. "We didn't make that up. We just noticed it."

*And here's the part I love,* the fish said, almost gently, for once. *You're going to keep noticing it. Aren't you. The same ruler. Site after site.*

Nobody answered him.

That's the door. We're leaving it open.
