# Chapter 3 — The Straight Line

The dew was still on it when they came through the gap in the wall, and the first thing Naila did was take her shoes off.

Frik watched her do it without comment, which was a kindness she noticed and filed. He had his own ritual — phone out, thumb flicking, narrating to nobody: "Observatory Circle, one thousand fifty-four feet across, twenty acres, joined to the Octagon by a causeway, and we are *standing inside it* and you cannot tell. You literally cannot tell, it's so big the curve looks straight." He turned a slow circle. "It looks like a park. It looks like somebody mowed a really nice park."

It did look like a park. That was the thing nobody put in the brochures, Naila thought — that the largest geometric earthwork on the planet, twice as precise as Stonehenge by the Ohio History Connection's own measuring, presents to the standing human eye as a long green bowl with a grass wall around it about as high as a man, and a sky on top, and the sound of Route 79 coming over the rim like surf. You had to leave the ground to see it. You had to be a bird, or a satellite, or dead, or carrying it in your head the whole time you built it, which was the part she kept coming back to and couldn't put down.

She walked the wall barefoot to feel the grade. The grass was cold and the soil under it gave, just slightly, the way deep-built soil does, soil that was carried here. Not bulldozed. Carried. Basket by basket, a handful of pounds at a time, by people with no metal, no wheel that mattered, no animal to pull a load — and no draft of the finished thing to work from, because the finished thing was bigger than any one of them would ever stand far enough back to see.

"Okay, here's my problem," Frik said, falling in beside her, scrambling up the inside face. "*Fort.* People used to say fort. There's a wall, it's a fort, the Indians were defending it, very *Last of the Mohicans*. And I want to be fair to fort, because fort is at least a guess that respects the labor —"

"The ditch is inside," Naila said.

"*The ditch is inside.*" He said it like a man laying down a winning card. "On the Great Circle, the one across town. Eight-foot walls, beautiful ditch, and it's on the *inside* of the wall. You don't dig your moat where the enemy's already standing. That's not a fort, that's — I don't know what that is, but it's not a fort. Fort is a potato."

"Fort is a potato," Naila agreed. It was their word for it. The instinct to make the astonishing thing small enough to hold in one hand. *It's a fort. It's a coincidence. It's a fluke.* The world reaching for the nearest dull box to put the wonder in so it could stop being responsible for it.

The LENS was thirty feet off, down on one knee in the wet grass, not shooting. The camera hung. He was looking through the gap toward the causeway where the circle handed off to the octagon, and his face had the still, evaluating quality it got when he was deciding whether a thing was his to take. Naila had learned to read that stillness. It meant *not yet*. It meant *whose is this*.

Dr. Tessa Redwing stood near the centre of the bowl with her hands in her coat pockets, watching the three of them arrange themselves across her grandmothers' work, and saying nothing, which Naila was beginning to understand was itself a kind of saying.

---

They had brought a wheel. Not a fancy one — a surveyor's measuring wheel from a hardware store, the kind that clicks off feet, and a hundred-metre tape, and Frik's GPS, and Naila's eye, which was the only instrument she fully trusted. She'd asked Tessa first. *May we measure it.* Tessa had looked at her for a moment that went on a beat too long and then said, "People have been measuring it for a hundred and forty years. Go ahead." Which was permission and was also something else, something with a hook in it that Naila hadn't worked out yet.

So they measured. Frik took the wheel and walked a chord. Naila stood at the centre — or what her body told her was the centre, the place where the bowl was equally far from her on every side, a thing she could feel before she could prove — and let the shape come up through her feet.

This was the part she could never explain to people who asked what it was like. They wanted a metaphor and she didn't have one, because it wasn't *like* anything; it was just that the relations were *there*, the way a chord is there in a struck string, and she heard them. The circle wasn't a circle to her. It was a circle and a square at once, the square sitting inside the curve and touching it, and the octagon out past the causeway built off the same square, all of it one figure thinking one thought.

She crouched and sketched it in the notebook. Circle. Square inscribed, corners kissing the curve. Then she drew the diameter of the circle and wrote *OCD* next to it, the way the papers did — Observatory Circle Diameter, the ruler the whole site was cut from. Then she paced off the distance to where the causeway started and counted under her breath and wrote *6* and circled it.

"Six," she said.

"Six what?" Frik dropped down next to her, breathing.

"The circles. The Observatory and the one out past the octagon. They're six of these apart." She tapped the diameter. "The thing's built in units of itself. The diameter is the brick. You don't measure it in feet. You measure it in *circles*."

Frik looked at the sketch, then up at the impossible flatness of the green, then back at the sketch. "That's — okay, that's the thing I came here to be annoyed by and I am instead going to be normal about it." He wasn't going to be normal about it. "They had a *standard length*. A custom foot. An — a Hopewell metre."

"Mhm."

"And they used it to lay out a fifty-acre octagon next door with eight five-hundred-and-fifty-foot walls, by *hand*, with *cord*, and the walls came out straight." He was up again, pacing, hands going. "How do you make a straight five-hundred-foot wall with no instrument? You can't sight it, the ground rolls. You'd peg a cord, but cord stretches, cord sags, cord's wet today —"

"You'd hand it down," Tessa said.

She had come closer without Naila hearing her, which on grass that gave underfoot was its own small marvel. She wasn't looking at the notebook. She was looking out across the bowl, at the long green nothing that was the most measured nothing in North America.

"You make the cord, you set the length, you teach somebody the length." Tessa's voice was even, conversational, the voice of someone who had given this answer many times to many people who heard half of it. "Then that person teaches it. The cord rots. The length doesn't, if you keep handing it on. You're not asking how one clever man built a straight wall. You're asking how a *people* kept a measurement true across the time it takes to build something nobody alive at the start would see done."

Naila stopped sketching.

"You keep talking about it like it's geometry," Tessa said. Not unkindly. There was even something close to affection in it, the patience of a teacher with a bright student who has the wrong end of the thing. "You came in here and took your shoes off — I saw that, thank you — and you went straight to the centre and felt for the middle. Like it's a puzzle. Like it's a beautiful machine and the question is how the gears fit."

"It is a beautiful machine," Naila said, because it was, and she wasn't going to pretend it wasn't to be polite.

"It is." Tessa nodded slowly. "And a song is just air pressure. You can measure a song with the right instrument and never once hear it." She let that sit. Out past the rim a truck downshifted on 79 and the sound poured over the wall and pooled in the bowl with them. "My grandmother's grandmothers carried this dirt. The unit you're calling OCD — the *length* — that got carried too. Hand to hand. It's not a number that happens to repeat. It's a thing somebody was *responsible* for. Somebody held the cord and somebody checked it and somebody said *no, again, it's long* and they did it again. For longer than any of them lived."

Frik had gone quiet, which almost never happened, and Naila could see him wanting to make the joke that would let him off the hook of the largeness of it, and not making it, which she respected more than anything he'd done all day.

"It's not just geometry," Naila said. Trying the shape of it.

"It's a *record*," Tessa said. "The geometry is how you can read it. But you don't write a record for a hundred years to do trigonometry." She looked at Naila then, full on, and there was the hook again, gentle and absolute. "You crack the math fast. You're good — I can see you're good. But the math being crackable is the least interesting true thing about this place. The interesting true thing is *who keeps a record longer than a life*. And that part isn't a puzzle you get to solve. That part's a people you have to ask."

---

The LENS lowered to a sitting position in the wet grass without a sound and let the camera lie in his lap. He had heard all of it. He didn't lift the lens. Naila understood, distantly, that this was the most useful thing he could do, and that it cost him to do it, and that the cost was the point of having him there.

She went back to the diameter. She wanted, suddenly and badly, to know the number. Not to own it. To stand next to it. To know what the cord measured when they finally laid it down satisfied.

Frik read out the wheel. They walked it twice. They argued the centre by a foot and a half and split the difference. Naila held the tape with cold fingers and Frik called the GPS and the two methods fought to a draw the way real measurement does, never clean, always a smudge of disagreement around the truth.

She wrote the number. She did the arithmetic against the published figure, the one in Hively and Horn, the one in the survey done before anyone here was born.

"Frik."

"Yeah."

"Read me the official diameter again. The good one. The careful one."

He read it. One thousand fifty-four feet, near enough, the figure that had stood through decades of being checked.

Naila looked at what they'd got, two amateurs with a hardware-store wheel and a tape and a freezing-handed argument about where the middle was, on a circle so large the curve read as a straight line to the standing eye.

It closed to within four feet.

Four feet. On a circle a fifth of a mile across, basket-loaded by hand, by people with cord and patience and no way to ever stand back far enough to check, the round of it held true to within four feet — and the gap between *their* clumsy morning's work and the careful survey was smaller than that. The error in the measuring was bigger than the error in the thing.

She sat down where she was. The grass soaked through and she didn't care.

The fish would have something to say about this, she thought — would lean in and go *four feet, on a fifth of a mile, with a basket, and you sweated through your good shirt to measure it worse than they built it, congratulations, genius* — and the fish would be right, and that was the joke and also not the joke at all.

Tessa was watching her sit down in the wet. Something in the older woman's face had eased, very slightly, as if a thing she'd been braced for had not happened.

"You felt for the centre with your feet," Tessa said again, quietly. "And then you measured it, and it beat you. Good. Hold onto that. The not-knowing how. People rush past the not-knowing how. They want it to be a clever trick or a lost secret. It's neither." She turned to look out toward the causeway, toward the octagon they hadn't walked yet, the eight straight walls and the gateway where, on the right dawn once in a generation, the Moon was said to come up dead through the middle. "It's just people. Being more patient than you can stand. For longer than you'd believe."

Naila looked at her notebook, at the circle holding the square holding the circle, all of it cut from one length somebody had carried in their hands and refused to let drift.

Four feet.

Fifty-three miles south, she knew, there was another one. The only other octagon they ever built. And the cord that laid out that one, the books said, was this same cord, this same length, this same impossible kept-true number — and she did not yet understand, sitting cold and stunned in the most measured grass on the continent, how you could hand a thing that exact across fifty-three miles and four hundred years and have it still close to four feet, or who had stood at the far end holding the other end of it.

"We should walk to High Bank," she said.

Tessa didn't answer for a moment. When she did, it wasn't yes and it wasn't no.

"We'll see," she said, "what you've earned by then."
