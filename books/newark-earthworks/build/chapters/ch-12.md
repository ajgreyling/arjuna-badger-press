# Chapter 12 — Lines Across the Land

Fifty-three miles is nothing in a car. Naila kept saying it to herself on the way down — *fifty-three miles, an hour with the radio on* — because the number had been doing something strange in her chest since Newark, and she wanted it to behave.

High Bank Works sits south of Chillicothe, where the Scioto bottomlands run flat and green and the road dead-ends in a way that feels less like arrival than like the land deciding to stop pretending. There's no visitor centre worth the name. There's a fence, a field, a sign that under-describes everything behind it, and the long low swell of walls that you don't see until you're standing on them, the way you never see the Octagon either, the way these things refuse to be a postcard.

Frik got out of the van and turned a full circle with his hands on his head. "Okay. Okay. So this is the *other* one."

"The only other one," Tessa said. She had driven separately, in her own truck, and she said it the way you'd correct a child gently and on purpose. "The Hopewell built earthworks all over this valley. Circles. Squares. They built exactly two octagons." She let that sit. "One up there. One here."

The LENS was already low to the grass with the camera, not shooting yet, just looking, the way he did when he was deciding whether a thing deserved to be looked at by a machine. Naila stood at the foot of the nearest wall and felt the old vertigo — the conviction, physical and absurd, that she was standing inside a figure she could only understand from a height she would never reach.

The official version is clean, and it is true, and the people who study this place will tell you so without flinching. High Bank is a Hopewell ceremonial and astronomical complex, a circle joined to an octagon, c. 100 BCE to 400 CE, basket-loaded by hand over generations by people who had no draft animals, no metal, no writing — and who nonetheless laid a circle so true that surveyors with instruments come to measure it and go home humbled. It encodes the lunar standstills, the same hard eight-point cycle the Octagon up north encodes. Hively and Horn read it here too. None of this is contested. None of it needs a single grain of woo to be the strangest thing in three states.

"Show me the unit," Naila said.

Tessa looked at her for a moment. Not unkindly. There was a thing Tessa did with a pause, a weighing, where you understood you were being decided about, and Naila had stopped finding it uncomfortable somewhere around day three. It was just accuracy. Tessa measured people the way the makers measured ground.

"You brought your own cord," Tessa said. It wasn't a question.

Naila had. A hundred metres of surveyor's tape, a couple of ranging poles Frik had liberated from a hardware store outside Lancaster, and the number written on the back of her hand in marker so she wouldn't fumble it: **1,054 feet.** The Observatory Circle Diameter. The OCD. The ruler that built Newark.

---

Here is the potato, and you should hold it honestly before you let it fall apart, because it falls apart better that way.

The easy story says: *coincidence.* Two big round things sixty miles apart, both circle-and-octagon, both built by the same broad culture — of course they'll look alike. Build enough earthworks over five hundred years and some of them will rhyme. People love a pattern. People will find a face in a tortilla. You can't get excited every time two ancient circles are roughly the same size, or you'll spend your whole life excited and broke and writing to magazines.

It's a good potato. It's the responsible one. Frik tried it on out loud, walking the south arc with the ranging pole over his shoulder like a fishing rod, because trying on the boring answer first is the only honest way to earn the interesting one.

"Roughly the same," he said. "*Roughly.* That's the word that does the work, right? Roughly is where all the crimes are committed."

"Then let's not be rough," Tessa said.

So they weren't.

They started at the Observatory Circle here — High Bank's circle, the one the octagon hangs off — and Naila did the thing she does, which is to stop being a person and become a measurement for a while. She found the entry causeway, paced the chord, set Frik with a pole at one node and walked the tape out across the flat with the wind pulling at it and the highway humming somewhere behind the trees, that distant traffic-hush that follows you everywhere these places survive, the sound of the present leaning on the past and the past not noticing.

The circle came up true. Not roughly. The diameter at High Bank ran out under her tape and her steps and her cross-checks and it sat where it was supposed to sit, where the same diameter sits a county away in Licking, the same 1,054 feet give or take the tolerance of two people and a tape on a windy afternoon — which is to say, give or take *nothing that matters.*

"Talk me through it," Tessa said. She wasn't asking because she didn't know. Naila understood that much. Tessa had walked this ground her whole professional life and a good deal of it before that, with her family's names in it. She was asking the way a teacher asks — to hear whether the student had the thing in their hands or only in their mouth.

"The unit," Naila said. "The diameter of the Observatory Circle up at Newark. That length. It's not just *a* length they happened to use. It lays out the whole site. The circles up there sit six of these apart, centre to centre. Six OCD. It's a module." She paused with the tape sagging between her fingers. "And it's *here.* The same module. The same ruler. Fifty-three miles south, built — when? Generations apart?"

"We don't have it to the year," Tessa said. "We have it to the lifetimes."

"The lifetimes." Naila tasted it. "So someone carried the number."

"Someone carried the number."

Frik had gone very quiet, which for Frik is a kind of alarm. He stood at his node with the pole planted and looked at the line of the tape running away from him to Naila, this hundred-metre fact lying across an empty Ohio field, and you could see him trying to make it into a coincidence and failing, the way you fail to un-see a word once you've read it.

"Hang on," he said. "Hang on, hang on. A standard unit of length. That's — that's not a tool. That's an *agreement.* You can't have a shared ruler without people who agreed to share it. Across — how do you even —" He turned to Tessa, and there was the puncture-instinct firing, but inward now, at his own building excitement, the way the best of him does. "Okay. Stop me. Tell me why this is nothing. Tell me everybody used the same arm-span or paced the same way or — give me the boring version, because I want it and I can't find it."

"I can't give you the boring version," Tessa said, "because there isn't one. Not that I'd lie to you about."

The fish — and there is always a fish, somewhere just off camera, heckling the moment so nobody gets to be solemn about it — would point out here that a shared unit of measurement is the single most aggressively *un*-mystical thing a civilisation can have. It's a standards committee. It's a weights-and-measures office. It's the least romantic possible explanation, and it is *enormously* more interesting than aliens, and that's the joke the whole place is telling at the expense of everyone who came looking for a flying saucer.

---

They aligned the gates after lunch, with the light going long.

This is the part the chapter has been circling, and it has to be done with a cold eye, because the temperature of the eye is what separates this from a séance.

The octagon's gateways are not random. At Newark, Hively and Horn established it past serious dispute: the walls and the openings between them frame the rise and set points of the Moon across the full standstill cycle, all eight of them, to within roughly half a degree. The far gateway clears the major-standstill moonrise within about half a degree of the figure's exact centre. Twice as precise as Stonehenge, the Ohio History Connection says, and they are not a body given to overstatement.

High Bank does it too. Same alignments. Same Moon. Sighted along its own axis, the octagon here opens onto the standstill points like the one up north — a second instrument, tuned to the same impossible-to-watch sky, built by the same ruler.

Naila stood in the gateway Tessa pointed her to and sighted down the axis to the horizon, and the cold thing arrived in her chest where the warm thing about the fifty-three miles had been all day, and the two of them sat together without arguing, which is the only honest way to hold this place.

Because here's the hole, and it is not a hole in the evidence. The evidence is whole. The hole is in the *easy feeling* you wanted to have about it.

The 18.6-year standstill cycle is invisible to one person. You cannot find it in a year. You cannot find it in ten. There is no clever observer, no lone genius on a hill with good eyes and a free evening, who *sees* this pattern and goes home and builds it. The cycle is longer than the patience of any single watching. You find it one way only: somebody watches the eastern horizon, and marks where the Moon turns back, and keeps the record — and dies — and hands the record to someone who keeps watching, and marks, and keeps it, and hands it on. For the better part of a lifetime, before the *shape* of the thing is even knowable. And then they build the finished shape into the dirt, basket by basket, to half a degree, in two places, with the same ruler.

"You keep saying *handed forward,*" Naila said. Her voice came out smaller than she meant. "The record. Handed forward."

"Yes."

"That's not a figure of speech for you."

Tessa stood in the gateway beside her and looked down the axis at the same horizon, and the LENS, off to the side and low, did not raise the camera, because some moments you photograph and some you let belong to the people standing in them, and the LENS, who has gotten people killed by knowing the difference too late, knows the difference.

"My grandmother handed me things she got from people who got them from people," Tessa said. "Not measurements. I'm not telling you there's a secret cord in a box somewhere with the OCD knotted into it. I don't have that, and if I had it, it wouldn't be yours." She said it flatly, no heat, a fence post going into the ground. "But I know what it is to be a link in something you didn't start and won't finish. To keep a thing accurately because keeping it accurately is the whole job, and the meaning of it is somewhere up ahead where you won't get to stand." She nodded at the gateway, at the gone Moon's appointed door. "*That,* I understand from the inside. That's not a mystery to me. That's a *family.*"

---

Frik had wandered up the wall and now he came back down it sideways, sliding a little in the grass, and he had the look — the one where he's about to push a soft thing too hard, and the value of him is that he knows it even as he does it.

"So if there's a shared ruler," he said, "and it's at two sites, and it's the same to within nothing — doesn't that mean somebody *taught* it? Like — a person, a teacher, somebody who went from here to there with the number, somebody who —"

"Careful," Naila said, before Tessa had to.

"No, I know, I know, I'm reaching, I'm watching myself reach —" Frik held both hands up, surrendering to his own arrest. "It just — it wants a single hand, doesn't it? Your brain wants one guy. One genius. The Sky-Watcher of Ohio, striding the valley with his magic cord."

"Your brain wants one guy," Tessa agreed. "Mine doesn't."

And there it was — the turn, delivered not as a revelation but as a correction, the way she'd corrected Frik about the octagons in the parking lot, gently and on purpose.

"One genius dies," Tessa said. "One genius takes the number into the ground with him. You can't build *this*" — the gateway, the axis, the half a degree, the fifty-three miles, the lifetimes — "out of one genius. One genius is exactly what *can't* do it. The cycle's too long for a life. The whole thing only works if the knowledge is bigger than any one person who held it. If it's *taught.* If there are people whose job is to hold the standard and hand it on and check the new ones against the old, so the ruler stays the same when the hands holding it have all changed." She looked at the two of them, the pattern-reader and the puncturer, standing in a doorway built by people who measured better than the woo-merchants would ever credit them. "You don't want a genius. You want a *school.*"

The wind came across the flat and the highway hummed and somewhere up north, sixty road-miles away, the same doorway stood open to the same Moon, cut by the same length, by people who agreed on a thing and taught it and died and were replaced by people who agreed on the same thing, on and on, until the shape closed in the ground and nobody who'd started it was alive to see it close.

Naila wound the tape back in slowly, the marker number sweat-faded on the back of her hand, *1,054,* and she did not say the obvious thing, which was the question the fish would heckle and the question the whole season had been leaning on like the highway leans on the field: *who taught the teachers. Who set the length the first time. Whose ruler is it, before it's anyone's.*

She didn't ask it. Tessa wouldn't have answered, and it wasn't Naila's to answer, and the not-asking felt, for the first time on the whole trip, less like a door closing than like one being held open for somebody else to walk through.

"A school," Frik said quietly, trying it on, and finding it fit, and hating a little how much better it was than the magic cord.

"A school," Tessa said. "And it's still in session."

She got in her truck and drove back to Chillicothe, and left them standing in the gateway with the number on Naila's hand and the Moon's appointment in the dirt, fifty-three miles long, and the one length running the whole way between, set by a hand they hadn't read yet.
