# Chapter 8 — Chasing the Standstill

The OHC conference room smelled of burnt coffee and toner, and Frik had covered the long table with paper.

Not in the metaphorical sense. Actual paper — a roll of brown banner stock he'd liberated from a closet, taped down at both ends with the desperate authority of a man who has had three coffees and intends to be vindicated. On it he'd drawn the Octagon. Eight walls, the gaps between them, the causeway running off to the Observatory Circle like a tail. He'd drawn it freehand first and it had come out looking like a stop sign that had eaten a doughnut, so he'd started over with Naila's protractor, and the second one was better, and the third one was actually good, and now he was drawing lines.

That was the part Naila wouldn't let him do casually. Lines from the centre of each gateway, outward, into the empty white where the horizon would be.

"You're at fifty-two point one," she said, not looking up from her laptop. The screen showed a sky simulator, a black field of stars wheeling over a flat green plane, a date readout ticking through the centuries. "Azimuth. Off true north."

"It looks like fifty-three."

"It's fifty-two point one. Use the protractor like a person."

Frik used the protractor like a person.

Across the table, the LENS had a camera in his lap and wasn't using it, which for the LENS was a kind of resting state, the way other people had a screensaver. He was watching the door. Dr. Redwing had said she'd come by once the morning's call with the Foundation's lawyers was done, and the way she'd said *the Foundation's lawyers* had put a particular flatness in the room that nobody had wanted to disturb.

"Okay," Frik said. "Okay. Here's my problem."

"You always have a problem," the LENS said. "It's your contribution."

"My contribution is *load-bearing.*" Frik pointed his pencil at the brown paper. "Here's the deal. I believe the math. I do. Hively and Horn, Earlham, 1982, came to call it a coincidence and left calling it the most precise lunar instrument north of the Rio Grande, fine, I have read it, I have *underlined* it. But."

"There it is."

"But the Moon doesn't rise in *one* spot. That's the whole thing people don't get when they hear 'aligned to the Moon.' The Sun, sure, you can build a doorway to the solstice sunrise, the Sun behaves, the Sun is a good employee. The Moon is — the Moon is *all over the sky.*" He flapped a hand at the ceiling, indicating the Moon's general lack of professionalism. "Month to month it swings way north, way south, north, south. Eighteen-ish degrees of horizon, back and forth, every twenty-seven days. So if you build *eight* walls and *eight* gateways pointing at *eight* directions, and the Moon spends all month sweeping back and forth across that whole arc anyway — "

"Then you'll catch *something*," Naila said. "Eventually. By accident."

"Then you'll catch something eventually by accident. *Thank* you." Frik sat back, vindicated, briefly. "That's my hole. That's the one I want to dig. Eight gates is a lot of gates. You throw eight darts at a wall the Moon's going to walk past, statistically you hit a moonrise. It doesn't *prove* anybody planned it. It proves you drew enough lines."

He meant it. That was the thing he'd want a reader to know about him, if readers thought about him at all: he wasn't *performing* the doubt. He'd lain awake on the air mattress in the rental with the doubt sitting on his chest like the cat he didn't have. He wanted the wonder, badly. He'd wanted it since he was nine and reading about this stuff under the covers. Which was exactly why he didn't trust it. The thing you want is the thing that lies to you.

"So model it," Naila said. "You don't get to feel clever about a hole you haven't measured."

This was, Frik would later admit, the most loving thing anyone said to him that week.

They modelled it. Naila ran the simulator, Frik ran the paper, and they did the cheap version of what two physicists had done with theodolites and forty years less computing. They picked the eight gateways. They took the azimuths off the published survey because Frik refused to trust his own protractor for the final numbers and Naila agreed, on the grounds that he was correct to distrust himself. They told the software: here is Newark, Ohio. Here is the flat eastern horizon, give or take the trees the Hopewell would have cleared. Run the Moon. Run it for the whole cycle.

"Watch the spread," Naila said.

On screen the Moon climbed the eastern edge night after simulated night, and its rising point crawled north, then stalled, then crawled south, then stalled, a slow pendulum that took a month to swing once. Frik watched the rise-points pile up. They didn't fill the arc evenly. They bunched at the ends — the Moon lingered at the extremes of its swing the way a swing lingers at the top before it comes back. The bunching had a name. He could feel the name in his mouth and refused to say it yet.

"It loiters," he said instead.

"It loiters," Naila agreed.

And the loitering points moved. Not within a month — across years. The northernmost rise the Moon reached this year wasn't the northernmost it would reach next year. The whole envelope of the swing breathed wider, then narrower, over — Frik checked the readout, watched the date counter spin — over about eighteen and a half years. At its widest, the Moon at its most northerly rose further north than the Sun ever managed even at midsummer. At its narrowest, nine-odd years later, the swing pulled in close.

"Okay," Frik said quietly. "Okay, that's the thing. That's the eighteen-six." He didn't sound triumphant anymore. "The far edges. The maximum north the swing ever reaches, once every eighteen and a half years. That's the *major standstill.*"

"That's the major standstill."

He looked at his eight lines on the brown paper. He'd been ready to argue eight darts, a wall, dumb luck. But the simulator wasn't showing him a wall the Moon swept past. It was showing him that the Moon only paused at the very edges, and the very edges only reached their furthest *once in nearly two decades*, and that furthest point — the rise-point at the major standstill — was a *single, specific azimuth.*

Not an arc. A spot.

"Where's it land," he said.

Naila was already overlaying it. She brought up his gateway azimuths, the ones from the survey, and laid the simulated standstill moonrise on top.

The door opened.

Dr. Redwing came in with a travel mug and the particular composure of a person who has spent ninety minutes on the phone declining, politely, to be hurried. She looked at the brown paper, at the protractor, at Frik's face, and something in her eased a fraction — not warmth exactly, but the recognition of work being done in the open rather than behind her back.

"You're at the alignments," she said.

"He's trying to break them," the LENS said.

"Good." She set the mug down and didn't sit. "People should. The two who proved it tried to break it first. That's why it held." She nodded at the screen. "What's your objection?"

Frik told her. The eight gates, the wide arc, the darts. He told it straight, didn't soften it for the room, because he'd have wanted someone to tell him straight. *Eight directions is a lot of directions. The Moon covers most of that ground anyway. Catching a moonrise in one of eight gateways isn't a miracle, it's arithmetic.*

Redwing listened all the way through. She didn't interrupt, which Frik had learned to respect in the small number of people who could do it. When he finished she picked up his pencil — asked first, with her eyebrows — and put the tip on the screen, on the loitering points at the northern extreme.

"You're right that the Moon covers the arc," she said. "You're wrong about which part you have to hit." She tapped the extreme. "It doesn't pause in the middle. It pauses *here.* And the place it pauses moves a little every year for eighteen and a half years, and then it's reached as far as it'll ever go, and *that* — " the pencil rested on one point, " — is the standstill. You don't get to throw eight darts at the whole arc. You only get to throw at the edge. And the edge is narrow. And you only see where the true edge is once in a generation."

"So how would you even know where to build the gate," Frik said. He wasn't arguing now. He was asking. There's a difference and the room could hear it. "If the furthest north only happens once every eighteen years — you'd build the gate, and then wait nine years to see the Moon *not* get there, wait another nine, and only then — "

"Only then could you check your work," Redwing said. "Yes." She let that sit. "You'd have to have watched the one before you build it. Which means somebody watched the one before *that.* Which means it was written down — kept — in the only way they had to keep it, which was in people. Handed forward. By the time you laid the basket that fixed that gateway, the person who'd seen the *previous* standstill from this spot was likely dead. You built it on their word. And whoever checks it after you is going to need *your* word, because you'll be gone before it swings back." She looked at the eight lines, fond and unhurried. "That's not eight darts, Frik. That's a relay nobody alive ever ran the whole length of."

The fish — Frik thought of him as the fish, the heckling skeptical thing that lived in his own head and looked, for reasons he'd stopped examining, like a cranky aquarium occupant — the fish opened its mouth to say *sure, but show me the number.* And Frik, for once, agreed with the fish. He wanted the number.

"Show me where it lands," he said. "Versus the gate."

Naila turned the laptop.

She'd done it cleanly. The Octagon's gateways as surveyed; the simulated major-standstill moonrise dropped on top; and the angle between them rendered out to a decimal because Naila would no more round it for drama than she'd lie. The crucial gateway — the one the moonrise was supposed to clear as it cleared the figure end to end, the longest sightline across the whole fifty acres — sat under the modelled rise-point.

The gap between the centre of the gate and the rise-point was half a degree. A hair over, a hair under, depending on how you treated the horizon trees and the wall heights, all of which the published survey treated with more care than Frik's banner paper ever could. But it didn't move out of half a degree. Frik nudged the parameters. He bumped the assumed observer height. He shifted the horizon. He gave it every chance to fall apart, because that was the job, because a hole you don't honestly test isn't a hole, it's a wish.

It stayed inside half a degree.

Half a degree. Frik knew what half a degree was, in the sky, with no telescope, with cord and stakes and baskets and the naked eye. Half a degree was about the width of the Moon itself. They'd built a gateway, across the better part of a quarter-mile, by hand, out of dirt carried in baskets by people without writing or metal or the wheel — and lined it up on a point in the sky that only shows its true edge once every eighteen and a half years, and they'd hit it to within the Moon's own width.

"That's not a fluke," Frik said. It came out smaller than he'd planned.

"No," Redwing said.

"You can't get to half a degree by accident. Not on the one azimuth that only — " He stopped. He looked at the eight gates again and this time saw the argument from the other side: not *eight chances to get lucky*, but *eight points that all have to be right at once*, the whole figure a single instrument, every wall a line in a sentence, and the sentence was the sky. "Eight gates isn't more chances. It's eight things that all had to land." He laughed once, not happily, the laugh of a man whose favourite objection just got up and walked off the table. "I came in here to throw darts. You're telling me they didn't have a dartboard. They had a *blueprint*, and the blueprint was a clock that takes eighteen years to tick, and they couldn't read the whole clock face in a lifetime, so they — they passed the clock down. Until somebody got to *finish* it."

"Somebody got to finish it," Redwing said. "And nobody got to see both ends."

She picked up her mug. The Foundation's lawyers had given her a deadline, the room knew, and the deadline had not been generous, and she had walked in here anyway to watch a man with banner paper try and fail to make the wonder smaller, because she'd seen people try to make it smaller before and she would rather watch it done in good faith.

"You should be there for it," she said, at the door. "Not on a screen. The standstill moonrise. It comes back — " she gave the date, the next major standstill the Octagon would catch, and it was close, close enough that the Foundation wanted to own the cameras for it, which was a whole other fight, which was the fight, really. "Stand at the right end. Watch it clear the far gate. Then you'll stop arguing with the half-degree." She almost smiled. "Or you'll argue better. Either way I'd take you."

The door shut.

Frik looked at his brown paper, at the eight lines he'd drawn to break a thing and had only managed to draw more carefully than before. On the laptop the modelled Moon sat in the gateway, half a degree off centre, exactly as patient as it had been for two thousand years.

"Half a degree," he said to no one.

"Half a degree," Naila agreed, and saved the file.

The LENS, who had not lifted the camera once, lifted it now — and then, watching the two of them lean over the same number from opposite sides of the same table, lowered it again without a frame.
