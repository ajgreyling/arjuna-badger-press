# Chapter 19 — The Overreach

Frik had been awake since four, and by the time the light came up grey over the Observatory Circle he'd filled eleven pages of a steno pad with numbers he was almost certainly going to embarrass himself with.

He knew the shape of it. He'd felt it the way you feel a sneeze building — that lovely, dangerous itch right behind the eyes that meant a pattern was about to arrive whether he'd earned it or not. ADHD gives you a thousand of these a day. The trick, the whole discipline of his life, was knowing which one to feed. Most of them you starved. This one he'd been feeding since midnight, and it had grown teeth.

He found Naila where he knew she'd be: standing dead centre of the Circle, where the morning frost hadn't burned off the grass, holding her arms out a little from her sides the way she did when she was reading a space with her whole body instead of her eyes. The wall ran around them, a thousand feet of it, low and green and impossibly round. From the ground it was just a hump in a field. You had to know to feel the size of it.

"Okay," Frik said. "Don't do your face yet. Let me finish before you do your face."

"I'm not doing a face."

"You're loading a face."

"I'm cold." But she turned, and there was something almost fond in it, which he chose to take as license.

He flipped the steno pad around so she could see the two ratios he'd boxed and underlined three times each.

"Great Circle to Observatory Circle," he said. "Diameter ratio. One point one three one. Right? You measured it, Tessa's people measured it, Hively and Horn measured it. Not in dispute."

"Not in dispute."

"Now." He tapped the second box, and his voice did the thing he hated and couldn't stop, climbed half an octave like a kid showing you a beetle. "The Moon. At perigee — closest — it looks *bigger*, right, fat harvest Moon. At apogee — furthest — smaller. The ratio of how big it looks, near to far. Apparent size. Perigee to apogee."

"Frik."

"One point one two seven."

He let it sit there in the frost. The numbers looked back at him from the page, and they were so *close*, so obscenely close, that he could feel the floor of the field tilting toward the idea like water toward a drain.

"One thirty-one versus one twenty-seven," he said. "That's a zero point three five percent match. The ratio of the two circles is the ratio of the Moon's two faces. They built the Moon's *breathing* into the ground. They put the fat Moon and the thin Moon fifty acres apart and made the dirt remember the difference." He was talking faster now, the sentences crowding each other. "And that's not even the alignment. That's not the standstill. That's a *second channel*. They encoded the cycle in the gateways and they encoded the size-change in the circle diameters, two completely different — Naila, that's two independent astronomical—"

"Where'd you get the perigee-apogee figure."

It stopped him the way a hand on the chest stops you. Not unkind. Just there.

"Astronomical apparent diameter," he said. "Standard values. I checked three sources."

"And the Great Circle diameter."

"Three sixty-six metres. OHC's own number."

"To what tolerance?"

He looked at the page. The thing about a sneeze is that the moment somebody asks you to describe it, it's gone.

"It's an earthwork," he said, more quietly. "It's a wall of dirt that's two thousand years old and got plowed and golf-carted and rained on for a century. The diameter's not a *number*, it's a — it's a fuzz. There's a few feet of fuzz on it either way."

"How many feet of fuzz turns one point one three one into one point one two seven?"

He did the arithmetic he should have done at four in the morning instead of underlining things. It didn't take long. It never takes long once you're honest about the error bars. A couple of feet on a thousand-foot circle was enough to swallow the whole 0.35 percent and ask for seconds.

"Okay," he said. "Okay. The match is inside the noise."

*"Frik,"* said the fish, who had appeared on the wall-top with the radiant cruelty of a creature that has slept eight hours, *"you just discovered that if you measure a fat thing and a slightly-less-fat thing, the bigness goes down. Call the journals."*

"I hate you and you're not real."

*"The Moon's apparent size also depends on where you're standing, what the air's doing, and whether you've had coffee. You did this on no coffee."*

"I did this on rage and a granola bar."

---

Tessa came up the causeway as the sun finally cleared the eastern trees, and she had two paper cups, and she gave one to Naila first, which Frik noted and respected. She'd done that every morning. The hierarchy of the cups was its own small grammar. He was learning to read it.

"You've got the look," Tessa said to him. Not a question.

"He found a ratio," Naila said.

"The size one," Tessa said. "The one-point-one-three-one." She blew across the cup. "Everyone finds the size one. It's the prettiest thing on the whole site that nobody can prove."

Frik felt the floor steady itself, because here at least was someone who'd walked the same drain. "So you've—"

"I had a grad student once spend a summer on it." Tessa looked out across the Circle, at the wall doing its slow impossible round through the burning-off frost. "Good student. She was sure. She had the perigee-apogee to four decimal places and she had a survey and she ran it past me like she was bringing me a kill." A small breath that might have been a laugh once. "I sent her to remeasure the Great Circle. Three times, three different baselines. You know what she got."

"Three different diameters," Frik said.

"Three different diameters." Tessa nodded slowly. "All within a few feet of each other. All within a few feet of three sixty-six. And the ratio she'd staked her summer on lived or died in those few feet. So." She drank. "It's not nothing. I want to be careful how I say this, because the Foundation will quote me. It is not *nothing*. The Hopewell knew the Moon's near face from its far face — you can't watch the eastern horizon for forty years and not see the Moon get fat and thin. They *had* that knowledge. I'd bet my career they had it." She turned, and the look she gave Frik was the one he'd come to think of as the floor under the floor, the place where she stopped being a host and was only the keeper. "But knowing a thing and *building* a thing are different verbs. I can't show you they built it. The dirt won't say. And I won't put words in my ancestors' mouths to win an argument with a man who's well-funded."

Frik thought about the Foundation rep — Devlin, perpetually pressed, perpetually warm — who that very week had used the phrase *the Moon's heartbeat encoded in the earth* in a slide deck Frik had seen over a shoulder. It had a beautiful kerning. It was, Frik now understood with a cold drop in the gut, *exactly the thing he'd been about to do at four in the morning.* Same reach. Better suit.

"He's going to publish the ratio," Frik said. "Isn't he."

"He's going to publish the ratio," Tessa agreed, "with a graphic. The fat Moon and the thin Moon and a little dotted line. And it'll be the thing people remember, because it's *cute*, and the standstill cycle isn't cute, it's just true and it takes eleven minutes to explain." She didn't say it bitterly. She said it like weather she'd lived under a long time. "That's the part that gets to me. The real thing here doesn't need the pretty thing. The real thing is the biggest thing I know."

---

Naila had crouched to the grass while they talked, and now she said, without looking up, "Walk me through what you'd actually have to do."

"To prove the ratio?"

"To *do* the thing he's claiming they did." Her voice had gone flat and careful, which meant she was building. "Not see the Moon fat and thin. Anyone can see that. To put the difference *in the ground*. You'd have to decide, before you laid a single basket, that this circle is the fat Moon and that circle is the thin Moon. You'd have to fix the ratio first. Then survey it. Then hold that ratio across — what, fifty acres — basket by basket, by hand, no instruments, no writing, for however many years it took. And the whole time you'd be holding two numbers in your head that no one could check until it was done, and that no one watching could ever see were *there*, because from the ground" — she gestured at the wall, the long green nothing of it — "it's just round. You can only see what they made from the sky." She stood up. "That's the potato."

"The what," said Tessa.

"It's a thing we say. The *easy* version of the story. The version that makes it small. 'The Hopewell saw the Moon change size and copied it into two circles' — that *sounds* easy. Like tracing." She turned to Frik. "But you just walked me through how it isn't easy. It's the opposite of easy. The easy story has more genius hidden in it than the hard story does."

Frik looked at his steno pad. Eleven pages. He thought about tearing them out and decided not to, because they were a record of a man being wrong in a way that taught him something, and that was worth keeping. Patience, basket by basket. He could do that with a steno pad, at least.

"So I don't get to have it," he said. He meant the ratio. He meant the lovely 0.35 percent. He meant the slide with the dotted line.

"You get to *say maybe*," Tessa said.

And there it was — the thing Frik had come to think of as the gift she kept handing out and watching to see who'd actually take it. Devlin couldn't take it; *maybe* didn't fundraise. The decoy debunkers couldn't take it either, the ones who needed every wonder reduced to coincidence by Tuesday. *Maybe* was the hardest stance in the whole field, because it didn't sell tickets in either direction.

"The ratio's a maybe," Tessa went on. "A real one. Not a 'we don't know yet because we're lazy' maybe — a 'the evidence genuinely doesn't reach' maybe. The dirt has a few feet of fuzz and the fuzz is bigger than the claim. So we stand in the fuzz. We say: they could have. It's there to find, if you measure the right way someday, with the right baseline, and even then it might just be that round things have ratios. We *don't know*." She said it like it cost nothing, which Frik now understood meant it had once cost everything. "And the not-knowing isn't the hole in the wonder. The not-knowing *is* the wonder. The standstill cycle's not a maybe — that one's nailed, half a degree, all eight points, go and check Hively and Horn, it's in the journals. But the size-ratio? That's mine to leave open. And I'm leaving it open."

The fish, who had been suspiciously quiet, said from the wall-top, *"He wanted to bring you a kill, too,"* and for once there was no heckle in it. Just the observation, landed once.

"Yeah," Frik said. "I did."

*"And she sent you to remeasure the circle."*

"She did."

---

The frost was almost gone now. Where it hung on in the wall's shadow you could still see, faintly, the lay of the land the way the morning had first shown it — the long curve, the gateway off to the east where in eighteen and a half years' time the major-standstill Moon would heave itself up and clear the far cut within half a degree of the centre of a figure no single person who started it had lived to see close.

Frik stood in the middle of it with his wrong pages and his cold coffee and the most useful failure he'd had in months, and he found he didn't want the ratio anymore. The ratio was a thing you could own — publish it, kern it, dot the line. The maybe was a thing you could only stand inside.

Devlin would never stand in it. That was, Frik thought, the whole quiet war of this place: not whether the math was real — it was, the hard part was settled and revered and twice as precise as the famous stones everyone looked at instead — but whether you could love a record enough to leave its softest part *un*-resolved. To let the keeper keep the gap.

"I'm going to write it as a maybe," he said.

"Good," said Naila.

"And I'm going to let her say the maybe. Not me."

Tessa didn't answer that. She was looking east, at the gateway, at the long slow door cut into the dirt by people who'd handed the watching forward across more years than any of them would get. She had the cup in both hands. She was a scientist and she was a descendant and right now she was only one of those, and it wasn't Frik's to name which.

He capped his pen. The highway sound came faint over the wall, the way it always did, the present tense leaning on the deep past, and the Moon was somewhere below the eastern trees getting fat or thin, breathing its 0.35 percent that maybe — *maybe* — somebody two thousand years ago had bet a lifetime of baskets on.

Frik decided he could live in that maybe.

He decided it was the best room in the building.
