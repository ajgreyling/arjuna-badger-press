# Chapter 4 — The Potato and the Fort

The Great Circle didn't announce itself. That was the first thing wrong with it, if you came looking for a fort.

Frik came over the lip of the embankment expecting — he didn't know what. A rampart, maybe. Something that said *keep out* in the universal grammar of dirt thrown up by frightened men. Instead the wall rose gentle and green under his boots, mowed like a municipal park, and on the far side it sloped down into a ditch, and past the ditch the ground opened into a bowl of grass big enough to lose a cathedral in. Eight feet of wall. A thousand-odd feet across. Geese on it. A man in a windbreaker walking a beagle along the inner crest as though it were the perimeter path of any suburb in Ohio, which it was, because it was.

"Okay," said Frik, to no one. "Okay, hold on."

He had a notebook. He had, specifically, a page headed FORT in capital letters with a box drawn around it, because he liked to argue with the boring answer before he let himself like the interesting one. Naila said this was the most ADHD thing about him, the need to wrestle the dull theory to the ground first, and she said it with affection, which was how she said most things to him, in a flat voice that took him a year to learn was warmth.

The fort theory was a good theory. He wanted to be fair to it. It was, in fact, the *first* theory — eighteenth-, nineteenth-century surveyors riding up on these walls and reaching for the only word their world handed them for big regular earth, which was *defensive*. Of course they did. You see a wall, you think someone's behind it with a bow. The whole continent of Europe had taught them to read a raised ring of earth as a thing that meant *war*. They weren't fools. They were doing exactly what Frik was doing right now: bringing the ruler they already owned to a thing that didn't come with one.

"So," he said, louder, to Naila, who was twenty feet off with her shoes already in her hand because she said she couldn't feel a grade through rubber. "Pretend you've never heard the real story. You ride up here in 1840. What do you write down?"

"Fort." She didn't look up. She was watching her own feet on the slope, the way she did, reading the angle through the soles. "Earthwork enclosure. Defensive. Probably."

"Right! Right. Big wall, you hide your village inside it, you shoot the bad guys off the top." He paced along the crest, arms going. "It's clean. It's the kind of answer that lets you go home and have dinner. I *like* it. It doesn't need anything weird. No calendars, no genius, no eighteen-point-six years of anything. Just scared people and a shovel."

*And it'd be a fine answer,* something in him said, in a voice that sounded suspiciously like the fish, *if you'd quit walking and look at where you put your feet.*

He stopped. He looked at where he'd put his feet.

He was on top of the wall. To his left, the inside — the great green bowl. To his right, the ditch.

The ditch was on the inside.

---

The thing about a defensive ditch is that a child understands it. You dig the trench, you pile the dirt up *behind* the trench facing your enemy, so the man charging you has to drop down into a hole and then climb a wall while you stand on top and discourage him. Moat, then keep. Trench, then parapet. Ditch *outside*. It is not a subtle piece of engineering. It is arguably the least subtle thing humans have ever agreed on. Every hillfort in Britain, every motte-and-bailey, every Iron Age rampart from here to Anatolia: ditch out, bank in. You could lose the blueprints for the whole species and rederive it from one frightened afternoon.

Here the ditch was *inside* the wall.

Frik stood very still and let it land, because it was the kind of fact that didn't land if you read it off a page — and he *had* read it off a page, he'd read it in three books, *the ditch is interior, indicating ceremonial rather than defensive function,* and it had gone in one ear and out the other as the dry kind of sentence experts wrote to fill space. It was a completely different animal standing on the wall with the trench at your right hand, knowing in your body that an attacker coming over this would *land on the high ground.* You'd have dug your own enemy a parapet. You'd have built the fort backwards. You'd have, with enormous patient labour, basket by basket, hand by hand, constructed the single worst defensive structure it is possible to imagine, a thing that doesn't merely fail to defend but actively assists the assault.

"Naila." His voice had gone strange. "Naila, the ditch is —"

"On the wrong side. Yeah." She'd known the whole time. She'd let him get there. "It's been on the wrong side for two thousand years."

"That's —" He laughed, the bark he did when a thing he'd been condescending to turned around and looked at him. "That's not a mistake. You don't make that mistake *once*, never mind around the whole —" He turned, following the green ring of it with his arm, all the way round, the geese, the beagle, the windbreaker man now a far comma against the far wall. "It goes the whole way round. The wrong side goes all the way round."

*Took you long enough,* said the fish, who was not there, who was a habit Frik had instead of a god. *That FORT page looking a little embarrassed, champ?*

He flipped to the FORT page and drew a line through it. Then, because he was honest, and because the whole job was being honest about your own reach, he wrote underneath: *me, ten minutes ago, very confident.* He'd put it in the chapter. He always put himself in. The day he stopped puncturing his own overconfidence was the day he became one of the men on the internet who knew everything.

---

Tessa Redwing watched all of this from down in the bowl, near the centre, where she'd set up nothing and was doing nothing, which Frik had come to understand was its own kind of doing. She had a thermos. She let them flail. She was a culture-bearer with a doctorate and the patience of a person who has watched a great many enthusiastic strangers discover, with the joy of a toddler finding its own foot, a thing her family had carried as ordinary knowledge for longer than enthusiasm had been a word. She didn't correct them. She didn't help. She watched the way you watch someone reading a letter that was addressed to you.

The LENS was further off, on the south arc, camera down for once. Frik clocked him clocking Tessa — the photographer's instinct to frame her against the wall, the visible decision *not* to, the lens swinging instead to the empty geese-dotted grass. The man had rules about whose face belonged to whom. Frik didn't fully understand them but he'd learned to trust the way the camera hung loose at the end of his arm when it meant *not mine.*

Frik came down off the wall to where Tessa stood, sliding the last few feet on the wet grass like a kid, recovering badly.

"It's not a fort," he announced, as though breaking news.

"No," she agreed.

"The ditch is on the inside."

"Mm."

"I drew a *box* around the word." He showed her the crossed-out page. He wanted, he realised, for her to be amused, and was annoyed at himself for wanting it, because that was a kind of asking-to-be-let-in, and it wasn't his to ask. She looked at the page. She was amused, a little, around the eyes, which was a gift and he knew it.

"People have been crossing out that word for a hundred and fifty years," she said. "And then drawing it again. There's a thing that happens." She screwed the cap back on her thermos. "They give up *fort*, because the ditch embarrasses them. And then they need a new word that does the same job, that lets them stop. So they reach for the next one."

"Coincidence," said Naila, from up on the wall. She'd been listening. She was always listening; she just didn't always look like it.

"Coincidence," Tessa agreed, and there was something under the word, the faintest weariness, a person who had heard this one too.

---

Frik had a coincidence page too. He flipped to it now, because Naila had come down and was already at work in the dirt with a length of mason's line and two tent pegs, doing the thing she did where the whole social world fell away and there was only the geometry, and he knew the look, and the look meant the chapter was about to get harder.

The coincidence theory went like this, and it was *also* a good theory, and Frik tried to give it the dinner-and-go-home respect it had earned: people see patterns. People are pattern machines. You build a big regular shape, you've got eight walls and a bunch of gateways, and you've got a sky full of things rising and setting in every direction, and *of course* if you go looking you'll find some gateway that lines up with some moonrise, because the sky is crowded and the gateways are many and human beings will draw a line between any two dots you hand them. Astrology runs on this. So does the face on Mars. You can align Stonehenge to the bus stop if you let yourself. The honest skeptic's whole job is to refuse the line your hungry eye wants to draw.

"This one's harder to kill," Frik admitted, crouching by Naila. "Because it's *true* about people. We do that. I do that. I aligned my breakfast to my mood this morning."

"Walk it with me," said Naila. She wasn't really asking. She'd run the line from a peg to the gateway gap in the wall and she was sighting along it with one eye shut, and her voice had the steadiness it got when the numbers were holding her up. "The coincidence story works if there are lots of targets and lots of lines. Lots of arrows, lots of haystacks, one's bound to hit. Right?"

"Right."

"So the killer isn't *is there an alignment.* The killer is *how many things does this one alignment have to hit at once, and how tight.*" She moved the line a hand's width and the gateway gap went from framed to not-framed, just like that, a door that was a door only from exactly here. "Hold this."

He held it. Through the gap, the far wall, the morning sky going pale over Newark, the hum of the highway coming over the grass like weather.

"Hively and Horn," Naila said, "came here in 'eighty-two to *kill* the alignment. Physicist and a philosopher. They wanted it to be coincidence. They expected coincidence." A peg in, a peg out. "And what they got was eight. Not one lucky moonrise. All eight standstill points. Both ends of the swing, north and south, rise and set, the whole envelope the Moon makes when it walks — built into the gateways. To half a degree."

"Half a degree's the width of —"

"The Moon. About. Yeah." She sat back on her heels in the wet. "You can have a coincidence hit one target. You start asking it to hit eight, all from the same figure, all to inside the Moon's own width, and the haystack runs out of needles. It stops being *the sky is crowded so something had to fit.* It becomes *somebody aimed.*"

The fish had gone quiet in Frik's head, which was how he knew it was real. The fish heckled the reach. The fish had nothing to say to a number that held.

"And here's where I want to overreach," Frik said, because he could feel it, the pull, the thing in him that wanted to take the lovely hard fact and yank it past where it would go. "Here's where I want to say *therefore* something. Therefore advanced. Therefore taught. Therefore —" He shut his mouth. He'd seen the documentaries. He knew the shape of the slide, the one that ended with a man in a leather jacket saying *now, mainstream archaeologists won't tell you.* "Therefore nothing. The fact's the fact. Eight points, half a degree. The fact doesn't owe me a *therefore*."

Tessa, who had drifted close, said nothing. But she was looking at him differently now, the way you look at a stranger who's just declined an easy thing, and Frik understood that the not-reaching was the entrance fee, and that there were further rooms, and that they were hers to open.

---

He climbed back up on the wall before they left. He wanted the ditch again, the wrong-side ditch, the embarrassing trench that the whole century of fort-talk had stepped over.

Down in the bowl Naila was coiling her line, and Tessa was standing where she'd stood the whole time, near the centre, on the spot that was the spot for some reason none of them had been told yet, and the LENS had finally raised the camera and was photographing the grass, only the grass, the empty patient grass with the geese on it and not one human face.

The fort theory was clean. Frik wanted to be fair to it to the end. It was clean and it was wrong, and it was wrong for the simplest reason in the world, a reason you could feel through your boots: the dirt was on the wrong side to keep anyone out. Whatever this was, it was not built by people afraid of an enemy at the wall. It was built by people who'd decided, with two thousand years of labour, that the important direction was *in*. Toward the centre. Toward whatever Tessa was standing near and not naming.

He looked at the crossed-out word in his book.

Then he turned the page, because the next theory was already lining up — *local fluke, one clever village, a one-off* — and he could see, from the way Naila kept glancing southwest toward Chillicothe, toward the second octagon fifty-three miles down the road with the same ruler in its bones, that the page was going to get a line through it too.

The geese lifted off the inner ditch all at once, the way they do, for no reason a person can see.
