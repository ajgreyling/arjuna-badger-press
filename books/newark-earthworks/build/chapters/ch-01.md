# Chapter 1 — The Mark in the Grass

The frost was the first thing that read as deliberate.

Naila saw it before she saw the earthwork — a long band of white grass lying lower and brighter than the grass around it, a seam where the cold had pooled in a hollow that ran arrow-straight across the fairway and then turned, at an angle she could feel in her teeth before she could name it, and ran straight again. Somebody had mowed the lawn. The frost had found the geometry anyway.

"That's it," she said.

Frik, who had been arguing with the parking situation, stopped. "That's what? That's a golf course."

"That's the wall."

He squinted along the line of her arm. Behind them the rental's engine ticked as it cooled. Ahead, past a low chain and a sign that said the things signs say — PLEASE RESPECT — the land rose into a swell so gentle you'd walk over it carrying coffee and never know you'd crossed anything at all. A man in a windbreaker was wheeling a bag-cart up the rise. He went up one side of the ancient embankment and down the other without a hitch in his stride, the way you'd cross a speed bump.

"Okay," Frik said slowly. "Okay. He just— he walked over a two-thousand-year-old astronomical instrument to go hit a small ball."

"He doesn't know."

"*Nobody* knows. That's insane. That's the most insane thing I've—" He got out his phone, held it up, put it down again. "You can't even photograph it. There's nothing to photograph. It's *grass.*"

That was the part Naila kept turning over. From the air — she'd seen the air, everybody had seen the air, the survey plates and the drone shots that turned the whole thing into a diagram — the Octagon was a thunderclap: eight walls, five hundred and fifty feet each, fifty acres of folded ground holding a near-perfect figure, hinged by a neck of causeway to a circle eleven hundred feet across. From above it was a proof. You could not argue with it. You could barely believe a person had stood on the ground that made it.

And here was the ground. From the ground it was almost nothing. A man with a five-iron crossing a rumple in the lawn.

The Lens came up beside her, camera down at the hip, not raised. He'd ridden the last hour in silence with the window cracked, and he had that stillness on him that meant he was already working, already deciding what he would not take. He looked along the frost-seam the way Naila had, and then he looked at the golfer, and something moved in his jaw.

"Don't," she said, quietly, before she knew she was going to.

"I wasn't."

"You were going to."

"I was deciding whether to," he said, which was honest, and lowered the strap an inch more, and that was that. The golfer reached the green and was gone behind the swell. The frost held its line. The highway, somewhere off past the far walls, made its long indifferent sound, the sound of the present tense going about its business over the top of the past.

---

Dr. Tessa Redwing was waiting by the gate, and she had been waiting in a way that made clear she had not been waiting *for* them, exactly. She'd been here. They had arrived into where she already was.

She was perhaps sixty, in a field coat the colour of the wet ground, with a thermos and a clipboard and reading glasses pushed up into grey hair, and she watched the four of them come up the path the way you watch weather you've seen before — not unkindly, but with a working knowledge of what it tends to do. When Frik stuck out his hand and started talking she let him finish, which took a while, and then she shook it once.

"You're the film people," she said.

"We're—" Frik started.

"You're the film people." Not a correction. A placement. "The Connection said you'd come. There's a research window open right now, so you'll want to keep off the marked grass and out of the way of the golf, what's left of it. The lease is winding down. It is not wound down. People still live their Saturdays out here." She nodded at the man's distant green. "He paid his dues. He gets his round."

"It's a *golf course*," Frik said, with the particular outrage of a man who has just learned a thing and needs everyone to feel it as freshly as he does. "On *this.*"

"For about a hundred years," Tessa said. "Mowed it. Watered it. Put in some sand traps." A beat. "Kept the developers off it, too, which is more than the City of Newark managed for the other nine-tenths of it." She let that sit. "So I'd hold the outrage for a minute and decide where you want to spend it."

Naila liked her immediately, with the clean fast certainty she felt about very few people and almost no rooms.

"Three thousand acres," Tessa went on, because Frik had gone quiet and somebody had to fill the air, and she'd clearly decided it would be her, on her terms. "That's what was here. Circles, squares, octagons, ellipses, miles of parallel walls running off across what's now subdivisions and the bypass and a Kroger. People drive over the parallel walls four times a day. Less than ten percent left. This." She turned and took in the swell of it, fifty acres of folded earth that from where they stood looked like a slightly interesting public park. "And the Great Circle, a couple miles that way. That's the survivors."

"Who built it?" The Lens, low.

"The people the archaeologists call Hopewell." A small pause on the name, a pause Naila clocked and filed and did not touch. "Which isn't what they called themselves, because we don't know what they called themselves, because they didn't write. The name's off a farm. A man named Hopewell owned a field with mounds in it in the eighteen-nineties, and the name stuck to a whole world of people across four hundred years because a farmer was standing nearby when the archaeologists showed up." Dry as the frost. "So when you say *Hopewell* in your film, you might let your viewers know they're hearing the name of a dead man's pasture, not a nation."

Frik wrote that down. Naila watched him write it and watched Tessa watch him write it, and understood that the doctor had handed them something true and small to see whether they'd take it greedily or carefully, and that this had been a test, and that they had — barely — passed it by Frik having a pen.

---

Naila walked the near wall while they talked permissions.

She did it the way she did everything, which was with her whole attention and none of her social cover, walking the top of the embankment with her eyes on her feet and then on the turn ahead, feeling the grade come up under her boots — gentle, gentle, three feet, maybe four, of raised ground, nothing, a child could run up it — and then the turn.

The turn was the thing.

She stopped at the corner where one wall ended and the next began, where the figure changed its mind by a precise and intended amount, and she stood in the cold morning with her arms out a little for balance and felt the angle the way you feel a chord resolve. It was *exact.* Not exact-looking. Exact in the body, exact the way a step is exact when the stair is built true and your foot lands on nothing because there's nothing there to land on, a perfect absence. The wall didn't wander to its corner and round it off the way a thing piled up by guesswork wanders. It came in straight and it turned through an angle somebody had *decided*, and it left straight, and the deciding was still in the ground after two thousand years of frost and golf and rain.

Basket by basket. That was the part her mind kept snagging on, the way a coat snags a nail. No machines. No metal. No draft animals — she'd read it on the plane and not believed it and now stood on the proof of it. People had carried this. People had filled baskets with earth from a borrow pit and carried the baskets up the rising wall and tipped them out and gone back for more, and they had done it to a *plan*, to a number, to a tolerance, for so long that the people who started the wall were dead before the wall was a wall, and the people who finished it had never met the people who'd surveyed it, and somehow the angle was still exact.

You did not get this by accident. You did not get this by feel. You got this by a number, kept.

She crouched and put her palm flat on the frost. It was just cold grass. It was just dirt. It was the most precise dirt she had ever stood on.

"She does that," she heard Frik say, somewhere behind her, fond and apologetic at once, the thing he said about her in new rooms so the new room would file it correctly. *She does that.* Translating her into a register the room could hold.

She didn't mind. She kept her hand on the cold ground and let the angle be exact in her body and didn't say any of it out loud, because there wasn't anybody yet she wanted to say it to who would hear the number under the words.

When she stood up, Tessa was watching her. Not Frik. Her.

The doctor didn't say *she does that.* The doctor didn't say anything. She just watched Naila come down off the wall with an expression Naila couldn't read, which was rare, which was almost unheard of — and then she looked away first, back to her clipboard, and the moment closed like water.

---

There was a fifth person, and Naila had not seen him arrive.

He was just suddenly there at the gate, weathered, in a canvas coat gone soft with age, holding a paper cup of gas-station coffee and looking at the earthwork with the particular flat attention of a man reading a thing rather than admiring it. He didn't introduce himself to the crew and the crew didn't introduce themselves to him, and Tessa gave him a single nod that had a whole history folded into it, the nod you give someone you've worked a hard site with before.

"Jakobus," Frik said, under his breath, to Naila. "That's the fixer. That's the—"

"I know who it is."

The man — Jakobus — didn't walk the wall. He looked at the gap between the far walls, the gateway out at the east end where the two embankments stopped and left an opening to the horizon, and then he looked back along the line of that gap toward the centre of the figure, sighting it the way you'd sight down a rifle or a rule. He did it once. He nodded to himself, the small private nod of a man who has confirmed that a made thing was made *for* something, and that the something points east. Then he drank his coffee and said nothing at all, and the saying-nothing was so complete it was almost a kind of manners.

He was the road, not the lead. Naila had been told that and not understood it. She understood it now: he came in, he read the thing as a thing-for-a-purpose, and then he stood back and let the gateway belong to whoever it belonged to. It belonged to Tessa. He knew that better than any of them, which was why he'd shut his mouth.

---

"You want the line you'll put under the picture," Tessa said.

They were back at the gate. The light had come up gold and the frost was going off the grass in patches, the geometry softening back into lawn as the sun took the cold seam away. In an hour you would not be able to see it at all. In an hour it would be a park again, and the man with the five-iron, and the highway.

"You want the sentence," Tessa said again, and there was the faintest edge to it, because she knew how the film people worked, knew they needed the line, and she'd decided to give them one rather than let them go off and invent a worse one. "Here it is. It's the Ohio History Connection's, not mine, so quote it straight. The surveying out here is twice as precise as Stonehenge."

Frik laughed — the bark of a man whose decoy has just been kicked out from under him. "*Twice—* Stonehenge is the most famous—"

"Stonehenge is famous," Tessa agreed. "Stonehenge gets the postcards. Stonehenge has a gift shop the size of a barn." She gathered her thermos and her clipboard. "And the people who measured *this* one, in this field, with cord and baskets and no writing and no metal and no wheel, were working to a tolerance that makes the famous one look approximate." She looked at the frost-line one more time before it went. "And they were tracking something a great deal harder than the Sun."

She let that hang. She did not finish it. She picked up her things and she did not finish it, and Naila felt the unfinished part go down into her like the cold going into the ground.

"Harder how?" Frik said.

But Tessa was already walking, and the answer, whatever it was, was hers, and she had decided — for now, for this morning, on her ground — to keep it.

Behind them, somewhere past the far gateway, the Moon was down and the Sun was up and the highway kept making its long ordinary noise over the top of the most precise dirt in North America, and nobody on it knew.
