# Chapter 9 — Permission and Custody

The conference room at the Ohio History Connection smelled of carpet glue and yesterday's coffee, and through the window the LENS could see the parking lot, the strip of grass, and beyond it the first faint swell of an earthen wall that most of the people in this building drove past without seeing. He had set the camera down on the table, lens cap on. A small discipline. There were rooms where you shot and rooms where you watched, and he had learned — slowly, expensively — to tell them apart.

This was a watching room.

Eight people around a table built for twelve. A woman named Diane from OHC with a binder she kept squaring against the table edge, the way you straighten a thing you cannot control. Two more staff, younger, taking notes nobody would read. Tessa Redwing at the head of the table — not because anyone had seated her there, but because she had walked in last and that was the chair that was open, and she had taken it without apology, set down a thermos, and folded her hands.

And across from her, the Foundation. A man named Marcus Hale, who wore the kind of soft grey jacket that costs money to look like it costs nothing, and who had a way of leaning in when he spoke, as if every sentence were a confidence.

"We're not asking for anything that isn't already in the public record," Hale said. "The Hively-Horn work is forty years old. Published. Cited a thousand times. What we're proposing is synthesis. A modern ephemeris run against the gateways, lidar over the whole figure, a clean popular account that finally tells the world what this place *is*." He spread his hands. "We'd credit OHC. We'd credit everyone."

*Synthesis,* the LENS thought. He had heard the word in other rooms, in other countries, usually from people standing on something that belonged to someone else.

Naila was beside him, very still, which was its own kind of loud. She had a notebook open and she had not written anything in it for four minutes. Her eyes were on the laminated site plan in the middle of the table — the Octagon and the Observatory Circle, joined by their little neck of causeway, the whole figure rendered in clean printer's lines as though it had come out of a drawer rather than out of the dirt. The LENS watched her watch it. He knew that stillness. It meant she had found the part of the diagram that was wrong, or the part that was right in a way nobody else had noticed, and she was deciding whether it was hers to say.

"Twice as precise as Stonehenge," Frik said, to no one, reading off the wall where OHC had hung the phrase on a banner in their own brand colours. "That's your line, isn't it? OHC's." He tipped his chair back. "It's a good line. Bit unfair to Stonehenge, but a good line."

Diane almost smiled. Hale did not.

The official story sat in the room with them, and it was true, and the LENS gave it its due the way you give a dead man his due — quietly, with both hands. A hundred BCE to four hundred CE. Three thousand acres of geometry raised basket by basket by people with no metal, no draft animals, no writing, no plan they could photograph from above and check. Eight walls of five hundred and fifty feet. A circle a thousand feet across true to within a few feet all the way round. And the gateways set so the Moon, at the far end of an eighteen-and-a-half-year swing, would rise and clear the far gate within half a degree of the figure's exact centre.

Half a degree. The width of the Moon itself, more or less, held over two thousand years and a golf course.

It was not woo. That was the part the LENS kept coming back to. There was nothing here to debunk. The miracle was on the plaque. The miracle was *settled.*

"Here's our difficulty," Diane said, and squared the binder again. "The lease transition isn't finished. We have public-access obligations now that we didn't have eighteen months ago. We have a tribal consultation protocol that — " she glanced at Tessa, the smallest tilt of the head, asking permission to describe a thing in front of the person it belonged to — "that we are bound to honour. And we have the academic community, who would like very much to be first."

"Everyone would like to be first," Tessa said.

It was not sharp. That was what the LENS noticed, lifting the cap off the camera now after all, slow, because some sentences want a face on them. It was not sharp at all. She said it the way you'd point out the weather.

Hale leaned in. "Dr. Redwing, with respect, the science here doesn't change hands. Hively and Horn didn't ask anyone's permission to measure the alignment, and the alignment is what it is. The Moon does what the Moon does. We're not interpreting a *belief*. We're documenting a *fact*."

The LENS had the shot before Hale finished — Tessa's hands, unmoved, and the thermos sweating a small ring onto the table, and behind her through the window the green line of the wall. He did not press the shutter. He had a rule, lately, about whose moment a thing was, and this was not his to take yet.

"Walk it with me," Tessa said.

—

The frost was off the grass by the time they got out there, but the cold had stayed in the ground, and the LENS could feel it come up through his boots as they crossed onto the Octagon. Hale had come too, in shoes that were wrong for it. Diane stayed by the cars, on her phone, doing the thing OHC was always doing now, which was standing in the gap between a law and a promise.

Up close the walls were not impressive. That was the trap of the place, and the LENS loved it for that. They were low, grassed, soft-shouldered, the height of a man's chest where they survived at all. You could step over them. Kids had sledded them. For a hundred years men in spiked shoes had walked them looking for golf balls, and the walls had let them, the way the truly large things let the small things crawl on them.

You could only see it from the air. That was the whole cruelty and the whole point. The people who built it never saw it. Not one of them. They moved baskets of earth for a shape they could only hold in their heads, passed down by mouth and by hand, basket to basket, and the shape did not close in a lifetime, and the *cycle* it tracked did not close in a lifetime, and they did it anyway.

Naila stopped at the causeway, where the Observatory Circle let into the neck.

"Six," she said.

"Six what," said Frik.

"The circles." She wasn't looking at any of them. She was looking at a thing that wasn't there, the way she did. "The diameter of that one." She pointed at the Observatory Circle, a thousand feet of grass curving away gentle as a held breath. "It's the unit. You can lay the whole site out with it. The two circles sit six of them apart. The Octagon's set off it. It's a ruler. They had a ruler and they kept using it." She turned, finally, and the LENS caught it — the small open astonishment she never bothered to hide, because hiding things cost her more than it cost other people. "It's not a drawing. It's *measured.* Somebody measured this."

"Somebody measured it," Tessa agreed.

"And then somebody measured High Bank with the same one." Naila's voice did the thing it did when the pattern got too big for the room. "Fifty-three miles. The same length. The only other octagon they ever built, and the same — " She stopped. "Sorry. Sorry, I'm — "

"You're not wrong," Tessa said.

Hale had got his phone out. Not to make a call. To make a note. The LENS watched his thumbs move and felt something go cold and familiar in his own chest, the thing he'd felt in marketplaces where men photographed grief and called it coverage. *He's writing her down,* the LENS thought. *He's writing the moment down so he can have it later.*

"The shared unit," Hale said, looking up, bright now, "is exactly the kind of thing a synthesis brings out. People don't know about High Bank. They should. The network angle is — frankly it's the headline. A continent-scale standard of measurement, two thousand years before — "

"It's not a headline," Tessa said.

The fish would have had something here. The LENS could almost hear it, that wet little heckle from off-camera somewhere — *oh, it's not a headline, it's a five-part docuseries with a merch tier* — but the fish, when it mattered, knew to shut its mouth, and this mattered.

"It's not a network," Tessa went on, mild, walking them slowly along the inside of a wall so they had to follow or be rude. "It's not a *system.* You keep reaching for the word that makes it a thing you can hold. A network, a standard, an instrument. I understand why. It's how you were taught to make something real — you give it a noun and you publish the noun." She stopped at a gateway, one of the gaps in the wall where, on a particular morning at the far end of eighteen and a half years, the Moon would come up and thread the eye of it. "It's a *record.* Somebody watched, and kept what they saw, and gave it to somebody who wasn't born yet. And that one watched, and kept it, and gave it on. For longer than any of them lived. That's not a system. A system you could buy. That — " she nodded down the long sightline, out past the grass, out past the highway you could just hear, a thin tide of trucks — "that you can only inherit."

Frik, who could not help himself, who the LENS loved precisely because he could not help himself, said: "There's the soft one, though. The size ratio. The Great Circle to the Observatory — somebody's done the maths, it comes out about one-point-one-three, and the Moon's own size changes by about — " he scrunched his face — "one-point-one-two-something between when it's close and when it's far. Apogee, perigee. Point-three percent off. People say they built the *Moon getting bigger and smaller* into the size of the rings."

"People say that," Tessa said.

"Did they?"

She looked at him with something that was almost fondness. "It's a pretty number."

"That's not a yes."

"No," she said. "It isn't."

And she let it sit there, the pretty number, unconfirmed and undenied, and Frik grinned at it the way you grin at a card someone won't turn over, and the LENS understood that this was the discipline of the place — not the alignment, which was a fact, but the refusal to close the thing that didn't need closing. The willingness to stand in the gap and say *maybe,* and mean it as a gift and not a failure.

—

Back in the watching room the light had gone flat and afternoon.

Hale made his ask cleanly, the LENS would give him that. No villain in it. A research window. The lidar flight, the ephemeris run, a publication embargo of six months so that the Foundation's account came out first, ahead of the popular flood. In exchange, money OHC needed, named credit, a permanent display.

"And the descendant nations are consulted throughout," Hale added, to Tessa, the leaning confidence again. "Of course."

"Consulted," Tessa said.

"At every stage."

The LENS watched Diane look at her binder and not open it. He watched the two young staff stop writing. He watched Tessa unscrew her thermos, pour a half-inch of something into the cap, and not drink it — just hold it, warming her hands, taking the room's clock and slowing it to her own.

"You can have the flight," she said at last. "The lidar. It's over public ground now, and the OHC can permit it, and there's nothing up there your machine can take that the morning sun doesn't already give to anyone who stands here long enough."

Hale started to speak.

"The dawn is ours," Tessa said. "The major standstill morning. When it comes around — and you know when it comes around, you've got the same ephemeris I have — that morning belongs to the people whose grandfathers' grandfathers kept it coming. You can be in the parking lot. You cannot be in the gate."

"That's — " Hale's confidence wobbled, found its feet. "That's a significant restriction on—"

"Yes," Tessa said.

Diane, very quietly, opened the binder. "We can structure the permit that way," she said, and the LENS heard the relief in it, the relief of a person handed back the authority she'd been holding without owning. "Survey access, with a closure on the consultation dates. I'd need to draft it."

"Draft it," Tessa said.

So the Foundation got its window. That was the shape of the turn, and the LENS named it to himself coldly, the way you name a thing so it can't surprise you later: Hale would fly his lidar, and run his ephemeris, and write his synthesis, and very possibly publish it first, and call it documentation of a fact. He had the law's narrow gap and the money to fill it.

But not the morning.

In the parking lot, packing the camera, Frik said, "We're going to have to work around him."

"Yeah," Naila said. She was looking back at the wall, at the gateway, doing the geometry again in the air with one finger, the angle of it, the half a degree.

"He gets the picture from above," Frik said. "The whole figure. The thing nobody who built it ever saw."

"He gets the picture," Naila agreed. "Tessa gets the morning." She lowered her hand. "I think she knows which one's the record."

The LENS got the shot then — not of the wall, and not of Hale's grey jacket disappearing into a rented car, but of Tessa's thermos, abandoned on the conference table through the window, the cap still full, the half-inch of coffee gone cold in it, waiting for a sunrise eighteen and a half years in the keeping that she had just, very calmly, refused to sell.

Then he lowered the camera, because that one was hers.
