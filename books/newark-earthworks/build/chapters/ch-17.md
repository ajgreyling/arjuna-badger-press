# Chapter 17 — Who Holds the Record

The thing about the relay is that nobody at the start of it ever held the finish.

Naila kept coming back to this, the way you keep returning your tongue to a chipped tooth. She was sitting on the low rise where the Observatory Circle's wall ran flattest, the morning frost still bluing the grass in the wall's shadow, and she had her notebook open to a page she'd ruled into columns. Year. Standstill. Observer. The third column was empty all the way down because there was nothing she could put in it. Nobody knew the names. Nobody would ever know the names. That was the whole trouble.

"You're doing the face," Frik said. He'd folded himself onto the slope beside her with a gas-station coffee and the wired stillness that came over him only when something had genuinely hooked him. "The face where you've found a hole and the hole is in your own head."

"It's not a hole. It's a column."

"Same thing, for you."

She tapped the empty column with her pen. "Eighteen point six years. Say you're twenty when you start watching the eastern horizon. You mark where the Moon clears it at its furthest north — the major standstill. Then you watch the Moon swing back south, season on season, and you wait."

"For how long."

"For nine years, roughly, before the Moon's even at the *other* extreme. And the extreme isn't a single sunrise you can circle on a calendar. It drifts. It hangs near the limit for months — that's *why* they call it a standstill, the Moon seems to stop wandering and just sit there. You can't catch the day. You catch a *window*, and you mark the edges of the window, and you do it again the next time around. And the next time around is eighteen years and seven months after the first."

Frik did the arithmetic out loud, badly, the way he did everything fast. "So you start at twenty. You see the cycle close once at — thirty-nine? You're thirty-nine before you've watched a single full turn of the thing."

"If you live. And one turn doesn't *prove* anything. One turn could be luck. You need the cycle to repeat to know it's a cycle and not a story you told yourself about some marks on a stick."

"So you don't finish."

"You don't finish." She looked at the wall, the long patient earth of it, fourteen hundred feet of curve held to under four feet of tolerance the whole way round, a circle so true you could not feel it bending under your feet. "Whoever started the count handed it to somebody who handed it to somebody. The person who *understood* the pattern got it as an inheritance. And the person who built it into the ground — into *this* —" she put her palm flat on the cold grass "— built it from a record older than they were, made by people they never met, about a sky none of them had watched all the way through."

The fish, who had been quiet, surfaced.

"So basically," Hecklefish said from somewhere around her elbow, in that tone of a creature who has read the back of the box, "the Hopewell invented homework you have to leave in your will."

Frik snorted into his coffee.

"It's not funny."

"It's a *little* funny. 'Dearest grandchild, the Moon owes us another forty years of observations, please find attached the family stick.'"

Naila didn't laugh, but the knot in her chest loosened a half-turn, which was what Frik was for, and what the fish was for, in the end. They heckled the awe back down to a size you could hold without it crushing you. Stonehenge got the postcards. Stonehenge got the bus tours and the druids at solstice and the whole world's idea of *ancient and mysterious*. And here was a thing the Ohio History Connection's own signage called twice as precise as Stonehenge — tracking a target eighteen times harder, in a town most of the country couldn't find on a map, with a *golf course* laid across it for the better part of a century, fairways mowed over a lunar instrument like it was rough that needed managing.

You could see the famous thing from space. You had to *deserve* this one. You had to do the math.

---

The Lens came down the wall toward them, camera at her hip, not raised. That was its own kind of report. When she was working a thing she shot it; when she'd decided the thing wasn't hers to take, the camera rode low and dark like a holstered hand.

"Tessa's coming," she said. "She's been on the phone with Pawnee Nation's office since six." She lowered herself onto the grass with the care of a body that had learned where it hurt. "And the Foundation's man is parked by the causeway with a laptop and a face."

"What kind of face," Frik said.

"The kind that's drafting a press release in his head and waiting to find out whether he's allowed to send it."

Naila felt the old itch start up, the one that had got her into half her arguments and all of her best work: the itch to *close the thing*. She had the columns. She had the geometry — she and Frik had walked the OCD off the ground themselves, the Observatory Circle's diameter stepping out across the site like a unit somebody had carried in a pocket, the same length showing up again fifty-three miles south at High Bank where the only other Hopewell octagon in the world encoded the same Moon. One ruler. Two sites. Generations apart. A shared standard meant a shared *people*, a network of watchers who taught each other the same length, and that was bigger and stranger than any of the things the bookshop fringe shelf would sell you. She had it laid out in her head, clean as the circle, and she wanted to *say* it. She wanted to walk up to Tessa Redwing and put the whole solved shape of it on the grass between them like a gift.

"You've got it," the Lens said, watching her. Not a question.

"I've got the structure of it. The relay. The unit. I can *show* how it had to work — the record handed down, the —"

"Yeah." The Lens turned her face to the gate where the far walls opened a notch onto the eastern horizon. "I've watched people do that. Walk up to somebody's grandmother's life with the structure of it all worked out, ready to hand it back to her explained."

It landed soft and it landed hard, the way the Lens's things did. Naila shut her notebook.

---

Tessa came up the causeway with her coat open and her hands jammed in her pockets, and she stood at the lip of the Observatory Circle the way she always stood — like she was checking on something rather than visiting it. She looked at the four of them sitting in a row on her wall and something in her face eased a fraction.

"You found the ruler," she said. Not asked. She'd seen the chalk-marks they'd left on the survey print, the stepped-off OCD, the line they'd drawn between Newark and High Bank.

"We found the ruler," Naila said, and then, because the itch was unbearable and because she trusted this woman more than she'd trusted anyone on the project, she let it go. "Dr. Redwing — it's a relay. It has to be. The cycle's invisible to one lifetime, so somebody kept the count across — I don't know — four, five generations of watchers, before anyone could even know the shape, and then they *built* the shape, and they used the same unit at High Bank, which means there was a — a school of it, a line of people teaching the same measure across the whole network, and that's the answer, isn't it? That's what it's *for*. It's an almanac. A handed-down almanac in the ground. We can — I can lay the whole thing out, the math closes, it —"

She stopped because Tessa had crouched down to her level, slow, knees in the frost, and was not looking at the notebook at all. She was looking at Naila.

"You're not wrong about the math," Tessa said.

"Then —"

"You're not wrong about the math," she said again, gently, and the second saying changed the first, the way a wall changes a circle. "The cycle's invisible. The count had to be kept. The unit's shared. Hively and Horn published the alignments forty years ago and I've spent half my life walking people through them. You can stand on every one of those facts. Nobody's taking them off you."

Naila waited for the *and*. It didn't come the way she expected.

"What you're calling the answer," Tessa said, "is the *mechanism*. How a record gets kept longer than a life. That's a beautiful piece of work and it's true and you can have it." She tilted her head toward the gate, toward the notch of horizon waiting empty for a Moon that wouldn't come for years. "But you asked what it's *for*. And that's a different word."

"I — isn't it the same word? The function. What it does. It tracks the Moon —"

"It tracks the Moon the way a church tracks Easter," Tessa said. "Yes. And no. You can write down the mechanism of a church — angles, dates, who rings the bell. You'd have all of it correct and you'd have missed the entire thing." She wasn't unkind. That was the part Naila would think about for a long time afterward — there was no edge in it, no *gotcha*, no closing of a door in a face. "Somebody kept this count for two hundred years before it was true enough to build. *Why* did they keep it. Not how. Why does a person hand a record to a grandchild they'll never see grown, about a sky they'll never watch close? What did they think they were *doing*."

The fish, for once, said nothing.

"And that," Tessa said, standing, knees cracking, "is ours."

---

Naila felt it the way you feel a step that isn't there. The itch to close the thing met something that wasn't a counter-argument — she could have fought a counter-argument, she was *good* at counter-arguments — but a wall. A true one, four feet of tolerance, held the whole way round.

"I'm not telling you that to shut you up," Tessa said, reading her exactly, which was its own small mercy. "I'm telling you because you're the kind of person who'll get it. You found the squared circle by *seeing* it. You didn't need me to point. So I'll trust you with the part I'm not going to explain, which is *that there's a part I'm not going to explain*, and it's not a hole in your work. Your work's whole. It just stops where mine starts."

Down by the causeway, the Foundation's man had got out of his car. He was looking up at them, and at the survey print, and Naila understood with a cold clarity that he wanted exactly what she had wanted ten minutes ago — to be the one who *said it first*, who laid the solved shape on the table and put his name on the lid. The difference was that he'd say *what it's for* and mean *what it'll sell as*. He'd publish the almanac and own the meaning by being fastest to the microphone.

She thought about her empty third column. Observer. Blank all the way down. She'd been treating it like a failure of the record. It wasn't. The names weren't lost. They were *kept* — somewhere that wasn't a peer-reviewed journal, in a line that hadn't ended just because a golf club fenced the wall for a century. Tessa wasn't standing at the lip of the circle as a visitor. She was the next watcher in a relay that the math could describe and could not enter.

"What do you want us to do," Naila said. It came out smaller than she meant and exactly the right size.

"Shoot the alignment when it comes," Tessa said, looking at the Lens. "Get the math right — get it *exactly* right, because the fast people get it sloppy and the sloppy version is how they steal it. Show everybody the relay, the ruler, the genius of the people who did it with baskets and cord and patience nobody alive has anymore." She put her hands back in her pockets. "Tell the whole true thing right up to the edge. And then leave the last word in the ground where my people can pick it up. The almanac's published. The *meaning* isn't yours to hand out, and it isn't his —" a tip of the chin at the causeway "— and it isn't even mine to give to *you*. It's mine to *keep*. Those aren't the same."

Frik, who punctured everything, including himself, especially the reach, looked at the gate and then at Naila and said nothing at all, which from Frik was a kind of bow.

The Lens raised the camera. Slowly. Pointed it not at the gate, not at the survey print, but down the long true curve of the wall — the work, not the workers — and then, deliberately, lowered it again without pressing the shutter.

Naila opened her notebook. She drew a line under the columns, closing the table, and where the third column ran blank to the bottom of the page she did not write a name. She wrote, in small letters in the margin, the thing she could stand on and the thing she couldn't, side by side, and left the gap between them exactly the width it was.

The frost was lifting off the grass now. Somewhere past the eastern walls the highway hummed, and beyond it the horizon held the notch where, in a year that none of them could circle on a calendar, the Moon would clear the far gateway within half a degree of the dead centre of a figure that nobody who started it had lived to see.

Somebody had kept that count anyway. Somebody was keeping it still.

What for, she did not write down.
