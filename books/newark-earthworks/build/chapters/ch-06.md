# Chapter 6 — The Almanac Nobody Saw

The frost held the field flat and white at seven in the morning, and the Lens shot it the way you shoot a thing you don't fully understand yet — wide, then wider, then a single frame down at his own boots to remember the cold was real.

Naila was already out on the wall.

She walked the crest of the Octagon's south embankment with her arms held a little away from her sides, the way she did when her body was busy doing the arithmetic her mouth wasn't. Two thousand years of grass had softened the wall into something a golfer would call a swale, but from up here you could feel it was a made line. The Lens framed her against it. The fairway markers were still in the ground a hundred yards off — little flags, a sand bunker raked into a crescent, a wooden bench with a brass plaque thanking somebody for forty years of membership. He left them in the shot. They belonged. The whole strange grief of the place was in the fact that they belonged.

"Okay," Frik said, jogging up the slope with a tape measure and a print-out flapping. "So talk me through it like I'm a golf cart. Somebody stands here—"

"Not here." Naila didn't turn. "There. The gateway."

She pointed across fifty acres to the far gap in the earthwork, where the wall broke and the causeway ran out toward Observatory Circle. The Lens raised the long lens and found it: a notch of pale sky framed in two grass shoulders, a thousand feet away and dead ahead, and centred — he could feel it before Frik's tape confirmed anything — centred the way a doorway is centred when somebody loved the room.

"You stand at the centre," Naila said. "You watch the Moon come up through that gap. And on one morning, once, the Moon rises as far north as it will ever rise. It clears the gateway." She breathed. "Within half a degree of the exact middle. That's the OHC line, twice as precise as Stonehenge, and it's not marketing, Frik, I checked the survey, it's *true*—"

"I believe you." Frik was crouched, sighting along the tape. "I'm not the potato. I just want to know how anybody *found* the morning."

That was the question. The Lens lowered the camera and let it hang. He had spent a career standing where the question was hardest and pressing the shutter instead of asking it, and the habit had cost him things he was still counting. So when Frik asked the right question, he listened with his whole body.

"Found the morning," Frik said again, to himself this time. He stood up. "Right. So I'm a Hopewell sky-watcher. Hundred BCE. I want to know where the Moon rises. I watch for a year." He turned in place, mapping it on the air. "The Moon's a liar, though. It doesn't rise in one spot like the Sun roughly does. It swings — way north, way south — every *month*. Twenty-eight days, the whole arc, north to south and back. So in one year I see the swing — what, a dozen times?"

"Twelve, thirteen full swings," Naila said.

"And every month it's a *different* width of swing." Frik's hands stopped. "Oh. Oh, that's mean."

"There it is," said Naila, and for a moment she almost smiled, which on Naila was a structural event.

---

Tessa Redwing came up the causeway on foot, no cart, in a wax jacket with a travel mug and the unhurried gait of a person who had walked this ground in every season and was not going to be rushed across it by anybody's morning. The Lens had asked, the first day, whether he could photograph her on the works. *You can photograph the works,* she'd said. *I'll tell you when I'm in them.* He'd kept the frame loose around her ever since. It was her edge to set.

"He's got it," she said, nodding at Frik, who was vibrating slightly. "Mostly."

"It's mean," Frik told her, delighted. "The swing changes width every month."

"And the *peak* of the width changes too." Tessa took a slow sip. "Some years the Moon's furthest-north rise is further north than other years. Creeps out, year on year, like a tide coming in over a tide. Reaches the maximum—" she lifted her chin at the far gateway "—and then for years it pulls back in. The whole breath of it, in and out."

"How long's the breath?" said Frik, already wincing because he could feel the shape of the answer coming.

"Eighteen years," Tessa said. "And about seven months."

The Lens watched Naila go very still on the wall.

"Eighteen point six," she said quietly. "That's the standstill cycle. That's what Hively and Horn found in the walls. All eight points — the far-north moonrise, the far-south, the sets, all of it, in the gateways." She looked down the field. "I knew the number. I've known the number for years. I didn't—" She stopped. Naila did not say *I didn't realise*; she let the gap stand, and the gap said it. "Eighteen years is most of a life out here. To find the *edge* of the breath, you'd have to watch one rise a month, mark it, and not lose the marks, until the marks themselves outlived the watching."

"You don't see it," Frik said. The delight had drained out and left something steadier. "That's the thing. You can't see it. You could stand on this exact wall every clear night for *ten years* and the pattern wouldn't close. There's no morning where it *clicks*. You'd just have a pile of marks that don't mean anything yet."

Hecklefish, riding in the Lens's jacket pocket the way he did when the morning was good, surfaced enough to be heard. *"So the genius move is, you die before the answer. Love that for them. Real strong life-goals energy."*

"He's not wrong," said Tessa, who had taken to the fish faster than anyone the Lens had ever introduced him to. "That's exactly the part people can't hold. They want a genius. One person, one flash, one *aha*. There's no aha here. There can't be." She turned the mug in her hands. "There's a girl who watches the eastern horizon and cuts a notch in something — a post, a tally, we don't know what kept the count, it doesn't survive. There's the man she taught to read the notches after her eyes went. There's *his* apprentice, who never met the girl, adding to a record she started, for a reason none of them will live to see proved. That's the instrument. Not the walls. The walls are just where they wrote the answer down once they finally had it."

The Lens lifted the camera then. Not at Tessa. At the gateway — the empty notch of sky a thousand feet off, with frost on the grass shoulders and a highway humming somewhere behind the south wall, and a sand bunker raked smooth in the foreground like a small white scar. He held the frame for a long time. *The almanac nobody saw*, he thought, and did not say it, because saying it would have been captioning a thing that was perfectly able to mean itself.

---

The Foundation rep arrived with the sun properly up, which the Lens privately thought was the most honest thing about him.

His name was Aaron Voss and he was good at his job, which was the problem with him. He had a soft voice and a real reverence for the place and a laptop bag that probably cost more than the crew's van, and he came across the causeway with two of his own people and an ephemeris program already open on a tablet he held the way other men hold a winning hand they're trying not to grin about.

"Dr. Redwing." Genuinely warm. "Naila. You're seeing it too." He turned the tablet so the screen caught the light, a black field of plotted dots, every standstill point modelled to a decimal Hively and Horn could only have dreamed of in '82. "We've run the alignments forward and back against modern lunar theory. The agreement is — honestly, it's better than the published figures. We're tightening the half-degree to a quarter. I'd love to get the crew on record for the rollout. The story tells itself: ancient observatory, modern math, the precision *confirmed*." He said *confirmed* the way you'd lay down a trump card.

"It was confirmed in 1982," Naila said.

"It was *suggested* in 1982." Gentle correction. "We're closing it."

The Lens kept the camera down and watched the field instead. He had photographed men like Voss in a dozen countries — the ones who arrive after the hard thing is over with a clean shirt and a press release, and the awful thing was that Voss meant it, he wasn't lying, the dots were probably real. That was what made the room dangerous. Not malice. Sincerity with a publication date attached.

"You can model it to a quarter degree," Naila said slowly. The Lens recognised the tone; it was the one she used when a pattern was wrong in a way she hadn't located yet. "Sure. You've got four hundred years of telescopes behind you and a satellite that—" She shook her head. "That's not what they had. That's not the achievement. You're confirming our own arithmetic with our own arithmetic. They *built* it before any of the arithmetic existed."

"Which is exactly the wonder we'd be selling," Voss said, and to his credit he heard the word leave his mouth and tried to walk it back. "Sharing. The wonder we'd be sharing."

Hecklefish again, muffled, vicious, fond. *"He said the quiet part with a footnote."*

Frik, who had been quiet a suspiciously long time, finally spoke. "Can I ask the boring question." It wasn't really a question. "Your model. It tells us the Moon *did* clear the gateway on the right mornings. Cool. Stipulated. Does it tell us how the people who built the gateway *knew that*, before they had your model?"

Voss opened his mouth.

"Because that's the only interesting fact on this whole field," Frik went on, "and your tablet can't touch it. You're proving they were right. We already knew they were right. The question is who *kept the record long enough to find out they were right* — and that data didn't survive, did it. There's no spreadsheet from 80 BCE. There's just the answer carved into the dirt and zero working shown." He spread his hands. "You can't publish the working. Nobody can. The working was a hundred and fifty years of people's lives and it's gone."

The Lens watched Voss take that, and to the man's credit again, he took it like a professional — a small recalculation behind the eyes. "Then the alignment's all we *can* responsibly present," he said. "The measurable part."

"No," said Tessa.

She didn't raise her voice. She just put the word down in the middle of the field where everyone could see it, and the Lens felt the morning rearrange around it.

"You can present a measurement," she said. "You can't present a record that isn't yours to read aloud." She looked at the gateway, not at Voss. "You keep saying *the data*. There's no data here, Mr. Voss. There's a count somebody kept for longer than they were alive, on faith that the keeping mattered, and they handed it down a line of people whose descendants are the ones the Park Service is consulting this month — not the ones with the better ephemeris. The alignment is a *fact*. What it's *for* is a relationship. You can publish the fact. The relationship isn't on your tablet, and it isn't mine to sell you either."

It was, the Lens thought, the cleanest thing he'd heard said on a field in years, and he did not photograph her saying it, because she had not told him she was in the works.

Voss was quiet. One of his people shifted. Far off, on the road behind the south wall, a truck changed gear and the sound came over the grass like weather.

"I'd still like the rollout," Voss said at last, but something had gone out of it, the trump card lowered.

"I'd like to walk High Bank with the crew before anybody rolls out anything," Tessa said, and it was not a no, and it was not a yes, and it was entirely hers to leave hanging.

---

They came off the wall as the frost let go and the green turned ordinary and the place stopped being a calendar and went back to being a field with old earth in it.

The Lens hung back, the way he'd trained himself to, and got the shot he actually wanted: not the gateway, not Voss, not even Naila. He got Frik standing at the centre point with the tape measure dangling forgotten in one hand, looking down the thousand feet of grass to the far gap, doing the thing in his head that nobody could do on the ground — running the impossible relay forward, watcher to watcher to watcher, none of them living to the end of it.

"It can't be one person," Frik said, mostly to himself. "It's the one thing that's actually impossible here. Not the math. The *math's* fine. You just need the lifetime." He turned. "You need *more* than the lifetime. You need somebody to start counting a thing they'll never see finish, and somebody else to believe the count enough to keep it going after the first one's gone, and a *third* one to trust two dead people's marks enough to build it into the ground at half a degree."

"You need a relay," Naila said.

"You need a relay," Frik agreed.

Hecklefish, from the pocket, very quietly for once. *"...and somebody had to hand the next runner the baton without ever seeing the finish line. Okay. Okay, that one got me."*

Tessa was already walking back down the causeway toward the cars, not waiting, because it was her ground and she didn't have to. Over her shoulder, not turning: "Fifty-three miles south, they built another one. Same gateway. Same Moon." A beat. "Same ruler."

She let that lie in the frost behind her, and kept walking, and did not explain.
