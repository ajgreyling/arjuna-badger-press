# Chapter 11 — The Ditch Inside

Frik had a theory, and the theory was a fort.

He liked it because it was the boring answer, and the boring answer is where you should always start. A man two thousand years ago looks at a flat place by a river, thinks *I would not like to be murdered here,* and builds a wall. Walls are not mysterious. Walls are the oldest idea humans have after fire and after complaining about the people on the other side of the wall. So Frik came to the Great Circle ready to like the fort, the way you'd warm up to a dog that doesn't bark — relieved, almost. No baskets-across-generations. No invisible lunar cycle making his teeth ache. Just engineering and the human wish to be left alone.

He stood at the gateway in the grey morning and the wall came up over his head, and for a second the fort theory was beautiful.

Eight feet, give or take. A clean green rampart curving away on both sides into the mist, swallowing the noise of Route 79 the way a closed door swallows an argument in the next room. Inside it was hushed. The grass was wet and the frost was going to silver in the low light. You could believe a hundred people had stood here once with the wall at their backs and felt the particular animal comfort of being inside something.

"Okay," Frik said, walking the line of it. "Sell me. Three hundred and sixty-six metres across. Walls higher than a man. You're the chief of the I-Don't-Want-To-Be-Murdered tribe. This is your wall."

"It's a good wall," Naila said. She wasn't looking at the wall. She was looking at the ground at the base of it, the way she looked at everything — as if the thing had a number written on it she was politely waiting for it to say out loud.

The LENS was a little behind them, camera down at the moment, just walking. He did that on the first lap of a place. Looked with his own eyes before he made them anyone else's.

"It's a *great* wall," Frik went on, warming up. "Look at this thing. You'd need — what — basket after basket, you'd need years, but people build forts, that's the most normal thing in the world to spend years on. I'm not embarrassed by this theory. I want it on record that the fort theory is dignified. There's even a name for the people who think it's a fort."

"There were," Naila said. "For about a century."

"Right! Whole reputable nineteenth-century guys. Squier and Davis came through, drew these as 'works of defence,' beautiful surveys, very serious moustaches." Frik patted the wall like the flank of a horse. "Earthworks. *Works.* Military word. Forts. I rest my—"

"Frik."

"—case, what."

"Walk to the bottom of the wall," Naila said.

---

He walked to the bottom of the wall.

There's a moment your body knows a thing before your mouth catches up, and Frik felt his feet do something his theory hadn't authorised. The ground didn't meet the wall and stop. It dipped first. Right at the foot of the rampart, on the *inside* — the side where his hundred imaginary defenders were standing — the earth fell away into a long, shallow, grass-filled trough. A ditch. It ran the whole curve of the circle, hugging the inner foot of the wall, going off into the mist in both directions like a moat that had got the geography exactly backwards.

He stood in it. The wall went up on his left. The wide open interior of the circle lay on his right, flat and innocent.

"Huh," Frik said.

"Say it," Naila said.

"The ditch is on the inside."

"The ditch is on the inside."

He climbed up out of it and went and stood on the crest of the wall to make sure he wasn't being fooled by the morning. He wasn't. From up here you could read it like a cross-section in a textbook: the high ground was the wall, and the low ground — the ditch, the place an attacker would have to slog through, the place you'd dig to make life hard for whoever was coming — was *behind* the wall. Tucked safe inside, where your own people would be.

"Okay," Frik said, "okay, okay, let me — let me defend the fort." He held up both hands. "Maybe they dug the dirt from inside because it was handy. You build the wall out of the nearest mud. You end up with a ditch where you took the mud. It doesn't *mean* anything, it's just where the dirt came from. That's not a moat, that's a — borrow pit. They borrowed the dirt."

He liked *borrow pit.* It had the right boring music to it.

"Then dig it outside," Naila said. She came up onto the wall beside him, and pointed down the curve, where the inner ditch ran on and on, deliberate as a drawn line. "If you're going to dig out the dirt anyway, and you want a fort, you dig it on the *outside,* where it does double duty — wall *and* trench against the enemy, in one move. Every hillfort in the world does exactly that, for exactly that reason, because dirt is heavy and you only want to move it once." She let that sit. "They moved it the wrong way. On purpose. Around the entire circle. For no defensive benefit and at extra cost."

"Maybe they were bad at it," Frik tried, and heard how that sounded the instant it left him.

"These are the people," Naila said gently, "who built an octagon four miles from here that tracks the Moon to half a degree."

"Yeah," Frik said. "Yeah, scratch that one. They were not bad at it."

A heron came low over the far wall, saw them, thought better of the whole arrangement, and banked off toward the river. Somewhere out past the rampart a truck changed gears on the highway and the sound came over softened, the way everything from out there came over softened, which Frik was beginning to notice was *the point* — the wall didn't keep arrows out, it kept the world quiet.

"So it fails," he said. "The fort fails."

"On the dirt," Naila said. "It fails on the dirt. You don't even need the calendar. You just need to notice which side the ditch is on."

---

They'd worked themselves down off the wall and into the great flat bowl of the interior when Tessa found them — Dr. Redwing, in a field coat with a thermos, coming through the gate with the unhurried walk of someone who had been to this place enough times that it owed her nothing and she owed it everything. Frik had learned in three days not to perform the cleverness at her. She had a way of letting your cleverness finish and then saying the actual thing, and you didn't want to be still talking when she did it.

"You found the ditch," she said. Not a question. She'd watched them from the gate. She'd watched a lot of people find the ditch.

"We found the ditch," Frik admitted. "I had a whole fort. It was a good fort."

"It usually is." She poured something into the thermos cup and didn't offer it, just held it, warming her hands. "People want it to be a fort. I understand why. A fort, you know what it's *for* the second you see it. You can stop thinking. The wall is between you and the thing you're afraid of, and that's the end of the story, and it's a story everybody already owns." She looked up the green curve of the rampart. "This isn't that story."

"Because the ditch is on the inside," Frik said, fishing — wanting her to confirm the win, the way you do.

She didn't confirm the win.

"You're still reading it like a wall," she said. "High thing, low thing, which side keeps you safe. You're thinking about *out.*" She turned, slowly, taking in the whole eight-foot ring of it standing around them in the mist, the gateway the only break in it. "Try the other direction. A defensive wall asks: how do I keep them *out.*" A small pause, and her voice didn't rise, it dropped. "This one asks: how do I keep something *in.*"

Frik felt the back of his neck do the cold thing.

"Keep what in?" he said.

And here is where a worse book would have her answer. Frik wanted her to answer. Naila, beside him, had gone very still — Naila who could read a squared circle like a face, standing there with her hands quiet, *waiting,* because she'd understood something Frik was still catching: that this wasn't theirs to take.

Tessa drank from the cup. Let the question hang in the wet air with the heron-sound and the muffled truck.

"You stand in the middle of this," she said at last, and she was looking at the centre of the bowl now, the flat empty heart of the circle where nothing was, where two thousand years of nothing very carefully was, "and the wall takes the world away. The road. The town. The arrow you were so worried about. All of it goes down under the rim. What's left over the wall is the sky and the horizon line, and that's *all* that's left." She nodded, more to herself than to them. "The ditch and the wall together. They don't divide the inside from an enemy. They divide it from the *ordinary.* You go down into the ditch and up onto the floor and you've crossed out of one kind of place into another kind of place." She looked at Frik, and there was something close to kindness in it, the kindness of someone handing a tourist back his small idea without breaking it. "You don't dig a moat to do that. You dig it because the crossing has to be *made,* in the ground, with your hands, so the people coming after you cross it too. The barrier is the meaning. It's a threshold you built so heavy that it'll still be a threshold when nobody alive remembers the word for it."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"That's a better fort," Frik said quietly. It was the most respectful thing he had.

"It's not a fort at all," Tessa said. Not sharp. Just closing a door he'd been leaning on. "It never was. The ditch told you that in the first ten minutes. You just hadn't decided to believe it yet."

---

Later — laps of the rampart, the frost gone, the LENS finally up and working the long curve of the wall with the gateway breaking the line — Frik couldn't let it lie all the way down. It's a disease he has and he knows it.

"Okay but here's the thing that's killing me," he said, half to Naila, half to the air. "The Great Circle here. The Octagon four miles that way with the Moon thing. They're not even the *same shape.* One's a ring, one's eight walls and a calendar. And then there's a whole other octagon fifty-odd miles south at High Bank doing the same Moon trick. Why isn't somebody saying these are three different ideas by three different—"

"The unit," Naila said.

"The unit."

"The Observatory Circle. Its diameter shows up over and over here, and it shows up at High Bank, fifty-three miles away." She said the number flat, the way she said all the numbers, but Frik had learned that the flatter Naila said a number the more it had cost her to keep her voice off it. "Same ruler. Two sites. Generations apart. Whoever laid out the second one used the length of the first." A beat. "You don't carry a ruler fifty-three miles by accident."

The LENS lowered the camera. Frik noticed him do it, and noticed Tessa, a way off down the wall, notice the LENS do it — the photographer choosing, for a second, not to take the thing.

"That's the part that gets me," Frik said. "Not the Moon. Not even the ditch. It's that somebody *taught* somebody the length. Same length, both sites, both Moons. Like a—" He stopped, because the word that wanted to come out was *school,* and the word after that was *who,* and he could feel himself reaching, feel the floor of the known thing dropping into the floor of the wanted thing.

"Don't," Naila said. Lightly. The fish-bite of it.

"I'm not reaching."

"You're about three words from reaching."

"I am *appreciating,*" Frik said, with enormous dignity, "the verified fact that two thousand years ago a person here knew exactly how long another person's circle had been, and used it. That's it. That's all I'm doing. I'm a model of restraint." He looked back across the bowl, at the ring of wall standing patient in the warming light, the ditch running its quiet wrong-way line around the inside of it. "I came here for a fort and I'm leaving with a threshold and a ruler and absolutely no idea who held it. This is a terrible vacation."

Tessa, far off, didn't laugh, but the line of her shoulders changed.

She had let them find the ditch. She had let them watch the fort die on the dirt. And the thing she hadn't told them — the *what's-kept-in* of it, the rest of the answer past the threshold — she still held, easy as the thermos cup, hers to pour out or carry away.

Frik looked at the gateway. The one gap in the wall. The single place the makers had left for the world to get in, or for whatever-it-was to get *out,* and he found he didn't want her to say which.

He let it stand open.
