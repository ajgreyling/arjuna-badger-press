# Daughters of the Dust Throne

*The Salt Veil — Book One*


---



---


# Prologue — The quiet of the pans

The night I will tell you of came down on the White Pan the way all of them came down, and the quiet of it was made of three things.

The first was the wind. Not the loud wind of the dunes, which has things to say and says them all night long. This was the thin wind that comes off the salt after the heat has died, the one that carries no sand and no scent, only the memory of having crossed a long flat distance with nothing to touch. It moved over the crust and found my tent and went on. It was the sound of a thing that had given up on speaking and kept moving anyway.

The second was the salt itself. People who have not slept on the pans think the salt is quiet, and they are wrong. It ticks. It cools in the dark and the crust shrinks against itself, and somewhere out past the firelight a plate of it cracks like a small bone, and then another, far off, answering. I have lain awake counting them. They never stop. The pans are the floor of a dead sea, and a dead sea, it turns out, is not done dying. It only does it slowly.

The third part was me, and I will not pretend otherwise. I have sat in enough silences to know which part of them is mine. I was quiet the way a knife is quiet in its sheath. I was the stillness of someone who has learned what her own breath costs and spends it carefully. That is the deepest quiet of the three, and the hardest to hear, and if you are listening for it in this story you will find it long before I admit it is there.

You have come a long way to hear me talk. I know why. You want the truth of it, the way it happened, not the way they sing it. There are songs now. A great many songs, and I have heard most of them, and they are wrong in the way songs are always wrong. Too clean. They give me a single face and a single reason and a tidy night with a beginning and an end. There was no tidy night. There was a girl who did not know what she was, and a desert that did, and the long, slow ruin of everyone who tried to keep me from finding out.

So. A story, then. I will give you one.

Listen close. The first thing I give you is not mine.

I was not there. I want that clear before I begin, because you will feel as if I were, and that is the trouble with the way I tell things. I make you stand where I am standing. But I was a child on the far side of the Deep when this happened, asleep against a goat that smelled of dust and milk, dreaming of nothing, knowing nothing. The girl who drowned that night drowned for the same crime that lived in my blood while I slept. I did not learn that for years. When I did, I went and found the women who had been there, and I made them tell me, and they did not want to, and I made them anyway. I have it from three of them. They agree on the shape of it. They disagree on whose fault it was. That, too, you should know is always the way.

Her name was given to me once and I will not give it to you. Some things you keep.

She was fourteen. She had been raised in the Salthold of Sett from the age of six, traded up out of a caravan like mine for being too bright, hearing too much, remembering things in the wrong order and too well. The House of Salt took her and was kind to her, in the way the House of Salt is kind, which is the way you are kind to a blade you intend to use. She was gifted. She was more than gifted. She held the still poise of one discipline and the hearing edge of another, and then, in the months before her drowning, the women who watched her began to see a third thing wake in her, and a fourth, and they grew very quiet.

You have to understand what quiet means in that house. They are all quiet there. When they grow quieter, someone has decided something.

What they had seen — what I have pieced together from the three who would speak to me — was the convergence. The thing the order has a single law against, older than its walls, sworn on salt at every raising and kept by the very House that had raised the girl. No daughter may reach toward the joining of all the disciplines. Not because the order fears the power. Because it has watched the power, three hundred years of times, take the woman who reached for it and burn her hollow, or kill her in the reaching, or fill a hall with the bodies of everyone she loved before anyone could put her down. They have the names of those women. They keep them. It is not a superstition. It is a ledger of corpses, and the girl had begun, without meaning to, to write her own name in it.

So they took her out onto the pan.

They did it at night, because everything that matters in the desert is done at night. They took her in the gentlest way, which I think is the part I cannot forgive, that it was gentle. No one struck her. No one bound her hands. A woman she trusted walked beside her with an arm around her shoulders and told her, in a voice trained to be obeyed by the body before the mind can argue, that everything would be well. And the girl, who could have heard that for the lie it was if she had been a little older, a little harder, a little less in love with the woman holding her — the girl believed it. She walked out onto the salt believing it. That is the Voice doing the kindest cruel thing it can do. It does not make you go. It makes you want to.

They had dug down to the brine. Under the crust of the great pans there is water still, old and heavy and bitter beyond bearing, the last of the dead sea pressed thin and dark in the dark. They had opened a way down to it. They told her it was a rite. They told her she had been chosen. And then the woman with the kind voice put her under, and held her, and the girl did not fight at first because she had been told not to be afraid.

I am told she fought at the end. I am told they let her. That is the mercy of the House of Salt — they will let you fight at the end, when it cannot save you and can only cost you the last of your dignity, because to deny you even that would be cruel, and they are not cruel. They are only right. That is worse. I have met cruelty, and you can hate it cleanly. I have met women who were right, and I have never found the bottom of what that costs.

When it was done they took her up and laid her on the crust and let the salt have her, the way the desert takes everyone in the end, king and killer and caravan child alike. And the salt did what it always does. By dawn there was a little rime of it on her lips and her lashes, a fine white bloom, and the woman who had held her under knelt and pressed a thing into the dead girl's hand and closed the fingers over it. A desert rose. You will not know what that is yet. You will. It is a flower the salt grows, hard as stone and beautiful, made of the exact thing that kills you out there. They press one into the hand of the loved dead. They had loved her. That is the whole horror of it in one small object — the thing that drowned her, blooming, folded into her fist by the hand that drowned her, because they loved her.

I have held a desert rose since. I have held several. I cannot pick one up without thinking of a girl whose name I keep, going gently out across the salt in the arms of someone she trusted, told not to be afraid.

They drowned her so that no one would ever have to fear what a girl like her could become.

A year after, in the dark on the far side of the Deep, against the warm flank of a goat, I slept and grew and carried in my blood every line they had drowned her to be sure of. All three. Not by accident. I would learn that, too, in time, and it would take more from me than the learning of anything else.

But that is further on. We have all night, and the salt is still ticking out there in the dark, counting something. Sit. Let me pour. I will tell you how a girl who did not know what she was came to be the thing the whole desert learned to fear.

I will tell it true. I will only choose what to say.

We begin with the caravan, and with the morning, and with the one small impossible thing I did in front of everyone, and shrugged off, and that the old woman did not.

# Chapter 1 — The Caravan

Begin with the caravan, then, and with the morning.

I have given you a drowning. That was a hard way in, and I am sorry for it, but a story has to start where its roots are, and the roots of mine were already in the salt before I was born. Now I will give you something kinder. I will give you the smell of beasts at dawn, and the sound of a woman singing low to settle them, and a child who did not yet know she was a danger to anyone.

That child was me. I want you to like her. I find that I still do.

We woke in the dark, always. You learn to think it strange that other people sleep through the cool hours and labor at noon, as the canyon folk do, baking themselves stupid for the sake of the sun. The Attuin keep a sane clock. We rose when the stars were still out and the sand had given back its day's heat, and we made our miles before the white eye opened over the dunes. By the time the heat came down like a hand, we were tented and dozing, and the work of the world could wait.

I loved the dark hours. I think I love them still.

There were forty of us, more or less, and a string of beasts I will not pretend to count for you now. The salt-singers say a caravan is a kind of animal, one body of many legs, and that is true the way the best lies are true. We moved as one thing. We grieved as one thing. When a child was born we all of us went a little drunk on it, and when an old man was returned to the pans we all of us went quiet for three days, which is the proper length of mourning and not a day more.

I was loved. I want that set down plainly, because much of what comes after is dark, and you may forget it. I was a child of the long water-line, kin to Tamar, and I was fed and teased and carried and scolded the way every child among us was fed and teased and carried and scolded. No one starved me of warmth. If I tell you I was apart, I do not mean I was unloved.

I mean that I noticed things, and being noticed for noticing is not the same as belonging.

Here is the morning I keep.

We had come off the high dunes onto a flat where the sand went pale and crusted, not true pan but the edge of it, the place where the Deep starts to remember it was a sea. Amma Reshi walked at the head with her stick, though she did not need the stick and we all knew it. She carried it the way a queen carries a thing that means *I could strike you and choose not to.* She was old past counting and warm as a banked fire, and she had taught me my first songs, and she was the nearest thing I had to a grandmother, which is to say she was my grandmother in every way that the salt cares about.

A man named Dav was leading the second string that morning. He was a good man, slow and laughing, and he had a wife heavy with their first child, and he was happy in the careless way of a man who has not yet been given anything large enough to lose.

He stopped his beasts to drink, and that was when I felt it.

I do not know how to give you what it was like, except to say that I have spent my whole life trying to learn words for it and the words are always too clean. It was not a voice. Nothing told me anything. It was more that the morning arranged itself in front of me and I saw the shape it was about to take, the way you see a cup is going to fall before it falls, in the half-breath while it is still on the table.

I saw the near beast set its foot. I saw the place its foot would go, where the crust dimmed in a way the rest did not, a softness, a thing the eye should not have been able to read and mine read anyway. I saw the leg go through to the brine-rot underneath, and the beast scream, and Dav under it with his hip wrong, and his wife's face. All of it, in a row, in no time at all.

I said, "Dav. Not there. Take it left."

I was nine years old. I had a child's high voice and a child's certainty, which is a different and more dangerous thing than the grown kind. He looked at me the way you look at a child who has said something out of turn, half a smile in it. But he was a good man, and a careful one with a wife to come home to, and he took the beast left.

The crust opened where the beast had been standing. A long soft sigh of it, and a smell came up like the underside of the world, and the hole was wide enough to break a leg in and deep enough to do worse.

There was a moment. There is always a moment.

Then Dav laughed, because that is what good men do when the world nearly takes them and does not, and he ruffled my hair with his big hand and called me *little sand-cat* and said I had eyes like a hawk's. The others took it up. *Eyes like a hawk.* Someone said my mother's line always had the sharp eyes. Someone else said it was a good thing one of us was watching where the rest of us were dreaming. They were warm about it. They were proud of me, the way you are proud of a clever child, and they folded the thing into the morning and walked on.

That is how I learned to fold it in, too. I have wondered since whether I might have been a different woman if even one of them had looked at me with fear instead of fondness that morning. But fear is a thing you teach a child by being afraid, and they were not afraid. They had no reason to be. A girl with sharp eyes is a blessing on the road.

I told you I would show you the seams, and I will not always point at them, but I will point at this one, because it is the first.

I have called this morning a memory of belonging. I have told it to you as the day the caravan was proud of me and held me close, and that is how I have held it for thirty years, turning it over in the dark like a smooth stone. *They loved me. They called me sand-cat. I was theirs.*

Read it again and you will see what I did not let myself see for a very long time. They were proud of me the way you are proud of a good dog that smells the snake. They put it in a story — *eyes like a hawk, the sharp-eyed line* — and a story is a box you put a strange thing in so you can stop looking at it. They were not holding me close. They were finding a shape for me that they could keep walking beside. And the difference between those two things is the whole of my life.

But I did not see that then. Then I walked on glowing, my chest full of being seen, and I would have done it again ten times before noon for the same look on Dav's face.

Amma Reshi did not put me in the story.

She had stopped. The whole head of the caravan had moved on, and she stood at the lip of the hole that had nearly eaten a man, and she was looking at it, and then she was looking at me. Not with fondness. Not with fear, exactly. With something older and quieter than either, a stillness in her face that I had no name for at nine and have too many names for now.

I went to her because I went to her about everything. I expected to be praised. I was a child, and praise had just been raining on me, and I wanted it from the one whose praise was worth most.

"Amma," I said. "Did you see? I saw it before it—"

"I know you did, dasha." She said it soft, as she always said it for me, the my-salt word, the vowed word, though she had vowed nothing to me but a lifetime. She crouched, which cost her, and she took my chin in her hand and turned my face up to the light.

"How," she said. Just that. Then, low: "How did you see it."

I did not understand the fear. I shrugged in her hand. "It was soft there. Anyone could see it."

"No," she said. "They could not."

She held my face a moment longer. There was sand in the deep lines around her eyes and the morning was going gold behind her and she smelled of smoke and milk and the bitter root she chewed for her knees. I have kept that, too. I have kept everything about her, which is its own kind of curse.

"When you see things before," she said. "You come to me. Not Dav. Not the others. Me first." A breath. "Will you."

"Why?"

She did not answer for long enough that I felt the question turn heavy in the air. Behind us the caravan was singing the going-on song, the easy one, all those warm voices folding the day's near-death into a tune. She listened to them sing about me, *eyes like a hawk,* and something moved in her old face that I would not understand for years, and that I understand now too well to write down lightly.

"The road is long," she said at last, which was the kind of thing she said when she meant something else entirely. "A thing that helps you in the morning can cost you at night." She looked at me, not away. "And I would rather know what I am walking beside."

Then she stood, with her hand on my shoulder for the standing, and she smiled, and the smile was real, and that is the part that undoes me. The fear and the love in her were not two things. They were one thing, the way they are one thing in anyone who has ever loved someone dangerous. She had buried a girl in the salt once, I learned much later. She knew the shape of what she was looking at. She had known it before I felt the crust go soft.

She took my hand and we walked to catch the singing, and I was happy, and she was not, and only one of us knew why.

We made camp before the heat came down. I slept that day with Amma Reshi's arm over me, as always, and I did not know that she lay awake. I have it from her, what she was thinking, the way you can have a thing from someone who is dead, which is to say I am guessing, and I am old enough to guess well.

She was thinking that she would have to teach me carefully, and slowly, and never let me reach too far. She was thinking she had time. She was wrong about the time. They are always wrong about the time. But that is later, and I promised you a kinder way in, and I have kept the promise as long as I am able.

It was a good morning, and a man lived, and a child was loved and called a hawk, and only the old woman with her arm across me knew that something in the salt had begun to come awake, and was already, even then, listening.

# Chapter 2 — What I Was Told I Was

The Attuin do not tell children what they are. They show them, and let the knowing grow up out of the doing, the way salt grows in the dark beneath the pans.

I want to be careful here. It would be easy to make my people simple. The songs do this. They paint us happy and barefoot and free, a brown smudge of folk who sing and steal and vanish into the dunes. There is truth in it, the way there is truth in a child's drawing of a horse. But a horse is more than four legs and a tail, and we were more than the songs allowed.

We were proud. That is the first thing, and the thing the songs forget.

I have heard it said, by people who should know better, that pride is a poor man's gold. They mean it as wisdom. It is not. Pride was the thing we had that no one could raid out of our wagons, and we wore it the way the canyon-folk wore their water-rights, openly, as a kind of armor. We owned the Deep. Not the way a king owns a gorge, with walls and a ledger. We owned it the way you own a thing you can survive and others cannot.

Let me give you a morning instead of an argument.

I was small. The fires were low and gray, and the cold of the desert night was still in the sand, and Amma Reshi sat with her back to a wagon-wheel, mending a strap by feel because her eyes were old. I was at her knee. There were other children somewhere, the way there are always other children, a noise off at the edge of things.

"Tell the long water," I said.

She did not look up from the strap. "You've had it."

"Again."

And here is a thing I have to tell you straight, because it matters. When Amma Reshi told a story, she was not remembering it. I thought she was, then. I thought all old women carried stories the way they carried scars, just things that had happened and stayed. I was wrong, and the wrongness is the whole of what I am.

She closed her eyes. She was quiet for a moment, and the quiet had the shape of someone setting down one weight to lift another. Then she told the long water, and the woman who walked it had a name I would not understand for years was not a name in any tongue still spoken, and a voice that came up through Amma's voice the way ground-water comes up through sand. She was telling it from the inside. She was *being* the woman who first found the night-road across the white death of the pan, who counted the cool spots by the smell of the air and lived where she should have died.

That is the warm Remembering, and I will tell you about it, because the cold kind nearly drowned me later and you should know they were one thing once, before the wall.

The salt-singers carry their dead. Not in grief, though there is grief. They carry them the way a wagon carries water, as the thing that keeps the rest alive. When a salt-singer is dying she gives what she holds to a daughter strong enough to take it, and into that daughter comes the whole long line, every road walked, every birth, every clever hand that ever tied a knot the Attuin still tie. It is not ghosts. I want that clear. No dead woman sat behind Amma's eyes wanting things. It was *memory*, the way your hand remembers a knife after your mind has let it go. But it was memory with the warmth still in it, sung and shared and lived at the fire, and that is why the song-roads are sung and not written. You cannot write the warmth in.

I did not know I would be able to do it. Neither did she, that morning. But she suspected, and the suspicion sat in her like a stone in a sandal, and she could not take her shoe off.

---

There is a wound under all this, and I have been walking around it the way you walk around a soft spot in the crust. I will step on it now.

We were bred. My people. Like beasts.

Not by ourselves. By the Salthold, by the cold sisters who broke down out of us long before any wagon I knew, who had decided across centuries that the Attuin grew a certain gift the way a particular valley grows a particular grape, and that the gift was theirs to harvest. They took children. They called it many things. They called it mercy, mostly, which is what people call a thing when they want to keep doing it.

I did not understand this as a child. What I understood was a feeling, and the feeling was that somewhere out past the dunes there were people who looked at us and saw a stud-book.

Tamar understood it. Tamar understood it the way you understand a thing that took something from your own hand.

She was older than me by some years that mattered then and matter not at all now. She was kin, close enough that I called her sister and far enough that I was a little afraid of her, which is the right distance for a child to love someone. She had chosen the spear already, or it had chosen her, and she walked like it, all shoulder and challenge, with that prickly brightness that makes you want to stand nearer the fire and also makes you afraid of getting burned.

"Don't let Reshi make a salt-singer of you," she told me once. We were sitting on the cool side of a wagon in the dead white middle of a day, when the world goes flat and bright and no one moves.

"Why not."

"Because then they'll want you." She said *they* the way you'd say a word for a sickness. "The singing-women. They get taken. Did you know that? They get *given*." She picked up a handful of sand and let it run out of her fist, watching it, not me. "The too-bright child. Send the too-bright child to the salt." She did not finish it. The hand opened. "A kindness."

I did not know yet that I was the too-bright child. She did not know it either. If she had, I think she would have put me on a horse and ridden me into the Deep until the wagons were a memory, and the whole long story I am telling you would have gone another way.

But she didn't know. So she only let the sand run out of her hand, and was angry at the world in the general way of the young, and I sat next to her and loved her with the helpless completeness children manage and the rest of us spend our lives trying to remember how to do.

---

I was happy.

I know how the story goes from here. You may know too, if you have heard the songs — and if you have, you have been told the wrong people to weep for. But I did not know, then. The girl at Amma Reshi's knee did not know. She had a grandmother who was not her grandmother and loved her more than blood, and a fierce bright sister who was not quite her sister, and a road under the wagon that the dead had found for her so she would not die where she was meant to die. She had cold sand to wake to and warm fire to sleep by, and a whole people who would have called her *ours* and meant it.

It was the last of it. The very last clean part of my life, and I spent it the way children spend everything, completely, with both hands, not knowing it was the only one I would get.

I have carried that morning a long way. I have carried it across worse country than the pans. And I will tell you what I have never told anyone, since we are here and the fire is low and you have been kind enough to listen.

I would give it all back. Every road I walked after, every name I learned, every fear I taught the desert to feel when it speaks my own. I would give all of it to sit one more morning at the wheel of that wagon, in the gray cold before the sun, and have her tell me the long water, and not yet know what I was.

But that is not a thing the salt gives back.

# Chapter 3 — The Cold Girl Who Came to Watch Me

I was not there for the part I will tell you now.

I want that said plainly, before I begin. I have a great many faults, and you will meet them all before this is done, but lying to you is not among the ones I mean to commit. So: I did not see the Salthold of Sett until I was grown, and what I saw then was nothing like what Sabra saw as a child. I have this from her. She told it to me in a small room a long way from any of it, with her hands quiet in her lap, the way she held them when she meant every word. Sabra does not lie to me. I have tested it more times than I am proud of. I give it to you as she gave it to me.

The Salthold sits on the lip of the White Pan, and it is white. That is the first thing. Not the white of bone or cloud. The white of salt, which is its own thing, and which hurts.

It has no windows that face the pan.

Think on that a moment. A great house at the edge of the greatest emptiness in the world, and every eye it has is turned inward, to its courts and its cisterns and its own cold halls. Sabra said you could live there a year and forget the pan was outside at all. Then the wind would shift, and you would taste it on your bread, and remember.

She came to it at six. She did not come willingly, because no one does, but she came without crying, and she told me she was proud of that for years. I think now it was the saddest part of the whole telling, that a child of six counted not-crying among her victories. But I have done worse counting in my own ledger, so I said nothing.

They taught her to notice.

That is the whole of it and none of it. There is a discipline the order keeps that they will tell you, if they tell you anything, is a kind of seeing. It is not. There is nothing in it that a sharp old woman in a market could not do, given a hundred years and a knife at her throat to make her quick. They call it the Reckoning. What it is, is noticing, drilled past the place where noticing stops and becomes a thing you cannot turn off.

She told me how they taught it. I will give you one piece, because it stayed with me.

They would sit a girl in a room with a woman she had never met. The woman would say nothing. The girl had a hundred heartbeats to look, and then the woman left, and the girl said everything she could about her. Where she had slept. What she had eaten. Whether she had borne children, and how many, and whether any had died. Whether she was afraid. Whether she was lying about being afraid. Get it wrong and you sat the room again. Get it right and they made it harder.

Sabra was very good. She told me this without pride, which is how I knew it was true. The proud thing would have been to pretend she had not been good, and she was past that with me by then.

"It's a beautiful thing to be good at," she said. "That's the trouble. No one tells you the cost. Not until you've bought it."

The cost, she meant, was that she could no longer not-see. She would look at a friend and know the friend had been weeping, and the friend had wanted it kept, and now it was not kept, because Sabra knew. You cannot give that back to a person. It sits between you. She lost a great many small kindnesses that way, the kindness of being lied to gently, and she did not understand she had lost them until she was older and lonelier than she wished to be.

The order calls itself a teaching house. *We only advise*, they say, and they say it the way you'd say *I only carry the knife, I do not use it*, and mean for you to be soothed. Sabra believed it until she was perhaps eleven. Then she watched a woman of the Closed Hand advise a king into a war and out of a brother, all in one afternoon, all in a voice like a hand smoothing a sheet, and she understood that *advise* was the longest word in the order's tongue, and the cruelest.

She did not run. I want you to understand that she was not a fool and not a coward. She loved the order the way you love a thing that is the only thing you have. That love is real. It is also a door that does not open from the inside. I have stood in front of a few of those.

When she was old enough to be useful and young enough to be deniable, they sent her out.

Here I have to guess at a thing, and I will tell you where the guessing starts. It starts now.

She was sent under a salt-trade cover. That much she knew and told me: a clerk's veil, a string of debts to collect among the caravans, a reason to ride into the Deep and ask questions and write small careful figures in a book. The book was the cover. The figures were real. The Reckoning, you see, hides best inside something true.

What she was *actually* sent to do, she did not learn for a long time, and so what I am about to say is mine, not hers. There had been a rumour. The order traffics in rumours the way the Attuin traffic in water, and most are nothing, and a few are everything, and the trick of the order is in telling which is which before it costs you. The rumour was thin. A caravan of the long-water line. A child in it who was, the word went, *too much.*

Too much what, the rumour did not say. It never does. That is what made it dangerous to the ones who knew enough to be afraid.

They did not send Sabra to take the child. You do not send a girl of seventeen to take anything. They sent her to *look.* To do the only thing she had been built to do, which was to sit a hundred heartbeats in a room with a stranger and come out knowing where she had slept and whether she was afraid and what, under the fear, she might be. They sent the noticing, dressed as a clerk, into the Deep, to find out if a rumour had a face.

I was the rumour.

I did not know it. She did not know it, not at first, not for certain, because the Reckoning is not knowing, whatever they pretend. It is a guess made so well it frightens you. She rode toward us with a book of true figures and a false name and a thing in her chest she had not put a name to yet, and she meant only to look at a child and write down what she saw.

I have wondered, since, what she would have written if she'd been honest. *The girl is too much.* Yes. But too much of what?

She found out. We all did, in the end, and it cost more than the order or the temple or any of them ever paid for anything.

But that comes later, and I have promised to tell it in its order.

For now there is only a cold beautiful house with its eyes turned inward, and a girl in it who learned to notice until she could not stop, and a clerk's veil packed in a saddlebag, and a long white road into the Deep where I was waiting without knowing I was waiting for anyone at all.

She came to watch me.

I have never decided whether to be glad of it. That is the truth, and it is the most honest thing in this chapter, and I will leave it where it falls.


---


# Chapter 4 — Qesh, and the Veil

I have never been to Qesh in the way you mean it. I was a child in the Deep when this happened, asleep under a wagon a hundred leagues from any fountain. So you must understand from the start that I am giving you a thing I gathered, not a thing I saw.

But I have chased this story the way a thirsty woman chases water. I have had it from three mouths, and two of them had reason to lie, and one of them was Kessa herself. So I will tell it whole, and where I have guessed I will say so, and you may believe me as far as that takes you.

Qesh is the fat heart of the world. Everywhere else in the Saltlands a city is a thing carved out of need — a gorge for shade, a spring guarded like a throat. Qesh is a city carved out of plenty. It sits on water that does not end, and it has forgotten how rare that is, the way the rich forget everything.

It smells of jasmine over rot. I am told you never stop smelling both.

The orders keep houses there. The thrones keep houses there. Everyone who matters keeps a house in Qesh, and a knife in the house, and a reason to use neither yet. It is a place where business is done slowly so the doing can be enjoyed. That is the thing to hold in your mind. Qesh likes the long way round.

So did Kessa.

I should tell you what the Edemre are, but the truth is I cannot, not really. I can tell you the words. They were a House of the Adima Ru once, and then they were not, and now they are the order's knife that the order is afraid to hold. They master the Stillness. They veil to kill.

There is a saying about them I heard before I ever heard the word *Edemre*. *To see her face is to be safe. To see her veil is to be already dead.* I thought it was a children's thing, the kind of dark rhyme you chant to scare a smaller cousin. It is not. It is the most precise thing anyone has ever said about them.

Kessa, when I knew her, was the most alive person I have ever stood next to. That is the part the songs leave out. They make her cold. She was the opposite of cold. She was a fire you would walk into knowing it was a fire.

In Qesh, that spring, she ruined a man named Theron.

Theron was the younger brother of a king. The Red Gorge was his blood, though he would never sit its throne — younger brothers do not, in the canyon-kingdoms, they are given to the temple instead, the way you give the gods a thing you cannot use. He was pledged to Sun-and-Stone. He had taken the vows that a man takes when his family decides what he is before he can argue.

He was, by every account, kind. I want that said. He was not a fool and he was not cruel. He was a young man who had been promised to stone, and he met fire, and he had no defense at all because no one had ever taught him there was a thing to defend against.

I have wondered, since, whether Kessa chose him on purpose. Whether she looked across whatever room it was and saw the temple-pledge on him like a brand and decided that was the one. I think she did. I think a great deal of what Kessa did looked like appetite and was in fact aim.

Here is what I know, because she told me herself, years and a war later, with a date-spirit in her hand and that particular smile she wore when she was telling the truth.

She said she did not touch him for two days.

She said she sat near him at three meals and asked him about stone. About the calendar, the astronomy, the way the Kiln's masons set a wall so it would stand a thousand years. She said he lit up like a lamp. No one had ever asked Theron about the things Theron loved. She found the thing he loved and she let him show it to her, and she paid attention — real attention, the kind that costs the watcher something — and that was the whole of it, for two days. She just *saw* him.

"That's the trick of it," she told me. "Not the bed. Anyone can do the bed. You ruin a man by seeing him."

I have never forgotten that. I have used it. I am not proud of all the times.

When she finally took him to bed it was not a conquest, the way the songs sing it. The songs make it a hunt with a kill at the end. She said it was slower than that and gentler than that and that he wept, after, and not from sorrow. She said she did not hurry any part of it, not once, the whole long night, and that this was the cruelty if there was one. She gave him a thing he would measure every other thing against for the rest of his life. And then she left before the heat broke, the way the Edemre always leave, with a knife she did not use still strapped to her thigh.

Theron of the Red Gorge went back to his stone and his calendar and his thousand-year walls, and something in him had been moved that would not move back. He married, later. He served his brother. By all accounts he did it well. But there was a window in him after that, and a wind came through it sometimes, and everyone who knew him saw it and no one knew the name of it.

The name of it was Kessa. He carried her the way some men carry a wound that healed wrong — useful enough, and aching on the cold nights.

I asked her once if she had loved him. We were old friends by then, in the way that two people are old friends who have each tried to be the death of the other.

She thought about it longer than I expected. "Loved him for a night." She turned the cup. "People think a night is nothing."

A breath, and then: "It's most of what there is."

I am telling you this because you have to understand her before you understand what came next. You have to know that this was a woman who did everything with her whole self and walked away from all of it. Because the morning after she ruined Theron, the Edemre handed her the ledger.

They do not kill for hate. They do not kill for hire, exactly, though coin changes. They kill against a debt entered in blood, and a debt once entered is *paid* — paid against the order itself if the order is what owes, paid against time, paid down a line of years until the one it points at is born to be its answer.

There was a debt in the ledger older than Kessa. It had come down her own line, the way the desert passes things down: silently, and without asking the one who must carry it. Her grandmother's debt, or her grandmother's grandmother's. It does not matter whose. It was Kessa's now.

And that spring, in Qesh, with the jasmine and the rot still on her, someone read her the name the debt pointed to.

It did not point at a person. That is the thing that froze me when I first understood it, lying awake later in my own life with the cold of it. The debt was old enough that the one who owed it was not yet born when it was sworn. It pointed at a bloodline. It pointed at a child not yet grown, somewhere out in the Deep, in a caravan that moved like all caravans move, here and then gone, salt and song and wagons under the stars.

A girl among the Attuin.

I have wondered, all my life, whether she knew that morning that it was me. I have decided she did not. The debt was a direction, then, not a face. A heading on the night-road and nothing more.

But I will tell you what I think, and you can take it for what a guess is worth from a woman who loves her.

I think the same eyes that found the one thing Theron loved and broke him open with it — I think those eyes were already turned, that morning, toward the Deep. I think she folded the veil away and looked out over the fat sweet city toward the dunes she could not see, and she felt the debt settle onto her like a second skin, and some part of her that did everything with its whole self was already, quietly, on the road.

She was. She just did not know yet whose face was at the end of it.

It was mine.

# Chapter 5 — The Watcher and the Watched

She came to us as a salt-trader's daughter, and I knew she was lying before she finished saying her name.

I want to be careful here. It is easy, looking back, to make myself wiser than I was. The girl I am telling you about was nine years old and could not have told you what the Reckoning was, or the Salthold, or any of the names I carry now like stones in my pockets. But she knew. She knew the way you know a wrong note in a song you have heard all your life. Sabra said *I am Sabra, of the salt-houses of Sett*, and something in me sat up like a hound.

It was the evening. Amma Reshi had let the strangers' fire be built beside ours, which was the old courtesy. Three traders, two camels, a wagon of pressed salt-cake. They greeted us properly. They asked for news, and where there was water, and only then for trade. Everything was correct. That was the first wrong thing. Nothing among real traders is ever that correct.

I remember her hands.

The men had desert hands, cracked and dark, hands that had hauled rope and butchered and bled. Sabra's hands lay in her lap like something she was guarding. They were not soft, exactly. They were *unused* in a particular way, the way a knife is unused that has only ever been sharpened. I was nine and could not have said any of this. I only knew I could not stop looking at her hands.

And she could not stop looking at me.

You must understand what that does to a child like I was. I told you already that I was loved and a little apart. The caravan loved me the way you love a clever dog or a strange weather — fondly, and from a step back. Tamar loved me hard and prickly and busy. Amma loved me with fear folded into it, though I did not know that yet. And here came a stranger with a still face who looked at me the way no one had ever looked at me.

She looked at me like I was worth the trouble of being read.

I shone for her. God help me, I shone.

---

I should tell you what she saw, because she told me herself, years later, lying in the dark with her back warm against me, the way you confess things you would never say to a face.

"You were impossible," she said. "You watched the wrong people. You laughed before the joke was finished. You knew which of us was lying and you didn't know you knew it." She was quiet a while. "Eleven years they trained me to notice. And I was sent to notice a child. And the child noticed me back."

I did not, then. I do now.

The order had drilled her to see the small things and say nothing — to read the lie in a throat, the grief in a gait, and let it lie in her like a coin under the tongue until it was worth spending. She had come to the Deep to assess a rumour. A too-bright child. A thing they breed for and dread to find. She came to watch a girl and write her down in the cold accounting of her House, and instead she sat by our fire and was *seen*, and she did not have a word for what that felt like because her House had taken the warm words out of her when she was small.

I have thought about that a great deal. Two girls who had each been made lonely by a gift, sitting on opposite sides of a stranger's fire, recognising each other and pretending not to. It would make a lovely song. It is even mostly true. The part the song would leave out is that I was already, that first night, deciding she was mine.

That is the thing I will not let myself round up. I did not want to be seen so that she would be less alone. I wanted to be seen the way a thirsty thing wants water — for me, only for me, and I would have taken it from anyone, and I took it from her.

---

She stayed.

The traders moved on after three days, the proper time, and Sabra did not go with them. There was a story about it. There is always a story. She had a cough that wanted dry air; the salt-houses could spare a daughter for a season; she would learn the song-roads and be useful. Amma let it happen. I see now that Amma let a great many things happen that season, watching, the way a woman watches a snake decide which way to go.

For me it was simply the best thing that had ever come to the caravan.

I followed her. I am not ashamed of it now, though I was clever enough then to hide it, or thought I was. I learned her habits and arranged to be where they took her. When she walked to the morning shade I happened to be walking there. When she sat apart to do the small still exercises she would not name, I sat where I could watch and pretended to mend a strap I had already mended.

She let me. That is the part that mattered. She could have frozen me out with a look — she had the face for it, the still terrible Salthold face that could make a grown caravan-man find somewhere else to be. She turned it on me once, the second day, and I remember the cold of it going through me like well-water. And then it broke. Not all the way. A hairline. The corner of her mouth.

"You're staring," she said.

"So are you."

She did not answer that for a long moment, and I have spent years inside that long moment. Then she said, "Yes. It's my work."

"What's your work?"

"Noticing."

"That's not work," I said, with all the scorn a nine-year-old can fit into four words. "That's just looking."

And Sabra — cold, drilled, sent to write me into a ledger of horrors — Sabra laughed. A real one. Short, surprised out of her, a sound I do not think her House had heard from her in years. She put her hand over her mouth as if to push it back in.

I would have walked into the pans at noon to hear it again.

---

So I set about earning it.

This is the part the songs get wrong, when they sing of us at all. They make me a sad bright child and her a sad cold woman and the love between us a gentle thing that grew like a flower out of two hurts. It was not gentle. It was a hunt. I hunted her approval the way Tamar hunted sand-hare, patient and merciless, and I was *good* at it, and being good at it felt the way I imagine drink feels to a man who should not drink.

I showed off for her. I will say it plainly because I have spent too long not saying it.

I would have her watch me move through the camp and I would let the little gift loose on purpose — the thing Amma had told me to keep small. I would tell her, before the rider crested the dune, that someone was coming, and how many, and whether they were kin. I would tell her which of the men had quarrelled by the way they stood at the fire not looking at each other. I would tell her a woman was carrying before the woman knew it, and watch Sabra's face go still in the particular way that meant I had landed something.

I thought I was dazzling her. I was nine and certain, and certainty has always been the bright sharp edge I cut myself on.

What I was doing — I see it now, I have had a long time to see it — was handing the Salthold its proof. Every clever thing I did to make one lonely girl love me was another line in the report she had been sent to write. I was the rumour. And I was performing the rumour, beautifully, for the very person sent to confirm it, because I wanted her to look at me and could not stop.

She knew it. Of course she knew it. She told me, that night years later in the dark, that every time I showed off she felt two things at once and could not tell which was worse — wonder, and the cold settling weight of a thing she would have to put in writing.

"I should have left," she said. "After the third day. I had more than enough by the third day."

"Why didn't you?"

She was a long time answering, and when she did her voice had gone somewhere I had not heard it go before.

"Because you looked at me," she said, "like I was a person and not a knife. And I had forgotten I could be looked at that way. And I was a coward, and I stayed to keep being looked at, and it cost everything. So." A breath. "We were the same, you and I. We both stayed for the wrong reason. We both got someone killed for it."

I did not say anything to that. There was nothing to say. We were in the dark and the dark was kind, and we let it be.

---

But that was years off, and the girl in the story did not know any of it. The girl in the story had a new and wonderful person at the fire and a gift she was learning to spend like coin, and a whole season of light ahead of her before the dark came down.

I am going to let her have it a while longer.

She deserved it. Whatever else is true — and a great deal else is true, and worse — that child sitting in the morning shade watching a stranger's guarded hands, hungry to be seen and not yet knowing what it costs to be seen by the wrong eyes, that child had done nothing yet.

That came later. I will get there.

I told you I would tell it true.
