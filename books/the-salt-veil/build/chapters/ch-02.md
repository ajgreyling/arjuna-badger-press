# Chapter 2 — What I Was Told I Was

The Attuin do not tell children what they are. They show them, and let the knowing grow up out of the doing, the way salt grows in the dark beneath the pans.

I want to be careful here. It would be easy to make my people simple. The songs do this. They paint us happy and barefoot and free, a brown smudge of folk who sing and steal and vanish into the dunes. There is truth in it, the way there is truth in a child's drawing of a horse. But a horse is more than four legs and a tail, and we were more than the songs allowed.

We were proud. That is the first thing, and the thing the songs forget.

I have heard it said, by people who should know better, that pride is a poor man's gold. They mean it as wisdom. It is not. Pride was the thing we had that no one could raid out of our wagons, and we wore it the way the canyon-folk wore their water-rights, openly, as a kind of armor. We owned the Deep. Not the way a king owns a gorge, with walls and a ledger. We owned it the way you own a thing you can survive and others cannot.

Let me give you a morning instead of an argument.

I was small. The fires were low and gray, and the cold of the desert night was still in the sand, and Amma Reshi sat with her back to a wagon-wheel, mending a strap by feel because her eyes were old. I was at her knee. There were other children somewhere, the way there are always other children, a noise off at the edge of things.

"Tell the long water," I said.

She did not look up from the strap. "You've had it."

"Again."

And here is a thing I have to tell you straight, because it matters. When Amma Reshi told a story, she was not remembering it. I thought she was, then. I thought all old women carried stories the way they carried scars, just things that had happened and stayed. I was wrong, and the wrongness is the whole of what I am.

She closed her eyes. She was quiet for a moment, and the quiet had the shape of someone setting down one weight to lift another. Then she told the long water, and the woman who walked it had a name I would not understand for years was not a name in any tongue still spoken, and a voice that came up through Amma's voice the way ground-water comes up through sand. She was telling it from the inside. She was *being* the woman who first found the night-road across the white death of the pan, who counted the cool spots by the smell of the air and lived where she should have died.

That is the warm Remembering, and I will tell you about it, because the cold kind nearly drowned me later and you should know they were one thing once, before the wall.

The salt-singers carry their dead. Not in grief, though there is grief. They carry them the way a wagon carries water, as the thing that keeps the rest alive. When a salt-singer is dying she gives what she holds to a daughter strong enough to take it, and into that daughter comes the whole long line, every road walked, every birth, every clever hand that ever tied a knot the Attuin still tie. It is not ghosts. I want that clear. No dead woman sat behind Amma's eyes wanting things. It was *memory*, the way your hand remembers a knife after your mind has let it go. But it was memory with the warmth still in it, sung and shared and lived at the fire, and that is why the song-roads are sung and not written. You cannot write the warmth in.

I did not know I would be able to do it. Neither did she, that morning. But she suspected, and the suspicion sat in her like a stone in a sandal, and she could not take her shoe off.

---

There is a wound under all this, and I have been walking around it the way you walk around a soft spot in the crust. I will step on it now.

We were bred. My people. Like beasts.

Not by ourselves. By the Salthold, by the cold sisters who broke down out of us long before any wagon I knew, who had decided across centuries that the Attuin grew a certain gift the way a particular valley grows a particular grape, and that the gift was theirs to harvest. They took children. They called it many things. They called it mercy, mostly, which is what people call a thing when they want to keep doing it.

I did not understand this as a child. What I understood was a feeling, and the feeling was that somewhere out past the dunes there were people who looked at us and saw a stud-book.

Tamar understood it. Tamar understood it the way you understand a thing that took something from your own hand.

She was older than me by some years that mattered then and matter not at all now. She was kin, close enough that I called her sister and far enough that I was a little afraid of her, which is the right distance for a child to love someone. She had chosen the spear already, or it had chosen her, and she walked like it, all shoulder and challenge, with that prickly brightness that makes you want to stand nearer the fire and also makes you afraid of getting burned.

"Don't let Reshi make a salt-singer of you," she told me once. We were sitting on the cool side of a wagon in the dead white middle of a day, when the world goes flat and bright and no one moves.

"Why not."

"Because then they'll want you." She said *they* the way you'd say a word for a sickness. "The singing-women. They get taken. Did you know that? They get *given*." She picked up a handful of sand and let it run out of her fist, watching it, not me. "The too-bright child. Send the too-bright child to the salt." She did not finish it. The hand opened. "A kindness."

I did not know yet that I was the too-bright child. She did not know it either. If she had, I think she would have put me on a horse and ridden me into the Deep until the wagons were a memory, and the whole long story I am telling you would have gone another way.

But she didn't know. So she only let the sand run out of her hand, and was angry at the world in the general way of the young, and I sat next to her and loved her with the helpless completeness children manage and the rest of us spend our lives trying to remember how to do.

---

I was happy.

I know how the story goes from here. You may know too, if you have heard the songs — and if you have, you have been told the wrong people to weep for. But I did not know, then. The girl at Amma Reshi's knee did not know. She had a grandmother who was not her grandmother and loved her more than blood, and a fierce bright sister who was not quite her sister, and a road under the wagon that the dead had found for her so she would not die where she was meant to die. She had cold sand to wake to and warm fire to sleep by, and a whole people who would have called her *ours* and meant it.

It was the last of it. The very last clean part of my life, and I spent it the way children spend everything, completely, with both hands, not knowing it was the only one I would get.

I have carried that morning a long way. I have carried it across worse country than the pans. And I will tell you what I have never told anyone, since we are here and the fire is low and you have been kind enough to listen.

I would give it all back. Every road I walked after, every name I learned, every fear I taught the desert to feel when it speaks my own. I would give all of it to sit one more morning at the wheel of that wagon, in the gray cold before the sun, and have her tell me the long water, and not yet know what I was.

But that is not a thing the salt gives back.
