# Chapter 4 — Qesh, and the Veil

I have never been to Qesh in the way you mean it. I was a child in the Deep when this happened, asleep under a wagon a hundred leagues from any fountain. So you must understand from the start that I am giving you a thing I gathered, not a thing I saw.

But I have chased this story the way a thirsty woman chases water. I have had it from three mouths, and two of them had reason to lie, and one of them was Kessa herself. So I will tell it whole, and where I have guessed I will say so, and you may believe me as far as that takes you.

Qesh is the fat heart of the world. Everywhere else in the Saltlands a city is a thing carved out of need — a gorge for shade, a spring guarded like a throat. Qesh is a city carved out of plenty. It sits on water that does not end, and it has forgotten how rare that is, the way the rich forget everything.

It smells of jasmine over rot. I am told you never stop smelling both.

The orders keep houses there. The thrones keep houses there. Everyone who matters keeps a house in Qesh, and a knife in the house, and a reason to use neither yet. It is a place where business is done slowly so the doing can be enjoyed. That is the thing to hold in your mind. Qesh likes the long way round.

So did Kessa.

I should tell you what the Edemre are, but the truth is I cannot, not really. I can tell you the words. They were a House of the Adima Ru once, and then they were not, and now they are the order's knife that the order is afraid to hold. They master the Stillness. They veil to kill.

There is a saying about them I heard before I ever heard the word *Edemre*. *To see her face is to be safe. To see her veil is to be already dead.* I thought it was a children's thing, the kind of dark rhyme you chant to scare a smaller cousin. It is not. It is the most precise thing anyone has ever said about them.

Kessa, when I knew her, was the most alive person I have ever stood next to. That is the part the songs leave out. They make her cold. She was the opposite of cold. She was a fire you would walk into knowing it was a fire.

In Qesh, that spring, she ruined a man named Theron.

Theron was the younger brother of a king. The Red Gorge was his blood, though he would never sit its throne — younger brothers do not, in the canyon-kingdoms, they are given to the temple instead, the way you give the gods a thing you cannot use. He was pledged to Sun-and-Stone. He had taken the vows that a man takes when his family decides what he is before he can argue.

He was, by every account, kind. I want that said. He was not a fool and he was not cruel. He was a young man who had been promised to stone, and he met fire, and he had no defense at all because no one had ever taught him there was a thing to defend against.

I have wondered, since, whether Kessa chose him on purpose. Whether she looked across whatever room it was and saw the temple-pledge on him like a brand and decided that was the one. I think she did. I think a great deal of what Kessa did looked like appetite and was in fact aim.

Here is what I know, because she told me herself, years and a war later, with a date-spirit in her hand and that particular smile she wore when she was telling the truth.

She said she did not touch him for two days.

She said she sat near him at three meals and asked him about stone. About the calendar, the astronomy, the way the Kiln's masons set a wall so it would stand a thousand years. She said he lit up like a lamp. No one had ever asked Theron about the things Theron loved. She found the thing he loved and she let him show it to her, and she paid attention — real attention, the kind that costs the watcher something — and that was the whole of it, for two days. She just *saw* him.

"That's the trick of it," she told me. "Not the bed. Anyone can do the bed. You ruin a man by seeing him."

I have never forgotten that. I have used it. I am not proud of all the times.

When she finally took him to bed it was not a conquest, the way the songs sing it. The songs make it a hunt with a kill at the end. She said it was slower than that and gentler than that and that he wept, after, and not from sorrow. She said she did not hurry any part of it, not once, the whole long night, and that this was the cruelty if there was one. She gave him a thing he would measure every other thing against for the rest of his life. And then she left before the heat broke, the way the Edemre always leave, with a knife she did not use still strapped to her thigh.

Theron of the Red Gorge went back to his stone and his calendar and his thousand-year walls, and something in him had been moved that would not move back. He married, later. He served his brother. By all accounts he did it well. But there was a window in him after that, and a wind came through it sometimes, and everyone who knew him saw it and no one knew the name of it.

The name of it was Kessa. He carried her the way some men carry a wound that healed wrong — useful enough, and aching on the cold nights.

I asked her once if she had loved him. We were old friends by then, in the way that two people are old friends who have each tried to be the death of the other.

She thought about it longer than I expected. "Loved him for a night." She turned the cup. "People think a night is nothing."

A breath, and then: "It's most of what there is."

I am telling you this because you have to understand her before you understand what came next. You have to know that this was a woman who did everything with her whole self and walked away from all of it. Because the morning after she ruined Theron, the Edemre handed her the ledger.

They do not kill for hate. They do not kill for hire, exactly, though coin changes. They kill against a debt entered in blood, and a debt once entered is *paid* — paid against the order itself if the order is what owes, paid against time, paid down a line of years until the one it points at is born to be its answer.

There was a debt in the ledger older than Kessa. It had come down her own line, the way the desert passes things down: silently, and without asking the one who must carry it. Her grandmother's debt, or her grandmother's grandmother's. It does not matter whose. It was Kessa's now.

And that spring, in Qesh, with the jasmine and the rot still on her, someone read her the name the debt pointed to.

It did not point at a person. That is the thing that froze me when I first understood it, lying awake later in my own life with the cold of it. The debt was old enough that the one who owed it was not yet born when it was sworn. It pointed at a bloodline. It pointed at a child not yet grown, somewhere out in the Deep, in a caravan that moved like all caravans move, here and then gone, salt and song and wagons under the stars.

A girl among the Attuin.

I have wondered, all my life, whether she knew that morning that it was me. I have decided she did not. The debt was a direction, then, not a face. A heading on the night-road and nothing more.

But I will tell you what I think, and you can take it for what a guess is worth from a woman who loves her.

I think the same eyes that found the one thing Theron loved and broke him open with it — I think those eyes were already turned, that morning, toward the Deep. I think she folded the veil away and looked out over the fat sweet city toward the dunes she could not see, and she felt the debt settle onto her like a second skin, and some part of her that did everything with its whole self was already, quietly, on the road.

She was. She just did not know yet whose face was at the end of it.

It was mine.
