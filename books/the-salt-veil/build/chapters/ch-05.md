# Chapter 5 — The Watcher and the Watched

She came to us as a salt-trader's daughter, and I knew she was lying before she finished saying her name.

I want to be careful here. It is easy, looking back, to make myself wiser than I was. The girl I am telling you about was nine years old and could not have told you what the Reckoning was, or the Salthold, or any of the names I carry now like stones in my pockets. But she knew. She knew the way you know a wrong note in a song you have heard all your life. Sabra said *I am Sabra, of the salt-houses of Sett*, and something in me sat up like a hound.

It was the evening. Amma Reshi had let the strangers' fire be built beside ours, which was the old courtesy. Three traders, two camels, a wagon of pressed salt-cake. They greeted us properly. They asked for news, and where there was water, and only then for trade. Everything was correct. That was the first wrong thing. Nothing among real traders is ever that correct.

I remember her hands.

The men had desert hands, cracked and dark, hands that had hauled rope and butchered and bled. Sabra's hands lay in her lap like something she was guarding. They were not soft, exactly. They were *unused* in a particular way, the way a knife is unused that has only ever been sharpened. I was nine and could not have said any of this. I only knew I could not stop looking at her hands.

And she could not stop looking at me.

You must understand what that does to a child like I was. I told you already that I was loved and a little apart. The caravan loved me the way you love a clever dog or a strange weather — fondly, and from a step back. Tamar loved me hard and prickly and busy. Amma loved me with fear folded into it, though I did not know that yet. And here came a stranger with a still face who looked at me the way no one had ever looked at me.

She looked at me like I was worth the trouble of being read.

I shone for her. God help me, I shone.

---

I should tell you what she saw, because she told me herself, years later, lying in the dark with her back warm against me, the way you confess things you would never say to a face.

"You were impossible," she said. "You watched the wrong people. You laughed before the joke was finished. You knew which of us was lying and you didn't know you knew it." She was quiet a while. "Eleven years they trained me to notice. And I was sent to notice a child. And the child noticed me back."

I did not, then. I do now.

The order had drilled her to see the small things and say nothing — to read the lie in a throat, the grief in a gait, and let it lie in her like a coin under the tongue until it was worth spending. She had come to the Deep to assess a rumour. A too-bright child. A thing they breed for and dread to find. She came to watch a girl and write her down in the cold accounting of her House, and instead she sat by our fire and was *seen*, and she did not have a word for what that felt like because her House had taken the warm words out of her when she was small.

I have thought about that a great deal. Two girls who had each been made lonely by a gift, sitting on opposite sides of a stranger's fire, recognising each other and pretending not to. It would make a lovely song. It is even mostly true. The part the song would leave out is that I was already, that first night, deciding she was mine.

That is the thing I will not let myself round up. I did not want to be seen so that she would be less alone. I wanted to be seen the way a thirsty thing wants water — for me, only for me, and I would have taken it from anyone, and I took it from her.

---

She stayed.

The traders moved on after three days, the proper time, and Sabra did not go with them. There was a story about it. There is always a story. She had a cough that wanted dry air; the salt-houses could spare a daughter for a season; she would learn the song-roads and be useful. Amma let it happen. I see now that Amma let a great many things happen that season, watching, the way a woman watches a snake decide which way to go.

For me it was simply the best thing that had ever come to the caravan.

I followed her. I am not ashamed of it now, though I was clever enough then to hide it, or thought I was. I learned her habits and arranged to be where they took her. When she walked to the morning shade I happened to be walking there. When she sat apart to do the small still exercises she would not name, I sat where I could watch and pretended to mend a strap I had already mended.

She let me. That is the part that mattered. She could have frozen me out with a look — she had the face for it, the still terrible Salthold face that could make a grown caravan-man find somewhere else to be. She turned it on me once, the second day, and I remember the cold of it going through me like well-water. And then it broke. Not all the way. A hairline. The corner of her mouth.

"You're staring," she said.

"So are you."

She did not answer that for a long moment, and I have spent years inside that long moment. Then she said, "Yes. It's my work."

"What's your work?"

"Noticing."

"That's not work," I said, with all the scorn a nine-year-old can fit into four words. "That's just looking."

And Sabra — cold, drilled, sent to write me into a ledger of horrors — Sabra laughed. A real one. Short, surprised out of her, a sound I do not think her House had heard from her in years. She put her hand over her mouth as if to push it back in.

I would have walked into the pans at noon to hear it again.

---

So I set about earning it.

This is the part the songs get wrong, when they sing of us at all. They make me a sad bright child and her a sad cold woman and the love between us a gentle thing that grew like a flower out of two hurts. It was not gentle. It was a hunt. I hunted her approval the way Tamar hunted sand-hare, patient and merciless, and I was *good* at it, and being good at it felt the way I imagine drink feels to a man who should not drink.

I showed off for her. I will say it plainly because I have spent too long not saying it.

I would have her watch me move through the camp and I would let the little gift loose on purpose — the thing Amma had told me to keep small. I would tell her, before the rider crested the dune, that someone was coming, and how many, and whether they were kin. I would tell her which of the men had quarrelled by the way they stood at the fire not looking at each other. I would tell her a woman was carrying before the woman knew it, and watch Sabra's face go still in the particular way that meant I had landed something.

I thought I was dazzling her. I was nine and certain, and certainty has always been the bright sharp edge I cut myself on.

What I was doing — I see it now, I have had a long time to see it — was handing the Salthold its proof. Every clever thing I did to make one lonely girl love me was another line in the report she had been sent to write. I was the rumour. And I was performing the rumour, beautifully, for the very person sent to confirm it, because I wanted her to look at me and could not stop.

She knew it. Of course she knew it. She told me, that night years later in the dark, that every time I showed off she felt two things at once and could not tell which was worse — wonder, and the cold settling weight of a thing she would have to put in writing.

"I should have left," she said. "After the third day. I had more than enough by the third day."

"Why didn't you?"

She was a long time answering, and when she did her voice had gone somewhere I had not heard it go before.

"Because you looked at me," she said, "like I was a person and not a knife. And I had forgotten I could be looked at that way. And I was a coward, and I stayed to keep being looked at, and it cost everything. So." A breath. "We were the same, you and I. We both stayed for the wrong reason. We both got someone killed for it."

I did not say anything to that. There was nothing to say. We were in the dark and the dark was kind, and we let it be.

---

But that was years off, and the girl in the story did not know any of it. The girl in the story had a new and wonderful person at the fire and a gift she was learning to spend like coin, and a whole season of light ahead of her before the dark came down.

I am going to let her have it a while longer.

She deserved it. Whatever else is true — and a great deal else is true, and worse — that child sitting in the morning shade watching a stranger's guarded hands, hungry to be seen and not yet knowing what it costs to be seen by the wrong eyes, that child had done nothing yet.

That came later. I will get there.

I told you I would tell it true.
