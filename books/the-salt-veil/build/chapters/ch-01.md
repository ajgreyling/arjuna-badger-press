# Chapter 1 — The Caravan

Begin with the caravan, then, and with the morning.

I have given you a drowning. That was a hard way in, and I am sorry for it, but a story has to start where its roots are, and the roots of mine were already in the salt before I was born. Now I will give you something kinder. I will give you the smell of beasts at dawn, and the sound of a woman singing low to settle them, and a child who did not yet know she was a danger to anyone.

That child was me. I want you to like her. I find that I still do.

We woke in the dark, always. You learn to think it strange that other people sleep through the cool hours and labor at noon, as the canyon folk do, baking themselves stupid for the sake of the sun. The Attuin keep a sane clock. We rose when the stars were still out and the sand had given back its day's heat, and we made our miles before the white eye opened over the dunes. By the time the heat came down like a hand, we were tented and dozing, and the work of the world could wait.

I loved the dark hours. I think I love them still.

There were forty of us, more or less, and a string of beasts I will not pretend to count for you now. The salt-singers say a caravan is a kind of animal, one body of many legs, and that is true the way the best lies are true. We moved as one thing. We grieved as one thing. When a child was born we all of us went a little drunk on it, and when an old man was returned to the pans we all of us went quiet for three days, which is the proper length of mourning and not a day more.

I was loved. I want that set down plainly, because much of what comes after is dark, and you may forget it. I was a child of the long water-line, kin to Tamar, and I was fed and teased and carried and scolded the way every child among us was fed and teased and carried and scolded. No one starved me of warmth. If I tell you I was apart, I do not mean I was unloved.

I mean that I noticed things, and being noticed for noticing is not the same as belonging.

Here is the morning I keep.

We had come off the high dunes onto a flat where the sand went pale and crusted, not true pan but the edge of it, the place where the Deep starts to remember it was a sea. Amma Reshi walked at the head with her stick, though she did not need the stick and we all knew it. She carried it the way a queen carries a thing that means *I could strike you and choose not to.* She was old past counting and warm as a banked fire, and she had taught me my first songs, and she was the nearest thing I had to a grandmother, which is to say she was my grandmother in every way that the salt cares about.

A man named Dav was leading the second string that morning. He was a good man, slow and laughing, and he had a wife heavy with their first child, and he was happy in the careless way of a man who has not yet been given anything large enough to lose.

He stopped his beasts to drink, and that was when I felt it.

I do not know how to give you what it was like, except to say that I have spent my whole life trying to learn words for it and the words are always too clean. It was not a voice. Nothing told me anything. It was more that the morning arranged itself in front of me and I saw the shape it was about to take, the way you see a cup is going to fall before it falls, in the half-breath while it is still on the table.

I saw the near beast set its foot. I saw the place its foot would go, where the crust dimmed in a way the rest did not, a softness, a thing the eye should not have been able to read and mine read anyway. I saw the leg go through to the brine-rot underneath, and the beast scream, and Dav under it with his hip wrong, and his wife's face. All of it, in a row, in no time at all.

I said, "Dav. Not there. Take it left."

I was nine years old. I had a child's high voice and a child's certainty, which is a different and more dangerous thing than the grown kind. He looked at me the way you look at a child who has said something out of turn, half a smile in it. But he was a good man, and a careful one with a wife to come home to, and he took the beast left.

The crust opened where the beast had been standing. A long soft sigh of it, and a smell came up like the underside of the world, and the hole was wide enough to break a leg in and deep enough to do worse.

There was a moment. There is always a moment.

Then Dav laughed, because that is what good men do when the world nearly takes them and does not, and he ruffled my hair with his big hand and called me *little sand-cat* and said I had eyes like a hawk's. The others took it up. *Eyes like a hawk.* Someone said my mother's line always had the sharp eyes. Someone else said it was a good thing one of us was watching where the rest of us were dreaming. They were warm about it. They were proud of me, the way you are proud of a clever child, and they folded the thing into the morning and walked on.

That is how I learned to fold it in, too. I have wondered since whether I might have been a different woman if even one of them had looked at me with fear instead of fondness that morning. But fear is a thing you teach a child by being afraid, and they were not afraid. They had no reason to be. A girl with sharp eyes is a blessing on the road.

I told you I would show you the seams, and I will not always point at them, but I will point at this one, because it is the first.

I have called this morning a memory of belonging. I have told it to you as the day the caravan was proud of me and held me close, and that is how I have held it for thirty years, turning it over in the dark like a smooth stone. *They loved me. They called me sand-cat. I was theirs.*

Read it again and you will see what I did not let myself see for a very long time. They were proud of me the way you are proud of a good dog that smells the snake. They put it in a story — *eyes like a hawk, the sharp-eyed line* — and a story is a box you put a strange thing in so you can stop looking at it. They were not holding me close. They were finding a shape for me that they could keep walking beside. And the difference between those two things is the whole of my life.

But I did not see that then. Then I walked on glowing, my chest full of being seen, and I would have done it again ten times before noon for the same look on Dav's face.

Amma Reshi did not put me in the story.

She had stopped. The whole head of the caravan had moved on, and she stood at the lip of the hole that had nearly eaten a man, and she was looking at it, and then she was looking at me. Not with fondness. Not with fear, exactly. With something older and quieter than either, a stillness in her face that I had no name for at nine and have too many names for now.

I went to her because I went to her about everything. I expected to be praised. I was a child, and praise had just been raining on me, and I wanted it from the one whose praise was worth most.

"Amma," I said. "Did you see? I saw it before it—"

"I know you did, dasha." She said it soft, as she always said it for me, the my-salt word, the vowed word, though she had vowed nothing to me but a lifetime. She crouched, which cost her, and she took my chin in her hand and turned my face up to the light.

"How," she said. Just that. Then, low: "How did you see it."

I did not understand the fear. I shrugged in her hand. "It was soft there. Anyone could see it."

"No," she said. "They could not."

She held my face a moment longer. There was sand in the deep lines around her eyes and the morning was going gold behind her and she smelled of smoke and milk and the bitter root she chewed for her knees. I have kept that, too. I have kept everything about her, which is its own kind of curse.

"When you see things before," she said. "You come to me. Not Dav. Not the others. Me first." A breath. "Will you."

"Why?"

She did not answer for long enough that I felt the question turn heavy in the air. Behind us the caravan was singing the going-on song, the easy one, all those warm voices folding the day's near-death into a tune. She listened to them sing about me, *eyes like a hawk,* and something moved in her old face that I would not understand for years, and that I understand now too well to write down lightly.

"The road is long," she said at last, which was the kind of thing she said when she meant something else entirely. "A thing that helps you in the morning can cost you at night." She looked at me, not away. "And I would rather know what I am walking beside."

Then she stood, with her hand on my shoulder for the standing, and she smiled, and the smile was real, and that is the part that undoes me. The fear and the love in her were not two things. They were one thing, the way they are one thing in anyone who has ever loved someone dangerous. She had buried a girl in the salt once, I learned much later. She knew the shape of what she was looking at. She had known it before I felt the crust go soft.

She took my hand and we walked to catch the singing, and I was happy, and she was not, and only one of us knew why.

We made camp before the heat came down. I slept that day with Amma Reshi's arm over me, as always, and I did not know that she lay awake. I have it from her, what she was thinking, the way you can have a thing from someone who is dead, which is to say I am guessing, and I am old enough to guess well.

She was thinking that she would have to teach me carefully, and slowly, and never let me reach too far. She was thinking she had time. She was wrong about the time. They are always wrong about the time. But that is later, and I promised you a kinder way in, and I have kept the promise as long as I am able.

It was a good morning, and a man lived, and a child was loved and called a hawk, and only the old woman with her arm across me knew that something in the salt had begun to come awake, and was already, even then, listening.
