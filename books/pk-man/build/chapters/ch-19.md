# Chapter 19

Marius had the code for six weeks and he did not enjoy it at all.

He began, as he had promised, by trying to take it away from her. He rewrote the occurrence pass from scratch rather than reading hers, so that any shared assumption would show up as a disagreement. He replaced her random-place control with three different controls, because a single control is a single point of failure: random place; real place but random claim text; and — the one that mattered — real place and real text, but drawn from the letters of *four other people*.

That last one cost him a month and it is the finest thing in the entire report.

He needed a corpus of dated, specific, forward-looking claims written by people who were not Ted Owens, in the same period, in the same idiom, to the same kind of recipient. There is no such corpus. So he built one, out of eleven hundred letters from four archives of what the profession politely calls unsolicited correspondence — the boxes that every physics department and every observatory and every weather bureau accumulates and never throws away.

Two of the four archives were digitised. He read the other two himself, in scans, for five weeks, and he said afterwards that it was the most depressing month of his professional life and that everybody who works in a university should be made to do it once.

"They're all the same letter," he told Aviva. "That's what nobody tells you. Eleven hundred people, forty years, four countries, and it is *the same letter*. There is a diagram. There is a claim that a named professor has stolen or suppressed something. There is a request for a hearing. There is a date, usually. And there is always, always a paragraph about how they are not asking to be believed, only to be recorded."

"So he's typical."

"He is entirely typical," said Marius. "That's my point and it's the point that makes the result survivable. On every surface characteristic — length, tone, capitals, the diagrams, the pleading, the persecution, the request for testing, the money — Owens sits in the middle of that distribution. He is not an outlier as a person. He is a completely standard example of a very large class."

He put up the histogram.

"And here is the class, on the specified-claim measure, with the infinite window and the place held fixed."

Eleven hundred correspondents. The distribution was a fat hump at the low end: most people scored between eight and fourteen per cent, which is what you get from writing down a plausible thing about a place and waiting forever.

"Median twelve," said Marius. "Ninety-fifth percentile is twenty-six. The highest single individual in eleven hundred is a man in Dundee in 1971 who scored thirty-one, and I have read his whole file and he achieved it by writing exclusively about the weather in Dundee, where it does in fact do all of those things, constantly."

"And Owens."

"Forty-seven," said Marius. "On two thousand two hundred claims, which is between six and ninety times more claims than anybody else in the corpus, so it isn't a small-number artefact. It is the opposite of a small-number artefact. He is out past the end of the distribution and he stays there when I cut the data eleven different ways, and I have cut it eleven different ways, and I would now like to say the thing I have been avoiding saying for six weeks."

Thandi said, "Say it."

"I cannot break this." He said it flatly, without any pleasure at all. "I have genuinely tried. I have tried in the way I would try if I were refereeing it and wanted it dead. The controls hold, the pre-registration holds, the post-hoc pass is flagged as post-hoc on every page and it doesn't need to carry the result anyway, and the effect is in the *pre-registered* half. I do not have a mechanism, I do not have anything approaching a mechanism, and I want it minuted that a result without a mechanism is a result with a hole in it. But I cannot make it go away by any legitimate means and I have stopped trying."

There was a silence of some length.

"There's the other thing," said Aviva.

"There's the other thing," Marius agreed, "and I've been dreading you getting to it."

The other thing was the offsets.

Because once you have two hundred and eighty-one claims that failed in their window and occurred later, the obvious and boring reading is that late is just noise — that if you wait long enough anything happens, which is precisely why Aviva had flagged the pass in red.

But the lateness was not noise-shaped either.

Aviva had plotted the delays in week eleven and had put the plot in a drawer for four days before showing anybody, because she did not trust her own reaction to it. A noise-shaped delay distribution is a long smear: mostly short, tailing off, no structure. What she had was a smear with three lumps in it. A pile at under six weeks. A second pile at around thirty-four to thirty-eight months. A third, small and stubborn, at nine to eleven years.

"That's a schedule," said Marius.

"That's what I thought. That's exactly the phrase I wrote in my notebook and then crossed out, because it's a phrase that decides the answer."

"It's still what it is." He looked at the plot for a while. "The August 1974 one is in the middle lump."

"March 1971 to August 1974," said Aviva. "Forty-one months. Just outside."

"And the middle lump is the largest and the tightest, and I would like to know what it means, and I already know that neither of us can tell the difference between *what it means* and *what I would like it to mean*, so I'm going to go and do something else for a week."

He did. He went and gave a lecture in Grahamstown on Southern Ocean cyclogenesis to nineteen undergraduates and said nothing about any of it, and came back on the Monday and said, from the doorway, before he had put his bag down:

"He wasn't predicting."

"No," said Aviva. "I know."

"You've had that for longer than a week."

"I've had it since the notebook."
