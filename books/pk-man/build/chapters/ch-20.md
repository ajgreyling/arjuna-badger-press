# Chapter 20

## 1977

The Bears thing is the stupidest thing he ever does and he knows it while he is doing it, which does not stop him for a second.

It starts because a man on the radio says his name.

Not his name — *the sort of person who*. A four-minute item, in the middle of the afternoon, in which a pleasant broadcaster and a guest have some fun with the cranks who write to the station, and read three letters out, and one of them is Ted's, and they do not name him, and they do the voice.

He is standing in a kitchen in Philadelphia with a cup in his hand when they do the voice.

He puts the cup down and he is fifty-seven years old and he goes and gets the paper and starts a list, and the list is: everything in that city I can reach.

He writes it on the twenty-second of September and he mails nine copies, and it is the longest single claim in the whole of the archive and the least dignified thing he ever puts his name to. It has eleven items on it. It has the weather. It has the airport. It has lights over the lake. It has *disorder among the people*, which by now is his standard formula and which he has stopped thinking about. And item seven, in the middle of a document that also promises electrical disturbance across a metropolitan area, is:

> 7. The football club of that city will not prosper this season and its errors will be remarked upon.

He sits back and reads it over and he knows, he *knows*, standing at that table, that item seven is the thing that will end him. He knows that a man who says he can move a hurricane and a football score in the same paragraph has just handed every laughing pleasant broadcaster in America the only sentence they will ever need.

He mails it anyway. Nine copies. Notarised.

Because — and this is the part he could not explain to Constance in 1985, or to the two men from the university in 1979, or to himself at four in the morning for the remaining ten years of his life — the football is *the only item on the list he can prove.*

The weather is deniable. A city's electrical grid is deniable; grids fail. Disorder is deniable and lights over a lake are the most deniable thing on earth. But a football season has a *number* at the end of it, printed in every newspaper in the country, and if you say in September that a team will fail and it fails, the number is there, and nobody can tell you the number is a coincidence of an ageing distribution network.

He has spent twenty-two years being right about things that leave no mark. Item seven leaves a mark. Item seven is in the paper on Monday mornings, in a column, in figures.

So he does it. All autumn. In among the grid and the airport and the lake, four days a week, he leans on eleven very large young men he has never met, in a stadium six hundred miles away, and what he is doing — he understands this precisely, he has understood it since the room above the bar — is not making them lose.

He is putting his thumb on the error rate.

That is all it ever is. A man at that level misses a thing he makes ninety-four times in a hundred, and Ted cannot make him miss it, cannot reach into a wrist from six hundred miles, cannot do anything of the kind. What he can do is stand in the six, week after week, and make the six slightly wider. Nobody in the stadium feels a thing. Nobody on the field does anything they have not done a hundred times before. It is simply that this season, for this team, the ball goes down slightly more often than it should, and the word the newspapers land on by November is *cursed*, which they mean as a joke.

They lose. It is a bad season. It is remarked upon.

And in December a pleasant broadcaster in that city does another four-minute item, because the letters keep coming, and this time he reads item seven out loud, and reads the date on it, and says — genuinely puzzled, for about two seconds, before the guest laughs — "well, he got that one."

Ted hears it. He has the radio on. He has had the radio on since September.

*He got that one.*

He sits in the kitchen in Philadelphia and it is the closest thing to being believed that he has had in twenty-two years, and it lasts about two seconds, and then the guest laughs and the broadcaster laughs and they move on to a woman who has written in about the Kennedys.

He gets up and turns the radio off.

And here is the thing he does then, alone, in a kitchen, at fifty-seven, which is the single worst thing in the green notebook and which he never tells anybody and which Constance will read in 2036 in a basement in Cape Town:

He does the airport.

Not because of anything. There is no reason. He has done the football and the football has bought him two seconds of a laughing man being puzzled, and there is nothing else left on the list to spend, and the leaning is right there, and he is angry in a way that has no floor under it.

Nobody is killed. He knows how to do it so that nobody is killed, and he does know, and that is not nothing and he takes what credit there is. Two aircraft are held. A ground crew makes a routing error that is investigated for eleven months. Four thousand people spend a night on a floor.

He writes in the green notebook that night, and the entry is nine words long, and it is not in capitals:

*Did the airport. No reason. This is the bottom.*

It is not the bottom. There are ten years left.
