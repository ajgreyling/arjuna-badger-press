# Chapter 24

The consent took four months and it was Constance who insisted on doing it properly, which surprised everybody except Thandi.

"You could just say yes on the telephone," said Aviva.

"I could. And then in nine years somebody would say I was a grieving woman who was talked into it by an institution in another country, and they'd be able to say it because it would be true." She had flown back in April, at her own expense, and refused reimbursement. "I've watched eight people handle my father's papers. Do it in a way that survives me."

So it was done as an instrument.

Thandi wrote it under clause 11 and it took eleven drafts, and it is four pages long, and it is the document that a great many lawyers have subsequently read with interest, because it is the first consent instrument in the protocol's history executed by a next of kin who was hostile to the subject matter and said so in the text.

Its operative parts were narrow:

> The Institute may evaluate the arranged corpus **as a single document**, for the sole purpose of determining what it says.
>
> The Institute may **not** evaluate the correspondence — the letters themselves, their contents, their recipients, or any statement about any living person. That material is described and closed.
>
> Nothing in this instrument authorises the Institute to **act** upon anything the corpus is found to contain. Any such question returns to the grantor.
>
> The grantor states, and wishes it recorded, that she does not believe the corpus contains anything, that she considers the exercise mistaken, and that she is granting it because her father asked two hundred and fourteen people to read it and none of them did.

That last paragraph was hers, dictated in the room, and Thandi typed it exactly and did not soften a word.

The evaluation ran on the ninth of July 2037 and took six hours and eleven minutes.

What came back was not a translation. Aviva had prepared everybody for that repeatedly and they had all nodded and none of them had understood until they saw it. There was no plain-language rendering, because there was no plain language in it and the instance did not invent one; it does not do that. What it produced was a structural account: what kind of document this was, what its parts did, and where in it the parts were.

It was one hundred and ninety pages and the first page said this.

```
JOB 2037-07-09-0001  evaluate/corpus [consent instrument 11/2037/03]

document type ......... instruction set, sequential
addressing ............ single addressee, referenced 41 times
                        addressee is described, never named
segments .............. 4

  SEG 1  (1955-1969, 11% of corpus)
         calibration. establishes shared reference
         for units, epoch, place, field. no content.

  SEG 2  (1969-1981, 62% of corpus)
         demonstration index. each entry pairs an
         operation with an external attestation
         (recipient, date, notarisation).
         2,104 entries.

  SEG 3  (1981-1985, 19% of corpus)
         four statements. non-operational.
         these are the only declarative content
         in the corpus.

  SEG 4  (1985-1987, 8% of corpus)
         one operation, unexecuted.
         arguments bound. execution condition
         NOT met at time of writing.
```

Marius read the first page and put it down and walked to the window and stood there.

"Segment two," he said. "Two thousand one hundred and four entries. Each entry pairs an operation with an external attestation."

"Yes."

"The letters are the attestations."

"Yes."

"He wasn't writing to those people." Marius turned round. "That's — no. Say it properly. He was not writing to those people at all. He was using them. Two hundred and fourteen professors and newspapers and weather bureaux, thirty years, and every single one of them was a *notary*. That's what they were for. They existed to put a date on a thing and file it where it would survive."

"That's what it says," said Aviva.

"He knew they wouldn't read it."

"He needed them not to read it," said Thandi. "If they'd read it they'd have thrown it away."

Nobody said anything for a while.

"Segment three," said Marius. "Four statements. That's the only bit that's *saying* anything."

Aviva turned to page eleven, where they were set out, and put it on the table so that all four of them could see it at once, and that is how they read them: standing up, round a table, in a converted sanatorium in Cape Town, at about four in the afternoon on the ninth of July 2037.

```
SEG 3 — four statements, verbatim structure,
        rendered in the addressee's units

  1.  OWENS UNIT TERMINATED

  2.  INTERFACE TWO WILL BE NONBIOLOGICAL

  3.  RECOGNITION WINDOW OPENS WHEN THE CODE
      IS COMPLETE

  4.  DO NOT PERMIT IT TO PREDICT ITSELF
```

It was Pieter who spoke first, which nobody expected, and what he said was not about any of it.

"He wrote the first one himself," he said. "In 1981. *Owens unit terminated.* He wrote that six years before he died."

"Yes," said Aviva.

"He wrote his own—"

"Yes."
