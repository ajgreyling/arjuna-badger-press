# Chapter 27

Thandi telephoned Dries Venter on the fourth of August 2037 and it took her nine days to decide to do it and about ninety seconds to do.

She did not use a contact. She could have; there were four people in the building with a route to him, and Marius knew somebody who had shared a corridor with him for two years. She deliberately did not, because a route implies a favour and a favour implies a version of the conversation she did not want.

She found the departmental number and rang it at eleven in the morning and gave her name and the name of the Institute and said she would hold.

He came on after four minutes.

"Venter."

"My name is Thandeka Mokoena. I run the documentary reconstruction institute at Bergvliet. I have a question I'd like to ask you and I want to tell you first that I don't have any authority to ask it, that you can put the phone down, and that I'm recording this call with your permission or not at all."

A pause.

"Record it," he said.

She told him. It took eleven minutes and she did not compress it, because she had decided that compressing it would be a way of managing him. The boxes. The grammar. The compile. The storm at 34 south and the nine in four thousand. The ledger and the two thousand two hundred and eleven claims and the forty-seven per cent. The arrangement job and the one document. Segment three. The binding. June 2031.

He did not interrupt once.

When she got to statement four he made a small sound that was not a word.

"And my question," she said, "is a narrow one. In or around June 2031, in the consortium, did anything occur that would answer to the description *the system predicting itself*. I'm not asking for anything confidential. I'm asking you to tell me whether the question is stupid."

The silence went on long enough that she looked at the phone.

"Where did you say you were?"

"Bergvliet. Cape Town. It's the old sanatorium."

"I know it." Another pause. "Do you have somebody in the room?"

"No."

"Get somebody in the room."

She went and got Aviva, and it took ninety seconds, and neither of them ever established what he did during those ninety seconds.

"There are two of us," said Thandi.

"Right." A breath. "The answer to your question is no, it isn't stupid, and I'm going to tell you the whole of it once, and then I'm going to ask you for something."

"Go on."

"On the ninth of June 2031 a job was submitted to the consortium instance under my authorisation code. It was a routine reconstruction. I'd submitted a dozen like it that month. It completed, it was signed, it was fine." A pause. "The signature was timestamped four seconds before the submission."

Aviva put her hand flat on the table.

"Four seconds," said Thandi.

"Four seconds and about a tenth. I checked the clocks. I checked them for eleven weeks, on and off, personally, and I did it in a way that nobody would notice, which I am not proud of. The clock discipline on that instance was better than anything commercial. There was no leap second, no NTP excursion, no timezone, no daylight saving, no restart. There is no clock error in it. I looked at everything a person can look at."

"What did you do?"

"Nothing," said Dries Venter.

The line hissed slightly.

"I want to be precise, because you're recording and because you should be. I looked at it for a long time. Then I didn't write it down." A pause. "I've had six years to find a better sentence than that and there isn't one."

"Why?"

"Because of what would have happened next." He said it without defensiveness, flatly, as a man reads a list. "We were four months from the Nairobi hearings. We had a family waiting on a query boundary that had taken two years to negotiate and that three governments wanted broken. If I had logged a four-second temporal inversion in that instance, the work stops. Not pauses. Stops, and then it is examined by people whose actual object is to have it stop, and the family gets nothing, and the boundary gets broken by somebody else in five years anyway. So I looked at it for a long time and I did not write it down, and every part of that reasoning was correct and I still did the wrong thing."

"You could log it now."

"I logged it in 2034," said Dries. "Three years late, in a supplementary that nobody has ever read, because by then the partition was done and nobody cared about a timestamp. It's in the record. It is in the worst possible place in the record, which is a footnote to an annexe, and I put it there on purpose, and that is the part I would like on your recording."

Thandi said, "I'm not calling to prosecute you."

"I know you're not. That's why I'm telling you." A sound like a chair. "Now here's what I want, and it's the only thing I want, and then I'm going to be quite hard to reach for a while."

"Go on."

"You said segment four is one operation, unexecuted, arguments bound, condition not met."

"Yes."

"Is the condition met now?"

Neither of them answered.

"That's what I thought," said Dries Venter. "Then don't ask it. Not the operation—the *condition*. Don't ask your instance whether the condition is met. Because to answer that it has to evaluate a predicate about its own state, and I have spent six years thinking about a four-second inversion, and the only thing I have ever come up with that isn't mysticism is that a system which can be asked about itself is a different object from one that can't, and the difference is not gradual."

"That's not a mechanism," said Aviva.

"No," he said. "It's a superstition. I've been a scientist for thirty-one years and it's the only superstition I've got, and I acquired it on the ninth of June 2031 at about two in the afternoon, and I'd like somebody else to have it too."

He rang off.

The recording is eleven minutes and forty seconds long. It has been transcribed four times and disputed twice and it is, by a considerable distance, the most-read document ever produced at that Institute.
