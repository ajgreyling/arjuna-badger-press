# Chapter 10

## 1971

The thing about the President is that Ted does not much care about the President.

That is what nobody will ever believe, afterwards. They will all decide it was politics, because a man who writes about a President must be doing politics, and Ted will spend years saying *it was not about him, it was never about him*, and the more he says it the more it sounds like the thing a person says.

The President is a *hard target*. That is all. That is the entire reason.

He has been reading in the public library four days a week for eighteen months, which is where a man goes when he has no job and no money and a head full of something, and he has worked out a thing he cannot say to anyone because he does not have the words for it and because the two men he tried it on both changed the subject.

The thing is this. Some pushes are cheap and some are expensive.

Weather is cheap. Weather is the cheapest thing there is, and he understood that early and it made him lazy for a decade. A storm is a hundred million tons of air that has *already decided* to do something; all you do is put a thumb on which of four things it does, and the four things were all going to happen to somebody anyway. Nobody ever believes weather. Weather is free and worthless.

A bridge is more expensive. A bridge is a thing that is going to fail on a schedule and you can move the schedule, but you can only move it so far, and it costs something to move it, and he has felt what it costs.

And then there are the things that are not cheap at all, and he has worked out why, sitting at a table in the public library with a newspaper, and it is this: **the more people are looking at a thing, the harder it is to move.**

That is the law. He is certain of it and he has never seen it written anywhere. A car crash on an empty road is nothing. A car crash in front of forty people is heavy. A President is the heaviest object in the country, because a hundred million people are looking at him every day, and every one of them is holding him where he is.

So if you want to know how strong you are, you push the President.

He does not want him harmed. He wants that on the record and he puts it on the record, in the letters, in capitals, and he means it. He is not asking for a death, he is not asking for an accident. He is asking for the *specific* thing, which is: this man will not finish. He will leave the office before the end and he will leave it in disgrace and it will be his own doing.

He writes it on the ninth of March 1971 to eleven people.

He gets it notarised, which costs him three dollars and forty cents that he does not have and which he has budgeted for since January.

He types eleven copies, badly, on the machine with the high *e*, and he numbers them, and against each number he writes the name of the recipient, and he keeps the carbons.

Two of the eleven reply.

One is a form letter. The other is three lines from a man at a university who has replied to him before, who says, more or less, *received, filed, thank you, please stop sending things to my home address.*

He reads the second one standing at the mailbox.

And here is what happens in that world, the one he is standing in at the mailbox on the twenty-third of March 1971 with his thumb on a three-line letter: **someone answers properly.**

Not the form letter. Not the man who wants him to stop using the home address. A fourth name on the list — a man Ted has read four papers by and has never met and never will — reads the ninth-of-March letter twice, in his office, on a Wednesday, and does not throw it away.

He does not believe it. He is not a fool. He writes back on department paper and what he says is: *I do not accept your explanation and I do not expect this to occur. But you have written it down and had it witnessed, and you have asked for nothing, and in four years I will look, and if you are right I will say publicly that you were right.*

And in that world, in 1975, he does.

It is small. It is one man, in one letter to one journal, three paragraphs, and he is careful and he is ridiculed for it and it damages him. But it exists, and once it exists it cannot be made not to exist, and in 1976 two graduate students come to a rooming house in Philadelphia with a tape recorder and a list of questions, and by 1979 there is a protocol, and by 1981 there is a small ugly building in Nevada with Ted in it four days a week, being *tested*, badly and stupidly and with the wrong instruments and by people who mostly want him to fail —

— and he is fifty-one years old and he is drinking one beer a day and not four, and he dies in 1996 of the thing that was going to get him anyway, and there are eleven pages about him in a textbook, and they are wrong in almost every particular, and they exist.

That world is right there. It is a hair's breadth away. It costs one man reading one letter twice on one Wednesday afternoon and not throwing it away.

Ted stands at the mailbox with the three-line letter in his hand.

He can feel it the way he felt the field. It is *there*, it is available, it is the cheapest thing in the sky — and it is not his to take, because a man reading a letter on a Wednesday is not weather and is not a bridge. It is one human being deciding one thing, in a room, alone, with nobody watching him at all.

And the law is that the fewer people are looking, the easier it is to move.

He stands at the mailbox for a long time.

He does not do it.

He has never been able to explain, afterwards, to himself, at four in the morning, in any of the years that follow, exactly why. It is not decency; he will do worse things than that to strangers within three years and sleep afterwards. It is not that he does not want it, because he wants it so badly that his hands are shaking.

The nearest he ever gets is this, and he never says it out loud to anybody:

*If I make him believe me, then I have not been believed.*

He puts the letter in his pocket and walks back up the path and puts the carbons in the box with the others, and in August of 1974 he watches a man resign on a television in a bar, and every single person in the bar is looking at the screen, and he is the only one looking at the room.
