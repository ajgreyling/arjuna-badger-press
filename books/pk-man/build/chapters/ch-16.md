# Chapter 16

Constance Owens-Hale came in November and stayed nineteen days, which was seventeen more than anyone had planned for.

She was not what Aviva had braced for. Aviva had braced for grief with a lid on it. What arrived was a retired hospital administrator with a folding trolley, reading glasses on a chain, and a list.

"I'd like to see it in the order it was acquired," she said on the first morning, "not in the order you'd like to show it to me. I've been shown things in order before."

They gave her the basement, a table, a chair, and Pieter, who turned out to be exactly the right person because he had no opinions about her father whatsoever and answered every question with a number.

She read for nineteen days. Six hours a day, with an hour off at one o'clock, during which she walked to the gate and back regardless of weather.

On the fourth day she said, without looking up: "He typed better than this in 1969. This is 1974 and it's worse. Was he drinking?"

"We don't know," said Pieter.

"I do," she said, and went on reading.

On the ninth day she asked for the coloured-pencil charts and was given eleven boxes of them, and she went through the whole of the winter of 1972 in a day and a half, sheet by sheet, and did not say one word about it to anybody, then or ever.

On the eleventh day she asked to see Marius's report, and read it twice, and then asked him to come and explain the four thousand runs, which he did, badly at first and then well, for two hours, on a whiteboard, to an audience of one.

At the end of it she said: "Nine out of four thousand."

"Yes."

"And if my father had never existed and somebody had simply pointed at that patch of sea on the twelfth of April and said *watch this*, you'd say the same thing."

"I'd say exactly the same thing. The pointing is the whole of it. The storm is ordinary."

"Thank you," said Constance. "That's the first time in fifty years anybody has explained one of these to me without wanting something."

On the fourteenth day she came up from the basement at four in the afternoon and asked Thandi for twenty minutes.

"I'm not going to tell you to stop," she said.

Thandi said nothing.

"I want to be extremely clear about why, because I don't want you feeling good about it." Constance sat down and put her hands on the arms of the chair. "I've spent fifty years managing this. Do you know what it consists of? It consists of being telephoned. It consists of a man in 1990 who wanted the papers and cried when I said no. It consists of a film in 2004 that I did not authorise and could not stop, in which an actor with my father's face stands in a field and makes lightning with his hands, and my son saw it at university before I could tell him it was coming. It consists of my mother's last four years."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't. I'm making a point." She adjusted the glasses. "Every one of the eight was in love with him. Not with him. With the *idea*. The wronged genius. The man they wouldn't listen to. And every single one of them, without exception, needed him to have been right, because if he was wrong then he was just a sick man who frightened his family, and there's no film in that."

"And us?"

"You," said Constance, "have a physicist who spent five weeks trying to prove nothing happened, and wrote down his thresholds first, and told me to my face that the storm was ordinary. You have an archivist who has apologised to me twice for buying the boxes. And you have written to me *before* publishing, which not one of the eight did." She paused. "You are the first people who have ever tried to make him *less* interesting, and you are the only ones who have ever found anything."

"That's not why we—"

"I know it isn't. That's why it works." She looked at the window. "So no. I'm not telling you to stop, and I want it recorded that my reason is not that I've changed my mind about my father. I haven't. Not one inch."

Thandi waited.

"He was ill," said Constance. "I watched it. You have eleven thousand pages and I had a kitchen. He sat at that table for four months in 1972 and my mother went to work at a laundry and I learned to walk quietly in my own house, and whatever your machine says about the sixteenth of April, it does not touch one hour of that. A man can be right about a storm and still have done that to a six-year-old, and the two facts do not weigh against each other, and the reason I've spent fifty years saying *he was ill* is that it is the only sentence that holds both."

She stood up.

"Publish it. Publish all of it, including the parts that make him look like what he was. If you leave out the 1985 letters to make him more sympathetic I will come back here and I will be extremely unpleasant."

"We won't leave them out."

"Then we understand each other." At the door she stopped. "One condition, and it isn't a condition really, because you'd have to be a monster to refuse."

"Say it."

"When you get to the end," said Constance, "whatever the end is, you tell me before you tell anybody else. Not because I'll believe it. I want to be the first person who doesn't."
