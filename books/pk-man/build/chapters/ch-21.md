# Chapter 21

It was Cleveland that changed Marius, not the statistics, and it took him a weekend.

He had come round to the offsets by December and had accepted, in the flat and unhappy way he accepted things, that the ledger was solid. But he had continued to say, in every meeting, in writing, twice in the draft report, that a result without a mechanism is a curiosity, and that curiosities are where careers go to die.

“I have a man who wrote things down and then they happened,” he said. “I do not have a *how*. And if there is no how, then the most probable explanation is that there is a how I am too stupid to see, and it is boring, and it is probably about how the archives were assembled.”

“You’ve tested that.”

“I’ve tested it four ways and it holds and I still believe it. That is what a mechanism-shaped hole does to a person.”

Then on the second weekend of January he read the Cleveland file, properly, all of it, for the first time. Not the letters, which he had read months earlier—the newspapers. Aviva had digitised eleven weeks of three Ohio papers and he was avoiding writing a discussion section.

He came in on the Monday and he had not shaved.

“I want to show you something and I want to say first that it is not evidence.”

“All right.”

“It is not evidence, it is not in the report, it will not be in the report, and I would fail a student who put it in a report.” He put the papers up. “But I have been looking for a mechanism for four months in entirely the wrong place, and I want to describe what I think we have been doing wrong.”

He had eleven weeks of Ohio local news on the screen.

“I have been looking at the disaster,” he said. “Everybody has always looked at the disaster. The heat, the grid, the nineteen hours of no lights. And the disaster is completely explicable: it’s an old network in a hot October and it fails, and the engineering inquiry is right about every word of it, and that’s why nobody has ever got anywhere with this file.”

“And?”

“And I stopped reading the front page.” He scrolled. “Look at page eleven. Look at page eleven of every one of these, for eleven weeks.”

They looked at page eleven.

A man drove a truck into a line and told the inquiry he did not know why he had turned left. Two employees of eleven years’ standing had a fight in a parking lot about a radio and one of them lost a job. A dog got into a stairwell at a hospital and set off a chain of events that made the paper for three days. A clerk transposed two digits on a work order and a substation inspection did not happen. A woman missed a train she had caught for nine years. Four separate people in three weeks reported that they had *come to* somewhere and could not account for the previous few minutes, and the paper made a joke item of it in November under the heading `THE OCTOBER FOG`.

“Now,” said Marius. “Any city, any eleven weeks, will give you a page eleven. People are idiots constantly. That is the null hypothesis and it is a strong one.”

“So?”

“So I counted.” He put up the second screen. “I have counted the page-eleven items in that city for the eleven weeks either side, and for the same eleven weeks in the four preceding years and the four following, and I have counted them in two comparable cities in the same period, and I did it blind. I had Pieter strip the dates and shuffle them and I scored them without knowing which was which, and it took him two days and he enjoyed it enormously.”

“And?”

“There is a bump,” said Marius. “There is a bump and it is not small and it starts about nine days before the heat and it decays for a fortnight after the lights come back.”

Nobody said anything.

“Now hear the rest, because this is the part that matters and the part I would put in a report if I could find a way.” He turned round. “Nine days *before*. The stupidity precedes the disaster. It is not a consequence of it. People are not being idiots because they are hot and frightened, which was my first thought, and it is ruled out by the timing.”

Thandi said, slowly: “You’ve found a cost.”

“I’ve found something that behaves like a cost.” He would not go further than that, then or ever. “If you wanted to push a city—if there were any such operation, which I am not asserting—you could not push the city. A city is not a thing. You would have to push four hundred thousand separate objects a small amount each, and what four hundred thousand human beings do when you push them like that is *make mistakes*.”

“You’d see it in the mistakes.”

“You would see it *only* in the mistakes,” said Marius, “because everything else is deniable. The transformer was old. The heat was heat. Every single account of that October is true, and the mechanism, if there is a mechanism, is not in any of them. It’s on page eleven. It’s a man who does not know why he turned left.”

He sat down.

“I have run it on eleven of the other specified claims,” he said. “It’s there in seven. It is not there in four and I have no explanation for the four and I have written that down. And I want to be extremely clear, because I have been the sceptic in this building for nine months and I am not resigning the post: **this is post-hoc, it was found by looking, the effect is small, and the base rate of human stupidity is enormous and poorly characterised.** If I saw this in someone else’s paper I would ask for the pre-registration and there isn’t one.”

“Then what is it?”

“It’s a place to look,” said Marius. “It’s the first place to look that isn’t the disaster. Sixty years everybody has been checking whether the storm was a coincidence, and the answer to that will always be *maybe*, forever, because that’s what a storm is.” He rubbed his face. “Nobody ever counted the dogs in the stairwells.”
