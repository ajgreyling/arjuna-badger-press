# Chapter 3

Thandi Mokoena killed the job on Wednesday morning, before nine, from her phone, in a queue at the licensing department.

She did it in four taps and did not feel conflicted about it. The Institute's whole reason for existing after the partition was that somebody had to hold custody locally and hold it properly, and holding it properly meant that the interesting jobs were the ones you stopped.

By the time she got to the office Aviva was already sitting in the chair outside it.

"You killed 0417."

"I killed the follow-on. The provenance job ran."

"It ran on two hundred sheets out of forty thousand."

"Then submit the rest as provenance and it'll run on forty thousand." Thandi put her bag down. "You didn't submit provenance. You submitted `structure/read`."

"I submitted it as a scoping job."

"Aviva."

"It's forty kilograms of paper and I need to know whether the non-prose content is one system or sixty. If it's sixty I describe it as marginalia and shelve it and that's eleven years of my life I don't spend. That is a provenance question."

"It is a content question wearing a provenance coat," Thandi said, "and you know that, because you wrote the coat."

She said it without heat. That was the thing about Thandi that people took a while to read correctly: she was not annoyed, and the absence of annoyance was not a technique. She had inherited the Witness Protocol as a set of procedures rather than as an argument she had been in, as you inherit a house rather than build one, and the effect was that she applied it more strictly than the people who had written it, and with less anguish. They had bled over the clauses. She just ran them.

"Talk me through the consent," she said.

"There is none."

"Who is the subject?"

"Theodore Owens, American, 1920 to 1987."

"Dead."

"Very."

"Who is the complainant?"

"There isn't one."

"Who consented to the ingestion of his private correspondence?"

"Nobody. He's been dead forty-nine years and the paper was bought at an estate sale by somebody who bought it for the maps."

"That was you."

"That was me," Aviva said. "I'm not pretending it was somebody better."

Thandi sat down. Through the window the wind was doing the thing it did in March, coming over the shoulder of the mountain and hitting the old sanatorium buildings side-on, so that the whole east wing made a low sound that new staff always asked about once.

"Here's my problem," she said. "The protocol has a provision for the living and a provision for the consented dead, and it has a provision for a complainant who wants a specific question answered inside a specific boundary. It has no provision at all for a dead man whose mail somebody bought by accident. There is no boundary because there is nobody to draw one. There is no question because nobody asked one. What you have is a stack of private letters and a machine, and the only reason to put the first into the second is that you're curious."

"Yes," said Aviva.

"Thank you for not arguing."

"I'm going to argue in a minute. I wanted you to have that first."

Thandi almost smiled. "Go on, then."

"Provenance is not content." Aviva had been assembling this in the car. "I am not asking what the diagrams *mean*. I am asking whether they are the same kind of object as each other. That is a physical question about the marks on a page. It is the same question as *is this ink from one pen or four*, and you let me ask that this morning."

"It is not the same question."

"It is exactly the same question at one level of abstraction up, and every description standard we work to says so."

"And when the answer comes back," Thandi said, "and it says *yes, one system, sixty-one sheets in two hundred*, what do you do on Thursday?"

Aviva did not answer immediately, which Thandi respected more than any answer.

"I don't know," she said. "I'd want to know what the system was."

"There it is."

"I'm not going to pretend I wouldn't."

Thandi looked at the desk for a while.

The honest position, which she did not say out loud, was that the protocol was not written for this and never would be. It had been built for a specific and terrible kind of case: a family, a disappearance, a boundary drawn around three questions and defended with everything the consortium had. Every clause in it assumed somebody in the room was owed something. It had been designed to be a wall around grief, and grief has an address.

A dead crank has no address. Nobody was owed anything here and nobody was at risk and the whole apparatus simply had nothing to grip.

Which meant the decision was hers, personally, without cover, which was the part of the job she had been doing for four years and had not once enjoyed.

"Narrow permission," she said at last. "Structure only. Are the marks one system or many. You get a boolean and a count and nothing else—no semantics, no translation, no attempt at content. Written into the job as a hard scope, not a note. And it runs at the back of the queue behind everything that has a client."

"That's a fortnight."

"That's a fortnight."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me," Thandi said. "I'm granting you a technicality. I want you to know that I know that."

Aviva stopped in the doorway. "Would you rather I hadn't asked?"

"No," said Thandi. "I'd rather you'd asked in 2029."

The job went into the queue on Wednesday at 11:40, position sixty-one, which nobody noticed at the time and which two people would later be unable to stop noticing.
