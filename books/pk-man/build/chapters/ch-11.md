# Chapter 11

Marius Kotze took the job because it was three weeks of contract money and because the brief, as Thandi wrote it, was the most attractive brief he had read in eleven years.

> We have an internal note suggesting a temporal coincidence between a machine process and a weather system. We believe the note is wrong. We are not asking you to evaluate the machine process. We are asking you to characterise the weather system, and specifically to determine whether its development was ordinary. Please assume we want the answer to be yes.

He wrote back the same afternoon: *Delighted. Nothing about 34S 12W is ever going to be extraordinary. Send the buoys.*

He was forty-four, he had spent nineteen years on Southern Ocean cyclogenesis, his funding line had been folded into somebody else’s in March, and he had the specific energy of a man who has been told his subject does not matter and has been handed a chance to demonstrate that it does.

He arrived on the second of June with a laptop, a hard case of instruments he did not need, and no intention of being polite about it.

“Right,” he said, in the seminar room, to the three of them. “Before we start. I want to say what I think, so that you can watch me be wrong at your leisure, or watch me be right, and either way nobody can accuse me of arriving with an opinion I didn’t declare.”

“Please,” said Thandi.

“There is a storm. It is a real storm, I have the buoys, I have four independent instruments, it happened. There is a computer that made some data requests three days earlier. Those two facts are in the same document because a person put them in the same document, and that person” (he glanced at Aviva, without malice) “wrote a careful note saying it was probably nothing, and was right.”

“That’s what I said.”

“It is what you said and I read it four times and it is a good note. Here is what happened. There are, at any moment, between forty and seventy incipient low-pressure features in the South Atlantic basin. Most die. Some don’t. Your machine fetched a global reanalysis field, which contains all of them, including the one at 34 south, and it bound its arguments to a real atmosphere, because a real atmosphere is what it fetched. Three days later one of the seventy did what one of the seventy always does.”

“The fetch was before the system was resolvable,” said Aviva.

“*Resolvable in the buoy record*,” said Marius. “Which is four buoys in an ocean, two of which nobody owns. Resolvable and existent are different words and the gap between them is where I have made my entire career. That system existed on the twelfth. It existed as a vorticity anomaly you could see in a reanalysis field and could not see in a pressure trace, because a pressure trace is a thermometer in a bathtub.”

Thandi said, “So the answer is no.”

“The answer is probably no, and I will not say no until I have done the work, because if I say no now and someone else says yes in eighteen months I will spend the rest of my life on a panel.” He opened the laptop. “I have four hypotheses. I intend to test all four and I intend to publish whichever one survives, and I want it in the contract that you don’t get to stop me.”

“It’s in the contract,” said Thandi.

“Then we’ll get on.”

He wrote them on the whiteboard, and they stayed on the whiteboard for eleven weeks, and the cleaner was instructed not to touch them, and she did not, and by the end all four had been crossed out in different colours because he ran out of the same pen.

> **H1—SELECTION.** The system was already present in the 12 April field. The compile bound to it. No anomaly.
>
> **H2—COVERAGE.** The system is ordinary; it only appears anomalous because 34S 12W is unobserved. Compare against the basin climatology and it vanishes.
>
> **H3—PIPELINE.** The buoy timestamps or the job timestamps are wrong. Somebody’s clock, somebody’s timezone, somebody’s daylight saving.
>
> **H4—RECONSTRUCTION.** The system is not anomalous in existence but in *development*: it deepened along a path that the initial conditions do not support.

“That last one isn’t a null hypothesis,” said Aviva.

“No,” Marius agreed. “That one’s the interesting one and I’ve put it last because I fully expect the first three to kill this before I get there. If I get to four you should all be extremely unhappy and so should I.”

He was at the Institute for eleven weeks. He was rude to Pieter twice and apologised both times. He learned everybody’s coffee order and got them all right. He did not once, in eleven weeks, say anything about Ted Owens, or ask to see a letter, or express curiosity about the contents of the boxes, and when Aviva finally asked him why, in August, he looked at her with genuine surprise.

“Because it would contaminate me,” he said. “I’m the control. You don’t tell the control anything.”

It took him four days to kill H1.

It took him nine days to kill H3. That was the longest of the four and by far the most tedious. He personally reconstructed the clock discipline of a fisheries buoy serviced twice since 2031, and he called it afterwards the single most valuable thing he did there—it was the one that would have been used against them.

H2 took him three weeks and a basin climatology he built himself from forty years of reanalysis, and it did not merely fail. It failed in the wrong direction, and that was the first thing that made him quiet.

On the fourteenth of July he came into Thandi’s office without knocking, which he had never done, and sat down, and said:

“I’m on four.”
