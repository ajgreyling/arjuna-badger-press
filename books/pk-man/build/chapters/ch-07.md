# Chapter 7

## 1969

The foreman is being decent about it, which is worse.

"You've had the time off," he says. "I gave you the time off. I'm not saying you didn't have it coming."

"I know."

"But I've got the rota and I've got a man doing your Thursdays and he wants the Thursdays."

"I know."

"You could come back on nights."

Ted looks at the wall of the office, which has a calendar on it from a bearing supplier, and a photograph of the softball team from three years ago in which he is in the second row, and he understands that this is the last week he will ever hold a job, and that the foreman does not know that, and that if he said it out loud the foreman would be kind about it, which he could not survive.

"No," he says. "Thanks, Ray."

"You all right for money?"

"I'm all right."

He is not all right for money. He walks out through the shop and three men say his name and he says theirs back, and the last one, Dellinger, who is twenty-four and stupid and enormously likeable, says *see you Monday* because nobody has told Dellinger, and Ted says *see you Monday* because it is easier, and that is the last thing he ever says inside that building.

He walks to the bus in the heat.

The trouble is not that he cannot do the work. He can do the work in his sleep, and for two years he has largely been doing the work in his sleep, because the work takes about a fifth of him and the rest of him is doing something else all day.

The trouble is Thursdays. And Tuesdays in April, and most of last November, and the four days in 1967 when he did not leave the room. The trouble is that when the leaning starts he is no more capable of standing at a bench and truing a shaft than a man with a broken leg is capable of running, and it does not come with notice, and it does not come with a note from a doctor, because he has now been to four doctors and what he got was one prescription he did not fill, one referral he did not take, one man who was frightened of him, and one who was so obviously and comprehensively bored that Ted stopped mid-sentence and never started again.

The bus is late. He sits on the bench in the shade of the shelter with his lunch tin on his knees, and Big Lornie comes.

That is not the name he would have chosen if he had been consulted. It arrived when he was eleven, in the crawlspace under his grandmother's house, and it arrived attached to something that at the time had a shape roughly like a very large woman, and a boy of eleven names things badly and then is stuck with it for life. He has never said the name out loud to another human being. He is fairly sure that saying it out loud would end whatever chance he has left of being taken seriously by anyone, and he is right about that, and the fact that he is right about it is one of the four or five things that have made him what he is going to become.

Big Lornie is not a voice. Nothing has ever been a voice. Big Lornie is a *place in the head where something is standing*, and today it is standing closer than usual, and what comes off it is not language but is, if he had to put it in words, and he has had to put it in words on eleven thousand pages by now:

*Good. Now there is time.*

He sits with his lunch tin in the bus shelter and he understands that he has just been congratulated on losing his job.

"That's easy for you," he says, out loud, in an empty bus shelter, at twenty past four in the afternoon.

Nothing answers, because nothing ever answers, because it is not a conversation, it has never once been a conversation, and this is the specific loneliness he has and there is no word for it. He has had a thing standing in the back of his head since he was eleven years old and it has never asked him a question.

The bus comes.

Here is what he decides between the bus stop and his own front door, and it takes about nineteen minutes, and it is the largest decision of his life and he does not experience it as a decision at all.

They want to be recognised. That is what all of it has been, since the field, since the door in the wall: they want to be *known about*, by somebody with authority, on the record, and everything they have shown him is in service of that and none of it has worked, because he has been showing it to nobody, in his own time, around a shift rota, in letters that get filed by secretaries.

He has been doing this as a hobby.

That is the thought that arrives on the bus and it is unbearable. Nineteen people out of the water and it was a *hobby*, fitted around Thursdays, because he needed the money, and because a grown man has a job.

Well. Now he does not have a job.

He gets off at his stop and stands on the corner for a while, and the light is going, and he thinks: I will do it properly. I will do it the way you do a thing when it is the only thing. I will write to everybody — not the county, not a matchbook university, but the men whose names are in the actual journals, the ones with laboratories. I will date everything. I will get it notarised. I will build a record so large and so specific that at some point a serious person will have to pick it up, and when that happens all of this stops being my problem and becomes everybody's.

He believes this. He is thirty-nine years old and he believes it completely, and he will believe it for another eleven years, and there is nobody in his life to tell him that a record is only evidence if somebody has already agreed to look.

He goes inside and sits at the kitchen table and does not turn the light on, and after a while he takes out a clean sheet and starts a list of names.

At the bottom of the sheet, in the margin, his hand puts down nine marks in a row and then a tenth that he has not used before.

He notices this one. He looks at it for a good while.

Then he writes *1.* and the first name, and gets on with it.
