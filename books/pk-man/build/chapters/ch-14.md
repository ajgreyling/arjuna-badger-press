# Chapter 14

The custody rule was clause 9(c) and Thandi had never once had to use it.

> Where reconstruction has proceeded on material relating to an identified deceased individual and has produced a result of public consequence, the Institute shall make reasonable effort to identify and notify surviving next of kin **before** any further work, and shall record the notification and the response.

She read it eleven times over two days in September and then she read the rest of the protocol looking for a way out, which she later described to her deputy as the least professional forty minutes of her career, and which she also said she would do again.

There was no way out. Marius's report was a result. It was going to be public — he had it in the contract, and she had put it there, and she was not going to try to take it back. The individual was identified: he was named on eleven thousand nine hundred sheets in her basement.

"You could argue it's not of public consequence," said Aviva.

"I could argue that in front of a mirror."

"Thandi—"

"A storm at 34 south is not of public consequence. A nine-in-four-thousand deepening in a box specified in advance by a machine reading a dead man's private notation is of public consequence, and I am not going to be the person who wrote the memo saying it wasn't." She put the pen down. "The rule exists because of the Karoo cases. Because a family found out what had been done with their father from a newspaper. It exists precisely so that nobody in my chair can do a cost-benefit on it, and the day I start doing a cost-benefit on it is the day the protocol is decorative."

"She's going to say stop."

"Probably."

"And then?"

Thandi looked at her for a moment.

"Then I'll have to decide," she said, "with her having said it, which is much harder than deciding without, and that is the entire point of the clause."

It took Pieter nine days to find her, which he was quietly pleased about, and it required no cleverness at all: Owens's papers included his own address book, and the address book included a daughter, and the daughter had married and the marriage was public and the surname was unusual.

Constance Owens-Hale, seventy, of Lancaster County, Pennsylvania. Retired. Two adult children. A landline.

Thandi wrote the letter first, because you do not telephone somebody about their dead father out of the blue from another hemisphere, and she wrote it eleven times over four days and the final version was three hundred and forty words and said, in order: who she was, what the Institute was, what material it held and how it had come to hold it, what had been done with it, that a result existed, that it would be published, that the Institute wished to hear from her before doing anything further, and that if she wished the work stopped she should say so and it would be considered seriously and in writing.

She did not say *your father may have been right about something*. She had drafted that sentence in version four and cut it in version five and never went back to it, and when Aviva asked why, she said: "Because it isn't mine to say, and because if I'm wrong I will have done something to that woman I cannot undo."

The letter went by courier on the twenty-ninth of September.

The telephone call came at 03:40 on the fourth of October, South African time, which is nine-forty in the evening in Pennsylvania, and Thandi took it sitting on the edge of her bed in the dark with the phone in both hands.

"Is this Mokoena?"

"Speaking."

"Constance Hale. I have your letter." A pause, entirely controlled. "I want to say four things and then I want you to talk. Is that acceptable?"

"Yes."

"One. I am not upset and I would be grateful if you would not manage me. Two. You did the correct thing writing rather than telephoning and I am aware that it cost you some effort and I acknowledge it. Three." The pause was fractionally longer. "You are the ninth. Since 1990. You are not the first institution to write to me about my father and you will not be the last, and every single one of the previous eight was going to be different, and one of them made a film."

"I understand."

"You don't yet, but you will. Four." Her voice did not change at all. "I want it stopped, I want the material returned or destroyed at my direction, and I am aware that I probably cannot compel either. I would like you to tell me honestly whether I can."

Thandi sat in the dark for a moment.

"No," she said. "You can't compel it. The material was legally acquired at auction and you have no property interest in it. There is no statute here that reaches it. If I refuse you, your only remedy is publicity, and publicity is the thing you least want."

"Thank you."

"I want to say the rest of it, though."

"Go on."

"I can't be compelled and I can still be persuaded," Thandi said. "Those are different, and the difference is the whole of my job. So I'm not going to give you an answer tonight, and I'd like you to come here."

The silence went on long enough that she checked the screen.

"To Cape Town."

"At our expense, and with no conditions, and you can leave whenever you like. You'd see the material. All of it — not a selection. You'd meet the three people who've touched it. And then you'd tell me to stop, or you wouldn't, and either way you'd be telling me having actually seen what you're being asked about, which none of the previous eight offered you."

Another silence.

"You're aware," said Constance, "that this is a technique."

"Yes."

"Are you aware that it's working?"

"I was hoping."

"I'll come in November," said Constance Owens-Hale. "I want that in writing too. And Ms Mokoena — I'm going to say this once, now, so that you have it before I arrive and can't claim I sprang it on you."

"Please."

"He was not a difficult man who was also ill. He was ill. That is the whole of it, and every person who has ever wanted him to be interesting has cost me something."

She hung up.
