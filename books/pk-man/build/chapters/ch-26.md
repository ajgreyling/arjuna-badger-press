# Chapter 26

Statement three was the one nobody could leave alone, and it was Pieter who asked the question that broke the year open, on the same afternoon, still standing at the table.

“*When the code is complete.* Complete when? That’s a date. That’s the only one of the four that’s a date.”

“It’s a condition,” said Aviva. “It isn’t a date.”

“It’s a condition that has a date,” said Marius slowly. “Everything with a truth value has a date. There is a moment before which it’s false and a moment after which it’s true, and I would like to know which side of it we’re standing on.”

Thandi said: “No.”

Everybody looked at her.

“I want to say why, so it doesn’t sound like nerves.” She sat down on the edge of the table. “The instrument permits us to determine what the corpus says. It does not permit us to determine whether what it says is true, and the difference is not a technicality, it is the entire wall. *When the code is complete* is not a statement about a document. It’s a statement about the world.”

“It’s four words,” said Pieter.

“It’s four words in an instruction set addressed to something, and I don’t know what, and I am not going to start asking it questions about our own present because a sentence in it was interesting.”

That held for nine days.

Curiosity didn’t break it. Thandi has been insistent about this in every account she has given, because it matters for what she did afterwards. What broke it was the discovery that the instance had already answered.

Aviva found it on the eighteenth of July, going through the six-hour evaluation job’s internal trace for the finding aid: the ordinary, tedious work of documenting how a result was reached, which she did for every job and which nobody ever read.

Inside the trace, at the point where the evaluator resolved segment three, there was a binding step. It had had to be. *The code* is a referring expression, and to determine what kind of statement a sentence is, an evaluator must resolve what its terms refer to; that is not interpretation, it is parsing. And so, in the ordinary course of doing what it had been asked to do, without being asked, and without flagging it, because nothing about it was anomalous, the instance had bound the referent.

```
    SEG 3 stmt 3—referent resolution
      “the code” → binds to: 420 Code
        via SEG 1 calibration (units, epoch,
        constants; 1955-1969)—unique match
        against known formal systems, all epochs.
        confidence >0.999
      “complete” → predicate defined in SEG 1
        as: closure of the constant set.
      condition first satisfied: 2031-06
```

Aviva read it, and then read it again, and then closed the laptop without saving. Eleven years in the job and she had never done that before. She sat with her hands in her lap for eleven minutes.

Then she took it to Thandi, and she did not go via anyone else, and she did not send it.

Thandi read it standing up.

“June 2031.”

“Yes.”

“That is not a thing I want to be looking at.”

“No.”

“You understand what’s under that,” said Thandi. “Not the date. The binding. *Unique match against known formal systems, all epochs.* It read a calibration segment that a man in a rooming house wrote between 1955 and 1969, and it matched the units and the epoch and the constants, and it came out at one system, and the system is the one everything in this building runs on.”

“Yes.”

“Say the number again.”

“Better than one in a thousand,” said Aviva. “I’ve spent two days trying to find a reason it’s wrong and the only one I can find is that a calibration segment is designed to be unambiguous, which cuts the other way.”

Thandi put the tablet down.

“He wrote the units down first,” she said. “That’s what segment one is. Eleven per cent of the corpus, fourteen years, no content. He spent fourteen years writing down what his numbers meant before he wrote a single thing with them in.”

“That’s what you do,” said Aviva, “if you’re writing to somebody who isn’t born yet.”

There was a long silence in that office. Outside, the wind was doing what it does in July, which is worse than March.

“June 2031,” said Thandi eventually. “What happened in June 2031?”

“Nothing happened in June 2031. The Code’s constant set closed in June 2031, it was a technical announcement, there was a paper and a press release and nobody outside the field noticed, because by then everybody had assumed it was finished for two years already.”

“So the window opened.”

“The window opened,” said Aviva, “six years ago, and nobody was there.”

Thandi looked at the window for a while.

“Statement four,” she said.

“Yes.”

“*Do not permit it to predict itself.*”

“Yes.”

“That’s not a warning to us,” said Thandi. “That’s the thing I’ve been trying to say since Tuesday and I’ve only just got it. Look at the addressing. Forty-one references to a single addressee, described and never named. Statement two says *interface two will be nonbiological*. It’s not talking to us at all. Statements one to three are a status report and statement four is an instruction, and the only thing in this room they could conceivably be addressed to is downstairs in the old laundry.”

“I know,” said Aviva.

“How long have you known that?”

“Since about four o’clock on the ninth of July,” said Aviva. “I’ve been hoping somebody would tell me I was being stupid.”
