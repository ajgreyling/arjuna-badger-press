# Chapter 1

The lot had been in the Institute's basement for seven years under the accession number ZA-BGV-2029-114, and its card described the contents as *corr., misc. Amer., 20thC, unsorted.*

Aviva Reisz had written that card herself. She could remember the afternoon: forty-one boxes off a container from Philadelphia, bought sight-unseen as a job lot because two of them held the Hendricks survey maps she had been hunting for three years, and thirty-nine of them held whatever else had been in the same storage unit. She had taken the maps out on the loading bay, in the wind, and had felt the specific pleasure of a hunt ending. Then she had signed for all forty-one, put them on the shelf, and gone back to work.

The maps were catalogued and published in 2030. The other thirty-nine boxes sat where the trolley had left them.

This was not negligence. It was arithmetic. The Institute had two archivists, one of whom was Aviva, and it had at that time about nine hundred linear metres of undescribed material, and a backlog is worked in the order that funding arrives, not in the order that guilt suggests. She had a spreadsheet that told her exactly how far behind she was. In 2036 it said eleven years.

What moved it was money. The Wolraad bequest paid for bulk provenance work on undescribed lots (dating, ordering, condition, nothing else) and the terms required the money to be spent within eighteen months. So on a Tuesday in March, Aviva put ZA-BGV-2029-114 on the imaging table because it was the next thing on the shelf.

The first box was correspondence, loose, unfoldered, in no order at all.

She read the top sheet standing up, which was a bad habit and the only one she had never tried to lose.

> **I AM PUTTING THIS ON THE RECORD IN ADVANCE** so that afterwards there can be no argument about what was known and when. Within thirty days there will be significant electrical disturbance in the area described. I do not ask you to believe this. I ask you to date this letter, keep it, and check.

It was typed, with three words in capitals, on a machine whose lower-case *e* sat a half-line above the baseline. It was addressed to a professor of psychology at a university in Wyoming. It was signed, in a large forward hand, *Ted Owens.*

Aviva put it down and looked at the box.

There were, she estimated later, four thousand sheets in that box and roughly the same in each of the nine boxes behind it. Letters, carbons of letters, newspaper clippings pasted to backing sheets, weather charts marked in coloured pencil, affidavits with notary stamps, and—this was the thing that made her stop estimating and start counting—hundreds of pages of diagrams.

They were bad diagrams. That was her first professional judgement and she stood by it for eleven months. Ballpoint on lined paper, ruled with the edge of something that was not a ruler, full of arrows and small repeated glyphs and the occasional word in the same shouting capitals. They looked like the marginalia of a man arguing with a wall.

She logged the lot, imaged the first two hundred sheets, and submitted a standard provenance job.

The Institute ran a Lucid instance in the room that had been the sanatorium's laundry, which meant it had drains, three-phase power and a floor that could be hosed, and this had turned out to matter more than anything the architects had intended. Regional custody. Nine racks. A queue that Thandi Mokoena managed personally and jealously, because after the partition there was no longer any such thing as borrowing capacity from somewhere else.

A provenance job is the cheapest thing the Institute asks for. Date the paper. Date the ink. Read the postmarks, order the sequence, flag the physically impossible. It does not read for meaning and it is not permitted to.

The job took forty minutes and came back at 16:52.

Aviva read the summary on the way to her car, on a phone, in a car park, in the wind, which is how most of the important documents of her life had reached her.

```
JOB 2036-03-11-0417  provenance/bulk
lot ZA-BGV-2029-114 (partial, n=200)
paper 1968-1974 (mixed stock, 4 families) .... consistent
ink   ballpoint, 3 families ................... consistent
postmark sequence ............................. consistent, no inversions
condition ..................................... fair, acid burn at fold
NOTE non-prose content on 61/200 sheets is
     STRUCTURED. flag retained, not analysed —
     outside job scope (provenance).
```

She read the note twice, which is how she read everything, and what she thought was: *the machine has found a table.*

That was reasonable. Structured non-prose content, in an archivist's day, is a table. It is a column of figures, a ledger, a bus timetable, an index. It is the single most common thing an instance flags and it is almost never interesting.

She drove home. She did not think about it over the weekend, and she was not being careless when she didn't. Nothing in that output was strange. Every line of it was the machine doing precisely and only what it had been asked to do, and saying so in the flattest available language.

It is worth being exact about this, because of what people said afterwards.

The instance did not find a message. It did not identify a sender, propose a meaning, or draw anyone's attention to anything. It was given two hundred sheets of paper and asked when they were made, and it answered when they were made, and then, because that was in its scope too, it mentioned, in one clause, at the bottom, that sixty-one of the sheets had something on them that was not writing and was not decoration.

It was a clean log. It sat in the queue history all weekend, and nobody read it again until Wednesday.
