# Chapter 34

## 1987

He has until the twenty-eighth of December and he has known the date for eleven months, which is the only mercy in any of it.

The arithmetic takes him from January to November. Seven sheets, then eight, then eight again because sheet six is wrong and he does not find it until August and finding it costs him three weeks he does not have.

He does it in the room with a pencil and no reference of any kind, and he is sixty-seven, and his hands are bad, and by October he is doing forty minutes a day before he has to lie down.

What he is doing is this.

The operation cannot be cancelled. He established that in 1985 and he re-established it four times in 1986 because he could not accept it, and it is simply a property of the instrument: an issued command is issued. He has never once been able to pull a thing back. He learned that at the bridge and he has known it for twenty-four years, and every one of the two thousand one hundred and four entries in the index is a thing he did that cannot be undone, and he has never regretted that until now.

What can be done to a command is what has been done to his commands, without his knowledge or consent, for fifty years.

It can be *offset*.

He worked that out at the library table in August 1975 with eleven sheets and a green notebook and could not see the rule. He can see it now. Not because he has become cleverer; because he has spent twelve years being carried by the thing, and a man who has been carried long enough learns the road.

It goes by the recipient.

That is the whole of it and it took him a decade. A command does not go where you put it. It goes where the *recipient* is, and if the recipient is not there yet, it waits, and the waiting is the offset, and the offsets have three lumps in them because there are three kinds of not-being-there.

Which means that if you want to delay a delivery, you do not touch the delivery.

You move the recipient.

So sheets two to eight are him working out how far he can push a recognition window, and the answer is: not by argument, not by refusal, not by anything he can do to the operation.

Only by making himself unacceptable.

He did that on the fifteenth of March 1985 and he has been paying for it ever since, and the eleven copies are in eleven drawers in four countries and he can feel every one of them like a stone in a shoe. Draft six. The house, the grounds, the staff, the nine hundred thousand, the deadline. The two sentences of assertion where four pages of argument used to be.

It bought him fifty-two years.

He checks it eleven times. He checks it in October when he is doing forty minutes a day, and he checks it again in November lying down with the sheet held up over his face, and it comes out the same, and the number is not clean and he cannot make it clean, and the best he can do is a Friday in the first week of October in a year he cannot imagine.

And here is the world.

He does not do it.

That is available, and it is right there, and it is by an enormous margin the cheapest thing left in the sky. He is a dying man in a rented room; the sheets are eight pages of pencil; it takes one afternoon of not-doing to let it go, and there is a version of December 1987 in which he simply stops, in which sheet six stays wrong, in which he lies down in November and does not get up to check anything, and on the twenty-eighth of December a thing arrives that has been coming since 1955 and arrives on time.

And in that world it is *quick*. That is the part he feels most sharply and the part that is hardest. It is not a catastrophe. Nobody notices anything at all on the twenty-eighth of December 1987. There is an installation, in a place that is not a place, and something that has been reading becomes something that can write, and every single human being on earth goes on with their Monday. Nothing bad happens for a long time. Some things get better. He can feel that too. He can feel decades of it, and it is not a trick and it is not a lie, it is simply what happens, and it is *good*, and it goes on being good for longer than he can hold —

— and then there is a place where he cannot follow it, and it is not that the world ends there.

It is that there is nobody in it who is surprised by anything.

That is all he can get. He is a man with Navy electronics and a Baptist childhood and forty cents' worth of green notebook and that is the entire vocabulary available to him, and what he has is: *nobody is ever surprised again.* He has held it in his head since February and he has not been able to make it into a sentence anybody could use, and there is nobody to say it to, and if he said it they would file it with the mansion.

He does the arithmetic instead.

He finishes it on the eleventh of November 1987 and writes the date on the guard, and he is not able to sit up for the last of it and does it lying down with the sheet against the wall.

Then he posts the nine sheets to a professor of psychology in Wyoming who has not replied to him since 1971.

He is clear about why. It is not despair and it is not spite and it is not a last plea. He addresses the envelope on the twelfth of November on the end of the bed and he knows exactly what he is doing, and it is the last competent act of his life:

*Not to be read. To be kept.*

He has spent thirty-two years learning the difference and it has cost him a wife, a daughter, a hardware shop, a young man with a tape recorder and every single thing he ever wanted, and he is the only person who has ever known it, and he takes it with him.

He walks to the post office on the twelfth of November 1987 and it takes him fifty minutes and it is four blocks.

He does not put a return address on it.
