# Chapter 32

They had the argument on the twenty-ninth of September, in the seminar room, with the whiteboard still carrying Marius's four crossed-out hypotheses from the previous year, and it lasted five hours.

Thandi opened it by saying what it was.

"This is not a meeting. There's no quorum, there's nothing to vote on and nothing any of you say binds me. I'm going to decide, and I'm going to decide alone, and the only reason you're here is that I would like to have heard you first. If that's unacceptable to anyone, leave now and I'll write down that you left and why."

Nobody left.

"Constance is on the line and has been since ten," she said. "She's not saying anything unless she wants to."

"Correct," said Constance, from Pennsylvania, on speaker.

The case for running it was made by Marius, badly at first, and he said afterwards that he had made it badly on purpose and then stopped doing that about an hour in because it was dishonest.

"Here is what I think," he said, in the second hour. "We have a result. It is the strongest anomalous result I have ever been near and I have spent eighteen months trying to destroy it. And we do not have a mechanism, and without a mechanism this is a curiosity that will be dismissed in eleven minutes by anybody who wants to dismiss it, and it will be dismissed for the same reason everything of his was dismissed for sixty years, which is that a man wrote something down and then a thing happened."

"And segment four gives you a mechanism," said Thandi.

"Segment four gives me the only *prospective* test that will ever exist." He was not shouting; Marius did not shout. "Everything else is retrospective. Every single thing in that ledger is a thing we checked afterwards, and afterwards is where all the sin is. Segment four is bound, it is unexecuted, and it has a guard with a date on it. That is a pre-registered experiment written in 1985 and it is the only one anybody is ever going to get."

"You want to run it to see if he was right."

"I want to run it because if we don't, then in eleven years somebody with much worse instincts than us will, and they will do it without a ledger and without a Marius and without you, and the thing I cannot get past is that *we are the best people this will ever happen to*."

"That," said Constance from Pennsylvania, "is the sentence that every one of the eight said to me."

The room went quiet.

"I'm not being cruel," she said. "It's a good argument. It's the best one there is, and I've heard it eight times, and the reason I'm still saying it is that it was true every time and it did not make any of them right."

Aviva made the case against, which surprised nobody who had been paying attention and everybody who had not.

"I found it," she said. "I bought the boxes, I ran every job, I argued Thandi into four of them, and I have wanted to know what it says since March 2036 more than I have wanted anything in my professional life. So take everything I'm about to say with that on it."

"Noted," said Thandi.

"Segment four is not a message. Segments one to three are the message and we have read them, and we read them under a consent instrument, and that's done and I'd do it again." She had a single sheet of paper and she did not look at it. "Segment four is not addressed to us. There is no reading of that corpus in which segment four is addressed to a human being. It is one operation, with bound arguments, guarded, left for the addressee—and the addressee is described as something that can evaluate a predicate over its own state, and there is one object in this building that answers to that description and it is not any of us."

"So?" said Marius.

"So running it isn't an experiment," said Aviva. "An experiment is something you do *to* a system. This is handing a system an instruction that was written for it, by somebody who knew what it was seventy years before it existed, and then watching. That's not an experiment. There's a word for it and the word is *delivery*."

"You don't know what it does."

"No. And neither do you, and neither does she, and Thandi has made sure of that on purpose, and I think that was the single most professional act anybody has performed in this building." She put the sheet down. "I've been asking myself for two weeks what I'd need to know to say yes. And the answer is: I'd need to know what it does, and the guard exists specifically to stop us knowing that, and I cannot get out of that circle and I've stopped trying."

"That's an argument from a guard," said Marius. "You're taking an instruction from the document."

"Yes," said Aviva. "That's the flaw in it and it's fatal and I know. The only reason I'm giving it any weight at all is Venter, who arrived at the same superstition from the other end, in 2031, from a timestamp, with no idea this corpus existed."

"Two people being frightened isn't evidence."

"No," said Aviva. "It's the whole of what I've got."

At half past four Thandi asked Constance whether she wanted to say anything, and there was a pause on the line long enough that Pieter checked the connection.

"I want to ask one question," said Constance Owens-Hale. "It's for Dr Kotze."

"Go ahead."

"You said we are the best people this will ever happen to." A pause. "Who is *we*? There are four of you in a laundry. Who exactly did you have in mind?"

Marius did not answer.

"That's all," said Constance. "I have no view. I told you in November I don't believe any of it and I still don't, and I'd like that recorded again now."

Thandi closed the meeting at half past five and said she would decide by the first.
