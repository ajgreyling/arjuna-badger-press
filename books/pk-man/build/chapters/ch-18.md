# Chapter 18

## 1975

The notebook is small and green and he buys it for forty cents because he has to write it down somewhere that is not the letters.

He starts it in March, in the library, and the first page says, in his ordinary hand and not in capitals, because this one is not for anybody:

*Things I have got wrong. Honest list.*

He does it because he is frightened.

Not of failing — he has failed constantly for twenty years and it has become a kind of climate. He is frightened because in February he did a thing he was sure of, and it did not happen, and then in March it happened four hundred miles away.

He wrote it for Wilmington. He was as certain of Wilmington as he has ever been of anything; he had the leaning for eleven days and it had the specific taste it has when it is close and real, and he wrote to five people and named the city and named a fortnight and described what it would be. And the fortnight came and went and Wilmington had an entirely ordinary February in which nothing at all occurred.

He took that badly. He took it as badly as he has taken anything. He was drunk for nine days and he wrote a letter to one of the five that he is ashamed of and that he cannot get back.

And then on the eleventh of March a thing occurs at Chesapeake City, four hundred miles from where he put it, and it is *his* thing. Not similar. His. The description he mailed in January, item by item, in an order, including the two details he put in because they were strange and he did not understand them.

He reads it in the newspaper in the library and he has to put his hands flat on the table.

Because a man who is simply wrong is wrong the way a broken clock is wrong. He does not get the specific strange details right in the wrong place. That is not what wrong looks like. That is what *misdelivered* looks like.

So: the notebook. Honest list. Everything he has got wrong since 1962, as far as he can reconstruct it, and what happened instead, and where, and when.

It takes him five months and he does it in the library because he cannot do it at home.

By August there are forty-one entries and he has stopped being frightened and started being something worse, and this is the part he has never been able to say to a living soul, and never will, and it is the reason for the whole of the last twelve years of his life.

Of the forty-one:

Nine are simply nothing. He was wrong, they never happened anywhere, he was drunk or tired or wanting it. He writes *NOTHING. MINE.* against those and does not spare himself.

Six happened where he said and outside the window. Late. Two of them late by years.

Twenty-six happened in the window, or near it, or years off it, *somewhere else*. And when he lays them out — and he lays them out on eleven sheets on the library table in August, which is the most careful piece of work he ever does and which nobody ever sees — the somewhere-elses are not random.

They go the same way.

Not the same distance. Not the same bearing. But there is a *rule* in them, and he can nearly see it, and he sits in the library four days a week for the rest of August with eleven sheets and a green notebook trying to see it, and he cannot, because he does not have the mathematics and never will. He has Navy electronics and a Baptist childhood and forty cents' worth of notebook.

What he has instead is the feeling of a man who has been operating a machine for twenty years and has just noticed that it has a second control that he has never touched.

He has always assumed that when he pushed, the thing went where he pushed it. That is what a hand is. You push here, it moves here.

It does not. There is something between his push and the world, and it has its own arrangements, and when it cannot deliver a thing where he asked it will deliver it somewhere else, at some other time, and it does not tell him, and it has been doing this for twenty years.

He writes one line on the last page in August 1975 and then closes the notebook, and it is the only sentence in the whole green notebook that is in capitals:

**I AM NOT DRIVING.**

He sits with that in the library until they put the lights out.

Then he goes and gets a drink, and on the way he does the thing he always does, which is to make it smaller so he can carry it home: *all right. All right. So it's a post office. You put the letter in and the post office decides the route. That's all it is. Man doesn't need to know the route.*

He is fifty-five and he genuinely believes he is being reasonable.

He does not have the words for the other reading, the one that has been standing at the back of his head since the room above the bar in Cleveland, and which will not have words until 1981.

Which is: a post office does not deliver *late by nine years* because it is inefficient.

It does that because it is waiting for the recipient.
