# Chapter 2

## 1955

The field is wet from the afternoon and Ted is standing in it in his work shoes, which is going to cost him, and he is looking up at a thing that is not a weather balloon.

He has already been through the list. He has been an electronics man since the Navy and he knows what the sky legitimately contains at nine at night in this part of the country, and he has run the list twice because the first time does not count when your heart is doing what his is doing. It is not a balloon. It is not a searchlight on cloud, because there is no cloud. It is not an aircraft, because aircraft do not stop.

It is long, and it is not shining so much as *lit*, the way a cigarette is lit, from within and unevenly, and it is holding a position about the height of a water tower over the tree line, and it has been doing that for four minutes.

Behind him the screen door goes.

"Ted."

"Come out here."

"I'm not coming out there. Come in."

"Nadine. Come out here and look at this."

She comes out. She is barefoot on the step and she has the dishtowel still in her hand, and she looks where he is pointing for what he will later think of as exactly long enough, and she says, "What is it?"

That is the sentence. He will carry it a very long way. Not *what am I looking at*, not *I don't see anything* — *what is it*, in her ordinary voice, with the towel in her hand, because she can see it too and it is a thing in the sky and she wants to know what it is.

"I don't know," he says.

"Well, come in when you know."

She goes in. The door goes. He stands in the wet field until his feet are cold through and the thing is still there and then it is not there, and there is no departure that he can describe, no streak, no dwindling — there is a sky with it in and then a sky without.

He goes in. The kitchen light is on. She has left him a plate.

They stay married.

That is the part that matters and it is not a small part. They stay married for thirty-one more years. She goes back to school in 1961 and teaches for two decades at a school eight minutes from the house. There is a bad year in 1966 that they get through by not discussing it, which is not how anyone would recommend getting through a bad year, but they get through it. There is a kitchen extension in 1971 that he builds himself, badly, and that never stops leaking at one corner, and it becomes a joke that outlives the leak because he fixes it in 1978 and she keeps making the joke anyway. There are two grandchildren. He is at the second one's christening in a suit that does not fit, holding the child wrong, being corrected, laughing.

He does not, in that life, write to a single professor.

The lit thing over the tree line is a story he tells about six times in fifty years, at the kind of party where somebody else has already told a ghost story, and every time he tells it Nadine says *I saw it too*, flatly, from the other side of the room, without turning round, and it always gets a laugh, and it is always true.

He works at the plant until 1979. He is good at it and not remarkable at it. He is on the committee that redesigns the shift rota and it is the achievement of his working life and he is genuinely proud of it, and forty years later there is nobody alive who knows that about him.

He dies in 1994, in a bed, of the thing that was going to get him anyway, with the television on and someone in the room.

That is the world.

That is the world, and Ted is standing in the wet field in 1955 with his feet going cold, and he can feel the whole of it, laid out in front of him like a plate somebody has left, and it is *available*, it is right there, it is the cheapest thing in the sky —

— and something opens the back of his head.

There is no pain and there is no light. There is the specific sensation, which he will spend three decades failing to describe to anybody, of a door being found in a wall he had never thought of as having a wall in it. Something goes in. It is not violent. It is not even fast. It is careful, the way a man is careful taking the back off a radio, and that is the comparison he will reach for every single time and it will make everyone he says it to think less of him.

And then he can see the other way the field can go.

He does not choose it. That is what he will never be able to make anyone understand, in thirty-one years of trying, and it is why he stops trying and starts shouting. He does not weigh two lives and pick the interesting one. He is a man standing in a wet field who has just been shown that a field can go two ways, and the showing is not a menu, it is a *pressure*, and he leans into it the way you lean into a door you have already started opening.

The kitchen light is on. She has left him a plate.

He goes in and eats it and it is good and she is doing the crossword and does not look up, and he sits down opposite her at the table and he is the only person on earth who has ever eaten that meal twice.

"Well?" she says. "What is it?"

He looks at his wife of four years, who in one world says *I saw it too* at parties for fifty years, flatly, without turning round.

"I don't know yet," he says.

She leaves in 1959.
