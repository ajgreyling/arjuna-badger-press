# PALINDROME — Story Bible
## The binding source of truth. Every chapter is measured against this.

A chamber-piece novella. One room, one night, one impossible claim, four men. The engine is
**escalating disclosure** (after *The Man from Earth*): no special effects, no leaving the room;
the spectacle is a premise interrogated by people who have something to lose by believing it. We
are writing the **novelisation first** — the screenplay comes after. (The book is, like its name,
built backward.)

---

## THE PREMISE (stated, never shown)

A dying man gathers the three people who have mattered most across his life and tells them, plainly,
the thing he has never told anyone:

> *"I have lived this life ten times. I die, and I wake in my own past — a child again, in my own
> bed, with everything I knew still in my head — and I live it forward to here, and I die, and it
> begins again. This is the eleventh. And this time I did one thing differently. Which means one of
> you, by the arithmetic of it, should not be alive."*

The film/book never proves it with an effect. It is proven — or not — only through **what he knows
that he could not know**, and the room's growing inability to explain him away. The reader, like the
skeptic whose eyes we are in, assembles belief one disclosure at a time, against their own will.

**The title.** A palindrome reads the same forward and backward. A life lived ten times, each time
returning to its own first page, is a palindrome of a man. And we are telling it backward — the
novel before the screenplay, and (structurally) a story that arrives, at its end, at its beginning.

## THE FOUR MEN (cast-locked)

- **AUGUST RENNER — the claimant.** (Edward Norton register.) Dying — weeks, maybe days. Controlled,
  precise, never theatrical; a man who has had ten lifetimes to learn exactly how to say a thing so
  it cannot be dismissed. The horror of August is that he is *not* mad — he is the calmest man in
  the room. You never catch him in the lie because there may be no lie. Was, in this life, an
  actuary / a man who calculates risk and death for a living — fitting for someone who has counted
  his own deaths.

- **DR. SOLOMON VOSS — the skeptic. OUR POV.** (John Malkovich register.) The sharpest mind August
  could have chosen — a clinical psychologist, late career, who has spent forty years taking apart
  delusion, confession, and the architecture of a lie. He came to comfort a dying friend and to,
  quietly, *diagnose* him. The novel is Voss failing — watching every tool he owns slide off the
  claim. His worldview is what the premise costs: if August is telling the truth, then everything
  Voss knows about the mind, about time, about what a person *is*, is wrong. He has the most to lose
  by believing, and we are inside him as he loses it. Urbane, courteous, his dread sounds like good
  manners.

- **EARL CADE — the quiet one.** (Billy Bob Thornton register.) Says least, sees most. An old friend
  from August's roughest years; a man who has done hard things and doesn't flinch at hard ideas.
  Earl believes August *early* — long before Voss — and the most frightening beat in the book is when
  Earl, who has barely spoken, says the thing that turns the room cold. He is not afraid of the
  claim. He is afraid of the *correction* — of which one of them shouldn't be here.

- **DANIEL "DANNY" REISS — the man who should not be alive.** (Brad Pitt register — *Meet Joe Black*
  / *Fight Club* stillness; mortality played dead straight, not movie-star.) The youngest, the one
  August loves most, the one whose ordinariness is the wound. Across the ten lives, in every single
  one, Danny died young — an accident August could never prevent no matter how he tried. This life,
  the eleventh, August finally changed the one thing, and Danny lived. So Danny is the proof and the
  cost: he is alive *only* in the life where the loop broke, which means either August is telling the
  truth — or Danny has to ask why this dying man needs so badly to believe he saved him. Danny does
  not want to be a miracle. He wants to be a man whose father-figure simply loved him.

(Names are placeholders the author may re-cast; the *functions* are locked.)

## THE DISCLOSURE STAIRCASE (structure = Q → worse Q → the line)

No plot in the event sense. The architecture is a staircase of escalating claims, each tested by the
room, each answer raising a worse question. Roughly:

1. **The gathering.** Why these three, tonight. The ordinary grief of a deathbed — Voss reading the
   room clinically. August asks them to hear something and not interrupt until he's done.
2. **The claim, flat.** August states it without drama. The room's first move is the obvious one:
   the morphine, the dying brain, kindness. Voss has a name for this; he reaches for it.
3. **The knowing.** August begins to prove it the only way he can — small, exact things about each
   man's past he could not know. The first one is explicable. The third is not.
4. **The history.** What it is like to live a life ten times. The unbearable parts: watching the
   same people die on schedule, the temptation to play god, the things he tried and could never
   change. (This is the *Man from Earth* "you knew Columbus?" tier — wonder turning to dread.)
5. **The correction.** August admits the arithmetic: every loop, Danny died young. This loop, he
   changed it. Which means the natural shape of the world had Danny dead — and someone, somewhere in
   the room's web of lives, is alive in his place, or shouldn't be.
6. **The break.** Earl says the cold thing. Voss's last tool fails. Danny refuses the role of
   miracle. The room turns on the question: if it's true, what is owed? If it's false, what kind of
   cruelty is this to do to the people you love on your way out?
7. **The line.** The ending lands on **one quiet sentence** — not an effect, not a twist — a thing
   August (or the dying-into-sleep last image) says or does that recontextualises the whole night,
   and is *just* enough to tip Voss, and the reader, without ever giving hard proof. Ambiguity held
   to the last frame. (Exact line: TBD in drafting; it must work like the final line of *The
   Prestige* — quiet, total, deniable.)

## RULES OF THE UNCANNY (the contract — break these and it becomes woo)

1. **No effect, ever.** Nothing supernatural is shown. No flashback we can trust as objective, no
   ghost, no glow. The only evidence is **talk** and **what August knows**. The reader's belief is
   the only special effect.
2. **The proof is always deniable.** Every impossible thing August knows has a just-barely-possible
   mundane explanation (he researched them; he guessed; Voss fed it to him). The film never closes
   that door. The dread is in the *accumulation*, not any single proof.
3. **The premise hurts people.** Like the Christian in *Man from Earth*, the claim must cost each
   man something he can't afford — Voss his worldview, Earl his peace, Danny his ordinariness. If a
   disclosure doesn't wound someone in the room, cut it.
4. **August is never mad and never smug.** The instant he seems delusional or manipulative, the
   spell breaks. He is calm, kind, and exact. He is not trying to convince them. He is trying to
   *warn* them — or to be forgiven.
5. **One room, one night.** No leaving. Time is the candle burning down. (A few controlled
   exceptions: a step onto a porch, a hand on a window — the world stays *outside*.)

---

## TONE

Quiet, adult, literary-thriller. Close and cold and humane at once — Voss's clinical eye slowly
fogging with dread. Sentences that reason. The horror is intellectual and then, suddenly, personal.
Think the chamber tension of *Sleuth* and *Twelve Angry Men* with the speculative ache of *The Man
from Earth* and the mortality-stillness of *Meet Joe Black*. No jump-scares. The scariest thing in
the book is an old man being patient.

## SCORE NOTE (for the eventual screenplay)
Hans Zimmer: a single low sustained tone, a clock made of cello, that the audience realises near the
end has been the *same phrase played forward and backward* the whole film — a literal musical
palindrome they only hear resolve on the last line.
