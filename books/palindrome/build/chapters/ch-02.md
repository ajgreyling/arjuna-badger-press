# Two

There is a thing Voss did with patients that he had never told a colleague about, because it could
not be taught and because saying it aloud made it sound like a parlour trick, which it was not. When a
man was about to lie to him, there was a half-second before the lie in which the face went *still* —
not blank, *organised*, the way a stage is still in the dark before the lights come up. Forty years
and he had never once been wrong about that stillness. He looked at August now and the face was not
organising itself. The face was simply waiting, tired, in the grey light, the way his own father's
face had waited at the end, past performance, past wanting anything from the room.

So Voss did the disciplined thing, which was to refuse the trick. He would not ask August something
private. To ask was to play the game on August's board, and the moment he asked, any answer, right
or wrong, became evidence in a court August had built. The clinical move was to decline the test and
name it. *You're inviting me to be amazed,* he would say, gently. *That's the structure of the
delusion. Let's not.*

"All right," Voss said. "Tell me something about myself, then. Since you're offering. Don't make me
ask."

He heard himself say it and understood that he had lost the first exchange already, because curiosity
was a kind of consent and he had just given it.

August didn't smile. That was the thing Voss kept catching on, like a nail in a doorframe — at every
point where a charlatan would have smiled, August didn't. He looked, if anything, sorry.

"Easy ones first," August said, "because the easy ones you'll explain, and you should. You went to
Grah­amstown for the first two years of your degree and you've spent forty years letting people assume
it was Cape Town, because Cape Town is the answer that ends the conversation and Grahamstown is the
answer that starts one you don't want to have. You had a brother. Older. He's the reason you became
what you became and he's the reason you've never once treated a member of your own family, not one, not
even when they begged. And you keep your father's watch in the left drawer of the desk in your study,
not because it runs (it doesn't) but because the one time you wore it, to his funeral, it stopped at
the hour the service started, and you have never been able to decide whether that frightened you or
comforted you, so you keep it in a drawer where you don't have to choose."

The kettle had gone quiet. Voss was aware of the others not looking at him, which was its own kind of
looking.

He made himself breathe and he made himself think, because thinking was the rampart and he would hold
it. *Grahamstown:* findable. A determined man with an afternoon and the right registrar could learn
where Solomon Voss did his first two years. *The brother:* a guess with good odds; men in his
profession were so often the survivors of someone, and August had known him thirty years; across
thirty years a brother surfaces in an unguarded sentence, a Christmas, a silence at the wrong name.
*The watch.* The watch was a colder thing, but it was the kind of detail a man might have let slip,
once, late, over the second bottle, and forgotten he'd let slip; the mind is a sieve for its own
confessions. Every one of them had a door, and Voss walked through each door in turn and came out the
other side still standing. It could be done. A clever, dying man, with thirty years of you in his
memory and nothing left to do with his afternoons but assemble it — it could be done.

"You're doing it right now," August said, not unkindly. "I can see you doing it. Door, door, door.
You're very good at it, Sol. You've had to be." He shifted against the pillow and the wet sound came
up in his chest and he waited it down. "So here's the third one, and the third one doesn't have a
door, and I'm sorry, because you're going to look for one anyway and not finding it is going to be
worse than if I'd just hit you."

"August," Danny said — the first thing Danny had said, low, a hand half-raised. "You don't have to —"

"I do, though." August didn't look away from Voss. "Sol. Eleven days ago you had a dream you haven't
told anyone, because there's no one left to tell it to who'd understand, and because it embarrassed
you, a man your age. You dreamed you were a boy in the Grahamstown house and the phone was ringing in
the hall and you couldn't reach it, the floor had gone long the way floors do in dreams, and you knew
— the way you know things in dreams — that if you could just reach the phone before it stopped, your
brother would be on the other end, and he'd be alive, and the whole of the rest of your life would
turn out to have been the dream. And you woke up with your hand out. In the dark. Reaching for a phone
on a nightstand that's been on the other side of the bed since your wife died." He paused. "You have
not told one living soul. You decided, lying there, that you never would."

The room was very quiet. Outside, the branch.

And Voss reached for the door, because that was what he did, that was the whole of what he was, and his
hand closed on nothing. There was no afternoon in a registrar's office for this. There was no second
bottle. There was no unguarded Christmas. The dream was eleven days old and it lived in one skull and
the skull was his, and he had told it to no one, had *resolved* to tell it to no one, in the specific
privacy of three in the morning, and the resolution itself was part of what August had just said back
to him.

The cold sheet down the back, the heart finding a higher gear; the clinician in him noted it and filed
it, even now, even here. *The response is data. The response is not proof.* There were always
explanations. He simply could not, in this chair, lay his hand on one.

"That's not possible," Voss said, and heard how thin it came out.

"No," August agreed gently. "It isn't. That's rather the point." He let it sit. Then, softer: "I'm
not doing this to win, Sol. I want you to understand that. There's nothing to win. I'm dying whether
you believe me or not. I'm doing it because I need at least one of you to take the next part
seriously, and you're the one the others will follow. If I can move *you*, I can move the room. And I
have to move the room, because the next part isn't about me." His eyes went, at last, to Danny, and
came back. "It's about which of us pays for what I did."

Earl, who had not moved in some minutes, who sat with his forearms on his knees and his hands loose
and his eyes on the cold hearth, said, without looking up, in his flat unhurried way:

"Ask him how he knows what he changed."

It was the first full sentence Earl had offered, and it dropped into the room like a stone into a well,
and Voss felt the temperature of the night change around it, because Earl had not asked *whether*.
Earl had skipped the whole country Voss was still standing in, the country of *is this true*, and had
walked out the far side of it to the only question that mattered if it was — *how do you know which
thread you pulled* — and he had asked it in the tone of a man asking another man to pass the salt.

August looked at Earl for a long moment, and something moved between them that Voss couldn't read, two
men who had known each other in some hard place a long time ago, and August said:

"Because I've watched him die ten times, Earl. You learn the shape of a thing you've watched ten
times."

And Danny Reiss, twenty years younger than every other man in the room and the only one with all his
life still ahead of him — or not, depending on which of them was right — set the pharmacy bag down on
the floor beside his chair, very carefully, as though it might break, and said nothing at all, and
looked at the man who had just said he had buried him ten times with an expression Voss had seen
exactly once before in forty years, on the face of a woman being told the body was her son's: the face
of a person discovering that the worst thing is also, unbearably, a kind of being loved.
