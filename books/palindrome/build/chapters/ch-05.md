# Five

Danny stood up.

He did it quietly, the careful way he did everything, but in that room, at that hour, a man rising to
his feet was an event, and three faces turned to him. He stood with his hands at his sides and he
looked down at August, and when he spoke there was no anger in it at all.

"I'm not going to be that," Danny said. "Whatever it is. I'm not going to sit here and be the reason
one of you —" He stopped. Started again, lower. "You want to know what it's like on this side,
August? You spent the whole afternoon telling us what it's like to live it ten times. Here's what it's
like to be the line you changed. You've just told me, in front of the two people I'd trust with my
life, that I'm walking around on borrowed time that got billed to one of them. That every breath I've
taken since twenty-six is coming out of somebody else's account. That you *love* me —" his voice
caught, held "— you love me so much that you broke the only law there is, and the bill came to this
room, and now I get to spend whatever's left of tonight watching the three of you wonder which one of
you I cost." He shook his head. "That's not a gift. I know you meant it as a gift. It's the heaviest
thing anyone's ever put on me and you did it because you couldn't stand to lose me, and I understand
that, I do, but August — I would rather have died at twenty-six not knowing, than live to eighty
knowing it was somebody in this room."

The silence after that had a different quality. Voss, who measured silences for a living, registered
its texture and did not like it.

"Sit down, Danny," Earl said. Not unkind. "You didn't choose it. A man's not guilty of being loved."

"That's exactly what a man's guilty of," Danny said, but he sat.

And Earl — Earl who had said perhaps two hundred words all afternoon, who had carried the chairs and
poured the water and looked at the cold hearth like there was a fire in it — Earl leaned back at last,
and laced his fingers over his stomach, and said the cold thing, in the flat unhurried voice of a man
who had worked it out a long time ago and had only been waiting for the room to catch up.

"It's me," Earl said.

August closed his eyes.

"You know it's me," Earl went on, to August, gently, the way you'd tell a man a thing he already knew
and had hoped not to have to hear. "You've known since you walked it back. That's why you can't say
the name. It's not that you don't know it. It's that it's mine, and you couldn't get the three of us
in a room and say *Earl, I traded your life for the boy's,* so you said *one of you* and you let me
do the arithmetic myself, because you're a coward about exactly one thing in eleven lifetimes and
it's me." He said it without heat. "We were in the Loubser thing together, August. Nineteen eighty-
one. You know what I did and what I didn't, and you know I should've gone in that river and didn't,
and you've never once asked me how I sleep, which is how I always knew you knew there was something
owed." A long breath. "I've felt it too. Not the way you feel it. But a man knows when he's living
past his stop. I've known for years. I just didn't have the words you've got for it." He looked at
Danny, and the closed door of his face opened, just at the edge, just for the boy. "It's a fair
trade, son. Don't you carry it. An old man who should've drowned in 'eighty-one, for you. That's the
best deal I've been offered in my life. I'd sign it twice."

"Earl —" Danny started.

"No," Voss said.

It came out of him before he had decided to say it, and they all looked at him, and he heard the
clinician's voice and the friend's voice arrive at the same word for once and he let them.

"No. This is —" He stood, himself now, because he could not say it sitting down. "This is the thing I
came here to stop and I have spent all afternoon failing to stop it and I am not going to fail at the
*last* part, which is the part that actually matters. Listen to what's happening in this room. A dying
man has built a story so complete that it has just persuaded a good and decent person that his own
death is *owed* — that he should welcome it, sign it twice, not carry it. Earl. Earl, look at me.
Forty years I have sat across from people who had decided they were the bill that had to be paid. Every
one of them had a story as airtight as this one. Every single one. And the story is always, always the
disease talking, dressed up as arithmetic, and the cruelest thing it ever does is make the person
*grateful* for the sentence. You are not living past your stop. You are a man who survived something in
1981 and has been quietly waiting forty years for permission to be punished for it, and tonight a
person you love has handed you that permission wrapped in a miracle, and I am telling you as the only
one in this room whose entire profession is the difference — *do not take it.*"

The room held. Outside the last of the grey was going to black against the window, and the unnoticed
clock ticked, and August lay against his pillow with his eyes still closed and his chest rising on the
wet, labouring rhythm Voss had clocked the moment he walked in, weeks, perhaps not weeks, and Voss
thought, with the cold clear part of him that never stopped: *there. That's the answer. There was
never going to be a death in this room tonight except the one that was always coming. He's dying. He
will die, maybe tonight, and his beautiful machine will make it look like the price being paid, and
we will all walk out of here believing what we were primed to believe, and that is how it works, that
is how it has always worked, the prophecy that arranges to be fulfilled by the one death that was
inevitable anyway —*

"Sol," August said, without opening his eyes. His voice had gone thin and far. "It's not me. I know
you've just decided it's me. It's the tidy answer and you've earned a tidy answer." The faintest
movement of his mouth. "But I've died ten times and I know exactly what mine feels like coming, and
this isn't it. Mine's a week off yet. Maybe two." The breath. "What's in the room tonight is faster
than me."

And then Earl made a small sound.

It was not a large sound. It was the sound a man makes when something he has carried for a long time
shifts — a short exhalation, almost relief — and Voss turned, already moving, already the doctor and
nothing else, and saw that Earl's flat closed-door face had gone slack on one side, just slightly, the
way a curtain goes slack when the hand lets go of it, and that the loose laced fingers over his stomach
had come unlaced, one hand drifting open, and that the man was looking at the cold hearth still, with
an expression that was not fear and was not surprise but was, terribly, *recognition* — the look of a
man greeting a thing he had been told all afternoon was on its way.
