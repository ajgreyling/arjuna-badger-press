# Four

For a while no one said anything, and the house did its house-sounds, and the grey at the window went
a shade greyer, and Voss understood that the afternoon was actually ending, that there would be a
night after this, that the candle of the light was a real candle and it was burning down.

It was Voss who broke it, and he broke it the only way he had left, which was to go back to work.

"Let me tell you what I think is happening," he said, and he was pleased to hear his voice come level
again, the consulting-room voice, the voice that had walked a thousand frightened people back from
their own ledges. "Because I think I owe you the truth more than I owe you comfort, August, and we've
known each other too long for me to sit here and play the credulous audience you need." He leaned
forward. "A man at the end of his life looks back and sees an accident. Just an accident — Danny's
accident, your illness, the ordinary butchery of chance. And the mind cannot bear an accident. The
mind is a machine for making meaning and it would rather build a cathedral of suffering than stand in
an empty field. So it builds one. *I have lived this ten times. I chose this. There is a system, and
a price, and a balance.* It's not madness, August. It's the opposite of madness. It's the sanest
thing a frightened animal can do — it's *authorship.* You've taken the worst thing, which is that
none of it meant anything, and you've made it mean everything. And the proof —" he lifted a hand
before August could speak "— the proof is that the one thing your system produced, the one
*intervention*, is the thing you wanted most in the world. Danny, alive. Of course the magic saved
Danny. The magic is *for* saving Danny. You didn't discover a loop. You built a machine whose only
output is the boy you couldn't lose."

It was, he thought, the best work he had done in years. It was kind and it was true and it left August
a way to be loved without being believed, which was the most a man could offer another at a deathbed.
He sat back, spent, and waited for it to land.

August listened to all of it with his eyes closed, and when Voss finished he kept them closed a moment
longer, and then he opened them and there was something in them that Voss had not budgeted for, which
was *gratitude.*

"That's beautiful, Sol," he said, and meant it. "That's genuinely beautiful. If I were you I'd believe
it too. I'd hold onto it with both hands." The dry warmth. "There's only the one problem with it, and
it's the dream, and you already know it's the dream, which is why you didn't mention the dream just
now. You built me a lovely cathedral and you walked us all the way through it and you very carefully
went around the one room you can't explain. The boy reaching for the phone in the dark eleven days
ago, that I had no door to. You'd have led with that, Sol, if you could have folded it in. You're too
good not to. You left it out because it doesn't fit, and a man who builds an honest cathedral doesn't
leave the load-bearing stone outside in the yard."

And Voss, who had indeed left it out, who had constructed the entire edifice of *authorship* precisely
so that he would not have to stand in the one room where his own private dream had been read back to
him by a dying man — Voss felt the cathedral he had just built go quietly transparent, and through its
walls he could see the thing he had walked around, sitting in the yard exactly where August said it
was.

"The morphine could —" he began.

"It could," August agreed. "Tell me the dose. You don't know the dose. You assumed the dose, just
now, because the dose is a door and you wanted a door. I'm on less than you think. Earl can show you
the chart." He didn't press it. He never pressed. That was the unbearable part. A charlatan presses,
a charlatan needs the win, and August kept *handing him the exits* and the exits kept opening onto the
same yard. "I'm not trying to take your cathedral, Sol. I'm trying to tell you there's a price coming
and I'd like the cleverest man I know to be looking at the real thing when it arrives, not at a
beautiful explanation of why it can't be real."

"Then tell us the price." This from Earl, flat, final. "Stop circling it. You said the books balance.
You said Danny's alive on this side, so somebody's the entry on the other. You've watched all ten of
our deaths. So you know whose turn it was supposed to be." A pause that had a floor missing under it.
"Say the name, August. You didn't bring three of us here to be vague. You brought three of us here
because one of us is the bill."

August was quiet for a long moment.

"I don't know the name," he said. "That's the truth, and it's worse than a name." He let the breath go.
"Here's what I know. Every life, all ten of them, Danny dies at twenty-six. Spring. The same week. I tried
everything, the early times. Moved him, warned him, locked the doors. He always found the one I missed.
By the fourth life I stopped trying, because trying was its own cruelty, watching it come and pretending
it might not. I let it come. Eight times I sat where one of you is sitting and I let that boy die on
schedule because I'd learned the river goes around." He stopped. His hands were not quite steady on the
blanket now. "This life I did one thing. Small. The smallest thing I had never tried, because it was
the one that cost *me* instead of him. I won't tell you what — not because I'm hiding it, because if I
tell you, you'll do the arithmetic out loud and I don't think this room survives it tonight. But it
worked. He's twenty-eight. He's sitting right there. For the first time in eleven lives I have seen
Danny Reiss turn twenty-seven."

Voss looked at Danny, and Danny was looking at his own hands, and there was on the young man's face the
specific horror of a person being told he is a miracle who has begun to do the sum that a miracle
implies — *if I am alive against the weight of the world, then the weight is somewhere, leaning on
someone, and these are the only someones in the room.*

"And the books balance," August said, very quietly. "They always balance. The world let me have him.
It has not told me what it's taking back. But I've felt it the way you feel weather coming in your bad
knee, all this last month, getting closer — and I know it's in this room, and I know it's one of the
three of you, and I have spent the last of myself getting you all in one place so that whichever of you
it is doesn't meet it alone in the dark the way Danny used to, every spring, for ten springs, with
nobody who knew."

The light at the window was nearly gone. Somewhere in the house a clock Voss had not noticed all
afternoon ticked, and he found he was counting it, and made himself stop.

"That's why you wanted the doctor," Voss said slowly. "Not the friend. The doctor. You think one of us
is going to —" he could not find a clinical word for it and that was its own small defeat "— and you
want someone in the room who knows what to do with a body."

"No, Sol." And now, for the first time all night, August's composure thinned, and underneath it Voss
saw the actual man, frightened, eighty years of it, all eleven lifetimes of it, looking out. "I wanted
the doctor because I needed someone who would fight me the whole way and lose, so that when it happens
— when it happens in front of all of you tonight — there's one person in this room who tried as hard as
a human being can try *not* to believe it, and couldn't help it anyway. I need a witness who didn't
want to be one." The thin smile. "I need you, Sol, specifically because you came here to prove me
wrong. You're the only verdict anyone would trust. Including me."
