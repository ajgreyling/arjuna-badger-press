# Six

Afterward Voss could account for every second of it, because the doctor in him had taken the body and
run it like a drill, and the drill did not care what the afternoon had been about.

He had Earl on the floor before the man had finished sliding, one hand behind the head so it didn't
crack the boards, and he was talking — *Earl, Earl, squeeze my hand, look at me* — and reading the
face the way he'd read a thousand faces, the dropped left side, the pooling slackness, and his own
mouth was already saying the word, *stroke, it's a stroke, Danny call them, now,* and Danny was on the
phone in the hall, the very hall, Voss thought from somewhere far off, where a boy had reached for a
phone in a dream eleven days ago, and the thought was obscene and he put it down and kept working.

He did everything. He knew, even as he did it, that he was doing it partly to prove a thing — that a
man does not die because a story said he would, that there is a left middle cerebral artery and a clot
and a clock, and the clock is just a clock, and he, Solomon Voss, would hold this man inside the
window of the clock by main strength if he had to, the way Earl had carried the chairs. He kept the
airway. He kept talking. He had Danny bring a blanket and then take it half away because the man's
colour — he watched the colour. He counted. He held Earl Cade's opening-and-closing hand in both of
his and he was, he realised distantly, praying, in the only way a lifelong unbeliever prays, which is
to say *no* to the room over and over with everything in you.

And the whole time, behind him, August did not move from the bed and did not call out and did not, God
help him, seem afraid — he lay with his head turned toward them and watched, and his face had the
specific stillness Voss had been waiting for all afternoon and had finally learned to read: not the
organised stillness of a lie, and not the slack stillness of the dying, but a *third* stillness, the
stillness of a man watching the last act of a play he has seen ten times, in a steady voice, so the
person listening isn't frightened before they have to be.

It was that stillness, more than anything, that Voss could not forgive and could not explain. Because a
man whose friend is having a stroke on the floor is not still like that. A man whose elaborate delusion
is in the act of being confirmed is not still like that either — he is vindicated, he is wild with it,
the cathedral is lighting up. August was neither. August was not surprised, and the not-being-surprised
sat in Voss's chest like a stone going down a well, because there are only two kinds of men who are not
surprised by a death: the ones who made it
happen, and the ones who already knew.

The ambulance came. Voss had given them a clean handover, the times, the presentation, the lot, his
voice flat and exact, and they had the trolley and the lift and they took Earl out through the door
into the dark where the world had been all night, waiting outside the window, and the room was suddenly
very large and very quiet with three men in it instead of four, and the cold hearth that Earl had been
looking at the whole afternoon stared back at them with nothing in it.

Danny came back in from the cold and stood in the doorway and did not sit. He looked at August on the
bed and his face was wet and he made no move to hide it.

"You let me argue with him," Danny said. "You let me sit here and tell him not to carry it and the
whole time you knew it was already —" He couldn't finish.

"I didn't know it was tonight," August said. The thin voice. "I swear that to you, Danny, with the
little I've got left to swear on. I knew it was close. I felt it close. I didn't know it was while you
were still in the chair." He turned his head. "I'd have spared you that. Of all of you, I'd have
spared you that."

Voss stood in the middle of the room with Earl's blood, what little there was, from where the head had
grazed the table edge on the way down despite his hand, drying on his cuff, and he made himself, one
last time, be the rampart. He made himself say it.

"It was a stroke." His voice came out steady. "A left MCA occlusion in a man of seventy-something with
a history I'd bet my licence includes untreated pressure and the kind of grief that thickens blood.
It was a stroke, August. It had a cause, and the cause was biology, and it was coming whether you'd
ever heard the word *loop* in your life or not. You did not trade Earl for Danny. There is no ledger.
There is a clot and a clock and an old man who'd carried something heavy for forty years, and the only
thing your story did was make sure he spent his last clear minutes believing he deserved it. That's
not arithmetic. That's the cruellest thing I've watched a kind man do, and you did it out of love, and
it's still the cruellest thing I've watched."

He was breathing hard when he finished. He wanted, badly, for it to be true. He had never in forty
years wanted an explanation to be true the way he wanted this one.

August looked at him for a long moment with the third stillness, the grieving one, and then he did a
small thing that Voss would carry out of that house and into every night of the rest of his life.

He didn't argue. He didn't defend the cathedral. He simply reached over to the little table by the bed,
slowly, the wet breath catching, and he picked up a folded piece of paper that had been sitting there
the whole afternoon, weighted under the water glass where Earl had set it down, and he held it out to
Voss with a hand that shook.

"I wrote it down this morning," August said. "Before any of you came. I couldn't say it out loud — I
told you, if I said it out loud the room wouldn't survive the night, and it didn't, it didn't survive
the night anyway. But I wrote it down. The thing I changed. The small thing that cost me instead of
him. And underneath it I wrote what I thought the books would take, because I did know, Sol, I did the
arithmetic this morning the way Earl did it in his chair, and I couldn't carry being right." The paper
trembled in the air between them. "Don't read it now. Read it when you've decided. Read it when you're
sure it was just a clot and a clock. And if I'm a frightened old man who built a cathedral, you'll
read a frightened old man's guess, and you can grieve me clean." The thinnest breath. "And if I'm
not — you'll know I knew his name before the sun came up, and you'll have to decide what that means,
with your forty years, in your study, with my friend's blood still on your sleeve."

Voss did not take the paper.

It hung there in the lamplight, in the old man's shaking hand, and Voss stood with his whole life's
training telling him that to take it was to step onto August's board for the last and worst time, that
a sealed prediction is the oldest trick there is, that of *course* it would say Earl, that he could
write Earl on a paper tonight himself and call it prophecy in the morning —

and the cold clear part of him, the part that never stopped, said quietly: *then take it. If it's a
trick, taking it costs you nothing. You only refuse to take it if some part of you has already
decided what it says.*

He took the paper.
