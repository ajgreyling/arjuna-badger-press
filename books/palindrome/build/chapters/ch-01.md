# One

The first thing Solomon Voss noticed, because noticing was the last profession he had left, was that
August had set out four chairs.

Not three and the bed. Four chairs, drawn into a loose arc around the cold hearth, and the bed pushed
back against the window as if August intended to leave it behind. A dying man arranges very little in
the last weeks; the body becomes a country he is being deported from, and most men spend their final
arrangements on the management of pain. August had spent his on furniture. He had moved chairs.

"You set these out yourself," Voss said.

"I had Earl do the lifting." August was in the chair nearest the window, a blanket over his knees in
the particular way of a man who is cold in a warm room. "But I told him where. Sit, Sol. You'll want
to be sitting."

Forty years of practice had taught Voss that *you'll want to be sitting* was a sentence with a shape,
and the shape was a confession. He had heard it from men who had killed their wives and from men who
had simply, finally, decided to say a true thing out loud after a lifetime of not. He sat, and he
arranged his own face into the thing he had worn into ten thousand rooms, the open, unhurried,
faintly bored attention that gave a frightened person nothing to push against, and he prepared,
with a small private grief, to be kind to a dying friend who had begun, at the end, to come apart.

Because that was the diagnosis he had brought with him in the car, against his own discipline, on the
strength of one line in the message: *Bring the doctor. Not the man. The doctor.* A man only wants a
witness to his mind when he has begun to doubt it.

Morphine did this. The dying brain built cathedrals, and Voss had sat with a hundred of them and
learned never to argue with the architecture — you did not tell a man his visions were chemistry, you
held his hand inside the cathedral. That was what he had come to do. He had not expected the chairs to
bother him, and they bothered him.

Earl came in from the kitchen with a glass of water he set at August's elbow without being asked, and
took the chair to August's left, and said nothing, which was Earl. Voss had known Earl Cade for thirty
years and could have totalled his recorded speech across that time at perhaps a long afternoon. Earl
had a face like a closed door in a wall you hadn't known was there. He sat now with his forearms on
his knees and his hands loose between them, and he looked at the cold hearth as if there were a fire
in it, and he waited.

And then the fourth chair, and the reason Voss's stomach had been wrong since he walked in, because he
had counted the chairs and counted the men and the arithmetic had left one chair over until the door
opened and Danny came through it.

Danny Reiss, who was not supposed to be the kind of man you noticed, and whom Voss had never been able
to stop noticing — the way you can't stop noticing the one warm thing in a cold room. He had a bag of
something from the pharmacy and the slightly hunted look of a person who has been sent on an errand to
get him out of a house, and he stopped just inside the door when he saw the arc of chairs, and the
fourth one empty and angled toward August, and he understood, the way Voss had understood, that this
was not a vigil. It was a hearing.

"You're all here," August said, and there was in his voice neither triumph nor fear, only a kind of
enormous tiredness finally being allowed to set something down. "Good. Sit, Danny. The chair's for
you."

Danny sat. He held the pharmacy bag in his lap with both hands like a man in a waiting room.

For a moment no one said anything, and the house made the small sounds a house makes around four
silent men — the tick of the cooling kettle, a branch against the gutter, the particular hush of a
high room at the end of a winter afternoon when the light has gone flat and grey and final. Voss
watched August breathe. The breathing was bad. There was a wet edge to it, a thing at the bottom of
each inhalation that Voss did not need to be a different kind of doctor to read. Weeks. Perhaps not
weeks.

"I'm going to tell you something," August said, "and I'm going to ask you not to speak until I'm done.
Not because I'm afraid of what you'll say. Because if you start trying to fix it too early, you'll
fix the wrong thing, and we don't have the time for the wrong thing." He looked at each of them in
turn, unhurried, and his eye when it reached Voss held a small dry warmth that was entirely sane, and
that was the part Voss would think about later, in the car, in the months after — that there had been
nothing in August's eye that a clinician could circle on a page. "Sol. Especially you. You came here
to listen to a dying man lose his mind. I'm asking you to do something harder. I'm asking you to
listen to him keep it."

Voss inclined his head a fraction, which was the gesture he used for *I am listening and I am
conceding nothing*, and which August, who had known him a long time, clearly read exactly, because
the corner of his mouth moved.

"All right," August said. He folded his hands on the blanket. He looked, for a moment, at the cold
hearth, the way Earl was looking at it, as if the two of them could see the same fire. And then he
said it, flatly, the way a man reads a verdict that has already been handed down, in the voice of
someone reporting a fact about the weather in a country none of them had been to:

"I have lived this life before. This exact life — this body, this town, the three of you. I have
lived it, start to finish, and died at the end of it, and woken again at the beginning, as a child, in
my own bed, with everything I am keeping in my head. And I have done it again. And again." He drew one
of the wet, effortful breaths. "This is the eleventh time. Ten times before this one I have sat where
I'm sitting and died in that bed by the window. I know how this room smells in the last week. I have
smelled it ten times."

The kettle ticked. Outside, the branch moved against the gutter.

"And this time," August said, "for the first time in eleven lives, I changed one thing. A small
thing. The smallest thing I have ever been brave enough to change." His gaze went, gently, to the man
holding the pharmacy bag in both hands. "And because I changed it, one of you is sitting in this room
who, by every other turn of this life, by all ten times before, has been dead for thirty years."

He did not look away from Danny. He let it sit there, kind and terrible, in the grey final light.

"I needed to tell you while I still could," August said, "because I think there's a price for what I
did. And I don't know which of you is going to be asked to pay it."

Voss became aware that he had not, for some seconds, taken a breath of his own. He took one now,
quietly, the way you take a pulse without letting the patient feel you do it, and he reached — out of
forty years of reflex, the way a man reaches for the rail on a stair he has stopped trusting — for the
name of the thing that was happening in this room.

He had a hundred of them. Confabulation. Terminal lucidity gone grandiose. *I am not dying at
random; I am completing a circle.* He had the name in his hand, the way you have a coin in your hand,
smooth and known and the right weight.

And then August turned from Danny and looked at him, only at him, and said, in the same flat patient
voice:

"Sol. Before you settle on the word you just reached for — and I know which word it is, I've watched
you reach for it ten times — ask me one thing. Ask me something only you would know. Something you
have never told me. Never told anyone." The dry warmth again, awful now. "Then pick your word."

And Solomon Voss, who had come up the hill in the flat grey afternoon to hold a dying friend's hand
inside a cathedral built of morphine and fear, sat in the fourth chair's arc with the coin of the
right diagnosis going slowly cold in his palm, and tried to think of a question that August Renner
could not possibly answer.
