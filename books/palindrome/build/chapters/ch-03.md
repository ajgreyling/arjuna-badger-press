# Three

"Tell it from the start," Earl said. "If you're going to."

So August told it, and Voss — who had come to hold a hand and was now holding the arms of his chair —
listened the way you listen to a thing you have decided not to believe and cannot stop hearing.

The first time, August said, he hadn't known it was a loop. You don't. You're a child in your own
bed and the ceiling is the ceiling you remember and you think, with the flat acceptance of a child,
*this is a very long dream,* and then you are eight and it is not a dream and you understand, slowly,
across a year, the way you understand cold water, that you are living it again and that you are the
only one who knows. "I was a strange child the second time," he said. "You'd have hated me, Earl.
Knew everything. Said nothing right. A forty-year-old man behind an eight-year-old's face, trying to
remember how to be afraid of the dark so my mother wouldn't look at me funny." A small dry sound that
was not quite a laugh. "I learned to perform the boy. That was the first skill. Everything after was
easier."

He had tried, the early times, to do the obvious thing. Who wouldn't. "You think you'll fix it," he
said. "You think: I have been handed the one gift no one gets, I will spend it well. I'll get the
medicine to the people before the bad year. I'll stand in the right square on the right morning and
say the thing that stops it." He shook his head, slowly, against the pillow. "It doesn't take. I don't
know why and I've had a long time to wonder. You move the big stone and the river just goes around it.
The bad year comes a different way. The man you saved in March is gone by August on a road you didn't
know to watch. I used to think I was being punished. Now I think it's just — weight. The world has a
weight and it wants to fall the way it falls. You can change *where* you stand in it. You can't change
much about where it's going."

"That's convenient," Voss said, because the rampart was still there and he would man it. "A claim of
miraculous knowledge that conveniently can never be used to do any verifiable good."

"Yes," August said simply. "It is convenient. It's also true. Both happen." He looked at Voss with
that terrible patience. "You've spent forty years listening to people explain their lives in ways that
happen to excuse them, Sol, and you've learned to hear the excuse under the explanation. I'm telling
you there's a kind of true thing that *sounds exactly like* an excuse, and the only way to tell them
apart is to have lived it, and you haven't, and I have, and that asymmetry is the whole tragedy of
trying to tell you. I know how it sounds. I knew how it would sound the first time I rehearsed it,
which was about sixty years and three lifetimes ago."

It was the offhand arithmetic that did it, more than the dream had. *Sixty years and three lifetimes
ago.* He said it the way a man says *last Tuesday.* No emphasis, no plea. And Voss, against every
discipline he owned, felt the floor of the ordinary tilt under him — not belief, he would not have
called it belief, but the first hairline crack in the certainty that the floor was there at all.

Danny had not spoken since the pharmacy bag. He spoke now, and his voice was careful, the careful of a
man walking on a frozen thing, testing it. "What's it like," he said. "When it — resets. When you die."

The room shifted toward him. Voss saw August's face change for Danny in a way it had not changed for
him, the cold patient surface going soft at one edge, and he understood, with the part of his mind that
never stopped working, that whatever else was true or false in this room, *this* was not performed:
the old man loved the young one. You cannot fake the particular gentleness of a person choosing easier
words.

"It doesn't hurt," August said. "I'll tell you that first, because I know it's the thing you're
actually asking. There's no — corridor. No light. You're tired, the way I'm tired now, and then
you're not tired, and you're small, and warm, and the ceiling is the ceiling, and for one morning
every time you get to be a person who doesn't know anything yet. That morning's the best thing there
is. I've had eleven of them. I think I'd have done the whole circle just for the mornings." He drew
the wet breath. "What it's *like*, the rest of it — Danny, it's like reading a book you love, out loud,
to someone, for the eleventh time. You know every turn. You know the sad part's coming. And you find,
to your surprise, that knowing doesn't make you feel it less. It makes you feel it *more*, because now
you can see it coming from three chapters off and there's not one thing you can do but keep reading in
a steady voice so the person listening isn't frightened before they have to be."

He was not looking at Danny when he finished. He was looking at the cold hearth, the way Earl looked at
it. And Voss thought: *that is either the most sophisticated emotional manipulation I have witnessed in
forty years of professional exposure to it, or it is the simple truth, and I have built my whole life
on being able to tell the difference, and I cannot tell the difference,* and the not-being-able-to-tell
was, he realised, the actual emergency — worse than the dream, worse than the arithmetic. A man's
competence is the last room he locks. August had got into it without seeming to try.

"You said three chapters off," Earl said. Still the flat voice, still the hands loose. "You can see the
sad part coming."

"Yes."

"Then you've seen all of ours." Earl lifted his head, finally, and looked at August, and his weathered
closed-door face had nothing on it that Voss could name, which was somehow the most frightening thing
the night had done yet. "You know how I go. You know how Sol goes. You've watched it ten times." A
beat. "And you brought us here to tell us you changed the book."

"I changed one line of it," August said.

"One line," Earl said.

"The line where Danny dies at twenty-six."

And there it was, set down in the middle of the room at last, plain as a coin on a table, and nobody
reached for it. Danny had gone very still. Not the organised stillness of a man about to lie — Voss
knew that stillness and this was its opposite — the stillness of a man who has just been handed his own
death certificate with a *cancelled* stamped across it, and cannot work out whether that is the best
news of his life or a sentence passed on someone else he hasn't met yet.

"How," Danny said. Just the one word.

August turned his head on the pillow and looked at the youngest man in the room with the full weight of
whatever a person becomes after eleven lives of loving the same people and losing most of them on
schedule, and he said, "Not tonight. Tonight you only need to know that I did, and that the world keeps
its books balanced, and that somewhere in this room is the entry on the other side of yours." His gaze
moved, then — not pointing, nothing so crude, just *moving*, across Earl, across Voss, the way a hand
moves across a ledger feeling for the line — and came to rest, for a moment that went on a beat too
long, on no one in particular and therefore on all of them.

"That's the part I need you sober for," August said. "Because I think it's coming due. And I don't know
which of you it wants."
