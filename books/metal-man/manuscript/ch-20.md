# Twenty

*Burbank, California—1 August 1973*

*(Fourteen months earlier. This is the chapter that was left out.)*

The props came in at five in the afternoon in two flight cases with the show's own seals on them, and
Shipi saw them go past and came and found him in the dressing room and said one word.

"Sealed."

"How many?"

"Two cases. Their people. Nobody of ours has been near them."

Uri nodded and went on looking at the mirror.

—

The story that everybody knows is that Johnny Carson, who had been a working magician before he was
anything else, took advice from James Randi on how to prevent trickery; that the show bought its own
spoons and its own boxes and its own sealed film canisters and kept them under guard; that Geller was
not allowed to touch or approach any of it before air; and that as a result, in front of a very large
audience on a Wednesday night in August, nothing whatsoever happened for twenty-two minutes.

Every word of that is true.

The inference everybody drew from it, that the controls prevented the trick—is the part this
chapter is about, and the man it happened to has never once contradicted it in fifty-three years,
and that is not because he could not.

—

He could feel all of it from the dressing room.

That is the fact. Two flight cases in a corridor eleven metres away, and Uri Geller sat in front of a
mirror with a paper cup of coffee and knew the contents of both of them: the spoons—cheap, new,
soft, stamped out that year, the easiest metal in the building; the canisters, aluminium, one of them
carrying a small differential load; the box with a die in it, sitting on one face.

He did not need to touch anything. He had never needed to touch anything. Touch made it faster and
easier and less costly, and in nine months in California he had established, with two physicists and
a magnetometer bolted down in another room, that it worked through a wall.

The controls were designed by a very good magician to stop a magician.

They were, against the thing he actually was, completely irrelevant, and everyone involved in
designing them would have been horrified to learn it, and none of them ever did.

—

He had eleven hours to think about it and he thought about it for all of them.

Here is what he was looking at, and it should be laid out coldly, because he laid it out coldly.

*The Tonight Show* in 1973 was not a television programme. It was the largest single voluntary
gathering of human attention that had ever existed in the United States. Fifteen, twenty million
people on an ordinary night. He had been on television before, he had been on television in six
countries, but never *this*, and never with the thing working properly, because the thing had been
working very well that summer.

If he sat down at that table and did what he could do—if he took a sealed case, chosen and guarded
by a hostile professional, and made every spoon inside it fold without opening it, live, with Randi's
own controls in place and Randi's own name attached to them—

Then by nine o'clock the following morning he would not be a performer any more.

He was twenty-six. He had worked out, in a dressing room, with total clarity and no sentimentality
whatsoever, the exact shape of what would follow, and it had four parts:

**One.** It would be undeniable, and it would be undeniable *in America*, on tape, with a magician's
controls. There would be no argument. The argument was the only thing keeping him ordinary.

**Two.** He would never be released. Not imprisoned, he did not imagine anything so crude. He would
simply become, permanently, the property of whichever set of serious men got to him first, and there
would be a great many of them, and they would be extremely polite, and it would begin within a week.

**Three.** Hanna. He had asked her in the spring and she had said yes and they had told almost
nobody. She was twenty-two. There was a life in which she was married to a man who did an act, and
there was a life in which she was married to a national asset, and he had seen enough by August 1973
—the man with the legal pad, the three questions, the pleasant unmemorable face—to know that the
second one had no exit.

**Four.** And the children.

Not his; he did not have any yet. The other ones. Because by August 1973 he already knew what
happened after a broadcast—Israel had shown him, and Germany had shown him, which was that
somewhere between one and three percent of the children watching would go into a kitchen drawer
afterwards, and that a fraction of those would find that it worked.

He did not know the word *contagion*. Nobody had shown him the memo and nobody ever would.

He knew the thing itself, though, because he had been getting the letters for two years, in
children's handwriting, from five countries, and he had a box of them, and there were nine hundred
of them, and he had read every one.

Do it on the largest stage in the world, with the controls in place, and undeniably, and you do not
create a wonder.

You create a *search*.

—

The floor manager knocked at ten past six.

He had not decided. That is the honest account and he has never dressed it up: he walked out under
the lights on the first of August 1973 with the decision not made, in a good suit, smiling, twenty-six
years old, and he sat down.

And then Carson, who was, whatever else he was, a professional of the highest order, and who was
also being extremely careful, and who was also, and this is in the tape if you watch his face, not
entirely comfortable with what he had been set up to do—pushed the tray of spoons across the desk
and did not offer to let him touch it.

And Uri Geller looked at fifteen million people through a lens.

And he did nothing.

—

Twenty-two minutes.

It is on tape and you can watch it and it is one of the most excruciating things ever broadcast. He
tries. He appears to try, he leans over the objects, he holds his hands above them, he asks to be
allowed closer and is not, he apologises, he says he does not feel strong, he says he is being
pushed, he says give me time. He looks at the table. He looks at the audience. He sweats.

He does that for twenty-two minutes on live television.

And the single hardest technical accomplishment of Uri Geller's entire career—harder than anything
in Menlo Park, harder than the wire in Maryland, harder than the fork on the BBC in November—is
that for twenty-two minutes, with the whole of it available to him, with a case of soft new spoons
screaming at him from a table eighteen inches away, he kept his hands where they were and made a
nation watch him fail.

Nobody has ever applauded that. Nobody has ever known it happened.

He learned how to do it on a wooden stool in Nicosia when he was twelve, sitting four feet from a
board of twenty-two keys, holding his attention on a ceiling fan.

—

He came off. Somebody said something kind in the corridor. He got to the hotel and was sick in the
bathroom and Shipi ran both taps until he had finished and then handed him a towel and did not ask.

They sat on the two beds for a while.

"They'll say you were caught," Shipi said eventually. He was eighteen.

"Yes."

"Forever, Uri. That is the one they will play forever."

"Yes."

Shipi looked at the carpet for a bit. Then he said, "Was it not there?"

And this is the only time in fifty-seven years that Shipi Shtrang came anywhere near asking, and Uri
looked at him, and Shipi saw whatever there was to see, and put both hands up briefly, palms out—
*forget it, I didn't say it*, and went and made tea.

They never discussed it again.

—

Except once, nineteen years later, sideways, in a car.

They were driving from Reading to Heathrow in 1992 and the radio was doing a thing about the
anniversary of something, and a clip came on—twenty seconds of it, the audio only, the specific
quality of an American studio in 1973, and Shipi reached over and changed the station without
saying anything, which is what he had done every single time for nineteen years.

Uri said: "Leave it."

Shipi left it. They listened to the rest of it, which was about ninety seconds, including the part
where a very young man says he is not feeling strong, and then a presenter in a London studio in 1992
said something amused and moved on to the weather.

They drove for a while.

Then Shipi said, to the windscreen: "I have never asked you."

"No."

"I want you to know that it was not because I did not want to know."

Uri looked out of the window at the M4.

"I know why it was," he said.

"Do you."

"You did not ask me because if I told you, you would be the only other person who knew, and then you
would be carrying it as well." He shrugged, which was as close as either of them came. "And you had
enough. You were eighteen and you were carrying the money and the bookings and my mother."

Shipi Shtrang drove for about two miles.

Then he said: "It was on television in forty countries and they still say it every time they say your
name," and his voice was not entirely level, "and I have had to hear it for nineteen years and say
nothing, and I would like it on the record that this has been extremely annoying."

And Uri Geller laughed until he had to hold onto the door, for about a mile and a half of the M4,
which is the only time in this book that the Carson show is funny, and it is the only way either of
them was ever going to be able to touch it.

—

There is a coda and it belongs here rather than at the end.

In 2007, thirty-four years after, Uri did an interview with a German magazine in which he said that
he would no longer describe himself as having supernatural powers, and would prefer the word
*mystifier*, and that he was an entertainer.

It went round the world in about a day. It is still the single most-quoted thing he has ever said. It
is in the first paragraph of a great many articles about him and it is treated, universally, as the
confession.

He walked it back within the month and everybody laughed at that too.

What nobody has ever asked, not one interviewer, in nineteen years—is what he was doing in 2007.

He was sixty. He had two children in their thirties. The children of 1974 were in their forties by
then and had jobs and mortgages and had long since stopped writing to anybody.

And a man who had agreed to something in a private room off Jermyn Street in 1975, and had maintained
it at his own expense every year since, quietly checked, at sixty, whether he could put it down.

He said the sentence. He watched what happened for four weeks, who called, who wrote, which
telephone numbers he had not heard from in thirty years suddenly rang.

Then he walked it back.

He has never explained why, and the standard reading is vanity: that he could not bear to be an
ordinary entertainer for more than a month.

The other reading is available to anybody who has read this far, and it does not require a single
additional fact.

—

He was famous by Friday.

That is the part nobody expects and it is the reason the whole strategy works. The failure did not
destroy him, it *made* him, in America, immediately and enormously, because twenty million people had
watched a young man in a good suit be humiliated and had felt sorry for him, and because the papers
ran it everywhere, and because within a fortnight every child in the English-speaking world knew the
name.

Bookings tripled. The BBC rang in September.

And a very small number of people in two countries, reading the coverage, concluded that the matter
was settling itself nicely, exactly as recommended, at no cost.
