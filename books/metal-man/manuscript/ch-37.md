# Thirty-Seven

*London — 16 to 19 December 2026*

Nobody would give him a camera, and this is the bill for chapter twenty-four, presented in full,
fifty-one years later, with interest.

—

He tried for three days.

He had the telephone numbers. That was never the problem — he had been in television for
fifty-seven years and he knew people at four networks in two countries, and a great many of them were
fond of him, and every single one of them took the call, because Uri Geller ringing up is a nice
thing that happens to your afternoon.

He got through to everybody. He was received warmly by all of them.

And it did not matter in the slightest, because what he was asking for was live network airtime, on
the shortest possible notice, at a time of his choosing, to make an appeal to the entire viewing
public — and there is no version of that request, from that man, in that year, that any commissioning
editor on Earth grants.

"Uri, I'd love to. What's the hook?"

"I cannot tell you the hook."

"...Right."

"I need eleven minutes. It has to be live and it has to be Sunday and it has to be as many people as
you can put in front of it."

A pause, on four separate calls, in which four separate professionals worked out that the eighty-
year-old spoon bender had rung up wanting an unspecified live intervention on a fixed date, and were
kind about it.

One of them offered him a pre-record for the following month.

One of them, gently, asked whether Hanna was all right.

—

On the evening of the eighteenth he sat in the kitchen at Sonning having been refused by everybody he
had ever worked with, and Hanna came in and found him with his hands flat on the table.

"They think I am confused," he said.

"Yes."

"They are being kind to me. Do you understand? They have all been kind. Not one of them was rude."
He looked up. "Fifty-one years, Hanna. I did it properly. I did it every year, I never once let it
slip, the crystals, the football, the car, all of it — I made myself into a man that nobody would
ever listen to."

He put his hand flat on the table.

"And it worked," he said.

—

This is the cost of the containment, and the novel has been carrying it since 1975, and here it is:

It worked perfectly. That is the whole tragedy. A man spent half a century making himself ridiculous
in order to be ignored, at enormous personal expense, with total professional discipline — and the
strategy succeeded absolutely, and on the one occasion in his life when he needed to be believed for
eleven minutes, there was not a single door left open anywhere in the world.

You do not get to be taken seriously on the one day you need it. That is not how any of it works.

The camouflage is not a coat. It is your face.

—

Danny Ofir rang at ten past nine on the morning of the nineteenth.

He was sixty-three. He ran a small production company in Bristol that made observational documentary
for people who no longer commission much observational documentary, and he had been trying to make a
film about Uri Geller for eleven years and had been turned down eleven times, and he rang about
every eighteen months, and Uri had always taken the call because he took every call.

"Mr Geller, it's Danny Ofir. I know, I know—"

"Danny. Not today."

"No — listen. Listen. Somebody at Channel Four told me you'd been ringing round asking for live
airtime on Sunday."

Uri did not say anything.

"Is it true?"

"Yes."

"What for?"

And Uri Geller, who had not told a single person the truth in three days because there was no version
of it that survived being said out loud, was too tired to construct anything, and said:

"Because I have to ask everybody in the world to pick up a spoon at the same time, and I have eleven
minutes to do it, and nobody will give me a camera."

There was a silence on the line.

Then Danny Ofir said, in a voice that was not quite steady: "Mr Geller, do you know who I am?"

"You are a man who has asked me eleven times for a film."

"I'm the kid from Reading," he said. "1974. I wrote to you. I was eleven. My mum hit me at the table
because I couldn't do it in front of my dad."

—

He had written in March 1974 and he had got a reply.

Not a form. Four lines, in bad English, in blue biro, on hotel paper, signed — and Uri Geller had
written nine hundred of those over about two years, in hotel rooms in four countries, one at a time,
because he had read every letter.

Danny Ofir had it in a frame in his office in Bristol and had never told anybody in fifty-two years,
including his wife, because a man of sixty-three does not explain that sort of thing.

He still had the fork.

—

"I can get you a camera," he said.

"Danny—"

"No, listen to me, because I've got about four minutes of being brave in me and then I'll talk myself
out of it." Papers, at the other end. "I can't get you a network. Nobody can get you a network, you
know that. But I have a live streaming licence, I have three cameras and a gallery in Bristol, and I
have a distribution deal that puts me on eleven platforms simultaneously with no editorial approval
because nobody has ever cared what I broadcast."

"How many people?"

"On an ordinary Sunday? About four thousand."

Uri closed his eyes.

"But that isn't the question," said Danny Ofir. "The question is how many people will be watching by
minute nine, and the answer to that is: it depends entirely on whether anything is happening."

—

Here is the arithmetic of the last chapter of Uri Geller's working life and he did it in about six
seconds at a kitchen table in Sonning.

In 1973 he had needed the BBC, because in 1973 there were three channels and a nation sat down in
front of them at the same time and there was no other way to reach fifteen million people
simultaneously.

It is not 1973.

There is now a machine, running continuously, in every pocket on Earth, which does exactly one thing
supremely well, which is that it finds whatever a very large number of people have suddenly started
looking at, and it puts it in front of everybody else, within minutes, without asking permission from
anyone.

He had spent fifty-one years being ignored by gatekeepers.

There are no gatekeepers any more. There has not been a gatekeeper since about 2010. The entire
apparatus that he had so carefully, so expensively made certain would never let him through had been
quietly dismantled while he was on a sofa talking about crystals, and nobody had thought to tell him,
because why would you tell Uri Geller.

"Danny," he said. "What time on Sunday?"

"Whenever you like."

"Four o'clock." He was already standing up. "It has to be four o'clock in the afternoon, London."

Twelve minutes to four is when the aircraft came apart, and nine clocks in a hangar near Farnborough
had said so, in step, on the twenty-third of November 1973.

He had never known that. He does not know it now. He said four o'clock because it was the first time
that came into his head.
