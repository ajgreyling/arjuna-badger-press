# Twenty-Six

*Everywhere—1979 to 1993*

The strangest fact about the ruin of Uri Geller's reputation is that it made him extremely rich, and
it did not do so by accident.

—

The mineral dowsing began in 1979 with a telephone call from a man in Australia and it is the part of
his life that nobody has ever been able to explain away, including the people who have tried hardest.

The proposition was simple and slightly mad. A mining company would pay him a very large fee, plus
an override on anything found, to fly over a region—or, later, and more often, to sit in an office
in London with a map, and mark places.

He did it for eleven years. He did it for at least five companies whose names are on record and
several that are not. He was paid, by the account of the people who paid him, sums that in one case
ran to seven figures, and he was paid *again*, on results, which is the detail that matters, because
a company that has been defrauded once does not pay a second time and these companies paid for a
decade.

The sceptical explanation is correct and complete and this book accepts it: he was an expensive
lottery ticket for firms who were going to drill anyway, whose geologists had already narrowed the
ground to a handful of candidates, and for whom a famous man's fee was a rounding error against the
cost of a hole. If he hit, it was a wonderful story. If he missed, nobody wrote it down. Add the fact
that Uri Geller was, by 1979, a man who had spent seven years in the company of physicists and
geologists and who was extremely good at reading a room and a map, and the whole thing dissolves.

Accept all of it. It is still true that he was paid an enormous quantity of money over eleven years
by hard-headed extractive companies to point at maps, and that he retired on it, and that this is the
single largest source of the wealth that let him spend the next forty years not needing to be
believed by anybody.

Being ridiculous turned out to pay considerably better than being credible ever had.

—

The crystals arrived in about 1983 and Hanna hated them.

This is the era of the sofa. He was on daytime television constantly, in eleven countries, and the
booking was never *Uri Geller, the disputed subject of a paper in Nature*. It was Uri Geller with a
lump of quartz and a bag of merchandise, at ten past eleven in the morning, between a cookery item
and the weather.

He wrote motivational books. He put his face on them. He sold a crystal that he had held. He did
seminars on positive energy in hotel ballrooms in secondary cities and they sold out. He appeared at
the opening of things. He endorsed a brand of orange juice.

And he bought the Cadillac.

It was a 1976 Cadillac Fleetwood and he had it welded over, the roof, the bonnet, the boot, the
wings, every panel—with bent spoons and forks. Thousands of them, eventually, donated and
collected over decades, until the car had a skin of cutlery like a scaled animal and weighed several
hundred kilograms more than it was designed to and drove, by all accounts, appallingly.

It is the single most vulgar object owned by any person in this book and he loved it more than any
possession he ever had.

—

Here is the double reading and it runs from here to the end of the novel.

**On the first pass**, and this is how it looked to everybody alive at the time including his family,
what you are watching in these fourteen years is decay. A young man who was once in *Nature*
becoming a man who sells crystals; a genuine anomaly becoming a brand; the seriousness leaking out
year by year until what is left is a tanned figure on a sofa with a quartz pendulum, doing the
football, doing the tabloids, doing whatever the eleven o'clock slot requires.

It is a sad story and it is the story that has been told about him for forty years, and if you watch
the footage in order it is very hard to argue with.

**On the second pass**, which is only available if you have read chapter twenty-four, you are
watching maintenance.

A man was shown, in a private room off Jermyn Street in 1975, exactly what would happen to four
thousand children if he remained credible. Nobody instructed him. Nobody ever checked. Nobody would
have known or cared if he had quietly gone back to being taken seriously.

And so every single year, for five decades, at his own expense, in front of his own children, Uri
Geller went out and made himself *slightly more absurd than he had been the year before*, and he did
it with the same relentless professional competence he brought to everything, and he never once
explained it to anybody, and the cost of it was his entire standing as a serious person in the world.

The Cadillac is not a joke he told. It is a wall he built.

The crystals are not a decline. They are camouflage, applied annually, thickly, by a man who knew
exactly what he was doing and could not be seen to be doing it.

**This book does not tell you which reading is true.**

It is worth noticing, though, that both of them are consistent with every fact, and that only one of
them explains why a man who was demonstrably capable of enormous discipline chose, at the precise
moment he acquired the money to stop, to become *more* ridiculous rather than less.

—

Hanna knew. Not the detail, she never asked for the detail, and he never gave it.

There was an evening in a car park outside a hotel in Birmingham in 1988. He had done a seminar. He
had done two hours on positive energy for two hundred people at thirty pounds a head and he had
signed books afterwards for another hour, and he came out into the car park at half past ten at night and
got as far as the car and then had to stop and put both hands on the roof.

She came round the other side.

She did not say *are you all right*, because she had been married to him for seventeen years.

She put her hand flat on his back, between the shoulder blades, and left it there for about a minute
while a man was quietly not all right in a car park in Birmingham, and then she said:

"How many more?"

And Uri Geller, who was forty-one years old and had been to Menlo Park and to Bloomsbury and had been
in *Nature*, said:

"All of them."

—

There was a night in Osaka in 1991 that he has never told anybody about and that is the clearest
picture of what those forty years actually cost.

He was doing a corporate booking, an electronics manufacturer, a sales conference, eleven hundred
people in a hotel ballroom, forty minutes and a very large fee.

And it went, all of it. Everything he tried worked, first time, easily, for forty minutes, the way it
used to in 1973 and almost never did any more. Cutlery, watches, a drawing, a compass. He came off
soaked and lit up and shaking and twenty-five years old again.

And a Japanese executive of about sixty came and found him afterwards in the corridor and said,
through an interpreter, that his daughter was a physicist at a university in Kyoto, and that she had
seen the SRI material as a student, and that she had a small research budget and would very much like
to talk to him.

Uri Geller stood in a hotel corridor in Osaka in 1991 with the best night he had had in eighteen years
still in his hands, and he looked at a man holding out exactly the thing he had wanted since he was
five years old in a classroom.

And he said no.

He was gracious about it. He said something about his schedule. He gave the man a crystal, because by
1991 he gave everybody a crystal, and the man was polite about that too.

Then he went up to the room and sat in the chair by the window in his coat for about an hour.

—

That is the shape of it, and it repeated perhaps a dozen times across those five decades, and it is
in no biography because saying no to something is not an event.

A university in Kyoto. A physicist in Milan who wrote twice. A programme in Sweden that wanted six
months rather than six minutes. A retired metallurgist in Sheffield in 1999 who sent a two-page
proposal, well argued and properly costed, and whose letter Uri answered warmly and evasively and
kept.

Every one of them a door. Every one of them declined, courteously, by a man who then went and did a
crystal seminar in a hotel ballroom in a secondary city.

And here is the thing that keeps both readings alive, all the way to the end:

**He never once wrote down why.**

There is no diary. There is no letter to Hanna, no note to himself, nothing in fifty-one years that
says *I turned down Kyoto because of the children*. If the containment is real he maintained it
without a single word of record, which is either magnificent discipline or the absence of the thing
entirely.

What there is, instead, is a filing cabinet in a boathouse with two hundred and nineteen newspaper
clippings in it, dated and located in his own hand.

He did not write down why he said no to the physicists.

He wrote down every time a piece of metal failed for no reason.

Then he straightened up and got in the car and they drove home, and the following Tuesday he was on
television at ten past eleven in the morning talking about crystals.
