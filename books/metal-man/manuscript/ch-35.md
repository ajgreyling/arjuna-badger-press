# Thirty-Five

*Buckinghamshire—November 2026*

A. Meir was ninety-four and in a home outside Netanya and it took a couple of telephone calls to establish
that he was still alive, and two more to establish that he would take a call, and the call lasted
eleven minutes.

Uri had not spoken to him since 1998.

He did not ask him for anything, because you do not, and because after fifty-seven years he
understood the etiquette better than he understood most things. He described the 1954 note. He
described the transfer in 1961. He said where it was.

Then he stopped talking.

There was a silence on the line long enough that he thought the old man had gone.

"Uri," said A. Meir.

"Yes."

"I want to say something and then I am going to put the telephone down, because I am ninety-four and
I get tired." A breath. "In 1975 I sat in a room and I let a man explain to you what would happen to
your wife, and I said nothing, and then I told you in Hebrew that it was a description and not a
threat. And it was a description. It was true. Everything I told you was true."

"I know."

"I have thought about that lunch for fifty-one years," said A. Meir, "and it is the only thing I did
in forty years of work that I am not able to put down. Do you understand me?"

"Yes."

"Then let me do this one and don't thank me. Thanking me would be the worst thing you could do."

He hung up.

Eleven days later a letter arrived at Sonning from a body Uri had never heard of, granting Dr Frances
Okonjo-Hale, materials scientist, access for the purpose of structural examination to a specified
item in a specified collection, on a specified date, accompanied by one assistant.

The assistant was not named.

—

The building was low and brick and had been built in about 1959 to look like nothing, and it had done
so successfully for sixty-seven years.

They signed in. There was a man on the desk. There was a corridor, and then another corridor, and
then a door that took two keys, and Frances Okonjo-Hale went through it and Uri Geller went through
it behind her at seventy-nine years old with a bent spoon in his coat pocket.

—

It was a hundred and ten yards long.

Steel racking down both sides, floor to ceiling, and past about the halfway point the racking stopped
and became a run of plain grey cabinets, and the perspective lines went away to a dark rectangle at
the far end where a set of double doors stood shut.

The air was two degrees cooler.

*It is cold*, he had written under a pencil drawing in a laboratory in California in June 1973, when
he had been asked to draw a bunch of keys, and he had been twenty-six and had not known what he was
drawing and had put his pencil down and gone out of the room.

He stood at the near end of it for a long time.

"Mr Geller," said the man from the desk, who was called Stennett, the grandson, as it turned out, of
another Stennett, which nobody in the corridor knew and which is not the sort of thing that gets
noticed. "Are you all right?"

"Yes," said Uri. "I have been here before."

—

The crackle in that corridor was the loudest he had heard since a street in Nicosia in 1958, and it
was not coming from the racking.

—

The mass was in the last bay, on a steel cradle, under a dust sheet, and it was the size of a small
car and it was black.

Okonjo-Hale did the work. She was there to do work and she did it: photographs, measurements, a
portable spectrometer, four hours of it, methodically, while Uri Geller sat on a plastic chair eleven
feet away with his hands in his lap and did not touch anything.

He did not need to touch it. He had never needed to touch anything.

—

Here is what he got, and it is the fourth appearance in this novel of the thing the metal gives him,
and it is the only one that has ever frightened him.

*Falling.*

Not the falling of a stone. There is no ground in it and no down. It is falling through a dark that
does not end and has never ended, at a temperature with no bottom to it, for a duration that does not
resolve into a number because there is nothing in a human being that can hold that shape—and
underneath it, and continuous with it, and going all the way back, the slow enormous cooling, a few
degrees per million years, the plates growing into one another in the dark for ten million years in
the core of something that came apart before the Earth was a place.

And then heat. Air. Ground. A very long stillness with sun on it.

And then hands, and a road, and a lorry, and rooms, and a corridor, and sixty-five years of two
degrees cooler than everywhere else.

That is what he expected. All of that is what an old meteorite is, and he had known it would be
enormous and he had braced for it and he could carry it.

What he had not braced for was that it was not a recording.

It was *going on*.

—

Every other piece of metal Uri Geller had touched in seventy-four years had given him the past. That
is what the thing is. It is a record, closed, finished, in the order it happened, and the newest
thing in it is the moment your own hand arrives.

This one was still writing.

Underneath the fall and the cooling and the road there was a layer that was **current**—happening
now, in the room, continuously, the way a pulse is current, and it was not slow. It was fast and
fine and enormously complex, and it was doing the thing that Okonjo-Hale had seen in a photograph and
had been unable to say out loud without qualifying her career.

It repeated. It nested. It was not the same size each time.

And it was, and this is the sentence he could not get out for four days afterwards and then said
once, to Hanna, in a car, looking straight ahead:

*It was going faster than it was in the picture.*

—

At some point in the fourth hour he stood up and walked to the cradle and stopped a metre short of
it, and Okonjo-Hale saw him do it and did not say anything.

He did not put out his hand.

He said, quite conversationally, in Hebrew, "What do you want?"

—

Nothing answered. It has never answered. There is no voice in this book and there is not one here.

What happened is that the current layer, the fast fine thing that was going on underneath—did
something it had not done in the four hours he had been sitting twelve feet away, which is that it
*stopped*.

For about two seconds.

The way a room full of people stops when somebody comes in.

Then it went on.

—

He got out of the building. He sat in the car in the car park for a while with his hands on his knees
and Frances Okonjo-Hale drove because he could not, and about six miles down the road he asked her
to pull in, and she did, and he got out and stood on the verge in the November cold.

He had understood the three figures.

—

It had come while the thing had stopped, in the two seconds, and it had not come as a message,
because there are no messages. It had come the way everything came to him—as a shape, arriving
whole, in the body, with no words on it.

Three, and then him.

He had spent fifty-three years assuming that the three hollow shapes on that piece of paper were
three beings, and every single person he had ever shown the drawing to had assumed the same thing,
and one of them had said *aliens* and he had never shown it to that person again.

They are not beings. They are not anybody.

They are the same shape three times, drawn hollow, with an empty oval where the head is, and what a
twenty-six-year-old had been drawing in 1973, without any idea what his hand was doing, was not a
portrait.

It was a **socket**. Drawn three times, because it had been filled three times, and drawn empty
because in every case the thing that filled it had worn away and gone.

One who was worshipped. There is metal in museums that is purer than the period that made it should
allow.

One who was burned, in a century that had a procedure.

And one who is alive right now, somewhere, in a facility, quite recently, who cannot speak.

*3. and then me.*

He had written it himself, at twenty-six, in his own hand, on the day he drew it, and he had no
memory of writing it, and he had carried the sheet around for eight months in 2025 showing it to
people at dinner.

It is not a picture of them.

It is a picture of the fourth one, and of what the first three look like afterwards.

—

He stood on the verge of a B-road in Buckinghamshire on a Tuesday in November 2026, twenty-eight days
before his eightieth birthday, and put his hand out onto the roof of the car to steady himself, and
found that he had the spoon from 1952 in his other hand and did not remember taking it out of his
pocket.

"Mr Geller," said Frances Okonjo-Hale, through the window. "Please get in the car."

—

He did not tell her about the three figures in the car and he did not tell her for ten days, and
when he did tell her she did the thing that she had done at every point in the nine years he had
known her, which was to take it seriously in the wrong direction and thereby be useful.

"You said worn away," she said. "Those were your words. Not *dead*. Worn away."

"Yes."

"That is a wear term. That is a mechanical term." She had a pen. "Mr Geller, when you said it, did
you mean it as a figure of speech?"

He thought about it.

"No," he said, slowly, and then again: "No. It was—" and he did the thing with his hands, the
narrow thing opening, that he did when the language ran out. "It was not that they were used up. It
was that the *shape* was still there and the person was not in it any more. Like a—" he searched
"— like a step. In stone. Where everybody walks."

Frances Okonjo-Hale put her pen down.

"That is not wear," she said. "That is *creep*."

—

Creep is the slow permanent deformation of a material held under a load below its yield strength, at
temperature, over time. It is not damage in any single moment. Nothing fails. Nothing cracks. The
material simply, over months and years and decades, stops being the shape it was, because it has been
held.

It is the reason turbine blades have a service life. It is why lead sags. It is what happens to
anything that is asked to hold something up for long enough.

She explained this to him in a kitchen at seventy-eight and he understood about half of it and the
half he understood was the half that mattered.

"So it is not that they broke," he said.

"On your account, no. On your account they were *loaded*." She was choosing her words with enormous
care. "For a long time. And the shape they were left in is the shape of the load and not the shape of
the person."

—

He asked the question and she did not have an answer and there is not one.

"Then how long do you have," he said, "before you are the step."

"That depends entirely on the load." She closed the laptop. "Which I cannot measure, and neither can
you, and I want to point out that we are discussing this as though it were engineering and it is
not."

"No," said Uri. "But it is the only way either of us can talk about it."

And Frances Okonjo-Hale, who had come to a birthday party nine years earlier to be polite about the
crystals, and who was going to be in a car park in Bristol on the twentieth of December with her
hands over her mouth—said, "Yes. I know. That is what has been frightening me since March."
