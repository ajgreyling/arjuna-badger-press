# Fifteen

*Menlo Park — June 1973*

There were, in the end, eleven drawings that did not correspond to anything.

They came out of the envelope trials across about five weeks in the early summer, and they were
handled in the only way that a careful laboratory can handle them, which is as noise. When a subject
produces a drawing and the target is a sailboat and the drawing is not a sailboat, the drawing is a
miss. That is what a miss is. You do not get to go back afterwards and decide that the miss was
interesting.

Targ, to his enormous credit, did not throw them away. He put them in a folder marked NON-TARGET and
wrote a line in the log — *subject produced non-target material of high internal consistency* — and
that folder sat in a filing cabinet at Stanford Research Institute for eleven years and then went
into a box and then into a different box.

Uri never saw it again until he was seventy-eight.

Five of the eleven matter.

—

**The corridor.**

He drew it on a Tuesday. The target was a bunch of keys.

It is a pencil drawing, competent — he could draw, he had a good hand — of a long straight passage in
perspective, going away from the viewer, with regular vertical divisions down both sides that are
clearly racking of some kind, and, past about the halfway point, a change: the racking stops and
becomes a run of plain cabinets, drawn with more pressure, and the perspective lines converge on a
dark rectangle at the end.

He wrote three words under it. He does not remember writing them and they are in his hand.

*It is cold.*

**The black sun.**

A filled circle, about eight centimetres across, gone over and over in pencil until the paper is
shining, with a ragged corona around it — and he pressed so hard in the middle that he went through
the sheet and marked the one underneath.

The target was a tree. He was asked afterwards what it was and said he did not know, and the note
says *subject appeared distressed*, which is a very Targ way of writing that a young man had put his
pencil down and gone out of the room.

**The three figures.**

The one that nobody in that building liked.

Three standing shapes, roughly human, in a row. Not aliens: no large eyes, no domes, nothing from any
cinema. They are simply people-shaped, and they are drawn *hollow* — outlines with nothing in them —
and the head of each one is an empty oval.

There is a note in Uri's hand at the bottom of the page and the note is not a description. It reads:

*3. and then me.*

He has no memory of writing that either.

**The coordinates.**

Two lines of figures in the top corner of a page whose main drawing was a perfectly good and
perfectly correct rendering of the actual target, which was a camel.

Degrees, minutes, seconds. Latitude and longitude, in a conventional format he could not have
explained and had never used, in handwriting that is his but tighter than his.

Somebody — probably Puthoff — plotted them at some point, because there is a pencil tick beside them.
They resolve to an empty stretch of the Negev about a hundred and forty kilometres south of
Beersheba, where there is nothing, and where a check with an atlas confirmed that there was nothing,
and where a second check some months later by somebody with better maps confirmed the same thing.

Nothing there. No installation, no settlement, no road.

There has been nothing there since 1952.

**And the date.**

This is the smallest of the five and the strangest, and it is on the back of the camel drawing,
bottom right, in pencil, in a European hand with the day first and full stops between:

**20.12.2026**

That is all. No note, no annotation, nothing else on that side of the sheet.

Somebody in the laboratory — the handwriting is not any of the three principals — has written beside
it, at some later date, in biro, with a question mark:

*telephone no.?*

—

The five of them went in the folder with the other six and the trials moved on, and this is not a
failure of anybody's intelligence. It is what a laboratory is *for*.

A protocol exists precisely so that you cannot go fishing in your own misses. The moment Targ and
Puthoff had started treating non-target material as significant, every critic they had — and they had
excellent ones — would have been entitled to burn the whole enterprise down, and would have been
right to. The rigour that made the *Nature* paper publishable is the same rigour that put the corridor
in a folder.

That is the tragedy and it is not anybody's fault. The most careful people in the story did the
most careful thing available to them, and it was correct, and it cost fifty-three years.

—

Uri asked about them once, in July, on the way to lunch.

"The ones that are wrong. What happens to them?"

"They go in the file."

"But what happens to them?"

Targ stopped walking. He had a way of stopping when a question was better than it sounded.

"Nothing happens to them," he said. "They're misses, Uri. We record everything. If we started
deciding some of the misses were secretly hits we'd have no experiment at all."

"I know that." He did know it. He had learned a great deal in nine months.

"Then what's the question?"

And Uri Geller, aged twenty-six, in a car park in California, in his fourth language, said the
most accurate sentence he ever said about his own life and was not understood, and could not have
been, and did not blame anybody.

"Where do the ones go," he said, "that are not answers to your question."
