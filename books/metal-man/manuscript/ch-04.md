# Four

*Tel Aviv — 1953*

The compass belonged to the school and lived in a wooden box with a sliding lid, and it came out
twice a year.

Mrs Bendori had it on the front desk on a Tuesday in the spring, for a lesson about north, and she
had drawn a map of the Mediterranean on the board in three colours, and the class of thirty-one was
supposed to come up two at a time and look at the needle and see that it pointed the same way from
everywhere in the room.

That was the entire lesson. The needle points the same way from everywhere in the room. This is why
you can find your way home.

It took about eleven minutes to fall apart.

—

Uri was near the back and was not attending. He was doing the room, which was what he did: the
buckles, the window catch, the ring, a boy called Amnon who had a steel plate in his shoe because of
his foot and who was the second most interesting object in the building.

He came up when he was called, with a girl named Ziva, and he stood at the desk and looked into the
little glass dish, and the needle swung round and pointed at him.

He did not do anything. He wants that understood and it has never once been believed. He was not
trying. He had his hands behind his back because that is where you put your hands when you are at
the front, and he was looking at the needle, and the needle came round through about a hundred and
ten degrees and stopped, quivering slightly, aimed at his sternum.

Ziva said, "It's broken."

Mrs Bendori came over and picked it up and shook it and put it down, and it swung round and pointed
at him again.

—

What happened next took perhaps four minutes and it determined the rest of his life, and the reason
it determined the rest of his life is that it went *wrong* in a way that was not dramatic at all.

Mrs Bendori did not gasp. Nobody screamed. There was no wonder in that classroom, not for one second.

What there was, was a busy woman with thirty-one children and a lesson to get through and a piece of
school equipment behaving stupidly, and a boy she already had marked down as a nuisance standing next
to it.

"Move away, please."

He moved away. The needle followed him.

He got to the window. It followed him.

And it was at this point — with the needle tracking a five-year-old across a classroom, which is a
thing that a hundred and forty million years of geology says should not occur — that Mrs Bendori made
the choice that everybody in this book is going to make, over and over, for seventy years, in
laboratories and studios and newspapers and courts.

She got angry.

"What have you got in your pockets?"

"Nothing."

"Turn them out."

He turned them out. There was a bottle top and some string.

"Give it here."

"It's *string*—"

"Uri Geller, give me what is in your hand."

There was nothing in his hand. She took the bottle top and the string anyway and put them in her desk
and put the compass back in its box and slid the lid shut, and told him to sit down, and got on with
the lesson using the drawing on the board, and the class learned that the needle points the same way
from everywhere in the room.

He sat down.

Nobody ever asked him about it again. Not that year, not ever. There is no note in any file anywhere
about a compass in a school on Betzalel Yaffe Street in the spring of 1953, because nothing happened,
because a boy was fiddling.

—

This is the first time the world chose ridicule over enquiry, and it is worth being exact about what
that means, because the same machine is going to be operated later by people with security
clearances and it will not look any different.

Nobody lied. Mrs Bendori did not decide to suppress anything. She was not incurious as a person — she
kept the class rabbit alive for six years and she was the only teacher in that school who ever wrote
a kind word about him.

What she did was much simpler and much harder to argue with. She was presented with two
possibilities:

*This boy is doing something with a bottle top.*

*The magnetic field of the Earth is negotiable.*

And she chose the one that let the lesson continue.

That is all containment ever is. It does not require malice and it does not require a conspiracy. It
requires only that the alternative is enormous and the afternoon is short.

—

He tried to tell her about the sound once, at the end of that year.

He had worked up to it for weeks. He waited until the room was empty and she was clearing the board,
and he said, in Hebrew, in the flat unpunctuated way that children say the things they have
rehearsed:

"Miss, the metal makes a noise and I want to know if you hear it too."

She turned round.

To her enormous credit — and this is why he thought about her, on and off, for seventy years, and why
he sent a wreath in 1991 — she did not laugh, and she did not say don't be silly, and she put the
cloth down and crouched to his height and thought about it properly for about ten seconds.

Then she said, "What kind of a noise?"

And he could not do it.

He stood in front of the one adult who had ever asked, and he opened his mouth, and there was
nothing there. He had no words for it. He had Hebrew and a bit of Hungarian, and he was six, and the
sound was not a ringing and it was not a buzzing and it was not a whistling and it was not any of the
things there were words for. He said "like rain," which was wrong, and he said "on tin," which was
closer, and he could see her trying, and he could see her not getting it, and he could see her
kindness beginning to tire, and so he said the other thing that was available to him.

He said, "Like this," and he made a face and a noise with his teeth that was meant to be the crackle
and was, unavoidably, funny.

She laughed. It was a nice laugh. It was affectionate and it lasted about two seconds and it was the
laugh you give a small boy who has made a silly noise, and it ended the conversation, and it ended
all conversations of that type for the next twenty years.

He laughed too. He was good at that already.

He went home. He never mentioned it to anybody again — not to his mother, not to Shipi, not to Hanna,
not to a single one of the physicists who would eventually put him in a shielded room and measure
everything about him that could be measured. Russell Targ never knew. Puharich, who wanted so badly
to know everything, never knew. The word *Barkhausen* was never once said in his hearing in his life.

He had made his attempt at being heard, at six, and it had produced a laugh, and he had drawn a
conclusion.

**The conclusion was: do the noise on purpose next time.**

If the only door available is the one marked *entertainment*, a child does not stand outside it on
principle. He goes through it. He goes through it and he gets very, very good at it, and by the time
anybody with the right instruments turns up, twenty years later, wanting the truth, the door has shut
behind him and the man they are talking to has been a performer for so long that he cannot tell them
anything without also, helplessly, performing it.

That is the whole tragedy and it starts here, in an empty classroom, with a kind woman laughing
kindly at exactly the wrong moment.
