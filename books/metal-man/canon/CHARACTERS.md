# CHARACTERS — *The Metal Man*

## Uri Geller — the antenna (POV, four of five acts)

Born 1946. The book follows him from five to eighty.

**The engine of him:** he is a man who was given the most extraordinary thing in human history and
absolutely no equipment for carrying it. No education in it, no tradition, no priesthood, no
vocabulary — a boy in a Tel Aviv apartment with a mother who worked as a seamstress and a father who
had left, and a thing in his hands that made the cutlery fold. Everything he has ever done wrong, he
did because **nobody ever taught him how to be this**, and the only models available to him were
show business.

**What he actually wants**, under all seventy years of it: to be *believed*. Not admired — he has had
oceans of admiration and it does nothing for him. Believed. The distinction is the whole character,
and he could not articulate it until he was about sixty-five.

**The wound:** he is the most watched man of his generation and has never once been seen. Fifty
thousand people in a hall, and not one of them looking at him — they are looking at the spoon.
*(House throughline: the ode to the unheard. Never named on the page, never labelled, present in
every act. See `memory/arjuna-badger-press/the-unheard-nd-ode.md`.)*

**The vanity is real and is not apologised for.** He is a showman. He loves the cars, the famous
friends, the crystals, the merchandise, the sheer noise of it. The novel finds this funny and
touching rather than shameful — a man who was told at twenty-three that the only permitted shape for
a prophet is *entertainer*, and who then became magnificently, exhaustingly good at it.

**The physical tells:** the hands held slightly away from the body near unfamiliar metal, an old
habit he does not know he has. The high crackle he has heard since he was five and assumed everyone
could. Nosebleeds after anything real. Grey afterwards — a specific, ashy grey Hanna can read across
a room.

**Arc:** *I bend metal* → *metal answers me* → *metal has been talking to me since I was five and I
was too busy performing to listen* → *I am not the speaker, I am the instrument* → *and I choose,
once, what to be an instrument for.*

## Hanna Geller — the one who is not impressed

Married to him for fifty years. **The most important person in the book after Uri, and the reader's
anchor to the real.**

She is not a sceptic and not a believer — she is a woman who has lived with it since she was
nineteen and finds it approximately as remarkable as his snoring. She has watched him do it ten
thousand times. She has stopped noticing. This is the deepest form of love available in this novel:
**she is the only person on Earth for whom he is not the metal man.** She has never once asked him
to do it.

She is also the only one who ever solves anything. Uri assembles the mystery over thirty years,
badly, in a state of high drama. Hanna solves the date in four seconds at a kitchen table while
drying a bowl. Comic and devastating in one beat.

**Rule:** she is drawn with maximum warmth and zero secrets. See
[`LIVING_PERSONS.md`](LIVING_PERSONS.md#2-the-five-hard-rules).

## Shipi Shtrang — the brother

Met as a boy; manager, closest companion, brother-in-law for fifty-five years.

**The book's quiet loyalty.** He handles everything — bookings, money, press, the hotel rooms, the
sealed cases, the moments when Uri cannot stand up afterwards. He is the person who was *there* for
every single event in the novel and has never given an interview about any of it.

Drawn as competent, dry, protective and unglamorous. He asks nothing and explains nothing. When Uri
is sick in a hotel bathroom after the Carson show, Shipi runs the tap so the sound of it is covered
and does not ask, and that is the whole character in one gesture.

**Binding:** the confederate allegation is not raised. It has no work to do here.

## Andrija Puharich — the one who was right and ruined it

American physician, inventor, parapsychologist. 1971: comes to Israel and finds him.

**The tragedy of the book.** He is genuinely brilliant, genuinely serious, and receives something
genuinely real — and he has only one vocabulary for it, and it is the wrong one. He hears a signal
and calls it *Spectra*, broadcasting from a craft called *Hoova*, and he writes the book that makes
it impossible for any serious scientist to stand near Uri Geller again.

He is not a fool. **He is a warning about interpretation** — the novel's own risk, made flesh. He
gets a scene near the end of his life, in 1994, in which he is entirely lucid and entirely wrong and
knows it, and it should be hard to read.

## Russell Targ & Harold Puthoff — the ones who did it properly

The SRI physicists. Portrayed as rigorous, methodical and careful, because that is what they were
and are. They are alive: no invented failings.

They function as the book's **procedural spine** in Act II. The pleasure of those chapters is
*method* — sealed rooms, double-blind protocol, controls tightened and tightened again. The horror
is that the tighter the controls get, the more clearly something is happening.

## Eldon Byrd — the man with the wire

Naval Surface Weapons Center. 1973. Nitinol.

Small part, enormous function: **he is the one who understands what he is looking at.** Everyone else
in the book is watching a spoon. Byrd is watching memory metal change what it remembers, and he
knows what that means for a firing pin, and he goes very quiet, and he files a report that is not
believed.

His scene is short, technical, and the coldest in Act II.

## James Randi — the antagonist who is right

See [`THE_CONTAINMENT.md`](THE_CONTAINMENT.md#3-what-randi-is-binding--get-this-wrong-and-the-book-is-worthless)
for the full binding treatment. In brief: a great man, an honourable career, a superb method with
one blind spot — and, held as unproven inference only, the possibility that he knew all along and
made a hard choice for the world.

**He is given his best arguments, at full strength, and the book never mocks him.**

The corridor scene, 1993: ninety seconds, both of them know, neither says it. Act III's peak.

## The metal — the second narrator

Not a character. A **register**. It appears as short interstitial passages between the acts and
inside a handful of scenes: present tense, no pronoun for itself, no opinion, no motive.

It never speaks in language. It delivers **sequence, pressure, temperature and duration** — what was
done, in the order it was done. It is always deniable as Uri's synaesthesia. It is the most
beautiful writing in the book and there must be very little of it: **six or seven appearances in the
whole novel.** See [`STYLE_GUIDE.md`](STYLE_GUIDE.md).

## The invented cast (composite — check names against the record)

- **A. Meir** — Israeli liaison, 1969–72. Recruits nobody, promises nothing, is simply always there.
  Ages through the whole book. In Act V he is very old and does one decent thing.
- **Dr. Frances Okonjo-Hale** — metallurgist, invented, Act IV–V. The one working scientist who takes
  the geometry seriously. She is the reader's technical proxy and the person who says the sentence
  *"this is a Widmanstätten figure and it is not a cooling figure."*
- **"The producer," "the editor," "the officer"** — deliberately unnamed functionaries of the
  containment. Given no menace and total reasonableness. See
  [`THE_CONTAINMENT.md`](THE_CONTAINMENT.md#5-the-rules-that-keep-this-from-being-conspiracy-slop).
- **Danny Ofir** — one of the British "mini-Geller" children of 1974, followed at three points across
  the book: eleven, thirty, sixty. He is the proof that it spread, the proof of what it costs, and
  the last voice in the novel. **He is fictional.**
