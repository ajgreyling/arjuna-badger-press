# CONTENT_RATING — *The Metal Man*

**Rating: adult general readership.** No sexual content, no graphic violence, no profanity of note.
The difficulty in this book is entirely psychological and historical.

## The gates

**War (ch. 8).** The Six-Day War is rendered from inside a twenty-year-old with no politics and no
overview: a boy, a noise, and afterwards a ward. **No strategy, no geopolitics, no commentary, no
body count as spectacle.** The wounded are individuals, briefly and with dignity. The chapter's
subject is a failure of power, not a battle.

> **Binding:** the novel takes no position on the Israeli–Palestinian conflict, then or now, and
> contains no material that reads as one. Uri's Israeli childhood and service are biographical fact
> and are rendered as personal experience only. The Arab garden of ch. 2 is a real feature of his
> account of his own childhood; it is written as a five-year-old's garden and nothing is built on it.

**The Holocaust adjacency.** His parents' generation, Hungarian-Jewish. It is present in the way it
is present in every Israeli household of 1952 — as the thing that is not discussed at the table — and
it is never used for effect, never dramatised, and never made a source of his power.

**Children (ch. 23–24).** Children who are tested, written to, and in a few cases visited. This is
handled with restraint and without any depiction of harm. The horror is institutional and implied.
No child is frightened on the page for the reader's benefit.

**Medical / bodily.** Nosebleeds, exhaustion, collapse. In ch. 39, an interior event involving the
iron in blood, written at kitchen scale and without gore. **No body horror.**

**Bereavement.** Puharich's death (ch. 29). A dead airman's watch, throughout.

**Real living persons.** The most sensitive material in the book is not violence — it is that the
subject is alive. See [`LIVING_PERSONS.md`](LIVING_PERSONS.md).
