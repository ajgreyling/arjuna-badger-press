# Nine

*Tel Aviv—1969*

He met Shipi Shtrang because a fourteen-year-old boy refused to be got rid of, which turned out to be
the boy's entire personality and the best thing that ever happened to Uri Geller.

He was twenty-two and out of the army and doing nothing in particular. He had modelled a bit—
knitwear, mostly, for catalogues, because he photographed well and cost nothing, and he had worked
in a factory, and he had taken a job that summer as a counsellor at a camp for children, which paid
badly and fed him.

There was an afternoon with rain and forty bored children in a hall.

And Uri, who had not done it in front of anybody except his mother in fourteen years, and who had
absolutely no excuse for what he did next except that it was raining and he was twenty-two and they
were bored and he wanted them to like him—Uri took a spoon off the tea trolley and bent it.

Forty children screamed.

Thirty-nine of them screamed the way children scream at a firework.

One of them did not scream at all. One of them, at the back, a skinny kid of fourteen with a sharp
face, went very quiet and watched the whole thing, and then watched Uri's hands for the rest of the
afternoon, and then came and found him afterwards while he was stacking chairs and said, without any
preamble whatsoever:

"You should charge for that."

—

That is the founding sentence of the entire enterprise and it was said by a child.

Shipi Shtrang did not ask him how he did it. He never has. In fifty-seven years, through
laboratories and lawsuits and three continents and every hostile journalist alive, Shipi Shtrang has
never once asked Uri Geller how he does it, and this is the reason Uri loves him.

What Shipi asked, that afternoon, stacking chairs, was: how many can you do in a night, do you get
tired, can you do it if you are tired, what happens if it doesn't work, and who is going to hire the
hall.

He was fourteen. He had worked out the whole business model before the chairs were stacked.

—

And then there was Hanna.

Shipi's sister was seventeen and did not care.

This has to be stated correctly because it is the axis the rest of a life turns on. She was not a
sceptic. She did not disbelieve him and she did not test him and she never, in the whole of their
lives together, asked him to prove anything to anybody. Nor was she a believer, she has never once
in fifty-seven years used the word *gift*, or *power*, or any of the seventy words that the world
has thrown at him.

She met a good-looking, jumpy, charming young man who was making her little brother extremely
excited, and at some point in the first month she watched him bend a key at her mother's kitchen
table, and her reaction—witnessed, and reported by Shipi with relish for the next five decades—
was to look at it for about two seconds and say:

"That was my key."

That is all. That is the whole thing. Somebody in the world was in the room with the impossible and
was mainly concerned about her key.

He asked her out three times and was turned down three, which he has always mentioned and she has
always disputed the arithmetic of.

—

The first paid show was in a small hall in Jaffa in the autumn of 1969, and the poster is the reason
this book has the title it has.

Shipi had it printed. He was fourteen and he had gone to a printer with money he had got from
somewhere and had chosen the wording himself and had not consulted anybody, and Uri arrived on the
night and saw it pasted up by the door.

It said his name. Underneath it, in the largest type on the sheet, it said:

**HA-ISH SHEL HA-MATEKHET**

*The Metal Man.*

He stood in the street and looked at it for a long time and did not say anything.

It was, he thought, the ugliest thing he had ever seen with his name on it. It sounded like a
wrestler. It sounded like a man who swallows razor blades at the end of a pier, and that, precisely,
is the register it was aiming for, because a fourteen-year-old promoter in Jaffa in 1969 was not
selling a mystery. He was selling forty seats.

"You don't like it," Shipi said.

"It's—" He looked for the English word and did not have it and used the Hebrew one. "It's cheap."

"It's full," said Shipi, and went in.

—

Hanna came to the third show and did not tell him she was coming.

She sat at the back with a friend, and afterwards, in the corridor, with Uri sweating and elated and
twenty-two, she said the thing that he has quoted in every long interview he has ever given and that
she has always maintained he has slightly improved over the years.

He asked her what she thought.

And Hanna Shtrang, aged seventeen, having watched a hall of ninety people lose their minds over a man
she had recently turned down twice, said:

"You talk too much."

"What?"

"When it works you say nothing and it is good." She was putting her coat on. "When it does not
work you talk. You talk and talk and you make jokes and you apologise. And everybody knows."

"They don't know—"

"Uri." She got the second sleeve. "*I* know, and I have seen you do it five times."

He argued about it for a week and then rebuilt the act around it, and it is the single most valuable
piece of professional advice he ever received, and it came from a seventeen-year-old who had no
interest in the paranormal whatsoever and never subsequently developed one.

The rebuilt version was this: when nothing happens, stop, and be silent, and let it be nothing.

It is unbelievably hard. It is the hardest thing in performance and most professionals never manage
it, and Uri Geller learned it at twenty-two in a corridor in Jaffa and then executed it, three years
later, for twenty-two minutes, in front of fifteen million Americans, on the biggest programme in
the world.

Everybody watching thought they were seeing a man fail.

They were watching him do the thing his wife taught him.

—

The other thing that happened in that first season, and it is in no biography because there was no
reason for anybody to write it down, is that he started giving the money away almost immediately.

Not all of it. He was not a saint and he wanted a car. But the pattern that would eventually become
conspicuous, the watches handed to strangers, the enormous unasked-for gifts, the compulsive
generosity that several people found slightly alarming—starts here, in 1969, in a hall in Jaffa,
with a doorman.

The doorman's name was Yitzhak and he was about sixty and he had a bad chest, and on the fourth night
Uri gave him a third of the take.

Shipi, who was fourteen and running the books, was extremely annoyed about it and said so, and Uri
could not explain himself then and could not explain himself for another fifty years.

The explanation, when it finally arrived, arrived in a kitchen in Sonning in about 2019, and it was
four words long, and Hanna wrote it down on the back of an envelope because she thought it was the
truest thing he had ever said about himself.

He said: "They paid to be lied to."

He did not believe that. That is the point. He knew perfectly well that what happened in that hall
was real, at least most of the time, at least on the good nights.

But he had done the sock spoon at six and he had used a stooge twice by the end of 1969 and he was
never afterwards able to be entirely certain, on any given night, which kind of night it had been—
and so he paid people back, at random, for the rest of his life, in case.

—

He did seventy minutes and he was, by every surviving account, mediocre.

He bent things. He guessed drawings. He started watches. He talked too fast and he apologised when it
did not work, which is the one thing you must never do, and twice it did not work, and he stood in
front of ninety people in a hall in Jaffa with a spoon that would not move and learned the most
important professional lesson of his life, which is that you fill the silence with charm or you die.

He filled it with charm. He got away with it. He came off soaked through.

And in the corridor afterwards a man he did not know, in his forties, in a jacket too warm for the
evening, was leaning against the wall.

"Do you do private work?" the man said.

"What?"

"Parties. Small groups." He had a pleasant face and an entirely unmemorable one. "Some people would
pay well to see that in a room rather than a hall."

"You are from a newspaper?"

"No," said A. Meir, and shook his hand, and did not give a name that night or for another eleven
years, and went away.

—

Here is the thing about that poster, and it is worth putting down now because it will be a long
time before it comes back.

Uri Geller hated it, and he was right to hate it, and it was accurate.

Not accurate about him, he was not, in 1969, the metal man. He was a young man with an unusual
talent and a fourteen-year-old manager and a girl he was trying to impress. It was accurate about
what the world was going to be prepared to do with him, and Shipi Shtrang, aged fourteen, with no
education in it whatsoever, had gone into a printer's shop and found the exact three words that the
twentieth century was willing to accept.

Not *the man who hears metal*. Nobody would come.

Not *the man who reads the history of forged objects*. Forty empty seats.

**The Metal Man.** Slightly pulpy. Almost dismissible. A poster you walk past.

He is going to spend the next fifty-seven years inside that poster, and he is going to hate it for
about forty of them, and then he is going to understand what it was for, and by then it will be the
only thing keeping his family alive.

He never took it down. It is in a frame in a converted soap factory in Jaffa and you can go and look
at it.
