# Thirty

*Sonning-on-Thames—1996 to 2016*

For twenty years nothing happened to Uri Geller, and it is the most productive period of his life.

—

The house was by the river and it had a lawn that went down to the water and a boathouse he never
used, and he lived in it with Hanna and their two children and, for long stretches, with Shipi, and
it was—this needs saying, because there is no other place in this book to say it, an extremely
happy house.

That is not the story anybody wants. The story people want about a man in Uri Geller's position is a
decline: the bitterness, the drink, the obsessive litigating, the mad old boy on the sofa. And there
is footage that supports it if you cut it right.

But the actual texture of those two decades is a man of fifty in a garden by a river with his family,
who was rich, who had good friends, who travelled constantly, who was recognised everywhere and
minded about none of it, who took up painting and was terrible at it, and who was—by the account of
everybody who spent time in that house—funny, generous to the point of being annoying about it, and
almost impossible to embarrass.

He was on television about crystals. He did the football. He bought an island. He was best man
material for famous people and got photographed at it. He opened things.

And every morning, for twenty years, at about seven o'clock, before anybody else was up, he sat at
the kitchen table with three newspapers and a pair of scissors.

—

It started, as far as he can date it, in 1997, and it started because of a bridge in Portugal.

A road bridge over the Douro went down in the spring of that year during a flood and a bus went into
the river and a great many people were killed. It was in the papers for two days.

He read the piece at the kitchen table and something in it stopped him, and he read it again, and he
could not have told anybody what had caught his attention, and he cut it out.

That is the whole of the beginning. A man cut a thing out of a newspaper because it snagged on
something and he did not know what.

What had snagged was one clause in the fourth paragraph, the pier had failed at a point where
scouring had exposed a foundation, and the failure had propagated through a steel element that,
according to a quoted engineer, *had not been considered to be near its limit*.

Uri Geller had been listening to metal complain about being near its limit since he was five years
old.

—

He did not have a theory. He wants that understood, and it is true, and it is the reason the folder
is worth anything at all: for the first eleven years there was no hypothesis whatsoever. He was not
looking for anything. He could not have told you what he was collecting.

He cut out things that snagged.

A turbine disc in a power station in Ohio, 1999, which burst in a way the manufacturer described as
without precedent in forty years of the type.

A crane in Rotterdam, 2001.

A reaction wheel on a satellite, 2003, that one he had to have explained to him by a man at a dinner
party, and he asked several questions and the man remembered it years later because they were good
questions.

An airliner, 2004, an inspection finding, a crack in a wing rib in a location that the fatigue model
said should be among the last places to go and that had gone first.

A gas centrifuge cascade, 2006, which he cut out of the *Financial Times* and which is the only one
in the folder he could not have explained his interest in to a policeman.

A bridge hanger. A pressure vessel weld. A landing gear. A reactor cladding inspection in Sweden. A
rail failure at Grayrigg. A wind turbine in Denmark that came apart in a moderate wind.

By 2016 there were two hundred and nineteen of them, in nine folders, in a filing cabinet in the
boathouse, and every single one had a date and a place written on it in his own hand in the top right
corner, because he had started doing that in about 2002 and had gone back and done the earlier ones.

—

Hanna knew about the folders and did not ask about them, which was the arrangement in that house for
fifty years.

Shipi asked once, in about 2009, and got an answer that was not an answer.

"What is it?"

"Metal that stopped."

"Stopped what?"

"Being what it was," Uri said, and went out to the garden.

—

Here is what he did not do, and it matters enormously, and it is the thing that separates this from
every other set of nine folders kept by an elderly man with a theory.

**He never told anybody it meant anything.**

Not once, in twenty years. He did not write about it. He did not mention it on television, and he was
on television constantly and could have. He did not put it in a book, and he published four books in
that period. He did not tell a journalist.

A man who was, by universal agreement, the most relentless self-publicist in the history of the
paranormal, a man who would ring a newspaper about a bent spoon—sat on the single most interesting
thing he ever assembled for twenty years and said nothing to anyone.

The two readings are both available and the reader should hold both.

**One:** he had nothing, knew he had nothing, and was doing what lonely obsessives do, which is to
maintain a private folder that feels like work.

**Two:** he had learned, in a private room off Jermyn Street in 1975, precisely what happens when
Uri Geller makes something credible, and he had spent forty years becoming a man nobody would listen
to, and he was not going to undo all of it for two hundred and nineteen newspaper clippings that he
could not read properly.

—

He was seventy in December 2016. There was a party. There were a great many people and one of them
was a metallurgist.
