# Thirty-One

*Sonning-on-Thames—December 2016, and after*

Dr Frances Okonjo-Hale came to the party because her husband did something in television and she had
been promised a garden, and she was polite about the crystals for about an hour.

She was fifty-one. She was a materials scientist—failure analysis, specifically, which is the branch
of the discipline concerned with the question *why did this break*, and she had spent twenty-six
years doing forensic metallurgy for insurers, regulators and courts, which meant she had stood in a
great many hangars and pumping stations looking at pieces of metal that had recently killed somebody.

She did not believe a word of anything. She was not aggressive about it. She simply had, as she put
it later, "no available slot."

Uri Geller found her in the kitchen at about ten o'clock and asked what she did, which he asked
everybody, and she told him, and he went very still.

"You look at the metal afterwards."

"That's the job."

"And you can tell? From the metal. What happened to it."

"Usually." She was not being drawn. "That's not mystical, Mr Geller. It's a fracture surface. It's a
record. You read it."

And Uri Geller—seventy years old, in his own kitchen, at his own birthday party, with a paper hat
somebody had put on him—said:

"Yes."

Something in the way he said it made her look up.

—

She agreed to look at the folders to be kind, and she said so, and she said so to him, and he said
that was fine.

She came on a Tuesday in January. She was going to give it an hour.

—

The first forty minutes were exactly what she expected and she has said so in every account she has
ever given of it: an old man, a filing cabinet, nine folders, two hundred and nineteen newspaper
clippings, and a theory that was not stated because there was no theory.

The clippings themselves were rubbish as data. Newspaper accounts of engineering failures are written
by people who do not understand engineering, for readers who do not either, and about a third of them
were simply wrong on the facts in ways she could see at a glance.

She said so. He said, "Yes, I know. I can't read the real ones."

"The real ones?"

"The reports. The inquiries." He shrugged. "They are on the internet, some of them. My daughter
showed me. But I cannot read them. I don't have the—" he did the thing with his hands. "I know
about eleven words."

She sat back.

"So what have you got?"

"I have the date and the place."

—

Here is what turned an hour into six.

She had assumed—reasonably, and she was wrong, that the folders were a collection of *anomalies*:
a credulous man's scrapbook of weird events, chosen because they were spooky.

They were not. She realised it at about the fifty-minute mark and it made the back of her neck cold.

They were a collection of failures of a very specific and technical kind, and the specificity was
consistent across two decades, and it was a kind that a layman could not possibly have selected for,
because it does not look like anything from outside.

Every single item in those folders was a **failure below expected load in a component with no
manufacturing defect.**

Not accidents. Not collapses from overloading or corrosion or bad maintenance or a lorry hitting
something. In every one of the two hundred and nineteen, the metal had let go while doing something
it had done a thousand times before, and the investigation afterwards had found, and this is the
part that made her put her coffee down—*nothing wrong with the metal*.

That is a real category. It is the least satisfying finding in her entire profession and there is a
term of art for the paragraph it produces, which is *no root cause identified*, and it appears in
perhaps one report in forty, and everybody in the field hates it, and everybody in the field has
about a dozen of them in a drawer that they think about at night.

He had two hundred and nineteen.

"How did you choose these?"

"They snagged."

"That isn't an answer, Mr Geller."

"I know." He looked genuinely apologetic. "It is the only one I have. I read the newspaper and
something in it—" He made a small gesture, opening his fingers. "It is the same feeling as when I
am in a room and there is a thing in the room."

—

She took photographs of every clipping and went home and did not sleep much and spent the next four
months doing, in her own time, the piece of work that ended her career as an ordinary person.

She pulled the actual reports. All of them that existed—inquiry findings, regulator bulletins,
manufacturer service notices, in six languages, using colleagues for the ones she could not read.
She threw out fifty-one of the two hundred and nineteen because the real report identified a proper
cause that the newspaper had missed.

That left one hundred and sixty-eight.

She built a table. Date, location to the best precision available, component, alloy, service history,
loading at failure, and, because she is a rigorous person and because this was the obvious thing to
check first—she assembled a control set: four hundred *comparable* no-root-cause failures from the
same period that were **not** in Uri Geller's folders.

Then she plotted both.

—

She came back in June and she had a laptop and she did not take her coat off.

"Where is your wife?"

"In the garden. Why?"

"Because I want somebody else in the room," said Frances Okonjo-Hale, "and because I have been
sitting on this on my own since March and it is making me unwell."

—

What she showed him took about twenty minutes and Uri understood perhaps a fifth of it, which was
enough.

The control set was random. That is what it looked like and that is what it was: four hundred dots on
a world map with a time axis, distributed exactly the way industrial accidents are distributed, which
is to say clustered where the industry is, and otherwise noise.

His one hundred and sixty-eight were not random.

They were not clustered where the industry was. They were clustered along something, a set of arcs
that did not correspond to plate boundaries, or magnetic anomalies, or shipping lanes, or grid
interconnectors, or anything she had been able to think of and she had thought of forty-one things
and tested every one.

And they were not evenly distributed in time either.

The interval between events was shortening. Not steadily, and not smoothly, but the trend was there
and it had been there for twenty years, and when she fitted it the fit was better than she wanted it
to be.

"That is a geometry," she said. "I don't know what of. I want to be extremely clear that I do not
know what of, and that there are eleven ways this could be an artefact of how you chose them, and I
have tested nine of them and I cannot get rid of it."

Uri looked at the screen for a long time.

Then he said: "What is it doing?"

"It isn't *doing* anything, it's a distribution—"

"No." He was not arguing. He had gone somewhere else and his voice had changed, and she said later
that this was the moment she stopped being able to manage him as a client. "Look. Here. And here.
This one, and then this one after it, and then this one."

He put his finger on three points in sequence.

"Each one is smaller," he said. "The metal that goes. The pieces are smaller each time, yes? And the
place where it goes is more—" he searched for it—"more chosen."

Frances Okonjo-Hale looked at the three failures he had picked out of one hundred and sixty-eight in
about four seconds, without reading the labels, in a language he could not read.

They were the three she had flagged herself, in March, at two in the morning, as the cleanest
examples in the set.

"Mr Geller," she said. "How did you do that?"

"I told you." He sat back. "It snagged."
