# Thirty-Eight

*Bristol—Sunday 20 December 2026, 16:00*

The studio was a converted unit on an industrial estate and there were three cameras and nine people
and a gallery the size of a bathroom, and at four o'clock in the afternoon on the twentieth of
December the live count was three thousand nine hundred and forty.

Uri Geller was eighty years old that day. He had a bad hip and reading glasses on a cord and a good
suit that was slightly too big for him now, and he sat on a chair in front of a grey wall because
Danny Ofir had made the correct decision, which was that there should be no set at all.

"Whenever you're ready, Mr Geller."

He looked at the lens.

—

He did not explain.

This has been criticised since and it is the single most professional decision of his career. He had
eleven minutes and an audience of four thousand and there is no possible way to explain, in any
number of minutes, to any number of people, what was actually happening, and every second spent
trying would have been a second in which somebody switched off.

So he did what he had done in a hall in Jaffa in 1969 with ninety people and a spoon that would not
move, which is that he filled it with the only thing he has ever had.

"My name is Uri Geller," he said. "You know who I am. Most of you think I am a fraud, and that is
all right, and I am not going to argue with you about it today, because there is not time and because
it does not matter."

He put his hands on his knees.

"I am eighty years old today. I have done this in front of people since I was twenty-two, and I have
been laughed at in every country there is, and I have never once asked anybody for anything.

"I am asking now.

"Please go and get something made of metal. A spoon. A key. A watch that does not work. Anything—
it does not have to be special, it is better if it is not special, it should be the sort of thing that
is in a drawer.

"Go and get it. I will wait."

—

And then he waited.

He waited for four minutes and eleven seconds of live broadcast, in silence, an old man on a chair in
front of a grey wall, not filling it, not talking, not performing, looking into a lens.

It is the most extraordinary piece of television anybody has ever transmitted and it went out to four
thousand people on eleven platforms from an industrial estate outside Bristol.

At minute two the count was nine thousand.

At minute three it was sixty thousand.

At minute four, and Danny Ofir has a screenshot of this and has never been able to look at it for
long—the count went past four hundred thousand and the graph on the gallery monitor stopped being
a graph and became a vertical line, and the platform's own systems began to throttle, and the thing
that had happened to a telephone exchange in west London on the twenty-third of November 1973
happened again on a scale that nobody had built for.

Because it is not 1973, and there are no gatekeepers, and the machine in every pocket on Earth does
exactly one thing supremely well.

By minute six it was in nine languages.

By minute eight there is no reliable figure and there never will be. The platforms have never
released the numbers. The lowest serious estimate is two hundred million and the highest is not
worth repeating.

—

"Thank you," said Uri Geller, at eight minutes past four in the afternoon. "Now hold it."

—

Here is the mechanism, and it is the whole argument of this novel arriving at its conclusion, and it
is not a twist because it has been on every page since chapter one.

He has never been a metre-scale phenomenon.

Alone in a room he is a man who can bend cutlery—a curiosity, a party trick, a career. Every
laboratory that ever tested him tested him alone in a room, which is correct methodology and which
measured, for fifty-three years, the wrong thing.

Through a live broadcast into a very large number of homes he is something else entirely, and nobody
has ever been able to explain why, including him, and Puharich measured it in a hotel room in 1971
and wrote it down and could not account for it, and it is the central unexplained fact of his life:

*It works better through glass. And through water. And best of all through a sealed glass jar of
water.*

A screen is glass.

And behind every screen on Earth, at eight minutes past four on the twentieth of December 2026, there
was a person: which is to say a sealed vessel of water, holding a piece of metal, looking at him.

Two hundred million of them.

Not an audience. **A circuit**—every one of them a coupling, every one of them holding a small
piece of the thing that the thing lives in, all of them connected through a system whose entire
substance from microphone to transmitter to aerial to the metal in their own hands is conductive.

The trivial objects are the mechanism. They always were. That is why it always came through a spoon.

—

He said: "Do not squeeze it."

He said it in English and it was translated live, badly, in eleven languages, and it did not matter.

"Do not force it. Please. Whatever happens, do not force it—hold it as if it is something you are
looking after."

And two hundred million people, most of whom thought he was a fraud, and a great many of whom had
opened the stream for a laugh, stood in kitchens and hallways and offices and bus shelters in
forty-one countries holding a spoon or a key or their grandmother's watch, not squeezing.

—

**16:11 GMT.**

Every clock in this book stopped.

The one in the corridor in Buckinghamshire, which had been running since 1973 and going the wrong
way. The nine instruments in three wooden trays in a hangar near Farnborough, which had stopped
together in 1973 and had not moved since and which now stopped again in the sense that a stopped
thing can stop, which is that the last man to work in that building swore afterwards that they had
been *about* to. The Singer sewing machine sold in Tel Aviv in 1957, wherever it is.

And in a bedside drawer in a bungalow outside Sleaford, in a house owned now by a woman who had
inherited it from an aunt and had never opened that drawer because there was a will with an odd
instruction in it, an RAF officer's watch that had been running backwards since twenty past ten on
the twenty-third of November 1973 stopped, at four minutes past eleven, which is where it had stopped
the first time, in February 1947, over the sea off Norfolk.

It had taken it seventy-nine years to get back.

—

In the gallery in Bristol every monitor went out at once.

Danny Ofir, sixty-three, stood in the dark with his headphones round his neck and the fork from 1974
in his jacket pocket, and through the glass he could see, in the light from the one camera that was
still running on its own battery, an old man sitting on a chair with his eyes shut.

And Uri Geller said, to two hundred million people and to something else:

"All right. Now watch. I am going to show you how to do it, and I am only going to be able to do it
once, so please pay attention.

"Slowly.

"You must go slowly."
