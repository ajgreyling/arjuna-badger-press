# Thirty-Nine

*Everywhere—16:11 to 16:12 GMT*

For one minute, everywhere on Earth, metal was soft.

—

**In a kitchen in Sonning-on-Thames**, Hanna Geller was drying a bowl.

She had not gone to Bristol. He had asked her not to and she had agreed with an ease that neither of
them examined, and she had spent the afternoon doing what she had done on the afternoon of every
performance of his life for fifty-seven years, which was something else.

The stream was on a tablet propped against the fruit bowl and she was not really watching it.

At eleven minutes past four the bowl in her hands went soft.

It was a stainless steel mixing bowl that had been in that house since 1994. It sagged between her
fingers like something warm, and the rim of it went out of round, and she stood at the sink and
looked at it, and it did not burn her and it was not hot and it did not make a sound.

She put it down on the draining board very carefully, the way you put down something asleep.

Then she looked at the taps, which had gone slightly oval. And the handle of the fridge, which had a
sag in it. And the hinges. And the little brass thing on the window catch that had never worked
properly since 1998.

The whole kitchen was standing there being not quite sure of itself.

And Hanna Geller, sixty-nine years old, who had never once in fifty-seven years asked her husband to
bend anything, put both hands flat on the counter and said, out loud, to an empty room:

"All right, Uri."

—

It was the same everywhere and it was not dramatic anywhere, which is the thing that made it
survivable.

Nothing collapsed. That is the entire point of the lesson. Two hundred
million people held a spoon and every piece of metal on the planet went, for one minute, into the
state that this book has been calling *not sure* since chapter two, and then came back, and came
back *where it had been*, because it was not being forced, because somebody was showing it how, very
slowly, for the last time.

Bridges did not fall. Aircraft did not fall. Reactors did not fail. Not one person died anywhere in
the world in that minute of a cause that could be attributed to it, which is a fact that was
established over about nine months afterwards by four separate insurance consortia who had every
commercial reason to establish the opposite.

What happened was smaller and it happened in about a billion households at once.

Cutlery drooped in drawers and straightened. Keys sagged in locks and came back. Wedding rings went
oval on fingers and returned, and a great many people looked at their own hands and then decided they
had not seen it. Railings went slightly slack under palms on a pedestrian bridge in Osaka and firmed
up again. Every spoon in a canteen in Rosario moved half a millimetre in unison and moved back.

And ten thousand kilometres of steel racking in a corridor in Buckinghamshire creaked all at once,
like a house in a wind, and did not move at all, because past the halfway point there was a black
mass the size of a small car on a steel cradle and it had gone completely still.

—

**In a converted unit on an industrial estate outside Bristol**, an eighty-year-old man sat on a
chair in the dark with his eyes shut and did the thing he had refused for fifty-nine years.

—

He had known where the door was since a school textbook in Nicosia when he was fourteen.

Four grams. Iron, in every human being who has ever lived, bound four atoms to the molecule into the
protein that carries the oxygen, and it is the reason blood is red, and it is the one place on this
planet where metal and a living person are not two things.

He had spent fifty-nine years not thinking about it, from a ward outside Beersheba in the July of
1967, where he had lain among men full of shrapnel and had tried for four weeks to move it and had
failed completely and had built his entire life on the failure.

He had been right about the fence. Flesh is not metal. It has never been metal and he could no more
have moved a fragment of casing in a sleeping engineer's thigh at eighty than he could at nineteen.

But he was not trying to move anything now.

He was the *join*, that is what Hanna had said in a sitting room five days earlier, and she was
right, and it had taken her about a second, and a join has to be made of something, and everything
that had ever attenuated in seventy-four years had attenuated at the surface of his own skin, because
that is where the metal stopped and the man began.

Except that it does not stop there. It has never stopped there.

There are four grams of it going round.

—

He opened it.

There is no describing this and the book is not going to try, because there is nothing in the register
of a novel or in any of Uri Geller's four languages that corresponds to it, and because he has never
been able to say one word about it, and because the man himself is on record for seventy years as
being unable to articulate.

What can be said is what it looked like from the one camera that was still running on its battery,
which is: nothing.

An old man sitting on a chair with his eyes shut for fifty-one seconds.

His hands were on his knees. He did not move them. At about the twentieth second his nose began to
bleed and went on bleeding and he did not lift a hand to it. At about the fortieth second his mouth
opened slightly. At the fiftieth second his right hand closed, once, on the arm of the chair.

That is the entire visual record of the largest event in the history of the species and it is
seventeen seconds of usable footage and it looks like a very old man having a small stroke on a
chair in a room with a grey wall.

—

And in the middle of it, at the place where he was neither one thing nor the other—where two
hundred million sealed vessels of water were holding a piece of it and looking at him through glass,
and the whole of it was loose and waiting and not being forced—Uri Geller taught something the last
thing it was ever going to learn from a person.

He did not use words. It has never used words and neither did he.

He gave it the only thing he had, which was the shape of what he had been doing since he was five
years old in a garden in Tel Aviv with both hands on an iron gate, and which he had said out loud
eleven thousand times on television in nine languages for money and had never once been believed
about:

*Do not force it.*

*Find where it is not sure.*

*Wait until it is willing.*

*And then put it back the way it was.*

—

At 16:12 GMT, everything came back.

—

The last thing that happened, and only one person in the world felt it, and he was in a chair in
Bristol with his eyes shut and his face wet with blood:

Something stopped.

The way a room full of people stops when somebody comes in.

For about two seconds.

And then, and this is the sixth and final appearance in this novel of the thing that has no voice,
and it is one syllable long, and it is not a word, and it arrived the way everything ever arrived to
him, which is as sensation and pressure and sequence with no language on it at all—

it went on.

—

Frances Okonjo-Hale was in the car park.

She had driven to Bristol on the Saturday and had not gone into the building, because she had decided
somewhere around Swindon that whatever this was, she did not want to be in the room as a scientist,
and there was no other capacity in which she was entitled to be there.

So she sat in a Vauxhall on an industrial estate with the engine off and the stream on her phone and
a spoon from her own kitchen drawer on the passenger seat, which she had brought and had not intended
to bring and had told nobody about.

At eight minutes past four she picked it up, because he asked, and because after nine years she was
not going to sit in a car park and not do the one thing he had ever asked her to do.

At eleven minutes past four the spoon went soft in her hand.

She has described what happened next in exactly the same words every time she has been asked, which
is four times in nine years, always reluctantly, and she has never allowed it to be recorded:

"I put my other hand under it. I don't know why. It wasn't hot and it wasn't going to fall. I just
did not want it to be unsupported."

Then, after a pause, every time:

"And then I cried in a car park, which I have not done since my mother died, and I want to be clear
that I still do not know what happened and I never will, and I have not changed my professional
opinion about anything."

—

The thing she could not say, and which she got closest to in 2031 at the funeral, talking to Hanna at
the back of a room in Sonning with a cup of tea she did not want:

She had spent thirty-five years reading metal. It was the whole of her working life. She could look
at a fracture surface and tell a court what had happened, and she had stood in hangars and pumping
stations and once in a school gymnasium, and every single time the metal had already failed and
somebody was already dead and her job was to explain, afterwards, in careful language, exactly how it
had gone.

Thirty-five years of arriving too late.

And for one minute on the twentieth of December, in a Vauxhall on an industrial estate, every piece
of metal on Earth had been *asked* something instead of tested to destruction, and it had been
asked politely, by a man in a chair who could barely speak English properly, and it had said yes, and
then everything had gone back exactly where it was and nobody had died anywhere in the world.

"It was the only good day," she said to Hanna. "In the whole of my career. It was the only time it
went the other way."

—

Inside the building, at twelve minutes past four, an eighty-year-old man opened his eyes in a room
with a grey wall and could not lift his hands off his knees.

Danny Ofir came through from the gallery at a run and got there first, and the thing Uri said to him
, which is not on any recording, because the camera battery had gone at 16:11 and forty seconds—was
not about the metal and was not about the world.

He said: "Did they hold them properly?"

"...What?"

"The people." His voice was not working well. "Did they hold them properly. Did they press."

And Danny Ofir, sixty-three, crouching on a studio floor in Bristol next to a man who had just done
whatever he had just done, said the only true thing available, which he had been reading off eleven
platforms for the last ninety seconds:

"No," he said. "Nobody pressed. They were all being so careful with them, Uri. Every single one."

And Uri Geller shut his eyes again and said, "Good," and that was the last thing he said for about
four hours.
