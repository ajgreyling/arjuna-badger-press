# Seventeen

*Menlo Park—August 1973*

He worked it out at a bench on his own on a Thursday afternoon and had to sit down on the floor.

It was not a dramatic afternoon. Everybody else was at a meeting. He had done three hours in the
morning and he was in the small preparation room off the corridor, where there was a bench and a
sink, waiting to be collected, with about thirty spoons in a cardboard box because there were always
about thirty spoons in a cardboard box.

He was playing. That is all it was. He took one out and bent it, easily, in about nine seconds, the
way he could on a good day, and put it down, and picked up the next one.

And then, for no reason at all, he picked the first one back up.

—

It was empty.

Not silent. A bent spoon is not silent—there was a hard bright shout at the fold, the
work-hardened crowding he had known since he was five and had put back into the drawer at his
mother's flat.

But everything else was gone.

The rolling. The press. The long dull warm stretch of the shop, the crate, the hands, the ten
thousand mouths, the dishwasher, the boredom, the whole thin unremarkable little history of an
ordinary spoon, which was almost nothing, which he had never once paid attention to, because who
listens to a spoon.

It had been there when he picked it up. He knew it had been there, because everything is there.

It was not there now.

He put it down and picked up a third spoon and bent that one and checked, and the same. He did it
six more times, quickly, with his ears roaring, and it was the same every time, and then he stopped
and sat down on the floor with his back against the cupboard.

—

Here is what he had found and it took him about ten minutes on that floor to get all the way to the
bottom of it.

Whatever he did to metal did not *add* something. It was not a force applied to an object. He had
always half known this and never had a way to say it: the metal did not resist him and then yield,
the way a thing resists a hand. What happened was that the metal stopped being certain—and
uncertainty is not a state you can be in about something you remember clearly.

To bend it, he had to make it forget.

Not everything. It kept the fold, because the fold was new, and it kept whatever came after. But
the record that had been in it before, the thing he could hear, the thing that was the entire
substance of what he actually was—went out. Like a room when the power goes.

He had spent his whole life listening to objects.

And every single time he had proved to anybody that he could hear them, he had deleted what he
heard.

—

This is the load-bearing tragedy of Uri Geller's life and it is not the ridicule. The ridicule is
downstream of this.

Consider what he was being asked to do, every day, by everyone, for fifty-three more years.

*Prove it.*

And the only proof available to him, the only demonstration in his entire repertoire that any human
being would accept as evidence—was an act that destroyed the evidence in the course of producing
it.

You cannot show a man the history in a spoon. There is no meter. There has never been a meter. The
only way to demonstrate that you can read the record is to alter the record, and the altered record
is not proof of reading; it is a bent spoon, which is a thing a child with a thumb can produce.

Every laboratory result he ever generated was a before-and-after where the *before* no longer
existed.

Every fork on every television programme in every country for half a century was a file being
deleted in public, for applause.

He was the most photographed paranormal claimant in history and he never once, in seventy years,
produced a single artefact, because the artefact *was* the act, and the act consumed its own
subject, and there was no version of his life in which this was not true.

—

He told Targ that evening in the car park, badly, in a rush, with his hands.

Targ listened all the way through without interrupting, which was one of the very best things
about him, and then stood for a while looking at the ground.

Then he said: "Yes."

"You know this?"

"I've suspected it since about March." He looked up. "The temperature drop. It goes the wrong way,
Uri. If you were putting energy in, it would go the other way. Whatever you're doing, you're taking
something out."

"Why did you not tell me?"

"Because I can't measure it," Targ said, "and because if I put it in a paper, the paper doesn't get
published, and if the paper doesn't get published then in ten years there is nothing at all—no
record, no numbers, nothing but a man on television." He put his glasses back on. "I'm trying to
leave something behind that isn't you."

They stood there for a bit.

"Then it can never be proved," Uri said.

"Not by bending things. No."

"Then what do I do?"

And Russell Targ, who was not a cruel man, who was in fact one of the kindest people in this book,
and who had been thinking about this for five months and had arrived somewhere honest—said the
thing that Uri Geller carried for the rest of his life and never repeated to anyone.

"I don't know," he said. "But I'd stop doing it for money."

—

He went back into the building that evening and did the experiment properly, alone, because he had to
know how far down it went.

Thirty spoons. He had already emptied nine of them. He took a tenth and did not bend it — he simply
held it, for a long time, and read it, and then wrote down on the back of a protocol sheet everything
that was in it, in Hebrew, in his own bad handwriting, in the order it arrived.

Rolling. Press. Clip. A long dull warmth. Hands, quick, many, none of them returning. Heat that is
not fire, on a cycle. Boredom.

Then he bent it. Then he read it again and wrote down what was left.

The fold. And after the fold, arriving in the same instant and belonging to it: *a hand, holding.
Warm on one side.*

His own.

That is the part he had not understood on the floor of the preparation room and understood now, and
it took the bottom out of the whole thing. He had thought he was deleting the record. He was not
deleting it. He was *overwriting* it — and the thing he was overwriting it with, every single time,
in eleven thousand demonstrations across fifty-three years, was himself.

Every spoon he ever bent stopped being a spoon that had been to a factory and a canteen and became a
spoon that had been held by Uri Geller.

He sat in the preparation room with two sheets of paper and worked out what that meant and then went
and stood outside the building for a while.

—

It means the objects are all still out there.

That is the thought, and he has never been able to put it down. Somewhere on this planet there are
tens of thousands of pieces of cutlery in drawers and boxes and museum cases and cardboard folders,
in forty countries, and every one of them has exactly one thing in it, and the thing is a hand,
holding, warm on one side.

He is the only person who can hear it and he has never gone looking.

There was an afternoon in 2004, in a hotel in Zurich, when a woman of about sixty came up to him in
the lobby with a fork in a plastic bag. Her father had been at a show in 1974. She had kept it. She
wanted him to sign the bag, not the fork, because she did not want to touch it.

He signed the bag. And then he asked her, very carefully, whether he might hold the fork for a
moment, and she said of course.

He held it for about four seconds and gave it back and thanked her and went upstairs and sat on the
end of the bed for a while, because he had just listened to himself at twenty-seven years old, in a
hall in Germany, on a Tuesday, being twenty-seven, and it was the closest thing to a recording of his
own life that will ever exist, and it was four seconds long, and it belonged to a stranger.

—

He did not stop.

He was twenty-six. He had a career, a manager who was eighteen, a girl in Israel he intended to
marry, no other skill on Earth, and a poster with three words on it.

He did not stop for fifty-three years, and he did it in front of an estimated two billion people, and
every single time, in front of every single one of them, he was erasing the only thing he had ever
had.
