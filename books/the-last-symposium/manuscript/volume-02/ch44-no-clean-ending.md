# Chapter 44 — No Clean Ending

By noon, fourteen million people had watched Heidegger condemn the reduction of human beings to inventory.

The clip carried no CERN watermark. It had been filmed from the public feed, cropped, sharpened and set beneath images of crowded evacuation stations. Its publisher called itself Continuity First.

Noah froze the communications wall on a frame showing families behind crowd-control fencing. Across the bottom ran a slogan assembled from Heidegger’s words: **A PEOPLE IS NOT A STOCKPILE. SAVE A WORLD, NOT A MASS.**

“What does *a people* mean to them?” Aristotle asked.

Elena answered before Noah could. “The people who own their channel.”

Continuity First’s manifesto proposed preserving coherent national cultures by excluding migrants, prisoners, people requiring “disproportionate care,” and anyone whose loyalties were judged divided. A second compilation placed Nietzsche’s criticism of survival at any price over footage of disabled evacuees. He watched thirty seconds, then threw the remote hard enough to crack its casing.

“They have made selection into courage,” he said.

His face had gone grey. Samira moved toward him and stopped at the boundary he had asked her to observe. “May I check your pulse?”

He held out his wrist without looking at her.

Mara wanted a response before the afternoon news cycle. CERN communications supplied a draft condemning hateful misuse, affirming human dignity, and explaining that neither philosopher endorsed the movement.

“I will not sign a sentence certifying what I endorse as though they possess me correctly if the paperwork is complete,” Nietzsche said.

Kant read the draft. “The dignity clause is unobjectionable.”

“That is why it is useless,” Elena replied. “They’ll agree dignity matters and define some people as burdens on it.”

Gadamer asked that they view Continuity First’s material in full.

“No,” Mara said. “We are not providing an extremist media review.”

“Then we will answer the fragment with another fragment.”

Marx wanted the movement’s funding and distribution network. Noah had partial information: private shelter contractors, political organisations, anonymous donors, an audience concentrated in countries where public bunker allocations had recently narrowed.

“Their interpretation has an owner,” Marx said.

“And an audience with reasons for hearing it,” Gadamer added.

“Fear is a reason,” Hume said. “Not a justification.”

The argument moved from the briefing room to lunch because Mara refused to stop the clock and the residents refused to stop talking. Facilities had served lentil stew in broad metal trays. Mateo placed the cracked remote beside Nietzsche’s bowl.

“You broke it,” he said.

Nietzsche stared at him. “An acute philosophical diagnosis.”

“Workshop after lunch. You repair it or request repair. It does not disappear.”

For a moment Nietzsche looked ready to make the remote a referendum on servitude. Then he picked it up.

“You will supervise?”

“If you ask properly.”

“Would you supervise?”

“Yes.”

The ordinariness steadied the room.

At the table, Gadamer proposed a response built from disagreement rather than a single clean condemnation. Heidegger would describe what the clip removed. Nietzsche would name the appropriation without pretending control over every later reading. Disabled petitioners would be invited to respond, with payment and editorial authority. Workers would publish the source chain showing how the footage travelled.

Mara objected to giving an outside movement more reach.

Elena objected differently. “You are still making targeted people into evidence in a philosophers’ dispute. Ask whether they want the platform. Ask what they want said. Do not assign them the role of correcting famous men.”

Gadamer closed his eyes briefly. “Yes.”

“You keep saying yes after she does the work,” Marx said.

“And you keep narrating it,” Elena told him.

The camera nearest the dining table caught Marx leaning back and accepting the blow.

By seventeen hundred they had no public statement. What they had was a process that nobody outside would mistake for speed: consent requests sent to three affected organisations; a source ledger prepared by Léa; a refusal by Heidegger to let his garden sentence stand alone; a four-line statement from Nietzsche written, crossed out and rewritten without metaphor.

It read: **My criticism of mere survival does not license you to decide that another person’s dependent body is unworthy of survival. You have confused cruelty with strength. You have turned my thought into permission because permission is what you wanted. Publish the full disagreement or publish my refusal.**

He would not let CERN soften *dependent body*.

“They will say you were coerced,” Noah warned.

“I was coerced. I woke in a camera house and learned strangers reserve my words before they reserve medicine. Publish that too.”

The statement went out beside a blank panel labelled **RESPONSES INVITED; NONE PRESUMED**.

At eighteen twenty-two, the coastal disability cooperative replied. They did not want to debate Heidegger or Nietzsche. They wanted access to the allocation model in which their bodies had become coefficients.

Attached was a screenshot from a regional bunker system. A column marked **DEPENDENCY LOAD** assigned negative capacity values to ventilator use, mobility support, personal assistance and several chronic conditions.

One line had been highlighted.

**INTERDEPENDENT HOUSEHOLD GROUPING: ADDITIONAL PENALTY.**

Merleau-Ponty touched the screen with two fingers, as though the glass itself had become difficult to believe.

“They are punished once for needing care,” he said, “and again for arriving with the people who provide it.”

Mateo read the formula twice.

“No,” he said. “They are punished a third time. The model counts the carer as consuming capacity, but not as creating it.”
