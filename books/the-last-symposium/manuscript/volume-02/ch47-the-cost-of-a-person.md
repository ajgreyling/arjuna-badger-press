# Chapter 47 — The Cost of a Person

The first reply came from a shelter beneath an old hydroelectric complex.

Its engineering authority accepted the council’s principle in full and rejected its implementation in the same paragraph. The facility had fixed ventilation, fixed emergency power and fixed sleeping volume. Meeting the proposed accessibility and care standard would reduce certified capacity from 8,400 people to 7,160.

The second system estimated a loss of nine percent. The third estimated twenty-one.

On the communications wall, human beings vanished into differences between totals.

Mara kept the council in session after dinner. Food congealed in trays while Amina walked them through the models. Wider turning spaces removed bunks. Medical redundancy consumed power and storage. Keeping households together reduced packing efficiency. Additional carers ate, breathed and occupied beds. Accessible sanitation required floor area. Some constraints were corrupt. Some were built concrete.

“If we insist,” Mara said, “fewer people enter.”

“If we do not,” Levinas said, “we announce whose absence counts as efficiency.”

“And if insisting excludes twelve hundred others?”

He did not answer with arithmetic.

Aristotle asked which changes preserved function at lowest cost. Mateo and Léa began a redesign list: rotating aisle clearances instead of widening every bay; distributed charging; shared lifting equipment; modular partitions; family support counted against staffing requirements. The projected loss fell to eight hundred, then six hundred and forty.

Hume watched the total descend. “Useful. Still not zero.”

“No honest design gets us zero,” Amina said.

Nietzsche’s headache had returned. He sat on the floor beneath the wall display because the chair pressed badly against his spine. His minus 0.38 profile remained open beside him.

“You are asking the wrong question,” he said. “Not whether a person costs capacity. Every person costs capacity. Ask why some costs are named as burdens while others are called the shelter.”

Kant looked at the armed-security allocation. The hydroelectric site reserved three hundred and twelve places for perimeter, command and internal order. Mara warned that removing them did not automatically make the bunker safe.

Marx pointed to private executive quarters occupying four times the standard volume.

“There,” he said. “Your physical limit has a bedroom.”

Noah verified the plans. Sixty-four executive residents used space equivalent to two hundred and thirty standard bunks. The authority classified the difference as command continuity, not capacity cost.

The room’s anger briefly became simple. Then Amina enlarged a different site: a community bunker with no executive wing, no luxury allocation and almost no spare power. Full implementation still reduced its capacity by fourteen percent.

“Keep looking,” Marx said.

“At what?” Amina asked. “There is no hidden ruling-class sauna in this one. They converted a mine with volunteer labour.”

He stood over the plans, searching for a structure that would make the moral answer easy. Elena watched him fail to find it.

“This is where you stop accusing the number of being fake,” she said. “Now what?”

Marx sat down.

They called the community bunker. Its coordinator, Priya, had twelve minutes before a construction shift. She told them the narrower aisles were not an ideological choice. They were the tunnels they had. Enlarging them meant blasting, reinforcement and months they might not possess.

“If I take your charter standard,” she said, “I tell four hundred people who built this place that there is no bed. If I reject it, I tell thirty-seven people already in our community that the place they built was never meant for their bodies. Which betrayal would the council like attributed to us?”

Nobody answered quickly enough to insult her.

Gadamer asked what those thirty-seven people had proposed. Priya sent their design notes. Some accepted narrower circulation in sleeping areas if refuge zones remained accessible. Some refused any plan requiring separation from carers. Two wheelchair users had designed a pulley evacuation system. One person with respiratory illness wanted the coal dust removed before anyone discussed bed geometry.

“There is no single disabled horizon,” Gadamer said.

Jo, still connected from the coastal cooperative, replied, “Congratulations again.”

He accepted it. “Yes.”

At twenty-three hundred the council amended the implementation note, not the principle. It did not require identical architecture. It required direct participation by affected residents, disclosure of trade-offs, independent appeal and proof that capacity losses had not been shifted onto those easiest to exclude. It prohibited counting care only as consumption. It acknowledged that compliance could reduce total places.

Mara read the final sentence twice. “We are publishing a standard that says following it may leave people outside.”

“Every standard leaves people outside somewhere,” Elena said. “This one makes them visible and gives them standing before the door closes.”

“That is not salvation.”

“Nobody authorised us to promise salvation.”

At midnight the hydroelectric authority released a revised plan. Executive quarters were reduced. Medical redundancy increased. Total capacity settled at 7,780—six hundred and twenty fewer than before, but six hundred and twenty more than its first compliance estimate.

The community bunker did not adopt the standard. It opened a forty-eight-hour resident design assembly and froze its selection list.

The third bunker rejected the principle outright.

There was no clean victory to edit.

Noah published all three responses beside the council text.

By one in the morning, residents had drifted from the table. Nietzsche remained on the floor, asleep against the wall. Levinas sat near him without waking him. Samira brought a pillow. Heidegger carried it the last few steps, then handed it to her rather than placing it himself.

Gadamer watched the transfer: care offered, limited, received through another person. No fusion. No innocence. Still a relation.

Below the house, the retrieval machinery began an unscheduled diagnostic pulse.

The water glasses trembled.
