# Chapter 37 — Before the Door

Emmanuel Levinas spent his first hour in the future looking at photographs of people who had been murdered.

The orientation team had debated whether that was cruelty. He had settled it by refusing to enter the residence until they answered three questions: what had happened to Europe’s Jews, what Martin Heidegger had done, and whether his own family appeared in the archive.

The last answer could not be given cleanly within the retrieval window they had chosen. Records were incomplete, identities uncertain, the difference between history and this retrieved continuation impossible to explain without injury. Noah showed him what was documented, marked what was not, and did not turn uncertainty into comfort.

Levinas removed the earpiece twice. Each time the room filled with languages that could no longer reach one another. Each time he put it back.

At nine forty he asked to meet Heidegger.

Mara proposed a mediated session. Levinas declined.

“You have mediated enough.”

The arrival corridor door opened. Heidegger waited alone on the other side because Husserl had refused to stage a tableau and Elena had refused to let CERN surround an encounter with officials.

For several seconds neither man spoke.

Heidegger looked older than the teacher Levinas remembered and younger than the record’s worst years. Levinas looked exhausted, medically scrubbed and fully awake.

“You have seen it,” Heidegger said.

“I have seen records.”

“I do not remember doing those things.”

“The dead do not require your memory.”

Heidegger flinched.

The sentence circulated through the house before lunch, stripped of the silence around it. Levinas hated it immediately as a slogan. He had not come to win a scene. He had come through a door and found another person already demanding a response by being there.

In the council chamber Mara began with procedure. Heidegger’s vote remained suspended. Levinas had observer status until orientation was complete. The worker council held four standing seats. External requests would resume after midday.

Levinas interrupted. “Who is outside?”

Mara indicated the communications queue.

“No. Outside this room.”

Mateo answered first. “Medical staff. Translation. Facilities. Security.”

“Outside the facility?”

Amina brought up the pending triage file. Forty-seven communities had petitioned after being grouped into relocation zones. Three were represented by live delegates. Tens of thousands more existed only as records because the communication team lacked capacity.

Levinas studied the wall of queue numbers.

“You call this access?”

“We call it constrained access,” Amina said. “If everyone appears at once, nobody can be heard.”

“And the order?”

“Urgency, evidentiary completeness, affected population, geographic balance and operational deadlines.”

“The face must first earn sufficient metadata.”

Amina’s eyes sharpened. “If you mean that as accusation, make it useful. My staff process human beings until they cannot see straight. A queue is also what prevents the loudest government from taking every hour.”

Levinas inclined his head. “Then the queue may be just. But its justice begins in what it fails to contain, not in its successful ordering.”

He asked them to open one petition before its briefing summary.

The screen showed Nuru Okafor, a nurse coordinating a displaced-persons camp in southern Italy. Behind her, wind pushed canvas walls inward. The model classified the camp as a high-dependency population with low continuity value and severe transport burden. Nuru did not begin with an argument. She turned the camera.

A child slept against a water container while an old man held the tube feeding him. Two women repaired a torn tent. A teenager translated instructions for new arrivals. None knew the council was watching.

“We received the relocation order this morning,” Nuru said. “The designated zone has no dialysis capacity and half the water allocation we currently receive. Your model says consolidation improves survival probability. Whose survival?”

Amina opened the underlying data. “The destination assumes mobile treatment units.”

“They do not exist.”

“They are marked funded.”

“Funded is not breathing.”

Levinas did not look away from the screen. “Before we ask what policy includes them, we are already answerable.”

Kant said, “Answerable under what principle?”

“Responsibility is not first produced by a rule I choose with equals. The other person contests my freedom before I have completed my system.”

“Without a common rule, responsibility may become partiality.”

“And without interruption, the common rule may make abandonment clean.”

Elena watched them begin to build the familiar bridge from a person’s emergency to two men’s disagreement. She cut across it.

“Nuru, what can change today?”

The camp needed the relocation paused, the mobile-unit claim audited, and delegates admitted to the regional planning call. Mateo identified a communications channel. Amina marked the false infrastructure assumption. Mara contacted the authority responsible and made clear the raw petition would enter the public archive if the order continued unchanged.

It was pressure, not sovereignty. By evening the relocation was paused for forty-eight hours.

Levinas stood before the blank screen after Nuru disconnected.

Heidegger had remained at the back, without a council seat.

“You taught us to ask about Being,” Levinas said, still facing the wall. “But the person before me does not wait for ontology to finish.”

Heidegger answered softly. “No.”

It was not enough. Nobody in the room mistook it for enough.
