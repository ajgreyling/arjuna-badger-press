# Chapter 45 — Minus Point Three Eight

The number beside Nietzsche’s name was minus 0.38.

Noah had built a sandbox copy of the leaked allocation model overnight. At breakfast, every person in the house received a trial profile generated from medical records, shelter assumptions and the model’s published rules. The exercise was voluntary. Almost everyone opened theirs.

Nietzsche laughed when he saw the score, then enlarged it until the digits filled his tablet.

“At last,” he said. “An exact measurement of decadence.”

Samira took the tablet from him before the joke could become armour. “It isn’t measuring you.”

“It has my pain, my eyesight, my medication and my expected labour output. Its manners suggest intimacy.”

“It is measuring a system’s willingness to accommodate you.”

The translation tone marked *accommodate*: house, make room, adapt.

Across the table, Kant’s profile carried a small age penalty and no dependency load. Hume’s reduced his ration efficiency. Gadamer’s assumed hearing support despite the earpiece. Merleau-Ponty’s historical medical data had been converted into forecast risk with confidence values nobody could defend. Heidegger had opted out. Levinas had not opened his.

Elena’s profile showed a positive maintenance score and a negative dependent-family score.

“They reward me for keeping the bunker alive,” she said, “then punish me if I bring anyone I love.”

Mateo’s score was higher than every philosopher’s. The model valued environmental systems expertise. It did not include the tremor in his left hand because he had never reported it to CERN medical.

He closed the tablet.

At ten-thirty Amina explained the designers’ defence. A bunker had finite power, oxygen, food, medicine, volume and staff time. Different bodies imposed different demands. Ignoring those demands could kill everyone, including the people a non-discrimination rule intended to protect.

“So the coefficients are true?” Aristotle asked.

“Some costs are physically real. The moral weight assigned to them is not a physical measurement.”

Husserl drew two columns on the whiteboard: what was given, and what the model added. Electricity draw belonged in the first. The assumption that a ventilator user contributed nothing belonged in the second. Expected lifespan straddled both, a projection disguised as a fact.

Merleau-Ponty asked everyone to stand.

Mara told him this was not the time for an exercise.

“It is exactly the time. The model imagines a body alone.”

He had them carry the dining table two metres toward the wall. No one person could lift it safely. Aristotle coordinated the corners. Kant counted. Hume abandoned counting and watched the legs. Elena protected the cable channel. Nietzsche could not lift and instead pulled chairs from the path. Levinas held the door. Mateo noticed the floor seam that would catch a wheel.

The table moved.

“Which body moved it?” Merleau-Ponty asked.

“The association,” Aristotle said.

“A coordinated system,” Amina said.

“People,” Elena said. “Do not let the example swallow them.”

Merleau-Ponty nodded. “The model assigns demand to individuals and capacity to infrastructure. But assistance is treated as cost even when it is the way capacity exists.”

Descartes objected that interdependence could not erase asymmetry. A person needing continuous electricity remained more vulnerable to failure than a person who did not.

“Yes,” Samira said. “And a surgeon without cleaners, sterilisation technicians and power is a person holding a useless knife.”

The debate grew ugly when Mara displayed a hard case from a northern shelter: admitting six people with high medical power requirements would displace eleven standard allocations under the present design.

Kant rejected the category *standard person*. Hume said renaming it would not produce electricity. Levinas refused a calculation in which named people disappeared into interchangeable units. Marx asked why the shelter had spent more on armed perimeter systems than redundant power.

“Because perimeter failure was modelled as catastrophic,” Amina said.

“And dependence was modelled as personal,” Elena replied.

They requested the budget history. Mara said it would take days.

“Then your urgent number has a slow politics hidden inside it,” Gadamer said.

At fifteen hundred the coastal cooperative joined by audio. Their elected speaker introduced herself as Jo. She used a ventilator and began by telling the council not to congratulate itself for discovering interdependence.

“Disabled people have been explaining this for generations,” she said. “Your philosophers get a camera house and suddenly needing each other is a revelation.”

Nobody answered.

Jo wanted three things: prohibit automatic exclusion by diagnosis; count support networks as capacity, not baggage; and require designers to prove that claimed constraints could not reasonably be changed. Not infinitely changed. Reasonably.

Mara asked who decided reasonable.

“Not only the people who built the inaccessible bunker.”

Nietzsche moved closer to the speaker as if proximity could improve audio. “Your first demand permits triage after individual assessment.”

“Of course it does. A doctor still has to decide what treatment helps. I’m opposing a civilisation deciding beforehand that my need makes me less of one of them.”

His hand covered the minus score on his tablet.

At the end of the call, Mateo disclosed his tremor to Samira. Not to the council. Not to the cameras if he could help it. He did it in the medical-suite doorway, where clinical privacy began.

“If it becomes part of my file,” he said, “do I lose the score?”

“It becomes part of your care.”

“That was not my question.”

Samira looked past him at the house built on maintenance nobody saw until it failed.

“I don’t know,” she said.

He stepped inside and let the door close.
