# Chapter 12 — The Petition of Nia Bako

The girl waited for the council to finish arranging itself.

She appeared on the communications wall from the waist up, seated at a plain table. She wore a green school jersey despite the heat visible in the dampness at her temples. Behind her, someone moved in and out of frame—a woman’s shoulder, a hand setting down a glass, the edge of a facilities uniform folded over a chair.

“My name is Nia Bako,” the girl said. “I’m seventeen. I study dryland agriculture through the regional continuity programme. My mother works for your facility.”

The image paused. A caption informed them that the petition had passed security screening and that the feed was recorded earlier that day.

Hume disliked the pause more than he expected. It turned the girl from a speaker into material under review.

The video resumed.

“Our application is for a family-linked place. My training score is high enough for the agricultural pool, but dependants reduce priority. My mother’s work classification does not include continuity protection.” Nia looked briefly past the camera. The unseen woman did not speak. “The public report says historical advisers have protected status. I want to know why people who already died have provisional places while living growers do not.”

Aristotle’s hands settled on the table. Descartes touched the printed transcript as if it might disclose a seam. Hume watched the hand behind Nia lift the glass and put it down untouched.

Nia continued. “If your answer is that you are more useful, explain how. If your answer is that you were selected before rules existed, explain why that should bind anyone outside CERN. If the report is wrong, say so.”

The screen went dark, leaving four small response options. Endorse. Decline. Request evidence. Defer.

“What does she grow?” Aristotle asked Mara.

“She is a student.”

“What has she done, rather than what programme contains her?”

“Her file is not available to residents.”

“Then the petition asks us to judge with the useful particulars removed.”

Descartes said, “We do not know the recording is genuine.”

Hume turned to him. “We do not know Mara is genuine.”

“Correct.”

“Yet you permit her to schedule your meals.”

“That does not establish the girl’s identity.”

Mara said, “Security verified her identity and programme record.”

“Security belongs to the party whose allocation she challenges.”

The hand behind the girl remained in Hume’s mind. Not proof. A gesture might be rehearsed, a mother planted, a uniform placed to stir precisely the conclusion he was drawing. Yet his response to it was not therefore meaningless. Feeling was one of the ways human beings detected another’s stakes. It was corruptible, partial, educable—and indispensable.

“Suppose every fact is verified,” Hume said. “What then?”

Aristotle answered, “Then we assess whether her capacities serve the settlement’s needs.”

“And her mother?”

“Family connection may contribute to stability.”

“May. Does affection enter your inventory as a skill or as equipment?”

Aristotle’s eyes narrowed. “Would you prefer that sympathy select the survivors?”

“No. I prefer that we notice arithmetic has acquired a face before claiming the face decides nothing.”

Descartes tapped **REQUEST EVIDENCE**. “The claim can be investigated.”

“And while investigated?” Hume asked.

“No endorsement.”

“Which is a decision with consequences.”

“As endorsement would be.”

Mara remained standing. “You were asked whether the council endorses review of her family-linked application. Not whether she receives a place.”

“The screen says endorse,” Hume said. “Not endorse review.”

Noah, near the door, said, “The petition category defines the action.”

“For whom?”

“For the allocation office receiving the result.”

“Then we are voting on a sentence whose verb means something different behind the wall.”

Aristotle asked for the original petition. Noah produced a longer document. Nia had requested not merely review but publication of the criteria governing provisional historical allocations. The response interface had compressed both demands into one.

Hume felt his geniality leave him.

“Play her again,” he said.

“The video is available on your tablets,” Mara replied.

“No. Let her occupy the wall while we decide.”

They replayed it. This time Hume watched Nia rather than the mother. The girl’s breathing changed before she said *already died*. She pressed her thumb into the side of her forefinger. At the end, when she demanded they say if the report was wrong, anger gave way for one instant to fear.

He wanted to help her. The desire was immediate, honourable in appearance and dangerously fond of itself.

“I endorse publication of the criteria,” he said. “I endorse review of her claim. I do not pretend those are the same act.”

Aristotle said, “I endorse review after competence records are supplied.”

Descartes said, “I endorse disclosure of the rule before application of the rule. I do not yet affirm the testimony.”

Mara surveyed the three different selections. “That is not a vote.”

“It is three judgements,” Hume said.

“The receiving body requires one recommendation.”

“Then it requires us to conceal disagreement.”

Aristotle drew a sheet of paper toward him and divided it into columns. “We can define what the vote authorises.”

“By what authority?” Descartes asked.

“By agreement among those voting.”

“And the absent claimant?”

They spent twenty minutes constructing a procedure and succeeded chiefly in proving that none existed. Hume proposed a provisional majority for requesting disclosure. Aristotle objected that a majority could not create relevant knowledge. Descartes objected that they had not established the reliability of the institution transmitting either question or answer. Mara reminded them that Nia’s file would be decided on another timetable, by another body, whether they spoke or not.

“There,” Hume said. “An ought smuggled from an is. Because the machine moves, we are told we should move in its direction.”

Mara’s gaze hardened. “Because the machine moves, silence also enters the result.”

The door opened. Samira waited outside in blue clinical clothes.

Mara checked the time. “Retrieval preparation begins in eight hours. Immanuel Kant arrives at six.”

“Is that intended as an answer?” Hume asked.

“It is the next fact.”

Noah gathered the papers. One page lay briefly uncovered beneath the petition summary: a restricted facilities rota, included by mistake in the communication packet. Hume saw **BAKO, ELENA — ENVIRONMENTAL / NIGHT**, followed by an amber access classification.

Nia’s mother did not merely work somewhere in the great institution. She worked in the invisible corridor behind their rooms.

Noah turned the page face down.

On the dark wall, Nia’s last frame remained frozen: waiting for dead men to decide what their disagreement meant.
