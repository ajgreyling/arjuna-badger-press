# Chapter 23 — Two Briefings

The numbers did not lie differently. They were taught different manners.

Léa placed four documents across the archive display and locked the terminal against remote closure. She had agreed to show Karl the comparison, not to give him control. Elena stood beside her with a written request for council standing. The control-room door remained shut.

“Same observation package,” Léa said. “Seismic activity, deformation, gas chemistry, satellite gravimetry where available. Same JANUS run family.”

“Then read the headings,” Karl said.

The resident briefing called it a high-consequence conditional forecast requiring ethical preparation under uncertainty. The worker briefing called it an operational disruption scenario and emphasised continuity obligations. The government version named strategic instability and population-management risks. The public summary described enhanced monitoring with no immediate cause for alarm.

“No number is fabricated?” Descartes asked.

“Not in these documents.”

“Material omissions?”

“Yes.” Léa highlighted time horizons. The resident document showed several. The public summary gave none. “Probability without horizon or defined event is easy to misunderstand.”

Hume read the four pages twice. “This is not evidence of a universal deceiver. It is evidence of four editors.”

“Who coordinate,” Descartes said.

“Often. That does not make the ice imaginary.”

“Nor does ice make their representations innocent.”

Karl watched the disagreement settle into its familiar shape. Descartes wanted to know what could be trusted by a mind stripped of dependence. Hume wanted belief proportioned to evidence. Both questions mattered. Neither explained why an environmental technician was instructed to continue working while a ministry was instructed to prepare coercive population measures.

“Suppose the physical forecast is entirely true,” Karl said. “Who may act on the severe version?”

“Governments,” Mara said from the threshold. She had arrived without being invited. “Infrastructure operators. Medical systems. People with responsibility.”

Elena held up her amber band. “Responsibility without information.”

“Your briefing contains what is required for your role.”

“Who defined the role?” Karl asked.

Mara ignored him. “Léa, this terminal contains restricted material.”

“All four documents were cleared for their named audiences,” Léa said. “The comparison was not.”

“End the session.”

Karl placed his palm on the table but did not touch the controls. “The question is no longer only whether the forecast is true. Your arrangement decides whose uncertainty becomes freedom and whose becomes an order. The government receives a reason to move populations. The public receives reassurance. Workers receive duties. We receive the moral drama.”

“And what do you propose?” Mara asked.

He deferred to Elena before answering. “That is not mine to announce.”

They moved to the dining room. Elena, Mateo and two staff representatives joined by audio from the service layer. The residents argued over whether participation in a stoppage would breach medical-support and safety commitments.

“No clinical service stops,” Elena said. “No action risks retrieval, ventilation, medication, food safety or sanitation beyond delay. We pause non-medical service, editing and translation correction. Bounded. Recorded. Reviewed after one cycle.”

Mateo’s voice came through the wall. “Facilities vote first. Residents can join after. Nobody borrows our refusal before we own it.”

Karl felt the truth of the rebuke. “Agreed.”

Kant asked for the stoppage terms in writing. Nietzsche called that predictable and then asked for a copy. Hume wanted to know what evidence would count as success. Aristotle asked which functions could degrade without concealing harms. Descartes supported access to uncurated information but objected to making truth depend on collective pressure.

“Truth does not,” Karl said. “Access does.”

“Then you seek power, not certainty.”

“At last you understand me.”

The staff vote was scheduled for eighteen hundred. Before it began, Noah entered with his tablet held away from his body as if it were hot.

“A national continuity agency has published provisional selection guidance.”

The wall display showed the document. Its language was polished, humane and familiar: preserve a representative range of human capacities; prioritise functions necessary for flourishing, not survival alone; allocate with impartial regard for those absent from the room.

Kant stood.

“That final phrase is from our rule deliberation.”

Noah swallowed. “It appeared in my internal summary.”

“Internal to whom?” Hume asked.

Mara read the release in silence.

Karl looked up at the cameras. The house had been debating whether it possessed enough knowledge to advise power. Power had already been listening through the walls.

Elena folded her request for standing and put it in the centre of the table.

“Vote,” she said.
