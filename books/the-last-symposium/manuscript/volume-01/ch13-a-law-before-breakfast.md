# Chapter 13 — A Law Before Breakfast

The first thing Immanuel Kant refused was the needle.

He did not strike the physician’s hand away. He raised his own between them and said, with difficulty, “You have not asked.”

The circular room swam at its edges. Cold air touched skin not yet understood as his. A machine spoke through something behind his ear, turning the woman’s unfamiliar language into comprehensible words a fraction too late.

She stopped immediately.

“You are right,” she said. “May I take blood from your arm? It tells me whether the retrieval has injured you in ways we can treat.”

He swallowed against pain. “What is retrieval?”

“The process that brought you here. I can explain before you decide. The blood draw can wait several minutes.”

Behind her stood a second woman, severe in a dark suit, and two masked assistants. The first woman—Samira—explained one claim at a time. She did not call his confusion consent. She asked before touching his wrist, before shining light into his eyes, before fitting the earpiece more securely. When he permitted the needle, she told him when the pain would begin and when it had ended.

This established no general trust. It established that one person recognised a boundary.

The woman in the suit introduced herself as Mara Voss and described catastrophe, resurrection and the council’s purpose. She spoke of selection committees and mandates already ratified.

“By whom was my participation authorised?” Kant asked.

“The project operates under a multinational accord.”

“That was not my question.”

“Your prior consent was impossible.”

“Then my present consent is necessary, not unnecessary.”

Mara did not retreat. “You may refuse advisory work. You may not presently leave the secure facility. The public-health and security constraints apply regardless.”

An honest coercion was preferable to a false gift. It remained coercion.

By the time Samira allowed him to walk, his borrowed clothes hung poorly from his slight frame and the room beyond smelled of bitter coffee and accusation.

Three men surrounded a kitchen counter. One held an empty metal canister. One studied a ration display. The third drank from a cup with evident satisfaction.

“Our fourth judge,” the drinker said. “You arrive at a constitutional moment.”

Mara made introductions. Hume was the man with the cup. Descartes held the canister. Aristotle stood at the display.

“He used the last coffee ration,” Descartes said.

“I prepared what remained,” Hume corrected. “Use is not destruction when three cups exist.”

“You did not ask.”

“You were asleep.”

“Then waiting was available.”

Aristotle pointed at the ration screen. “The house inventory assigns a daily quantity. Hume prepared all of it before the common meal.”

“And offered it commonly.” Hume raised the pot. “There is still one cup, if our new companion drinks it.”

Kant’s stomach contracted at the smell. He wanted it sharply. He also wanted a chair, warmth and ten minutes in which nobody made humanity depend upon him.

“Before I accept,” he said, “state the rule under which you acted.”

Hume brightened. “I found coffee, inferred that coffee is customarily drunk, and made enough for those present.”

“That is a description. What permission did you claim?”

“That shared food may be prepared for sharing.”

“Including all of it?” Descartes asked.

Hume regarded the pot. “I grant the addition makes the principle less handsome.”

Kant turned to Descartes. “What rule do you claim?”

“No person may consume or transform a scarce common resource without the agreement of those entitled to it.”

“If agreement cannot be obtained?”

Descartes paused. “Preserve it.”

“Then no one eats while another sleeps,” Aristotle said. “A household cannot function if every ordinary act awaits unanimity.”

“Your rule?” Kant asked him.

“Goods should be used according to their purpose, under the judgement of whoever has relevant competence, for the good of the association.”

“Who appoints the competent person?”

Aristotle’s glance was almost appreciative. “That is the political question.”

Kant accepted the remaining coffee but divided it between four cups. It was lukewarm and weaker than the smell had promised. Hume drank his portion without complaint.

On a sheet from Aristotle’s notebook, Kant wrote a temporary rule: **No resident shall appropriate a common ration under a permission he could not grant equally to every other resident in relevantly similar circumstances. Common preparation must preserve a reasonable share or opportunity for absent residents.**

Hume read over his shoulder. “You have made breakfast sound indictable.”

“It was indictable before. Now the charge can be answered.”

“And what of desire?”

“Desire explains why you acted. It does not legislate for everyone else.”

Descartes asked, “What secures the rule?”

“We do, if each can regard himself as both author and subject.”

Aristotle said, “A law applying identically may ignore differences in need and function.”

“Then the difference must be one every person can publicly recognise, not a privilege named by its beneficiary.”

Kant asked for the household roster. The refrigerator displayed four occupied rooms and sixteen prepared ones. Names had been assigned to the next arrivals, though several remained locked. He copied the ration rule onto a clean page and added procedures for privacy, common stores, speaking order and revision.

“You include people who are not here,” Hume said.

“Their absence does not make it permissible to consume their share.”

“It makes it difficult to ask whether they want coffee.”

Kant ignored the joke because it concealed a real difficulty. Consent could not be manufactured by an empty chair. But neither could absence erase standing.

He fixed the page to the refrigerator with two magnets. At the top he wrote **HOUSEHOLD RULES — PROVISIONAL 1.0**.

Near the bottom, the rota listed tomorrow’s resident: Friedrich Nietzsche. Room Five. No duties assigned pending medical clearance.

Kant added the name to the rule’s scope.

Behind him Hume said, almost kindly, “The man has not yet been dragged out of eternity and already you have reserved him a morning with the dishes.”

Kant studied the unopened room on the plan. “Precisely because he cannot object, we must not write as though he does not exist.”
