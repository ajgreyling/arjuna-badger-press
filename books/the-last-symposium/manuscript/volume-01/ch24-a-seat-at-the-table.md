# Chapter 24 — A Seat at the Table

At nineteen-oh-three, Mara’s office stopped being cleaned.

The change was almost invisible. A cup remained where she had left it. A strip of discarded seal backing clung to the floor near the document cabinet. The air cooled by one degree because Mateo’s team had shifted the nonessential comfort schedule to its contractual minimum.

Across the house, public translation corrections paused. Edited footage accumulated. Food arrived in sealed bulk containers rather than plated portions. Waste stayed safely bagged where residents left it. Medical, retrieval and environmental safety continued without interruption.

It was a careful stoppage. That made it harder to dismiss as chaos.

Mara convened the council at twenty hundred. Six residents sat at the long table. The workers remained behind the wall, linked by audio only. Elena’s request lay unopened before Mara because opening it on camera would imply a process she had not authorised.

“I will establish worker consultation,” she said. “Two representatives may attend the first thirty minutes of each council. They may submit recommendations for the record. Voting standing remains with the retrieved council under the programme mandate.”

“Consulted by whom?” Elena asked through the speaker.

“The council.”

“And who decides whether our recommendation changes anything?”

“The council.”

“Then tomorrow changes nothing.”

Kant pushed Rule Sheet 1.0 toward Mara. A brown shadow from Nietzsche’s cup remained on one corner despite cleaning.

“Our own rule states that each person affected by household authority must be treated as an end, not merely an instrument of another’s purpose.”

“Household rule,” Mara said. “Not programme governance.”

“A distinction written by the party that benefits from it.”

Nietzsche leaned back, pale behind his dark glasses. “Careful, Kant. You are beginning to ask where a value came from.”

“Do not make my correction your victory.”

“I would never insult it so.”

Hume tapped the sealed food container. “For six days we have behaved as though this house operated by convention among six men. Today we learn the convention rested on habits maintained by people excluded from it. I see no sufficient reason to preserve the first arrangement merely because we became accustomed to it.”

Descartes said, “We also cannot assess the documents supplied to us when those who manage their circulation are barred from deliberation.”

“You want technicians as evidence witnesses,” Marx said. “That is not standing.”

“I want the curators of evidence unable to disappear behind evidence.”

Aristotle had said nothing. Mara called on him because the first resident had often found the sentence that allowed a practical next step.

“This association was commissioned for philosophers,” she said.

“For what purpose?” he asked.

“To produce a survivors’ charter.”

“A charter governs an association among survivors.”

“Potential survivors.”

“Then the people maintaining survival conditions are not accidental to its purpose.”

“Expertise remains necessary.”

“Necessary is not sufficient. We have mistaken one excellence for the whole of rule.”

Mara surveyed the six faces selected by committees she could not fully name in public. The retrieval schedule would continue at dawn. Governments had already copied their language. Closing the council might preserve control for one night and destroy the experiment’s remaining claim to legitimacy.

“Workers may enter,” she said. “Tonight. Standing provisional, subject to written scope tomorrow.”

Elena answered at once. “Standing recorded tonight. Scope argued tomorrow.”

Mara almost refused. On her office feed, the cup remained where she had left it.

“Agreed.”

The resident door opened.

Elena entered first, followed by Mateo, Léa and two elected representatives whose names Noah recorded without titles. They did not line up against the wall. They approached the table.

There were enough chairs in the room, stored around its edges. Yet the six philosophers had arranged their usual places so thoroughly that the empty chairs appeared decorative.

Elena remained standing.

“Before this council decides anybody else’s eligibility,” she said, “you will determine your own. Not in general. Each of you. Your nineteen protected allocations. Who issued them, whether they transfer, what work they purchase, and why a dead philosopher outranks a living nurse, technician, farmer or child.”

Nietzsche’s chipped cup stood clean beside his hand. He touched its handle but did not lift it.

“Excellent,” he said. “The honour has found its creditor.”

“No,” Elena said. “I am not here to complete your metaphor. What changes tomorrow?”

Marx lowered his eyes, accepting that the correction belonged in the room.

Kant proposed that no selection principle be issued until the residents’ own status could survive the same rule. Descartes demanded the original allocation instruments. Hume asked what practical deadline governed the inquiry. Nietzsche said the protected seats should be made visibly conditional. Marx asked workers to nominate the first documents required. Léa named translation priority logs. Mateo named service classifications and shelter criteria.

No agreement followed. More people did not make the problem smaller. They multiplied the knowledge, interests and claims the old council had mistaken for absence.

Aristotle considered the chair he had occupied each evening: near the table’s centre, with a view of both doors. Nobody had assigned it. He had chosen it on the first day, and repeated use had made the choice resemble a fact about him.

He stood.

“Elena,” he said, “take this place.”

“I asked for a seat, not yours.”

“Quite right.”

He went to the wall, lifted another chair and carried it to the table. He did not place it at the end. He opened a gap between his usual position and Mara’s, set the new chair there, then moved his own farther down.

Elena sat in the added chair. The others brought theirs forward. Wood and metal scraped across the floor, an ugly sound the cameras could not turn into harmony.

“Record the decision,” Mara told Noah.

“Worker representatives admitted to—”

“Workers have standing,” Elena said.

Noah deleted the softer sentence. “Workers have standing in the council from twenty-one forty-six on Day Six. The service stoppage is suspended pending the first open session. It is not settled.”

“Good,” Mateo said. “Now the air can come back up one degree.”

Hume opened the bulk food container. “Can standing include supper?”

“If you serve it yourself,” Elena said.

He did.

They argued past artificial night. At five-thirty, warning lights woke along the arrival corridor. A vibration entered the table through its legs, too deep to be sound at first. Cups trembled. The retrieval chamber began drawing power.

Mara’s wrist display filled with medical checks and schedule protests. She read them, then faced the enlarged council.

“The seventh retrieval proceeds at six.”

“During an unresolved labour action?” Kant asked.

“The stoppage is suspended. The dispute is unresolved. Both statements are true.”

Below them, machinery gathered around a man who had spent his life asking thought to suspend what it took for granted.

Around the table, nobody remained uncommitted enough to welcome him cleanly.
