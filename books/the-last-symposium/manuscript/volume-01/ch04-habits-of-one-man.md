# Chapter 4 — Habits of One Man

The onion taught Aristotle that the knife was sharper than it appeared.

His first cut went too deep, his second too shallow. The third produced something close to the pieces Mateo had shown him. By the ninth, his eyes watered for reasons unrelated to grief, which he appreciated.

He could have summoned assistance using the wall panel. He did not. A virtue delegated at every inconvenience was only an opinion about virtue.

The black cooking surface recognised the pan. The oil became hot faster than flame would have warned him. He burned the first onion, removed it, lowered the numbered control and tried again. His meal was too salty and partly raw. He ate it at the long table under the cameras.

Repeated action, he thought, shaped a person towards what he practised. It did not guarantee that what he practised was good.

After dinner he arranged tomorrow on a page: waking, movement, food, observation, study, deliberation. The routine steadied him because it was chosen. The house’s schedule, delivered on his tablet, steadied him less.

The device yielded maps of the world, descriptions of engines and governments, a history so broad it made Alexander’s ambitions look provincial. When he searched his own name, the screen displayed a lock and the words **PERSONAL LEGACY ACCESS PENDING**.

He tried *Lyceum*. Locked. *Theophrastus*. Locked. *Macedon*. A general article opened, but the years after his remembered life seemed to retreat behind mist whenever they approached him.

He set the tablet face down.

His bedroom was private by rule. He did not trust rules he had not helped make, yet he saw no camera. On the bedside table lay the stitched notebook. He held it closed while he spoke, uncertain whether he addressed himself, the hidden listeners, or the absent companions whose deaths the machine claimed had already happened.

“The good life cannot be lived by one man alone,” he said. “Not because another person supplies what he lacks as a tool supplies a hand. A friend is another self, and no self can fully exercise justice, generosity, truthfulness or affection without another who may answer.”

The translation earpiece, still in its charging cradle, could not clean the words for an audience. His own Greek sounded strange in this sealed room.

“Yet I have begun arranging the house as if solitude made me its master.”

He thought of Mateo’s face withdrawing when Aristotle had praised his fitness for service. Praise could be a fence. He had known men who arranged every condition of domination, then called the result natural.

Knowing this did not mean he had ceased doing it.

Sleep came in fragments. At artificial dawn he walked the garden until his legs warmed and his throat stopped scraping with every breath. Camera 4B remained covered. He set the loose cabinet screw beside it on the floor—a small sign that the watcher had been watched.

Then the floor vibrated.

Doors sealed softly along the bedroom wing. Somewhere beneath the house machinery rose from a hum into a pressure he felt in his teeth.

The second retrieval.

Aristotle returned to the dining room. Yesterday’s single place setting remained where staff had restored it after his meal. He found another plate and cup. He placed the two settings opposite one another, reconsidered, then moved them to adjacent corners. Opposition need not be built into the furniture.

He put water on to heat. He chose bread, fruit and honey, then removed the honey because the unknown man might be ill. He added it again because illness did not erase preference.

By the time Mara brought the new resident through the arrival corridor, Aristotle had decided he would not greet him as a subordinate, specimen or guest.

The man was pale, narrow-faced and furious at the cold. One hand covered the earpiece. His eyes moved over Aristotle, the table, the cameras, the false morning beyond the garden doors.

Mara said, “René Descartes, this is Aristotle.”

The new man did not take the offered chair.

“Before that name means anything,” he said, “tell me how any of us know that you are real.”
