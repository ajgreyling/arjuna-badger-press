# Chapter 22 — The People in the Wall

Elena could close the panel and become an incident report, or leave it open and become somebody else’s lesson.

Neither appealed.

The new philosopher sat in her path with his chair angled so she could still retreat. That restraint had been chosen, which made it more dangerous than ignorance.

“Move,” she said.

He stood at once.

Behind him, the residents watched. Aristotle held his notebook. Nietzsche looked ill and alert. The cameras had already marked the encounter for review.

“You want to know who cleans,” Elena said to Karl Marx. Léa had identified him over the staff channel minutes after retrieval. “Come and see. But you do not speak for me, and you do not touch anything without asking.”

“Agreed.”

She pulled him through and shut the panel on the philosophers.

The service corridor was built for carts, not revelations. Laundry sacks narrowed the turn beside an environmental console. The artificial daylight leaked through maintenance grilles in hard stripes. Marx stared at everything with an appetite Elena recognised from auditors: the desire to make the whole hidden system legible before lunch.

She took him first to laundry. Two staff sorted resident clothing by treatment requirement and contamination protocol. One had been awake since before the retrieval window. Neither wanted to be filmed, so Elena did not name them.

“The clothes return folded,” she said. “Inside, that looks like a feature of the cupboard.”

At waste staging, she showed him separate clinical, food and general streams. At food preparation, he watched sealed trays move from a secure lift to the residents’ visible pantry. At camera maintenance, edit flags stacked faster than the night operator could clear them.

The scorched pan waited on a shelf.

“Hume’s,” Marx said.

“Ours now, apparently.”

“Who cleaned Nietzsche’s cup?”

“A contractor whose name will not become a symbol in your speech.”

He opened his mouth, then closed it.

At the translation rack, green queues moved steadily across three displays. Red priority markers interrupted them whenever a philosopher coined a phrase or forced the system to preserve a term. Staff corrections sat below.

“A resident waits seconds,” Elena said. “A worker can wait a shift. If a public clip is involved, the resident jumps again.”

“Who chose the priority?”

“A policy group. No names on the interface.”

“Access?”

She held up her amber band. “Waste, laundry, environmental systems and resident-adjacent service. Not archives. Not control. Not the room where our bunker classifications are assigned.”

“Yours?”

“Continuity-support, review pending.”

“Meaning?”

“Meaning I keep the sealed environment fit for human beings but may not qualify as one of the human beings kept in it.”

He swore softly. The translator rendered it without a flag.

“Different contracts, different agencies,” Elena continued. “Researchers have provisional places. Medical staff have priority pathways. Facilities have risk classifications. Contractors have acknowledgements.”

“And your family?”

“No derived status.”

They reached the staff rest room. Its light was always the same white. The air register above the door clicked twice before supplying a thin current.

Marx saw the name on her locker. “Bako.”

“Yes.”

“The petition.”

“Nia is my daughter.”

His face changed into recognition and then, too quickly, interpretation.

“So the person outside—”

“No.”

He stopped.

“Do not make her the perfect representative of a class you have just discovered. She studied soil and crop systems because she loves the work. Her petition entered through an employee programme because that was the only door offered. It was screened, shortened and put before you because someone thought a daughter with a useful skill would make good ethics.”

“What do you want?” he asked.

The correction was small and real.

“A seat in the council. The worker briefings. The allocation criteria. A recorded vote, not consultation after decisions.”

Security found them at the control-room threshold. Mara arrived behind two red bands, her anger compressed into perfect posture.

“Elena, return to your station. Mr Marx, residents are not authorised in this layer.”

“He came under my authorisation,” Elena said.

“You do not possess that authority.”

“Then record that as the first matter in my request for standing.”

Marx said, “I will return when she returns through the resident door.”

Mara held his gaze. “This is not a negotiation.”

“Then you do not need my presence.”

Elena disliked his use of refusal on her behalf. She also saw the cameras over the threshold and knew the image had leverage.

“I will accompany him,” she said. “I am not his condition. I am making my own request.”

Mara let them pass.

Before they entered the house, Léa caught Elena at the translation rack. She turned one monitor so the queue order was visible: Nietzsche’s flagged word at priority one, Kant’s at two, a facilities safety correction at fourteen.

“If you ask for standing,” Léa said, “ask for equal semantic priority too.”

Elena studied her. “Will you put that in writing?”

Léa hesitated, then opened a new log entry.

“Yes.”
