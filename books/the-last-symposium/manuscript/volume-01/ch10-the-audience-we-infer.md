# Chapter 10 — The Audience We Infer

Hume greeted the red light as if it belonged to a shy dinner guest.

“Good morning,” he told it. “I am assured that you exist.”

The confession room offered no reply. Its single chair faced a black glass eye. A line of text appeared on the wall: **DESCRIBE YOUR FIRST IMPRESSION OF THE MNEMOSYNE PROJECT.**

“My first impression was chiefly medical and would embarrass us both.”

The prompt vanished. After a pause, another appeared.

**WHAT DO YOU BELIEVE HUMANITY OWES THE FUTURE?**

Hume shifted in the chair. The camera tracked nothing visibly; it did not need to. He imagined a clerk behind the wall, a minister watching later, perhaps a schoolchild in a century that had inherited either their wisdom or their ruins. Each imagined listener altered what seemed polite, useful, or true.

“A future person is a convenient creditor,” he said. “They cannot correct the account.”

The red light remained red.

He leaned forward and lowered his voice. “But if you are frightened, as I suspect you are, then I am frightened in your company. That is something.”

The wall displayed **THANK YOU.**

Interesting.

He found Léa in the lounge with a tablet open beside a tangle of translation equipment. She wore a green band and had the exhausted alertness of a person whose machines had behaved all night but whose users had not.

“Who says thank you?” Hume asked.

“The prompt system.”

“Does it approve of sympathy?”

“It detects completion.”

“By what sign?”

“A pause, semantic closure, the prompt category, some timing rules.” She looked up. “It does not know whether you are sincere.”

“A reassuring limitation. Do you?”

“No.”

He liked her immediately.

Léa explained that every communal room was recorded, that the confession room produced higher-quality direct testimony, and that selected clips might be released for education. *Might* carried the faint translation tone, not because the word was difficult but because the institution had put too many intentions inside it.

“Selected by whom?”

“The public communications team proposes. Ethics reviews. Mara authorises.”

“And the remainder?”

“Raw archive. Restricted.”

“May I restrict myself?”

Léa’s eyes moved to the camera above the lounge display. “You can choose not to enter the confession room.”

“The house itself remains a confession room with sofas.”

Descartes, seated at the far table with printed translation logs, said, “You assume an audience because a lamp illuminates.”

“I assume an audience because people spent considerable money installing lamps that look back.”

“That establishes an apparatus, not another mind.”

Hume sat opposite him. “How did you establish mine?”

“I have not established it with the certainty I have of my own thought.”

“Yet you complained when I used the coffee.”

“That required only that your body had acted.”

“And when you laughed?”

Descartes lowered his eyes to the logs. “I experienced an occasion of amusement.”

“You are very determined to be lonely.”

The remark landed harder than intended. Hume saw it in the stillness around Descartes’ mouth and regretted the neatness of it. Sympathy did not provide proof of another mind. It did, however, provide evidence that one’s words had entered a world not wholly one’s own.

“Forgive me,” Hume said. “That was made for an audience.”

Descartes gave him a small, unwilling nod.

Hume returned to the confession room after lunch carrying his stitched notebook. He placed it closed on his knee, beyond the camera’s likely view, and offered the first performance.

He sat upright. He praised the project’s courage. He described humanity as a society extended through time, with obligations to those not yet born. He said uncertainty should proportion action rather than prevent it. Every statement was defensible. He selected only those statements likely to comfort the sort of person who funded underground houses.

The prompt system thanked him after four minutes.

He left, walked once around the lounge, drank water, and returned.

For the second performance he slouched.

“I have met two philosophers and six doors,” he said. “The doors are more certain of their authority.”

He described the burned pan, Descartes’ suspicion, Aristotle’s appetite for categories and the hidden corridor through which invisible people maintained visible order. He confessed to vanity. He said he enjoyed seeming moderate because moderation placed the other man at an extreme. He admitted that the imagined viewer had already changed him and that this admission was itself calculated to seem candid.

The prompt system waited longer before thanking him.

In the lounge he wrote:

**Experiment 1. Same witness, contrary manners. Observe which account institution names true.**

He labelled the first recording **PUBLIC VIRTUE** and the second **MANUFACTURED CANDOUR** on the release forms Léa had provided. The forms described usage, archive classes and safety review. The line for resident consent was marked *orientation acknowledgement pending*.

Late in the afternoon Noah Vale entered carrying a tablet against his chest. His manner suggested the pleasure of good news softened for a solemn occasion.

“David, the team reviewed your testimony.”

“Which David?”

Noah laughed. “Your second recording is remarkable. Warm, self-aware, accessible without simplifying. It feels authentic.”

Hume glanced towards the camera above the lounge.

“Does it?”

“People need to see that this is not a museum of marble busts. You gave us the human stakes.”

“I gave you a performance labelled *Manufactured Candour*.”

Noah’s smile survived, though it required effort. “All communication is shaped. Naming the shaping is part of the honesty.”

“And the first?”

“More formal. Valuable, but less alive.”

“It was no less sincere.”

“The second lets people connect.”

There it was: not a lie, perhaps, but an appetite. The institution preferred the self that displayed its wounds cleanly. It called him authentic when he had arranged the light.

“Who are these people?” Hume asked.

“The educational release audience.”

“Which consists of?”

“The initial distribution is still being negotiated.”

“Then we infer them.”

Noah glanced at Léa, who had stopped pretending to work. “For now.”

“Show me the agreement by which I allowed either performance to leave this house.”

Noah opened the tablet. He moved through two screens, then a third. “You were briefed on recording during orientation.”

“I was ill, newly manufactured and wearing another century’s underclothes. Did I consent?”

“The project authorisation covers documentation.”

“That is not what I asked.”

Léa said quietly, “There is no resident-signed release contract.”

Noah’s inclusive calm hardened into institutional calm. “Then we should formalise one.”

Hume opened his notebook and added a second line beneath the first.

**Result: they reward the confession they can use.**

Then, after considering Noah’s face, he added:

**Do not mistake usefulness for malice. Do not mistake it for consent either.**
