# Chapter 19 — A Place Refused

Kant found Nietzsche waiting in the briefing room with his refusal written on a single numbered page.

The paper had been torn from Nietzsche’s notebook. That mattered: the house could copy it, film it, archive it, but the numbered absence would remain in the nearest thing each resident possessed to privacy.

“You are early,” Kant said.

“You are disappointed.”

“I am surprised.”

“A punctual rebellion offends twice.”

Nietzsche’s skin had the grey cast Samira had warned them to notice. His right hand pressed flat against the table. Kant did not ask about pain. Samira’s instruction was precise: offer assistance, do not convert observation into command unless capacity changed.

Mara entered with Noah and a security officer who remained beside the door.

Nietzsche slid the page across the table. “I relinquish the allocation attached to me. Give it to Nia Bako and the members of her household she identifies.”

Noah read it first. Mara did not.

“You cannot transfer the allocation,” she said.

“You have not examined the document.”

“The allocation is not property. It is a programme-continuity designation tied to a retrieved resident.”

“Then remove the designation.”

“It would return to the authority that issued it.”

“With the instruction that it pass to her.”

“That instruction would have no force.”

Nietzsche smiled without pleasure. “How beautifully the gift defends itself from refusal.”

Kant held out his hand for the paper. Nietzsche let him take it.

The declaration named Nia, not merely “a child” or “a family.” Kant approved of the precision. It made the intended beneficiary visible. It did not make the act effective.

“If Dr Voss’s account is correct,” Kant said, “you know your refusal will not secure the end you claim.”

Nietzsche turned on him. “Then compel the institution to become correct.”

“I intend to challenge the rule. That is different from pretending the rule has changed because you have purified yourself of association with it.”

Noah’s stylus stopped.

“Purified?” Nietzsche said.

“You object to an honour that places you in debt. Very well. But if the place returns to an unnamed authority while Nia remains outside, the only immediate change is to your relation to your own conscience.”

“Conscience is your instrument, not mine.”

“Then your image. Your rank. Your refusal to be the sort of man who accepts.”

Pain made Nietzsche’s eyes water, but his voice grew sharper. “And you would remain the sort of man who files an objection in the proper form while a child dies according to procedure.”

“A child does not live because you stage your innocence.”

“Nor because you universalise her corpse.”

The translation flag sounded over *universalise*. Léa’s system placed three pale alternatives beneath the transcript. Nobody looked at them.

Mara said, “The allocation question is under review. The forecast window is changing. Places cannot be reassigned ad hoc without medical, logistical and jurisdictional consequences.”

“Who, exactly, reviews it?” Nietzsche asked.

“The continuity authority.”

“Names.”

“Not available.”

“Then there is your god, Kant. Invisible, lawgiving, beyond appeal, and very well sheltered.”

Kant felt anger rise and tested it before speaking. “You may insult me. You may not use this girl as a prop in a contest over which of us is less compromised.”

Nietzsche pushed back his chair. “I am the only one giving up anything.”

“You have given up nothing that the institution permits you to lose.”

The sentence revealed its cruelty at the same moment as its truth.

Nietzsche stood. His face emptied. One hand reached for the table and missed.

Kant caught him under the arms before his head struck the floor.

For one instant Nietzsche fought him with surprising force.

“Do not,” he said.

“You are falling.”

“Do not make me your duty.”

“At present you are a man whose head must not strike stone.”

Samira arrived at a run, knelt and asked Nietzsche to name the pain. He answered once, then retched. She sent Noah away, ordered the security officer to clear the corridor and told Kant exactly where to place his hands.

They moved Nietzsche onto a wheeled bed. His refusal remained on the briefing table beneath the camera.

In the medical corridor the bed would not fit cleanly around the open equipment trolley. Samira struck a square section of wall with the heel of her hand.

“Assistance, medical. Now.”

A panel Kant had taken for seamless cladding opened inward.

Cold work light spilled from the gap. A gloved hand pulled the trolley through. Behind it, for less than a second, Kant saw shelves of linen, yellow waste containers and a person in an amber access band.

Then the wall closed.

The house had acquired another interior.

Samira pushed Nietzsche toward treatment. Kant followed until she stopped him at the camera boundary.

“You know his medication schedule now,” she said. “You do not know his diagnosis unless he tells you. Do you understand?”

“Yes.”

“Then bring water at the agreed times. Offer it. Do not supervise him drinking it.”

Kant looked back at the wall that had opened. “Who answered you?”

“A member of staff.”

“Who?”

“Ask the project. Right now, leave me the patient.”

He returned to the briefing room. Mara had gone. Noah had placed Nietzsche’s declaration in a transparent archive sleeve. It was recorded, preserved and without effect.

Kant set a glass of water beside it for later, then moved it away from the paper before the condensation could leave a mark.
