# Chapter 7 — Mind the Gap

Samira deleted the telemetry request for the third time.

It returned marked **EXECUTIVE PRIORITY**.

Across the medical suite, René sat beneath a warming blanket reading the written translation of his own speech. He had corrected three renderings and circled a fourth. Retrieval had left him cold, sleep-starved and intermittently nauseated. The control room wanted full cognitive telemetry mapped against the nine-second archive discontinuity.

Mara entered without her coat. “You saw the request.”

“I declined it.”

“We have an unexplained gap involving a retrieved consciousness.”

“We have an unexplained archive gap involving a patient.”

“If it is neurological—”

“Then I investigate clinically with his consent. You do not receive his raw cognitive data because the programme is anxious.”

René looked up. “Can such data reveal thought?”

“It can reveal correlates of activity,” Samira said. “Patterns associated with sleep, seizure, attention, injury. Not a transcript of your mind.”

“Yet the machine claims to have instantiated that mind in a body.”

Mara said, “The machine makes no claims. People do.”

“Machines are fortunate in their advocates.”

Samira almost smiled. Mara did not.

René folded the transcript. “If this body were damaged, would my thought change?”

“Almost certainly, depending on the damage.”

“Then what you call mind is governed by matter.”

“I said injury can alter thought. I did not settle what thought is.”

“If a violin is damaged, music changes. That does not make the wood identical to the music.”

“Nor does it prove music survives without an instrument.”

He accepted this with a slight inclination of his head. The question mattered because his body hurt, not because history expected him to defend a doctrine. Samira recorded the temperature of his hands and adjusted the room.

Aristotle arrived at the open threshold carrying broth.

“Mateo said he had not eaten.”

“You may offer it,” Samira said.

Aristotle entered. “Did the examination reveal injury?”

“That is René’s information.”

“His health bears on our common task.”

“So does yours. Would you like me to give him your clinical notes?”

“If they affect our work.”

“Your permission is not his.”

Aristotle set the bowl down too hard. Broth slopped over the rim. “A household cannot act wisely while relevant facts are hidden.”

“A household that owns every fact about a person owns the person.”

The translation flagged *owns*. Aristotle heard property; Samira had also meant mastery. The brief delay held them apart long enough for René to speak.

“I asked her not to provide the telemetry.”

Aristotle turned to him. “Then I will ask you only this: are you able to deliberate tonight?”

“Probably.”

“That is sufficient for tonight.”

He left the broth.

At dinner René wore the earpiece willingly for the first time. He also brought the printed translation log. Two microphones hung above the table. He pointed to the sentence beside his circled correction.

“When I said I could not doubt that thinking occurred, the system rendered *I know that I am thinking*.”

Léa sat with a tablet open to alternative renderings. “The confidence score was high.”

“Confidence is not identity.”

“No.”

“If the only version others hear is a plausible sentence, how can first-person certainty be communicated without becoming third-person manufacture?”

Aristotle tore bread. “No account of oneself enters another person unchanged.”

“Then testimony is always less certain than its speaker’s act.”

Samira tasted the broth Aristotle had made. Too much salt, but improved from yesterday.

“Medicine depends on testimony,” she said. “Pain cannot be measured from outside in the way temperature can. That does not make it useless. It makes trust part of the instrument.”

“And if the patient lies?” Mara asked.

“Then the instrument is difficult. Not abolished.”

René studied Samira. She had refused him neither doubt nor care. More important, she had denied Mara access when compliance would have been easier. A boundary had done what reassurance could not: it showed that Samira recognised an interior she did not own.

After dinner Samira entered the confession room. Camera 4B’s red light waited above the prompt.

“It would be easier,” she told it, “if these men were texts. Texts do not need sleep. They do not withhold consent. They do not become frightened when you ask whether their minds survived a machine.”

She leaned closer.

“It would also be false.”

Her wrist display vibrated before she reached the door. A new directive from the continuity office filled the screen.

**CLASSIFY RESIDENTS: MEDICAL SUITABILITY FOR PROTECTED ALLOCATION.**

Below it, nineteen empty rows waited for bodies.
