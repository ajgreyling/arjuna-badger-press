# Chapter 9 — A Reasonable Breakfast

David Hume had burned the butter but not, he thought, the egg.

The distinction seemed worth preserving. The black square before him had glowed red beneath the pan when he touched one of its little white signs; another touch had made the glow disappear. The pan continued to grow hotter. He had therefore moved it to a square he had not yet touched. The butter ceased its furious complaint.

This was not knowledge of the apparatus. It was breakfast.

Behind him, two men stood in the kitchen doorway as solemnly as magistrates at an execution. One was broad through the shoulders, grey in the beard, and had the proprietary displeasure of a man whose household had been entered by a guest who knew where the eggs were kept. The other held himself narrowly inside his borrowed clothes. His fingers covered the object fitted behind his ear.

Hume lifted the egg with a wooden implement and examined its underside.

“You have been awake less than an hour,” the narrow man said. “You cannot know whether that is food.”

The voice arrived in Hume’s ear in clear English, though the man’s mouth made French sounds. A faint tone accompanied *know*. The machine had hesitated over the word, which improved it in Hume’s estimation.

“I was told it is an egg,” Hume said.

“By those who claim to have drawn us out of death.”

“A circumstance which does not make them wrong about poultry.”

The bearded man’s mouth tightened. The narrow man stared at the egg as though treachery might be visible in the white.

Hume had woken under lights with a pain in every joint and a woman asking permission before touching his wrist. He had been shown water, cloth, a lavatory whose operation seemed both miraculous and offensively wasteful, then a tablet of coloured pictures describing centuries that had apparently happened without him. The physicians had advised rest. His body had advised food. Of the two authorities, his body had the longer acquaintance with him.

He cut the egg and ate half.

The narrow man waited.

“Well?”

“It is under-seasoned.”

For a moment the man tried not to smile. The attempt failed at one corner of his mouth.

“René Descartes,” said the bearded man, indicating him. “I am Aristotle.”

“So I was informed. David Hume.”

Aristotle entered and inspected the scattered shells, the open cold cupboard and the unfamiliar jars Hume had arranged in order of confidence. “You have used three eggs.”

“One was an inquiry into freshness. One was defeated by the pan. This one is breakfast.”

“There are rations.”

“Then we should inquire whether they are generous before adding remorse to hunger.”

The refrigerator display showed rows of little green bars beside categories of food. Hume had learned only that opening the door caused a light, and closing it preserved the cold. He trusted both propositions more than he had twenty minutes earlier. Neither had become necessary truth.

Descartes came closer. “You concede you have no demonstration that the next bite will resemble the first.”

“Entirely.” Hume ate it.

“Then why?”

“Because I have observed men survive eggs and very few survive the permanent postponement of meals.”

“You observed that in another world.”

“Another time. The question of worlds remains rather grand for this hour.”

Descartes rested both hands on the counter. “If the senses are administered here—light, language, even memory—past experience may be precisely what deceives you.”

Hume considered him more carefully. There was fatigue beneath the exactness, and fear beneath the fatigue. This was no parlour game. The man had been made to doubt because doubt was the only tool the house had not issued him.

“It may,” Hume said. “But you came into the kitchen when you smelled burning.”

Descartes glanced at Aristotle.

“You inferred a fire,” Hume continued, “or at least a danger, from an odour. You expected the floor to bear you. You expected that door to admit you. You have trusted a hundred habits in order to dispute my one egg.”

“I do not call those expectations knowledge.”

“Then we differ less than your posture suggests.”

Aristotle took down another pan. “We differ over whether repeated experience reveals what a thing ordinarily does or merely trains the observer.”

“An excellent difference,” Hume said. “Will it scramble an egg?”

Aristotle set the pan on the hob, touched the sign once, and waited. Hume noted the sequence. The old Greek had acquired a household competence and was pleased to display it. Descartes watched the controls rather than Aristotle, perhaps seeking a rule that could survive a malicious engineer.

“In most cases,” Aristotle said, “we reason from what happens for the most part. Practical judgement is not geometry.”

“There,” Hume said to Descartes. “Your friend has smuggled custom into the house wearing a respectable robe.”

“And you,” Aristotle said, “have renamed practical judgement so that appetite may claim it.”

“Appetite had the prior claim.”

Aristotle’s pan warmed. Hume touched the same sign on his own square. Nothing visible changed. He touched it again, as he had before. A small numeral brightened. Satisfied by the repetition, he returned the burned butter to the heat.

Descartes said, “The woman called Mara expects an answer before noon.”

“About the grain?”

“Agricultural reserves,” Aristotle corrected. “Whether they should be diverted into protected storage now, at the cost of present distribution.”

Hume had been given the summary during his medical examination: a mountain under ice, instruments, a probability, governments constructing elaborate cellars for the future while people in the present argued over whose future counted. Forty-two chances in a hundred, though no one had yet explained what counted as the event or how long the hundred had to unfold.

“What commonly follows from waiting?” he asked.

“Lost construction time,” Aristotle said.

“And from acting?”

“Higher prices. Seizure. Hunger in places already vulnerable.”

“Then uncertainty has not spared you a decision. It has only denied you innocence.”

The pan began to hiss.

Aristotle frowned. “That is too hot.”

“It behaved differently before.”

“You placed the pan on another ring.”

Hume looked down. The first square bore a small sign like two linked circles. The new one bore a single broad circle. He had taken their equal blackness for equal function.

Smoke lifted from the butter in one elegant ribbon.

A shriek filled the kitchen.

Hume flinched hard enough to strike his hip against the counter. Red lights pulsed above the doors. Aristotle seized a cloth and dragged the pan aside. Descartes stepped backward, one hand clamped over his earpiece, his earlier amusement gone.

“It is an alarm,” Aristotle shouted.

“That inference seems well supported!” Hume shouted back.

A section of wall beside the pantry opened.

Not the white door through which staff brought them during the day. A narrow grey panel, flush enough that Hume had taken it for decoration, folded inward. A man in amber work clothes came through carrying a small extinguisher. He moved directly to a control under the counter, keyed something, and silenced the room.

The sudden quiet rang.

The man surveyed the pan, then Hume. “Oil fire?”

“Butter and optimism.”

The man opened a vent. “You need less of both on boost mode.”

Aristotle was not looking at the pan. He stared through the open panel. Beyond it lay an undecorated corridor under hard white light. Metal carts stood against one wall. On the nearest were folded towels and trays of clean dishes.

“That door was sealed,” Descartes said.

“It was described as a services enclosure,” Aristotle said.

The worker glanced back. “It is a service corridor.”

“Mateo Ruiz,” Hume said, recalling the name from orientation.

Mateo nodded once.

“Who works there?” Aristotle asked.

“People who stop breakfast becoming evacuation.” Mateo took the scorched pan with a gloved hand. “This one stays. Facilities will assess it.”

He stepped back through the opening. Before the panel closed, Hume saw a woman farther down the corridor wheel a cart around a corner. She did not look toward them. The grey wall returned and concealed her so perfectly that, had the alarm not sounded, the three philosophers might have continued believing clean towels occurred in the night by nature.

Hume wrote the first line of an experiment in his mind.

The house had habits too.
