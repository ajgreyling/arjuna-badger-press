# Chapter 21 — Who Cleaned Before Dawn?

Karl woke beneath a machine and five dead men told him the future.

They did it badly.

The physician was competent. She asked him where he hurt, gave him no information he had not requested and did not permit the men behind the glass to call his confusion miraculous. Once she judged him able to sit, however, the explanations began.

The oldest said they had been retrieved from different periods. The Frenchman said sensory evidence about the event remained mediated and incomplete. The Scotsman suggested breakfast before metaphysics. The small Prussian started with rights. The sick man in dark glasses said nothing and looked amused.

Karl touched the cloth of the shirt they had put on him.

“Who made this?”

Mara Voss answered from the end of the bed. “It was fabricated for you from measurements in the historical record and adjusted after retrieval.”

“By whom?”

“The clothing unit.”

“The unit has hands?”

Hume laughed.

The director did not. “Project staff and automated equipment.”

“Who fed the equipment? Who cleaned this floor before I arrived? Who built the machine?”

“Thousands of people across multiple institutions.”

“And which of them are in this room?”

Nobody answered.

They took him through the corridor. Doors stood at regular intervals, each with an invisible resident and visible number. The house was expensive in the institutional manner: its cost disappeared into flawless surfaces.

In the kitchen, food waited in identical containers. Karl opened cupboards, inspected labels, lifted the waste bin and examined the clean liner. He found the scorched pan in a tray marked for repair.

“Who burned it?”

Hume raised a hand.

“Who cleaned it?”

Hume lowered the hand.

“This cup?” Karl asked. A small chip marked its base.

Nietzsche said, “I dirtied it. An angel of sanitation restored it to innocence.”

“A worker,” Kant said.

“Name?”

No one knew.

Karl looked around at the shelves filled before anyone woke, the clothes laundered without laundry, the stable air, the table at which humanity’s representatives had apparently discovered only yesterday that rubbish moved.

Aristotle brought him a notebook.

The entries were careful: likely waste removal, observed panel opening, environmental adjustment, dish sanitation. Aristotle had left the actor blank whenever he was unknown.

Karl read two pages. “When did you begin this?”

“Last night.”

“Why?”

“We discovered effects without visible causes.”

“The labour became interesting when it became mysterious.”

Aristotle accepted the rebuke without pretending it was praise. “Yes.”

“Before that, the clean room appeared as a property of the room.” Karl tapped the counter. “The meal appeared as food. The shirt as clothing. A finished thing arrives with its history removed, and because the relation among people is hidden, the thing seems to possess the power itself.”

“You mean we misclassified the cause,” Aristotle said.

“You classified what stood before you and called the missing people circumstances.”

Mara summoned them to formal orientation at ten-thirty. Karl declined.

“The forecast briefing is time-sensitive,” she said.

“So is breakfast production.”

“Facilities staff are not available for resident interviews.”

“I have not asked for an interview.”

He took a chair to the kitchen wall and sat beside the panel through which the gloved arm had appeared. He ate bread. He asked Aristotle to describe precisely how the panel opened. Descartes inspected the seams. Kant requested the access policy. Mara ended the wall connection.

At eleven-seventeen there was movement behind the panel: a trolley wheel, a clipped exchange the earpieces could not render through the wall, then silence.

Karl waited.

“This may be a poor use of our deadline,” Kant said.

“For whom?”

At twelve-oh-three the panel opened inward.

A woman in grey work clothes and an amber band stood on the other side holding a sack of clean linen. She saw Karl’s chair less than a metre from her and stopped. One hand moved toward the control on her wrist.

“If that calls security,” Karl said, “call them. But tell me your name before they arrive.”

She looked past him at the camera, then back into the bright corridor behind her.

“Elena Bako,” she said.

The name meant nothing to Karl. It changed every face behind him.
