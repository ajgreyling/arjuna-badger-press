# Chapter 1 — The First Animal

At 05:40, Mara Voss put both hands around a coffee she had forgotten to drink and watched sixteen governments fail to agree on whether a dead man counted as news.

Their representatives occupied tiles along the control-room wall: some still in evening clothes, others in offices bright with another hemisphere’s morning. The argument had begun with disclosure and slipped, as such arguments did, into ownership.

“The retrieval is not a national asset,” Mara said.

“Then you will have no objection to simultaneous access.”

“You have simultaneous access. You are using it to delay the event.”

Beyond the glass, technicians moved around the circular chamber. Nobody hurried. That was worse than hurrying. Each check had been practised until panic could wear the clothing of routine.

Samira’s voice came through Mara’s headset. “Clinical team ready. I want silence in the room during emergence.”

One of the delegates was saying the public could not be trusted with an incomplete explanation.

“There is no complete explanation,” Mara said. “The window opens in three minutes.”

She muted the wall.

The chamber lights lowered. A shape lay beneath a white thermal sheet where, an hour before, there had been no one. Instruments supplied numbers with the serene colours of ordinary medicine. The machine’s deepest workings were concealed behind seamless panels, as if tidiness could make the event less obscene.

At 05:59 the body convulsed.

Samira caught one shoulder without pinning it. “Do not restrain him.”

The man dragged in air. His throat worked around a cry that emerged twice: once raw from his mouth and once, a fraction later, as clean modern speech in every earpiece.

“It hurts.”

The translation was too calm.

Mara entered only when Samira signalled. The man’s eyes moved before his head did: ceiling, lamp, gloved hands, door, Mara’s red access band. His gaze held none of the drifting confusion she had prepared for. It collected.

“I am Samira,” the doctor said. “You are in a place of medicine. May I touch your wrist?”

The earpiece in the man’s left ear rendered her words. His fingers rose toward it and stopped when pain caught his throat.

“The voice is here,” he rasped, touching the device, “but the woman is there.”

A small tone sounded: the translation system warning that *voice* had carried more than one possible sense.

“Yes,” Samira said. “The device helps us understand one another. May I touch your wrist?”

“For what?”

“To measure the blood moving through it.”

He considered the answer, then turned his palm upward. Samira fastened a white medical band around his left wrist. She asked separately before lifting his eyelid, before placing the sensor against his chest, before offering water. He answered each request rather than granting one general permission.

After the water, he asked, “Are you the one who commands here?”

“In this room, where your body is concerned, I am.”

He looked at Mara.

“And she commands you?”

“Not in this room.”

For the first time, something like approval touched his face.

Mara had rehearsed her introduction until it contained no word that could be mistaken for worship. “My name is Mara Voss. You are at a research facility near Geneva, in a time long after the life you remember. We believe you are Aristotle of Stagira.”

“You believe.”

“Yes.”

“And what do I remember?”

“That is one of the things Doctor El-Khoury will ask you when you are stronger.”

“Then you do not yet know whether your belief is true.”

On the wall behind Mara, a green line confirmed that his pulse was settling. Its reassurance irritated her.

She gave him the mandate without numbers. Beneath ice at the end of the world, multiple physical signs indicated a system becoming unstable. Models described outcomes severe enough to break harvests, states and the ordinary movement of food. Governments were preparing places of continuity. The project had brought him here to help decide the principles by which human life and knowledge might be carried through.

He listened with his eyes half closed. Whenever the earpiece hesitated over an abstract noun—*civilisation*, *continuity*, *probability*—the warning tone made his brow tighten.

When she finished, he asked Samira for more water.

“You wish me to advise a city?” he said.

“Many cities. Potentially all of them.”

“But I see no citizens.”

“You will meet the others one at a time.”

“Others like me?”

“Thinkers chosen from history.”

“Chosen by whom?”

“An international committee.”

“Who appointed it?”

“Participating institutions and governments.”

“Who feeds this place? Who guards it? Who may leave? Who sees what I say?”

Mara felt the prepared briefing buckling under questions that did not dispute any of its facts. “Those arrangements can be explained during orientation.”

“They are not arrangements beside your question. They are the question’s shape.”

He tried to sit. Pain stopped him, and Samira’s hand hovered without touching until he nodded. Together they raised the bed.

“Every association is formed for some good,” he said, slowly, testing the translated words as though they might be counterfeit coins. “Or for what appears good to those who form it. Before I speak of saving your association, I must know what association you have made here.”

Through the glass, Mara heard the muted representatives resume their argument. She had expected gratitude, horror, perhaps grandeur. Instead he studied the pattern of access bands in the room.

A technician whispered, “First resident stable.”

Aristotle turned toward the sound.

“You call me the first.”

“Operational shorthand,” Mara said.

“Not Aristotle.”

“We did not know whether you would answer to it.”

“Yet you knew what answer you required from me.”

He was exhausted. His voice scraped. Still, the room had reorganised itself around his questions. Mara had come to present humanity’s emergency to an adviser. The patient on the bed was examining the institution that had manufactured his authority.

Samira told him he needed rest.

“Soon,” he said. He looked again at Mara. “First show me where I am to live. Then I will agree to learn how your world ends.”
