# Chapter 16 — Nineteen Places

Aristotle counted the people twice before he counted the beds.

The exercise displayed three hundred and twelve candidates for one hundred and eighty protected places. Families were grouped by dependency. Specialists carried coloured skill markers. Officials occupied a separate column whose stated function was continuity of legitimate command. The interface invited the council to change priorities and observe the resulting population.

It was the sort of instrument he had asked for on his first day: lives arranged so that relevant differences could be examined.

He distrusted it at once.

Five empty chairs remained around the briefing table from Kant’s morning session. Noah had wanted them removed for the recording. Kant had refused. Now each vacancy seemed to contain one of the families on the wall.

“This is not a real site,” Mara said. “It combines constraints from several facilities to test principles.”

“Do the numbers correspond to real capacities?” Amina asked from the side wall.

“In aggregate, approximately.”

“That word carries most of the building.”

Mara did not disagree. “The bounded question is whether allocation should prioritise transferable skills, family integrity, demographic resilience, existing public office, lottery, or some combination.”

Aristotle selected water systems. Nine candidates brightened. He selected food production. Twenty-three. Medicine produced eighteen, though several had dependants who displaced other specialists under the fixed capacity.

The interface encouraged a peculiar moral cleanliness. Touch a category and persons became green. Touch another and children vanished into grey without crying.

Kant kept his hands away from the controls. “Who designed the categories?”

“A planning consortium.”

“Who appointed the consortium?”

“Participating governments and facility operators.”

“What interests do those selectors possess in the outcome?”

Mara said, “That is not represented in this exercise.”

“Then the exercise is unfit.”

Aristotle said, “An interested judge may still identify a necessary physician.”

“And may define his own office as necessary command.”

“That is an argument for examining the function, not refusing all selection.”

Hume sat beside the empty chair marked for outside claimants. “Nia Bako would ask whether agricultural skill counts before or after one’s mother becomes a dependant.”

No one answered.

Descartes had moved closer to the screen. “The total is wrong.”

Noah checked his tablet. “Capacity one-eighty. Selected one-seventy-four. Six remain.”

“There are one hundred and ninety-three occupied allocations.”

“No.” Noah pointed at the footer. “The selected count is—”

“It excludes the protected block.”

Descartes enlarged a narrow band beneath the population chart. It had no family, skill or office categories. It contained nineteen white markers, fixed under every scenario. The interface would not deselect them.

Aristotle felt recognition before understanding. **RESIDENT CONTINUITY: 19.** The phrase he had seen redacted on his first day returned whole.

“What are those?” Kant asked.

Mara’s face became still in the way it did before a decision she had delayed reached her anyway.

“Provisional allocations associated with Project Mnemosyne.”

Hume looked from the markers to the residents. “Associated how?”

“One for each scheduled historical resident.”

The room did not react together. Descartes stepped back from the display as though it had emitted heat. Hume looked at Nia’s empty chair. Kant closed his eyes for one moment. Aristotle continued looking at the markers because looking away would have been a choice too.

“Physical beds?” Amina asked.

“Protected allocation tokens. Sites are not final.”

“But they displace candidates in the exercise,” Kant said.

“Yes.”

“Were we expected to recommend rules governing allocations while ours remained outside the count?”

“The places were intended to preserve the council through the advisory period and, if required, beyond it.”

Hume’s voice had lost its warmth. “The dead receive dependants of none and displace families of several.”

“You are not dead.”

“An expensive correction.”

Aristotle returned to the categories he had made on Day One. Food. Medicine. Building. Governance. Education. Memory. He had argued that a settlement required not merely bodies but capacities directed toward a common life. Philosophy might belong under judgement, civic education or preservation of knowledge. He had never written *Aristotle*. He had not needed to. The selection committee had written it for him.

“My taxonomy,” he said.

Mara waited.

“You asked what kinds of persons were necessary. I supplied functions. Those functions can be used to place us among the necessary.”

“The allocations predate your list.”

“The justification need not.”

Kant stood. “No person here can judge this exercise impartially while his own place is protected from its rules.”

“No person anywhere is without interests,” Aristotle said, hearing how quickly the defence served him.

“Then interests must be disclosed and constrained.”

“Agreed. But if our judgement is useful—”

“Useful to whom?” Hume asked.

The question was gentle again, which made it worse.

Aristotle studied the white markers. He could argue that preserving the council preserved a capacity needed by all. He could argue that practical wisdom was itself a survival skill, that settlements failed from faction as surely as hunger, that nineteen places among global capacities were symbolically large but materially small. Each claim might be true. Each now arrived carrying his own body.

On the screen Nia’s composite analogue—a young grower with one dependant—turned grey when philosophy and governance weights rose.

“Remove our block,” Descartes said.

Mara shook her head. “The interface does not permit it.”

“Then the exercise is a demonstration, not a decision.”

“It is both. That is how policy exercises work.”

Kant addressed the visible camera. “The council cannot endorse any allocation principle until its members’ protected status is included openly and subject to the same justification.”

Noah said, “Is that a consensus statement?”

Hume gave a short, astonished laugh.

Aristotle did not answer. To surrender the place immediately would be clean and perhaps useless; the token might return to the project, not Nia or anyone like her. To keep it while designing justice was corrupting even if his work genuinely mattered. Action required particulars the institution still withheld. Delay preserved his benefit.

He had taught that virtue was not possession of a sentence but choice formed in action. No available action left him unstained.

An alarm sounded far below them—not the kitchen’s shriek but a deep paired tone felt through the table. The displays cleared. On the side wall appeared **RETRIEVAL CYCLE FIVE — PREPARATION ACTIVE**.

Samira entered carrying a medical file. She spoke to Mara quietly, but the translation devices rendered the words for everyone.

“The target window is confirmed. The reconstructed health profile predicts severe chronic pain, visual impairment and episodic incapacity. I need authority to delay if stabilisation fails.”

Mara took the file. “You have clinical authority.”

Room Five illuminated on the house plan. Beneath it, the retrieval field entered its first live phase; the cycle would run through the night toward the dawn threshold.

Beside nineteen protected places, the machine began spending its impossible power to bring back a man whose body might not survive comfortably enough to use one.

Aristotle left his marker white.

So did they all.
