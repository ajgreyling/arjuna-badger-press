# Chapter 17 — The Honour You Did Not Earn

The first thing Friedrich knew was that someone had put metal inside his voice.

He tried to ask where he was. The word reached his own ear twice: once as the German he had made with his tongue, once as a clear and toneless language that arrived an instant later. Between them came a small chime, apologetic and obscene.

The second thing was pain.

It had begun behind his right eye and travelled backward, driving a bright nail through his skull. Light hung above him in a white ring. Faces leaned over its edge. A dark-haired woman in blue gloves blocked the ring with her hand.

“Do not sit yet,” she said.

“Then remove the theatre.”

The translated sentence came out flatter than he intended. The chime sounded again over *theatre*.

“Pain?”

“Yes.”

“Where?”

He touched the right side of his head. She asked him to follow her finger. He could see one finger and then two, then only the flare behind it.

Beyond her stood five men. One was broad and comfortable in his body; one narrow and stiff; one watched the room as though it were evidence against itself. The oldest studied Friedrich without hiding his interest. Not pity, exactly. Classification.

The pity came from the others behind glass.

He could not see them clearly, but he knew the posture: heads inclined, mouths made gentle, the little inward congratulation of those permitted to witness suffering without suffering themselves.

“Send them away,” he said.

The physician did not look around. “The viewing glass is part of the retrieval chamber. I can make it opaque.”

“You can, or you may ask permission?”

She turned her head. The glass whitened.

That was better. Not because the eyes were gone. Because she had acted.

“I am Samira El-Khoury. You are in medical care. I will tell you more when the pain allows it.”

“You know my name.”

“Yes.”

“And I do not know yours until you give it. An excellent beginning.”

Her mouth moved at one corner. Not a smile offered as medicine. He approved of her slightly.

She gave him water in a soft cup and something for the sickness. He asked what it was. She named its purpose, its known risks and what she did not know about its effect on a body newly arrived by the machine. She did not call the machine resurrection. That, too, he noticed.

When he could stand, the five men were waiting in the arrival corridor. The narrow one stepped forward.

“I am Immanuel Kant.”

Friedrich laughed once. It hurt enough to bend him against the wall.

The physician put a hand near his elbow without taking it. Kant looked offended on behalf of the circumstance.

“You find the introduction amusing?” Kant asked.

“I find it ambitious.”

The broad man said, “David Hume,” with the air of a person offering a chair rather than a monument. “I had much the same difficulty.”

“René Descartes,” said the watchful man.

The oldest inclined his head. “Aristotle.”

Friedrich looked from one to the next. “Of course.”

The fifth man was not a philosopher. His clothes were modern and carefully neutral, his expression trained toward reassurance.

“Noah Vale. Ethics secretariat.”

“There,” Friedrich said. “At last, an honest impossibility.”

Hume laughed. Kant did not.

They showed him Room Five. Its door had no lock on the inside that he could discover, though it had a strip of black glass on the outside. Clothes lay folded on the bed in his size. A tablet stood on a desk beside a stitched notebook. The room was clean in the peculiar manner of a room nobody had lived in and many people had prepared.

“No cameras in here,” Noah said.

Descartes, from the corridor, said, “That is the rule. It is not yet a fact.”

“We have tested it,” Kant said.

“We have tested some claims about it.”

Friedrich sat on the bed. “How industriously you have all built your little monastery.”

“The door may be closed,” Kant said. “Others knock before entering. Medical staff may enter in an emergency. At ten-thirty we meet for briefing. Meals are—”

“Stop.”

Kant stopped.

“Who made these rules?”

“We did, after conflicts revealed the need for them.”

“Which conflict made you hungry for knocking?”

Kant glanced at Descartes.

“Ah. And which weakness required the schedule?”

“Coordination is not weakness.”

“Everything that needs to command itself calls itself coordination.”

The translation chime sounded over the sentence, unsure where command ended and compulsion began. Friedrich touched the device behind his ear.

“Remove this.”

“You may remove it in your bedroom,” Noah said. “Without it, the others will not understand you.”

“A strong argument for solitude.”

He pulled it free. The corridor filled at once with meaningless human sound. Faces remained: irritation, amusement, professional concern, patient curiosity. Better. The face often betrayed the grammar.

He closed the door but did not latch it. There was no need to make privacy resemble captivity.

On the tablet, a map showed a world crossed by borders he did not know. A timeline waited. So did a document bearing his own name, locked beneath a grey panel.

He turned it face down.

At the dining table, his place had already been marked.

Not with a name. Five residents sat along one side and a sixth setting waited at the end nearest the medical corridor: shallow bowl, water, plain food, the choices of people who had read a file about his stomach. A sheet of rules lay at each place. Beside it stood an empty chair with a small metal marker: 5.

Mara Voss introduced herself without warmth or apology. She displayed the Asterion forecast in layers: observations, model, conditional futures, decisions already moving through governments. Friedrich listened until the coloured plume spread over the globe.

“You have brought dead men to advise the living how to remain alive.”

“Yes.”

“Because the living have failed?”

“Because this problem exceeds any living government’s legitimacy.”

“So you borrow legitimacy from graves.”

Noah made a note.

Mara continued. “You were selected as one of nineteen thinkers whose work materially shaped humanity’s understanding of value, knowledge, society and obligation.”

“Selected by whom?”

“An international committee advised by scholars and public institutions.”

“Names.”

“Those records are not currently open to residents.”

“Then I have been decorated by cowards.”

Kant leaned forward. “The merit of an argument does not depend upon the courage of its administrator.”

“No. But the origin of an honour tells us what the honour is for.” Friedrich addressed the cameras, black lenses set visibly into the pale wall. “What do they gain by naming us great?”

“Your participation,” Mara said.

“At last.”

She did not retreat. “Each retrieved resident also receives a provisional continuity allocation.”

Noah’s polished language could not conceal the object. Friedrich made him say it again until the translation stopped calling it *continuity* and allowed *bunker place*.

“Nineteen places,” Hume said quietly. He had heard this before and disliked it more on repetition.

“Tied to the programme,” Mara said. “Non-transferable at present.”

Friedrich took in the prepared bowl. The numbered chair. The medicine selected before he woke. The world had built an altar, placed him upon it and sent him an invoice for the stone.

“You call me valuable,” he said, “and therefore I owe you the labour that proves your judgement. You preserve me and make preservation my chain. Honour is the debt that smiles while it fastens itself.”

“You are free to disagree with the project,” Mara said.

“Am I free to leave?”

Silence did more than her answer could have.

Kant put one finger on Rule Sheet 1.0. “Until the status of the catastrophe and our own legal standing are clarified, we have agreed to household duties shared among residents. This is not payment for survival. It is respect for persons with whom one lives.”

Friedrich drew the sheet toward him. There were lines for meals, cleaning, quiet hours, medical support, privacy, deliberation. Beside each resident’s designation stood assigned duties.

“You have made virtue printable.”

“We have made expectations public.”

Friedrich tore the paper once, carefully, removing only the line that carried the number of his room. He returned the rest to Kant.

“Then let my expectation be public. I refuse the rota. I refuse the seat. I refuse the honour I did not earn and the obligation concealed inside it.”

“Someone else will perform the work,” Kant said.

“Then perhaps we shall discover who has been performing it all along.”

Hume watched him with a look almost companionable, but cooler than admiration. “A puncture is useful,” he said. “Provided one remembers who must mend the tyre.”

Friedrich smiled despite the pain returning behind his eye. “You and I may yet dislike each other honestly.”

He rose, leaving the food untouched.

At the door he turned to Mara. “Show me the rejected names.”

“No.”

“Not the chosen. The refused. Greatness is always most truthful at its border.”

He walked back to Room Five and left its door open to the corridor. Behind him, Kant flattened the torn rule sheet with both hands, as if pressure could restore the missing line.
