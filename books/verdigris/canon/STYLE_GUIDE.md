# STYLE_GUIDE — *Verdigris*

## Two registers, one book

1. **The clinical human** (default). Close third on Ezra, past tense. Precise, mycologically literate,
   sensory about copper and rot. Horror delivered flat and exact — the aflatoxin hollowing described
   the way a scientist describes it, which is worse than adjectives. The more transported Ezra
   becomes, the *more* exact the prose reaches — precision as a symptom.
2. **The hyphal chorus** (rationed). First-person plural, patient, geological, present-tense — the
   bloom's "voice," surfacing only in Ezra's entheogen chapters and in short interstitials between
   parts. It never states a checkable fact (MYTHOS 6). It is always deniable as Ezra's chemistry.
   Use sparingly — three or four times a part at most, or it stops being uncanny and becomes a
   gimmick.

## Prose laws

- **Copper is the narrator's palette.** The world tastes, smells and looks of the weapon: metallic,
  green, oxidised. Verdigris everywhere, literal and moral.
- **Beauty and lethality in one frame.** The bloom is gorgeous. Never write it only as menace; the
  seduction is the horror. A luminous ruin that will hollow you by nightfall.
- **No gore-swarm.** Body horror is intimate and personal (Thabo), never spectacle. One hollowed
  friend beats a hundred running corpses.
- **Entheogen chapters are not "trippy."** No word-salad psychedelia. The visionary material is
  rendered with clinical control precisely so the reader can't dismiss it as a drug scene — which is
  the ambiguity engine at the sentence level.
- **The debate is dramatized, not lectured.** Reading A vs B lives in Nomsa-vs-Ezra and Priya-vs-Ezra
  scenes with real stakes, not in essayistic narration.

## The title word
Deploy "verdigris" as an image, not a label — the green on a wrist, on a bell, on a bloom, on a
statue's face — and let the reader feel it mean both cure and rot before it is ever named as theme.
