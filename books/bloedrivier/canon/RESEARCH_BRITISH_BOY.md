## 0. The decision the research forces — settler's son, not red-coat regular

The first instinct (STORY_BIBLE) was a *soldier* — a poor English/Scots/Irish kid in the red coat,
shipped to the bottom of Africa for a flag he barely understands. That archetype is real and usable
(see §2, kept for texture and for the *adult* machine around him). **But the record hands us
something better and truer for 1837–38 specifically:** at Port Natal in those exact months there was
no Crown garrison fighting the Zulu — there was a tiny, half-wild settler-and-hunter community, and
inside it a boy of *exactly the canon's age* who was *exactly where the canon needs him to be.*

**George Biggar** — younger son of Alexander Biggar (hunter, founder in 1837 of the Port Natal
Volunteers, the region's first white militia). George was **~18 in Feb 1838**, and was **120 miles
inland at the Bloukrans / Blaauwekrans Voortrekker camps** when the news came that Dingane had killed
Retief's party — and the impi that followed fell on those camps (the Weenen killings). The Biggars'
story is documented: Dick King was sent to warn the camps; the whole European population of Port
Natal sheltered nine days aboard the *Comet* in the bay; Alexander Biggar lost both sons and then
**joined the Voortrekker commando to avenge them**, dying later at the Battle of the Tugela (1838).

**Why this is the right anchor (binding for the British POV):**
- **Age & position match canon exactly** — a ~17–18 boy, English, *at the camps*, able to physically
  cross the paths of the Voortrekker girl and (at the edge of) the Zulu youth. No contrivance needed.
- It keeps the boy **powerless inside a machine** without making him a Crown trigger-man at a battle
  the book refuses to glorify. He is empire's *child* (English, the settler edge of the colonial
  project) without being the one firing the volley.
- It lets the **red-coat / Crown indictment** (the camps sixty years on, §STORY_BIBLE) land through
  the *adult* frame and the wider history, exactly as the book wants — institution indicted, child
  grieved — rather than pinning it on the boy.
- **Class is still in it:** Port Natal's whites were hunters, traders, beachcombers, deserters,
  the flotsam of empire's edge — not gentry. The boy is poor-ish, rough, far from any home.

## 1. Port Natal, 1837–1838 — the lived texture (documented)

- A **trading post, not a colony**: a handful of whites — hunters (Alexander Biggar, Dick King),
  traders, a few missionaries — living among, trading with, and **having children with Zulu women**
  (documented practice in the community). Not a garrison; a frontier scrape of a settlement.
- **Politically nowhere**: fleeing the Cape's British rule were the Boers *behind*; the Zulu kingdom
  *around*; the Crown itself had not yet annexed Natal (that comes 1843). The Port Natal English were
  squatters on the king's tolerance — they knew it, and lived light because of it.
- **Oct 1837:** the Port Natal English (Biggars among the signatories) signed an **address of welcome
  to Retief** — they *chose a side*, the trekker side, before the catastrophe. Useful: the boy is not
  neutral; he is already entangled with the Voortrekkers when it all breaks.
- **Feb 1838:** Retief's party killed at uMgungundlovu → the impi falls on the inland camps
  (Bloukrans/Weenen) → Dick King's warning ride → the nine days on the *Comet* → the port half-
  abandoned, then the Boers push the English out and declare the Natalia Republic (May 1838).
- **The set-piece the British thread bends toward** is NOT Blood River (the boy isn't there) but
  **Weenen / the camps** and the **flight to the *Comet***, then the *adult* world's vengeance — which
  lets the British POV carry the book's "institution vs human" thesis from a different angle than the
  Boer or Zulu threads.

## 2. The red-coat texture (kept — for the adult machine & the cold shadow forward)

Even though the *boy* is a settler not a regular, the **British-soldier research stands** and feeds
the frame [F] and the cold forward-glance to the camps of 1900–02:

- **Why a poor boy wore the coat:** poverty, not patriotism. The "king's shilling" and the enlistment
  **bounty** (a cash lump sum) drew the unemployed and the hungry. Recruiting parties worked the
  poorest towns and villages; press-gangs and militia ballots filled the rest. *Half the army came
  from the Scottish Highlands and Ireland — the poorest parts of the British Isles.*
- **Pay & conditions:** a private earned ~8 pence a day (a civilian labourer made ~2 shillings —
  roughly 3× more); after stoppages for food and kit, almost nothing was left. Rations were bread/
  biscuit, salt meat, oatmeal or rice, a beer or rum allowance. Camp life "dirty and cramped," disease
  fast, malnutrition common. **Discipline by terror:** flogging, running the gauntlet, execution for
  desertion — public humiliation as deterrent.
- **The marching load:** blanket, knapsack, canteen, ammunition — the soldier carried his world and
  was worth, to the machine, exactly what he could carry and shoot.
- **The cold forward-glance (STORY_BIBLE, binding):** this same empire, these same poor-boy ranks,
  are the institutional ancestor of the men who built the **concentration-camp wire of 1900–02**. The
  book holds the boy human and indicts the machine that made him — the wire is the machine's mature
  form. Carry it as the frame's quiet shadow, **never** as the boy's knowledge or as grievance.

## 3. Voice notes for the British POV (so he doesn't sound like the others)

- **Register:** plainer, dryer, more *transactional* than the Boer girl's scripture-cadence or the
  Zulu youth's drilled interiority. An English/edge-of-empire boy: weather, money, hunger, distance
  from a home he didn't choose to leave. Wry where the others are devout or fierce.
- **His fear is exile, not invasion or culling:** he is the one who is *nowhere* — not defending a
  laager, not charging in an impi, just a poor boy a very long way from anything, caught in other
  people's war. That homelessness is his note.
- **No flag-loyalty speeches.** He barely understands the Crown; he understands his father, hunger,
  the bay, the people he trades with. Empire is the weather he was born under, not a thing he believes.
