# *BLOEDRIVIER* — Plot / Beat-Map

> Inherits the binding STORY_BIBLE. Four-POV coming-of-age over 1837–1838. The historical frame is
> **fixed and documented**; the children's paths are the invented connective tissue *within* it. Theme
> never spoken. Each contested event rendered as honest divergence, not verdict.
>
> **POV legend:** **[V]** Voortrekker girl (protagonist, author's heritage — solid) · **[Z]** Zulu youth

## Shape — four movements

**MOVEMENT I — THE LEAVING (the unknown ahead).** Establish three children inside three machines, each
afraid, each told who the enemy is by adults.
1. **[V]** The trek rolls north. Her world is wagon-dust, scripture, and the rifle she's better with than
   her brothers — "poor shots went hungry." She is leaving the only home she knew because the grown-ups
   are afraid of the British behind and hopeful of land ahead. *Fear of change, dressed as faith.*
2. **[Z]** The boy and the thornfields — the making of the weapon; the culling of those who flinched; the
   pride and the cost. Dingane's court reads the trekkers arriving as an invasion that already broke
   faith. *(PROVISIONAL — pending read.)*
3. **[B]** The red coat at the Cape / Port Natal — a poor boy far from a home he didn't choose to leave,
   serving an empire that the trekkers are fleeing and the Zulu are bracing against. *Fear, in a uniform.*
4. **[F]** The documented frame: who the Voortrekkers were, why they left the Cape (British rule,
   abolition, frontier policy), the kingdom they were walking into, the empire at their backs.

**MOVEMENT II — THE BREAKING OF FAITH (Retief & the murder).** The midpoint catastrophe.
5. **[F]** Retief's embassy to Dingane; the land "treaty"; the cattle demand; the documented sequence.
   *Both readings laid side by side: treachery / a king defending his land from bad-faith invaders.*
6. **[V]** She is in the laager when word comes of Retief's party riding to the king's kraal — and then
   the silence, then the worst news. The adults' certainty hardens into a reason to kill. She is asked,
   for the first time, to see the Zulu as *only* an enemy.
7. **[Z]** Inside the king's decision — the fear of a people who have seen what comes after the wagons,
   the logic that is not madness from where he stands. The boy is near it, or hears of it; what does the
   command do to him? *(PROVISIONAL — the heart of the gate; his interiority is the colleague's to render.)*
8. **[V/F]** Weenen — the killings in the camps; grief made into resolve. The night the trek stops being
   a journey and becomes a war.

**MOVEMENT III — THE LONG FEAR (the attrition).** Months of dread; the three paths bend toward one river.
9. **[V]** Survival, defence, the laager learning to fight; her competence is now the difference between
   eating/living and not. The toll on a girl who can do what's needed and is unmade a little each time.
10. **[Z]** The impi mustering; the boy carried toward a battle by a machine bigger than him; the scared
    kid under the leather. *(PROVISIONAL.)*
11. **[B]** Empire watches from the edge — the British boy's distance from a fight that isn't quite his,
    and the cold institutional calculus around it; his private flickers of seeing the others as human.
12. **[V/Z]** A crossing — the one human encounter at the book's heart: the girl and the Zulu boy (or
    she feeds/spares/hesitates over a wounded enemy), each *misreading* the other, each glimpsing the
    scared child across the line. The mercy-for-the-enemy beat the whole book is built toward.

**MOVEMENT IV — THE RIVER (Bloedrivier) & after.** The set-piece as horror, not triumph.
13. **[F]** The Wenkommando, the laager at the Ncome, the vow; the documented battle (16 Dec 1838).
14. **[V]** Bloedrivier from behind the wagon-wheel — not glory, *horror*: the river running red, boys
    dying in numbers, the noise, the after-silence. She survives and is permanently altered.
15. **[Z]** The battle from the charge — the thorn-hardened feet carrying him into rifle-fire; the
    machine spending the boys; what's left of the scared kid. *(PROVISIONAL — handle with the deepest care.)*
16. **[B/F]** The aftermath and the wide shot: what the river meant to each people then and since; the
    cold forward-glance to the empire's later wire (the camps) — the institution indicted, the children
    grieved. **End on the universal:** three frightened kids, three machines, one ground that ran red —
    and the quiet, devastating question of who the villain ever really was.

## Notes
- **No verdict, ever.** The narrated frame presents divergence; the children carry the felt truth; the
  reader is trusted to hold the both-ness.
- **Voices must be distinct** — same age, three worlds; a Zulu 17yo, a Trekker 17yo, a British 17yo
  should *sound, fear, and see* completely differently. Sameness of voice = failure.
- **The girl is the centre of gravity**; the Zulu and British boys are the others she meets, misreads,
  and slowly learns to *see* — so the not-the-author's voices are held *through her growing sight* and
  the colleague's read, never floated as solo invention.
