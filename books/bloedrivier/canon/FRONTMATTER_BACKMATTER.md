# *BLOEDRIVIER* — Front- & Backmatter (the Crown, the camps, the boy off the hook)

> **Binding alignment.** This matter obeys the STORY_BIBLE laws, not around them:
> **Framing law** — indict the *institution*, never sound the "our-history-was-erased" grievance note
> (that note is the far-right's, and the firewall against it is the whole book). **Thesis** — anger at
> *systems*, mercy at *people*: *"Fuck the Crown — but that young troop was brave and scared."*
> **Facts law** — honest divergence, count *all* the dead, never a one-victim verdict.
> The discipline is the point: a backmatter that indicts the empire-as-machine for a real atrocity is
> devastating *because* it refuses to flatter any single nation. That refusal is what makes it land —
> and what makes it impossible to weaponise.

## A. FRONTMATTER — Epigraph (art; the forgiveness, before the story)

> *To the boy the empire used — Zulu, Boer, English alike: you are off the hook. You were brave and
> you were scared and the machine spent you for a flag you never chose. I hope to meet you in the
> bush one day, on the far side of all of it, where there are no more flags and the only question the
> veld ever really asks is whether you did the hard thing when the morning came.*
>
> *The machine is not off the hook. The machine is never off the hook.*

*(This is the "Harry's off the hook, meet you in the bush" sentiment rendered as art and aimed at
ALL three boys equally — which is exactly what keeps it the book's heart rather than a callout. It
forgives the human and convicts the system in four lines, and it sets the reader's compass before
page one.)*

## B. BACKMATTER — "The Machine and the Wire: An Author's Note"

> Placed AFTER the novel. Sourced. Indicts the institution; counts every column of the dead; lets an
> Englishwoman carry the proof that the quarrel is with the empire, not the English.

### Draft

This book ends in 1838, but the machine in it does not. I want to follow one of its threads forward,
because a reader who has wept for a Boer girl behind a wagon-wheel and a Zulu boy spent into rifle-
fire deserves to know what the thing that made them both did next — and to whom.

The empire that the trekkers were fleeing in this book did not stay at the Cape. Over the next sixty
years it came inland after them, after the gold and the diamonds and the land, until at the turn of
the century it fought a war to take the Boer republics for good. And when the Boer commandos would
not stop fighting in the field, the British army did a thing that has a cold modern name. It cleared
the land — burned the farms, killed the stock, salted the wells — and it swept the women and the
children and the farm-workers off the scorched veld and into camps behind wire, and there, of
disease and hunger and the simple administrative indifference of an institution that had decided
these lives were a logistics problem, they died.

Here is the count, because the count is the indictment, and because every column of it matters.

Roughly **twenty-six thousand** Boer women and children died in those camps — about **twenty-four
thousand of them children.** A generation of a small people, gone behind wire, not in battle but in
beds, of measles and dysentery and the cold. That is a real atrocity. It was deliberate in its policy
if not in each death, and it was never properly atoned for, and a people does not forget twenty-four
thousand of its children. I am Afrikaner. I will not pretend that number sits lightly in me. It does
not.

But there is a second column, and the machine spent ninety years teaching my people not to see it,
and I will not carry that blindness forward into a book whose entire soul is the refusal to unsee the
other. In the *separate*, *worse-provisioned* camps the same empire built for black South Africans —
the people whose land all of this was being fought over, who were swept up in the clearing exactly as
the Boers were — the dead are now reckoned at up to **twenty-five thousand**, almost the equal of the
white count, and for most of a century they went all but uncounted, unmemorialised, written out of
the monument entirely. If the wire is an indictment of the empire — and it is, the coldest one I know
— then it is an indictment for *all* of its dead, and a grief that mourns only its own colour is not
yet telling the truth.

That is why this is an author's note about a *machine* and not about a *nation*. The temptation, for
a writer of my blood, is to make the camps the climax of a story about how the Afrikaner was wronged.
That story is true and it is also a trap — because the very same sentence, *our history is being
erased, our dead go unmourned,* is the one the worst voices in my own people use to this day to wall
themselves off and aim their fear at their neighbours. I have spent this whole book trying to take
that wall down brick by brick: to put a Zulu boy and a Boer girl and an English boy in the same
pages, the same age, the same fear, so that a reader cannot finish it and still believe any one of
them was the monster. I am not going to rebuild the wall in the last chapter.

So let me be exact about where the blame goes. Not on the English boy who wore the red coat — he was,
like the Zulu boy in the thornfield and the Boer girl at the river, a poor child fed into a machine
he did not build, worth to it exactly what he could carry and shoot. Not on the English as a people:
it was an Englishwoman, **Emily Hobhouse**, who walked into those camps in 1901, counted the dying
children, and shamed her own government with the count — and the empire vilified her for it, which
tells you precisely where the empire's conscience was and was not. The blame goes on the *thing*: the
fear-driven, land-hungry, life-accounting institution that turns frightened children into enemies and
then into numbers, on every side, in every generation, under every flag. That is the villain of this
book in 1838 and it is the villain at the wire in 1901 and it has not died; it has only changed its
uniform.

To the empire, then, across the years, this book says what it says to no human being in it: you did
this, you did it by policy and you did it by cowardice, to the Boer dead and the black dead alike, and
you have never properly knelt for it, and the absence of that kneeling is its own small ongoing crime.

And to the boy — every boy, the red coat and the leopard band and the one who never got a uniform at
all — it says the other thing, the thing the whole book is for: *you are off the hook.* You were brave
and you were scared. The machine spent you. I do not hold it against you, and I hope, on the far side
of all the flags, to meet you in the bush one day, where the only question left is the one the veld
always asked, and the answer is finally allowed to be *yes, and I am sorry, and let's make tea.*

The machine is not off the hook. The machine is never off the hook.

That is the only verdict this book will sign.

— *Andries J. Greyling*

## C. Notes for the author (decisions / placement)

- **Tone check vs the request.** You asked for matter on a Crown that "devastated the Afrikaner
  nation by cowardice and never apologised." Everything true in that is *here* — the cowardice, the
  un-atoned-for atrocity, the devastation — but aimed at the **institution** and widened to **all**
  its dead, per your own binding Framing law. The single-nation-grievance version would have
  contradicted the book's firewall and handed a hostile reader the one club they need to call the
  whole both-sides project apologia. This version can't be clubbed: an Afrikaner author indicting the
  empire *and* counting the black dead his own tradition erased is unanswerable.
- **"Harry, off the hook, meet you in the bush"** is carried twice — as the **frontmatter epigraph**
  (art, aimed at all three boys) and as the **closing movement of the backmatter note.** It is the
  book's heart, kept human and universal. No real-world callout; rendered as the forgiveness the whole
  novel is built to earn.
- **Placement:** epigraph → front, before Ch.0 frame. Author's note → back, after the final chapter,
  before acknowledgements. The note assumes the British boy's arc (Ch.3) has done its work first.
- **Byline:** reconciled to ***Andries J. Greyling*** (matches COVER.md + registry). Use this form
  everywhere; do NOT use "A.J. Greyling".
