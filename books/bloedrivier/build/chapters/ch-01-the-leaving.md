# Chapter 1 — The Leaving

The horse had to be shot before breakfast, and they gave it to Marretjie because she was the one who
could be trusted to do it on the first try.

She did not want it. That was the thing nobody said out loud and everybody understood: that wanting
had nothing to do with it, the way wanting had nothing to do with most of what the trek asked of you.
The grey gelding had gone down in the night with its foreleg folded the wrong way in an antbear hole
it could not have seen in the dark, and it lay now in the grey-blue light before the sun with its
neck stretched along the cold ground and its eye rolling white at her, and it had been her father's
second-best horse for six years and had carried her brother Hendrik to his confirmation, and she
loved it, and it had to die, and she was seventeen.

"You'll do it clean," her father said. It was not a question and it was not unkind. He had loaded the
*roer* himself and he handed it to her with the powder already seated, and he put his hand once on
the back of her neck, briefly, the nearest thing to tenderness the morning would allow, and then he
went to see to the oxen because the oxen were what kept them all alive and a horse that could not
stand was not. She understood the arithmetic. She had been doing the arithmetic of the trek since she
was eleven and they rolled out of the Tarka with everything they owned creaking behind a span of
sixteen and her mother singing a psalm to keep the small ones from crying. *Poor shots went hungry.*
Her grandmother said it the way other grandmothers said grace. A girl who could shoot ate, and a girl
who could shoot kept the others eating, and so a girl learned to shoot the way she learned to pray
and to knead and to deliver a calf with her sleeves rolled to the shoulder, because the veld did not
care that she was a girl, and the lion did not care, and the dawn that brought the impi — though no
impi had yet come, then, in the high clean country before the mountains — the dawn would not care
either.

She knelt by the grey's head. She did not let herself hurry, because hurrying was how you flinched,
and a flinch was how you made a clean thing into a cruel one. She put her hand flat on the long bone
of its face and felt the warmth still coming off it, the breath going in and out fast and shallow
through the wide soft nostrils, and she said its name once, low, the foolish private name she had
given it at nine and never told anyone, and the ear came round toward her voice. Then she set the
muzzle where her father had taught her, on the cross of an imaginary line from ear to opposite eye,
and she breathed out the way you breathed out to be still, and she shot it, and it was clean, and the
sound rolled away flat across the veld and came back off nothing, because there was nothing out here
to come back off, only grass to the round edge of the world.

She was crying before the echo died. She let herself, for exactly as long as it took to stand up,
because she had learned that too — that you could have the feeling or you could fight the feeling but
you could not do both and also reload, and reloading was the thing that kept you alive, so you had
the feeling fast and whole and then you put it down and picked up the powder horn. By the time
Hendrik came at a run, fourteen and white-faced, having heard the shot and feared it was people, she
had her face back and the *roer* broken open over her arm and she was able to say, in a voice that
only shook a little, "It was the grey. The leg. Go and help Pa with the disselboom, the wood's split
again," and watch the fear go out of him and something worse and quieter come into its place, which
was the understanding, arriving in him as it had arrived in her, that this was simply how it was going
to be now. That things you loved would lie down in the dark and have to be shot before breakfast, and
that you would be the one to do it, and that the day would go on afterward and want its work from you
regardless.

This was the part the men who would later write it all down would leave out. They would write the
journey and the battles and the names of the leaders and the text of the vows. They would carve the
women into granite, tall and grieving and brave, a monument you could walk around. What they would
not put in, because they did not know how and because it did not flatter anyone, was the way Marretjie
Pretorius stood in the wet grass at fifteen past five in the morning with her dead favourite cooling
at her feet and her little brother's life her responsibility and the whole impossible day ahead of
her, and felt — under the grief, under the competence, under everything the trek had made her — a
single clear thread of something she would never have said aloud and barely had a word for, which was
that she was *afraid all the time now,* and had been for two years, and that the being-afraid had
stopped feeling like a thing that would end.

---

They were going north because the grown-ups were afraid, though the grown-ups called it other things.

They called it the will of God, and they called it the search for land where a man could be his own
master, and her father called it, on the bad nights, *getting away from the English* — said it the
way you'd say the name of an illness, the English, the smous-government down in the Colony that had
freed the slaves and not paid for them, that ran its courts in a tongue he could not follow and gave
the verdict to the rooinek every time, that had lost his cousin's farm to the *Xhosa* in the war and
then told him the war was his own fault for being where he was. Marretjie had been small for most of
that and remembered it the way you remember weather: a long pressure in the house, her father coming
back from the *drostdy* with his hat in his hands and his jaw set, the word *Engelse* spoken low after
the children were supposed to be asleep, the way you'd speak of a thing in the rafters.

She did not hate the English the way her father did, because she had never really met one, and you
could not properly hate weather. But she had learned that the fear of them was the great fact of the
grown-up world, the thing under all the other things, and that the trek itself — this whole creaking
nation on wheels, the wagons strung out for half a day's ride along the wagon-road they were cutting
into country no wagon had crossed — was fear with oxen in front of it. They were not, whatever the
psalms said, riding toward something. They were riding away. The land ahead was a rumour. The fear
behind was the only solid thing, and her father had loaded the whole family onto it and driven it
north into a country he did not know, where there were peoples he had never seen, ruled by a king
whose name the men said carefully, the way you said the name of a thing in the rafters.

She thought about that more than she let on. She thought about it while she rode — and she rode well,
better than Hendrik, nearly as well as the men, sitting the new bay her father had given her to
replace the grey with her *roer* slung and her eyes doing without being told the constant slow sweep
of the veld that you learned or you died, the reading of the grass for movement, the donga-lines, the
kopje that could hide a hundred men or a herd of eland or nothing at all. The men underestimated her.
She knew they did and she let them, because there was a use in it: an underestimated person sees more,
is asked fewer questions, can think her own thoughts behind a face the others have decided is simple.
Her uncle Gerrit called her *ons skerpskutter*, our sharpshooter, and meant it as the kind of praise
you give a clever dog, and she smiled and said nothing and out-shot him at the next *skiet-kompetisie*
by a clear two birds and watched him decide it was luck.

And what she thought, behind the simple face, riding north into the rumour, was this: that everyone
had told her who to be afraid of, and that everyone was very sure, and that the very sureness had
begun, lately, to frighten her more than the thing they were sure about. The men spoke of the Zulu
the way they spoke of the English — a weather, a darkness, a thing in the rafters — and she had never
met one of those either, not really, not to speak to, and she noticed that the not-having-met did not
make anyone less sure. It made them *more* sure. The less you knew a thing, the easier it was to be
certain of it, and the whole grown-up world seemed to run on exactly that fuel, and they were burning
it now to drive a thousand wagons into the unknown.

She did not have the words for it as an idea. She had it as a feeling, the way she had everything that
mattered — fast and whole, under the competence. It would be years, if she lived years, before she
could have said: *I think the being-sure is the dangerous thing. I think we are most willing to kill
the people we have most carefully refused to see.* At seventeen she only had the discomfort of it, a
small wrongness she carried the way you carry a stone in your boot you have not yet stopped to shake
out, riding north under a sky that went on forever, toward a king's country, with a dead horse behind
her and her little brother's life slung beside her own *roer*, brave because she had to be and scared
because she was not a fool — both at once, all the time, which was simply, she was beginning to
understand, what it was going to mean to be alive in the years that were coming.

The mountains came up out of the haze on the third day: the Drakensberg, blue and enormous and
toothed along the top like something that bit. Beyond them was the king's country, and the green, and
the warm sea the men talked about, and the land that was going to be theirs because God and a rumour
said so.

Her father reined in beside her and looked at the mountains for a long time without saying anything,
and she watched the side of his face, the fear and the hope sitting on it together the way they sat
on everything now, and she did not tell him about the stone in her boot. There was no use in it. They
were going over. Everyone was going over. The only question the veld ever really asked was whether
you could do the thing that had to be done when the morning came and asked it of you, and she could,
she had proven she could, before breakfast, with a horse she loved.

"It's beautiful," she said instead, because it was, and because it was true, and because saying the
true small thing was sometimes the only mercy on offer.

"It's a wall," her father said. "God put a wall there. And He gave us oxen to climb it." He believed
it. She could hear that he believed it, all the way down, and she envied him it the way you envy
someone a country you can't get a passport to. Then he clicked his tongue and rode on toward the
front of the train, where the men were, to talk about the passes, and she sat her bay a moment longer
and looked at the wall God had put there, and felt the whole weight of the unknown country on the
other side of it settle onto her like a coming weather, and was, as she had been for two years and
would be for one more terrible one, brave and scared in exactly equal measure, and entirely alone in
the only way that mattered, which was that nobody — not her father, not Hendrik, not God as far as she
could yet tell — was going to take the *roer* out of her hands and do the hard thing for her when the
morning finally came.

She checked her powder, because checking your powder was a prayer you could be sure was heard, and
she rode on toward the wall.
