# Chapter 3 — The Bay

George Biggar was eighteen and a long way from anything, and the thing he could not get used to,
after two years at the bottom of the world, was how little the bottom of the world cared who you were.

At the bay they called Port Natal there was no church to be confirmed in and no magistrate to be
afraid of and no England to speak of beyond the few dozen souls who carried it around in their heads
like a coin gone smooth in the pocket — hunters, mostly, and a trader or two, and his father, who had
brought them all out here to make a fortune in ivory and had so far made instead a reputation, a
fever, and a great many friends among the Zulu, which George had come to understand was the only
currency the bottom of the world actually honoured. You could not eat a flag here. He had watched men
try. The land took your English politeness and your English certainty the way the surf took a thrown
stone, without comment, and gave you back exactly what you could carry and shoot and trade, and
nothing else, and George — who had been a poor-enough boy in England and was a poorer one here — had
decided he did not mind it as much as he was supposed to. There was an honesty in a place that would
not pretend to love you.

He was not a soldier. His father had raised a little militia the year before, the Port Natal
Volunteers, a dozen men with hunting guns and grand ideas, and it had fallen apart inside a season,
which George thought was the most English thing that had happened since they arrived: the *idea* of an
army, the uniform of an idea, and then the South African afternoon simply dissolving it because there
was nothing here for it to be an army *against* that an army was any use for. The real powers of the
bay were the king inland, whose tolerance they squatted on, and the weather, and the slow fevers off
the lagoon. Against none of those did a dozen men with muskets and a flag mean anything at all.

What George understood — and he understood it the way the girl two hundred miles to the southwest
understood her stone-in-the-boot, as a feeling under the competence rather than an idea he could have
spoken — was that he belonged to nobody's story. The Boers coming over the mountains had their God and
their grievance and their wagons; the king had his kingdom and his fear; the great Empire that had
stamped its coin into George's head before he could read had its flag, somewhere, far away, not yet
caring about this bay at all and one day caring far too much. And George stood at the edge of all of
it, an English boy on a beach at the bottom of Africa, carrying an England no bigger than a coin,
useful to the machine for precisely what he could carry and shoot — which was the same value the
machine put on a Boer girl and a Zulu boy his own age, though none of the three of them knew yet that
they shared it.

In February, his father would send him inland to the trekker camps along the rivers, because the
Biggars had thrown in with the Boers and a man looked after his allies. George would go because his
father told him to, the way the girl shot the horse because her father told her to, the way the boy
in the thornfield ran because the machine told him to — three children, three machines, each doing
the hard thing the morning asked because saying no to your own machine was not yet a thing any of them
knew how to do.

He did not know any of that, that early. He knew the bay, and the smell of low tide, and that he was
a long way from anything, and that the bottom of the world did not care who he was — which was, he was
beginning to think, the truest and almost the kindest thing anyone had ever told him.
