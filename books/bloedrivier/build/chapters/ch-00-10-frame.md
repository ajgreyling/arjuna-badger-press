# Chapter 0 — The Frame: What the Record Holds

*This is the narrated factual frame [F]. It is the wide shot the children cannot see. It tells what
the record holds — and, where the record forks, it lays the forks side by side and refuses the
verdict, per the Facts law.*

---

Here is what can be checked.

In the years after 1834, several thousand people who called themselves Afrikaners — farmers, mostly,
of the eastern districts of the Cape Colony, Dutch and Huguenot and German by blood and a hundred
and fifty years South African by birth — loaded what they owned onto ox-wagons and drove north, away
from the only government they had ever known. They had reasons, and the reasons depended on who you
asked, which is the first thing to understand about all of this: that almost nothing in it has only
one true account, and that the having of two accounts is not a failure of the record but the truest
thing the record contains.

The British Crown had taken the Cape from the Dutch for good in 1806, and ruled it the way an empire
rules a far place it does not love — by decree from London, in a language the farmers did not speak,
through courts that did not always believe a Dutchman over an Englishman or a black man over either.
In 1834 the Crown abolished slavery across the empire, and the farmers who had owned slaves were paid
out in paper that could only be cashed in London, which for a man on the Winterberg was the same as
not being paid at all. They had lost wars on the eastern frontier and been told the losing was their
own fault. They felt, in the particular way of people who have stopped being heard, that they were
governed by strangers who held them in contempt.

So they left. *We go,* one of them wrote, *to a land where we shall govern ourselves.* They called it
later the Great Trek, and made of it a sacred thing, and built a granite monument to its women, and
that is a problem for anyone trying to see it plainly — because a thing that has been made sacred has
also been made simple, and this was not simple. They were not pilgrims walking into an empty land.
The land was not empty. It had never once been empty.

To the northeast, beyond the Drakensberg, in the green well-watered country that ran down to the warm
sea, lay the kingdom of the Zulu. It was perhaps fifty years old as a single kingdom and it was the
most formidable power in the southern half of the continent — a state built, like every state, partly
by genius and partly by blood, with a standing army organised into regiments, the *amabutho*, that
could put many thousands of disciplined men into the field faster than any European force on the
subcontinent could dream of. Its king in 1837 was Dingane, who had come to the throne by killing his
brother Shaka, and who watched the wagons gathering on his western marches the way any king watches
armed strangers arrive at his border: as a question that would have to be answered, and answered hard.

Both of these peoples were afraid. That is the second thing to understand, and the record supports it
on every side. The trekkers were afraid of a power they could not measure and did not understand. The
king was afraid of a thing he understood entirely too well, because he had heard what had happened
elsewhere when the wagons came and did not stop coming — had heard of the Cape itself, and what
sixty years of arriving had done to the people who were there first. Fear is not an excuse for what
either of them did. But it is the engine under all of it, and a history that leaves the fear out is
not telling you the truth; it is selling you a side.

And at the edge of all of it, almost too small to see, there was a third presence: the Crown the
trekkers were fleeing, in the form of a ragged little settlement of English hunters and traders down
at the bay they called Port Natal, squatting on the king's tolerance, trading ivory, taking Zulu
wives, signing their names to whatever piece of paper seemed likely to keep them alive that season —
and behind them, far over the sea, the largest empire the world had yet made, which did not yet care
about any of this and would, in time, come to care a great deal, and bring with it sixty years on a
kind of wire and a kind of camp that even Dingane could not have imagined.

In February of 1838, a trekker leader named Piet Retief rode to the king's great place with a party
of some seventy men to collect, he believed, a signed grant of land. What happened there is the hinge
of everything that follows, and it is exactly the kind of event this book exists to hold honestly,
because there are two accounts and both of them have evidence and the gap between them is where the
whole tragedy lives. One account — the trekker account, the one carved in granite — says treachery:
that the king feasted them and disarmed them and then, on a word, had them seized and dragged to the
killing-hill and clubbed to death, women and servants and a child among them later, in cold blood,
for no reason but a king's bad faith. The other account says something harder to carve: that a king
who had watched armed strangers take land everywhere they went, who had already caught these
particular strangers in what looked to him like a lie or a theft of cattle, did what a frightened
sovereign does to an armed embassy he has decided is the leading edge of an invasion — struck first,
and struck to the root, because he believed, perhaps rightly, that if he did not, his kingdom would
go the way of the Cape.

The record does not let you choose. The record only lets you stand in the gap.

What is not in dispute is what came after. The impi went west in the weeks that followed and fell on
the trekker camps strung out along the rivers below the mountains — at the place the trekkers would
afterward call Weenen, which is the Dutch word for *weeping* — and killed several hundred people:
men, and the women who could shoot as well as the men, and a great many children and servants who
could not. It was a catastrophe and it was meant to be one. And it did the thing that catastrophes
do: it took a journey and made it a war, and took grief and made it into a reason, and hardened
something in the survivors that would have its answer, ten months later, at a river.

That river ran, before the sixteenth of December 1838, under a Zulu name: the Ncome. After that day
the trekkers called it Bloedrivier — Blood River — and the name is a verdict, which is why this book
will not use it as its own, because the book is not on a side. On that day a trekker commando of
fewer than five hundred, drawn up behind a ring of wagons lashed together on a tongue of land between
the river and a deep donga, met the charge of a Zulu army many times their number, and broke it,
hour after hour, with massed gunfire against men who had only the weapons of the body and the spear.
By evening the commando had lost three men wounded and none dead. The impi had lost, by the
commando's own count, perhaps three thousand. The river ran red. The trekkers said God had given
them the victory in answer to a vow, and built their faith on it for a century and a half. The Zulu
remembered it as the day a generation of their young men was spent into rifle-fire, and built a
grief on it that has no monument.

That is the frame. It is fixed, and it is documented, and where it forks this book will show you both
forks and trust you to bear the weight of them.

But a frame is not a story, and a count of the dead is not the dead. Inside that fixed frame there
were people, and most of them were young, and a great many of them were afraid in the particular,
total way that only the young can be afraid — for whom the world ending is not a figure of speech but
the literal news of the morning. This book is about three of them. It does not know their real names,
because the record did not think the young worth naming unless they led or died notably. So it has
given them names, and lives, and the fear they certainly had, and set them down inside the true frame
to see what the count of the dead leaves out.

They were the same age. They were forged by three different machines. And not one of them, when you
finally see them, will be the enemy you were promised.
