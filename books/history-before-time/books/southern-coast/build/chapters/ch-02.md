# Chapter 2 — A Man Who Drives Like He's Closing a Drawer

He did not phone back. He did not text back. For two days there was nothing at all, which Mark had learned, the few times their lives had crossed, was simply how the man worked — a silence that was not rudeness and not even really silence, more the quiet of a thing moving toward you that you couldn't hear yet.

On the third morning Mark was up on deck with coffee, watching the harbour wake, when he heard it.

You heard the Beast before you saw it, everyone said that who'd ever heard it, and now Mark understood why. It came down the road above the harbour as a *sound* first — not loud, that was the strange part, not a boy-racer's announcement, but a low even note with a fine hard edge threaded up inside it, a whine wound into a bass, the sound of an engine being fed more air than an engine that size had any business getting, and it carried across the water and turned Mark's head before he'd seen a single panel of the thing. Then it came round the last bend and down toward the slipway, and it was exactly what he'd been told and somehow still nothing he was prepared for: an old khaki Land Cruiser, double cab, faded to the no-colour of dust, dinged along one flank, a square canvas water-bag weeping a dark stain down its side, looking for all the world like a wreck on its last legs to the harbour — and moving, down a steep camber, with the flat composed ease of a thing that could have gone up the side of the mountain if it felt like it.

It rolled to a stop at the top of the slip, nose-out, the way a man parks who likes to know where the exits are, and the engine died, and the morning got quiet again, and a soft-bellied old man in a bad shirt climbed down out of it and stood and looked at the boat across fifty metres of still water, and lifted one hand, and Mark felt his whole chest do something it had not done in a long time, which was to be glad, simply and uncomplicatedly glad, to see another human being.

"*Harpur,*" Jakobus said, when Mark had rowed the little tender across and tied up and climbed the slip. He had a grip like a tool and let go before it meant anything. "You've gone and got yourself a boat."

"You've still got the truck."

"I've still got the truck." He turned and looked at it, fond, the way you look at an old dog. "She came over on a ship. Don't laugh." Then he turned back and looked, properly, at Mark — that slow grey-green look that took the whole of you in and somehow did not feel like being assessed, only like being *seen,* which Mark had forgotten, in the years between, and which landed now like a hand on the back of the neck. "You look better," the old man said. "Lighter. You found something out there that's been good for you."

"How do you—"

"Your face." A glance back at the boat, the silent clean lines of it, the solar along the rail. "And the boat. A man builds a thing like that — quiet, no fuel, takes the wind and asks nothing — when he's worked out he doesn't need to make a noise to go somewhere. That's a good thing to work out. Took me longer." He said it without weight, the way he said everything, and then, because Mark had not seen him in years and had texted him at midnight about a stone, the old man got, gently, to the point. "Now. You said it's old, and you said it's a drawing, and you don't use words you don't mean, even at midnight. So." He held out one broad hand, palm up. "Show me."

They sat on the slip in the early sun and Mark put the camera in the offered hand, the frame already up on the screen, and he watched the old man's face while he looked at it, because he had spent his life watching faces and there was no face in the world he'd less expected to be able to read, and he was right, he couldn't — except for one thing.

Jakobus went still.

Not surprised-still. Not a flinch. A deeper stillness than that, the stillness Mark had only ever seen in him once before, years ago and a long way from here, the stillness of a man for whom a great many alarms have just, very quietly, gone off at once, and who has decided not to let a single one of them reach his face. He looked at the small red stone in the bottom corner of the frame, and the weave of scored lines, and the small wrong gleam at the bottom of the deepest line, and he did not say *good Lord* and he did not say *where is this* and he did not say any of the things a normal man says. He just looked, for a long time, his thumb moving once to zoom, and the harbour woke up around them, and a gull complained, and a fishing boat went out past the wall.

"Where," he said at last. Flat. The way Mark would learn he said the most important things.

"In the dunes. Past the harbour. There's a midden—"

"I know there's a midden." Still looking at the screen. "There's middens all down this coast. Shell heaps. The old people sat here and ate the sea for ten thousand years and the leftovers are still in the sand. That's not this." He turned the camera a few degrees, so the low sun ran across the screen and the scored lines stood up sharper. "*This* is older than the shells, Harpur. A lot older. The shells are on top of where this came from, which means this washed out from *underneath,* from down where the dune's eating into something the wind hasn't finished uncovering." A pause. "And it shouldn't be here. That's the thing your eye caught and your head hasn't got to yet. It's the wrong thing in the wrong place, and your camera saw it because your camera doesn't know what it's supposed to expect, and so it didn't skate over it the way a person would. The way *I* would have, probably, if I'd walked past it. You did a thing this morning that two hundred years of clever people with degrees have walked straight past." He handed the camera back, and looked at Mark, and there was something in the soft ruined face that Mark could not name and would spend the rest of this trying to. "You found it because you weren't looking for it. That's usually how the real ones get found."

"Found *what,* though? What is it?"

And the old man did not answer that, which Mark would also learn was a thing he did. He answered the question under the question, or no question at all, and made you wait for the one you'd actually asked. He stood, knees cracking, and looked out at the dunes past the harbour wall, the long pale shoulders of sand going gold-grey in the morning, and the sea beyond them, and further than the sea, south, where there was nothing but water and then ice and then the bottom of the world.

"You know what they call it," he said, "what you do. Photography." He said the word like he was turning it over. "Drawing with light. That's the actual meaning of it, the Greek. *Drawing with light.*" He nodded, slowly, at the dunes. "And you came down here to draw with light, and the first thing you point your light-drawing at, the very first morning, is the oldest drawing on earth." He turned and looked at Mark, and the dry came up under it, the warmth under the dry. "I don't believe in much, Harpur. But I've learned to pay attention when a thing rhymes like that. The country was waiting for the right man to walk past with the right machine, and it waited a very long time, and this morning it got you." A beat. "So. We're going to go and look at it. And then I'm going to make some phone calls to people who are allowed to dig, because it's not ours and it's not for us. And you're going to take more photographs, because it turns out your eyes are the best instrument anybody's brought to this coast in a hundred years, and the photographs are going to matter."

He started toward the truck, then stopped, and looked back at the boat one more time, the silent clean thing on the still water, and something passed over his face that Mark thought, later, might have been envy, or might have been its gentler cousin.

"Nice boat," Jakobus said. "You went forward. I went and got the heaviest thing I own and dragged it across an ocean." He almost smiled. "We're the same animal, hey. We just can't agree on what to pack."

And he got into the Beast, and it woke under him with that low impossible note, and Mark stood on the slip with the camera in his hand and the gleam of the wrong yellow stone still on the screen, and understood that whatever this trip had been going to be — a few weeks of light and surf and moving on, the surface skated, the frames banked, the next place already calling — it was now going to be something else entirely, and that for the first time in twenty years he did not want to move on.

He wanted to dig.
