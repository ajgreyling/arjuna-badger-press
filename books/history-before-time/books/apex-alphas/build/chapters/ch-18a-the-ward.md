# The Ward

The thing nobody in that house had ever seen was the thing they had been assembled to answer.

Jakobus worked that out in the second week of the lottery and could not put it down afterwards. Twenty-six months. Fifty-two people. Six hundred hours of Porch, a broadcast to eight billion, four hundred and eleven trials, twenty-two pages of arithmetic and a machine under a mountain — and every single one of them knew the grey quiet the way you know a war from a newspaper. A number on a Monday. Andrew's face when he brought it up in the lift.

Not one of them had been in a room with it.

He raised it with the woman with the scrubbed voice and she said no, and gave four reasons, and every one of them was good: security, spectacle, the feed, and the fact that a house full of the most recognisable people alive walking into a medical facility would turn a ward into a set.

"Then not the house," said Jakobus. "Three of us. No cameras. Same rule as the white room."

"Which three?"

"Me, because somebody has to run it and because I'll be the one who says no if it goes wrong." He put his cup down. "And the two who never wrote anything."

She was quiet for a while.

"You understand what you're asking me to authorise," she said. "You want to take the two most dangerous names in human history into a ward full of people with no defences, and you want to do it with no record."

"Ja."

"Why them?"

"Because everybody else in that building would try to *do* something," said Jakobus. "Including me. Especially me. It's what we're all for — that's why the Machine picked us, we're the ones who fix things, and there's nothing in that ward to fix." He looked at the no-place where the voice came from. "There are two men under this roof whose entire mastery is sitting still in front of a frightened person without needing anything back. It's the only faculty in the building that hasn't been used once."

---

It was a converted rehabilitation hospital forty minutes outside Lyon, in a valley, with a chapel that had been made into a store room and a car park full of the cars of people who came every day to visit somebody who did not look up.

Three hundred and eleven beds. Two hundred and eighty-four occupied.

They went on a Tuesday in a plain vehicle: Jakobus, Siddhārtha, Yeshua, a medic called Amelie who had asked to come and who Jakobus had vetted himself, and nobody else. No feed. No log entry beyond a line in the vehicle book. Andrew knew, the woman knew, and that was all.

The senior clinician met them at the door and was, for the first eleven minutes, extremely professional and extremely angry.

"I want to say something before you go in," she said, in the corridor, in English, with her arms folded. "We have had six visits like this. Two ministers, a cardinal, a man with a foundation, and two — I do not have the English — *influencers*. Every one of them came for forty minutes and stood at the end of a bed and had a spiritual experience about *my patients*, and left, and my staff spent a week putting the ward back together." She looked at Jakobus, and then, for a long moment, at the barefoot man in a house sweatshirt and the man beside him with the carpenter's hands. "So I do not care who you are. If you make one of my patients into a demonstration I will end the visit on the spot and I will not be polite about it."

"Understood," said Jakobus.

"How long do you want?"

"Nine days," he said.

She unfolded her arms.

---

Nobody had described a ward on the feed. That is a real thing about those two years and the book will not pass over it: the species watched four hundred million people go out, and looked at the number every Monday, and did not look at the rooms, and the rooms were not hidden — there were documentaries, there were reports, nobody was stopping anybody. They just were not watched. A cage fight was watched. Two old men arguing about music were watched.

Jakobus walked into Ward C at nine in the morning and understood inside four seconds why.

It was not horrible. That was the whole of the horror. There was no screaming, no restraint, nothing to be shocked by and nothing to film. The light was good; somebody had put flowers in. There were eleven people in that room and every one of them was awake, and sitting up, and clean, and their eyes worked and followed you across the room, and they knew exactly who they were and where they were and what year it was.

They simply could not find a reason to say anything about it.

There was a woman of about sixty by the window who had been a violinist. Her sister came four times a week and did her hair and talked to her about the family and left. There was a boy of twenty-two who had been a chef, whose girlfriend had stopped coming in the eleventh month and had written to the ward to say she was sorry, and whose mother came instead. There was a man called Bernard, who had been a foreman on a bridge for thirty years, and who sat in a chair by the third bed with his hands on his knees looking out of the window at a car park.

Amelie had explained the phenomenology in the vehicle and Jakobus had not fully believed it until he was in the room. They were not depressed, in the ordinary sense; the medicines did nothing at all, which was the finding that had broken four hundred psychiatrists. They were not unconscious. They could tell you, in a flat and entirely lucid voice, that their daughter was called Élise and that they loved her and that they knew they should get up and that there was no reason to and that they were not in any distress about it.

*Being read,* the prologue calls it, and that is the word the survivors use. Not attacked. *Read* — the sense of something turning your pages, patiently, to the end, and then setting you down.

The house had spent twenty-six months trying to answer that. Jakobus stood in Ward C and thought: *and we did it from a colonnade.*

---

Siddhārtha sat down.

That was all he did, and he did it for nine days, and it took Jakobus about four of those days to understand that he was watching a master work.

He did not go bed to bed. He did not introduce himself; he had asked the staff not to introduce him either, which was itself an instruction that had to be argued for. On the first morning he came into Ward C at nine, and looked at the room for a while, and then went and sat on the floor — not on a chair, on the floor, with his back against the wall between the third and fourth beds — and stayed there until one.

He did not speak to anybody. He did not look at anybody, in the way a visitor looks. He sat.

At about eleven o'clock the man called Bernard turned his head and looked at him, for perhaps twenty seconds, and then looked back out at the car park.

On the second day Siddhārtha was there at nine again. On the third day he was there at nine and one of the nurses had put a cushion where he sat, which he used, and thanked her for, and which he later said was the most difficult thing that happened all week, because it meant the room had made a place for him and a place is a claim.

On the fourth day, Bernard said, to nobody in particular, in French: "You are not doing anything."

"No," said Siddhārtha.

"Why."

"Because there is nothing to do," said Siddhārtha, "and because I would like to be here anyway, and those are two separate facts and neither of them is about you."

Bernard thought about that for the better part of two hours.

"Everyone who comes here wants me to get up," he said.

"Yes."

"My daughter cries in the corridor. She thinks I do not know. There is a — a *pressure*, when someone comes." He was speaking very slowly and entirely without emotion, the way they all did. "You have not brought any."

"No."

"Why."

"Because I have sat where you are sitting," said Siddhārtha, "for six years, in a forest, when I was thirty. Not the same thing. I want to be exact with you: it was not the same thing, and I will not dress it up as one. But I know what it is to be looked at by people who need you to be better, and how much of your remaining strength goes on managing their faces." He shifted slightly against the wall. "So I have brought no face. That is all I have brought. It is not a treatment and it will probably not help."

Bernard looked out of the window for the rest of the day.

On the sixth day he asked Siddhārtha to sit closer, and Siddhārtha moved about a metre, and sat.

On the ninth day, when they left, Bernard was still in the chair.

Two months later he was on his feet, and eleven months after that he was doing three days a week on a bridge inspection contract, and he has never once claimed a cause for it, and when the ward's own follow-up study asked him he said: *a man came and sat on the floor and did not want anything, and after a while I stopped bracing.* The study logged that as *unclassified*. There are four hundred and six entries in that study logged as *unclassified* and the sitting protocol is now used in one hundred and forty facilities in nineteen countries and nobody has ever been able to say why it works or how often.

Siddhārtha, asked about it by Sagan on the feed a year later, refused the framing entirely.

"I did not do a therapy. There is no technique. If you write it down as a technique it will be a technique in four years and then it will be a *service* and then somebody will bill for it." He was as close to sharp as he ever got. "Say it in a way that keeps it useless: somebody stayed, and did not need them to change, and did not leave when nothing happened."

---

Yeshua did the opposite and it took him about eleven minutes to start.

He came into Ward C on the first morning, stood in the doorway for a moment the way everyone does, and then went straight to the sluice room and asked the nurse what needed doing.

"...I'm sorry?"

"You're two staff short on this floor," he said. "I counted at the desk. What needs doing that isn't getting done?"

She looked at him.

"The chairs," she said, eventually. "We're supposed to move them every two hours — for the pressure sores — and we do it, but we do it late, and it's four minutes each, and there's eleven of them."

He moved chairs for nine days.

That was the whole of it and Jakobus watched a man do it without one gesture of significance. He moved them at the correct interval; he learned each person's angle; he learned that the violinist liked to be turned toward the window at eleven and away from it at three, because at three the light came off a car in the park and into her eyes, and nobody had noticed that in fourteen months, and after he mentioned it to the staff nobody let it happen again.

He talked while he worked. Nothing large. He asked names — everybody's, staff included, on the first morning, and used them, and got them right, which the ward remarked on for years. He asked the chef about knives and got, over four days, in flat sentences with long gaps, an entire education in how to break down a chicken, most of which he already knew and none of which he said so about.

He fixed the second-floor tap, which had been dripping since the spring.

On the seventh day, the boy of twenty-two — the chef — said, in the middle of nothing, while being turned: "Why are you doing this."

"Because the chairs need moving."

"No." A long pause; they all took long pauses. "You know what I mean."

Yeshua crouched down so that they were at a level, which was a thing he did constantly and which Jakobus never once saw him do for effect.

"When I was your age I worked with my father," he said. "Building. Doors, mostly, and yokes for oxen, which is not what people imagine when they hear it — it is a fitting job. You make the yoke for the *animal*, not for the pair in general. A bad yoke is one somebody made for an idea of an ox." He shrugged slightly. "I was good at it and nobody wrote that down. Then I did the other thing for about three years, and everyone wrote that down, and they wrote a great deal that I did not say, and if you ever get out of that bed and go and look at what they made of me you will find a man in a painting with a face that is not mine." He straightened up. "So. The chairs need moving. And I would rather be a man who is useful in a room than a man who is *significant* in it, and I have had two thousand years to think about the difference, and it is the only thing I know that I did not have to be told."

The boy did not answer.

On the eighth day he asked Yeshua to bring him a knife and something to cut, and Amelie authorised it after a discussion Jakobus stayed out of, and a twenty-two-year-old who had not stood up in nine months sat in a chair and broke down two chickens badly and then a third one properly, with his hands shaking, and then put the knife down and said he was tired.

He is a chef. He has a restaurant in Clermont-Ferrand. There is no plaque and no story on the wall.

---

**The recognition came on the fifth day** and Jakobus had been braced for it since the car park.

It was not a patient. It was a woman of about seventy in the family room — a visitor, the mother of the man in bed nine — and she had come out for coffee and she saw him through the glass moving a chair.

Jakobus watched her face do the whole thing in about two seconds. He had spent forty years reading faces and he had never seen anything move that fast.

She came through the door and she was already going down.

And Yeshua — who had his hands full of chair — did the thing that Jakobus, afterwards, in a private ledger nobody has read, described as the single most skilful act he witnessed in two years, and he had watched Bruce Lee teach and Khabib bind a man's wrist and a Roman take a house apart with eleven words.

He put the chair down and got to her before her knee reached the floor, and took her hands, and pulled — physically, with a carpenter's shoulders, so that she came back up onto her feet and had to take a step to keep her balance, which meant she had to think about her feet, which meant the moment broke.

"No, no," he said, "no — you'll hurt yourself, this floor is terrible—" and he was already turning her, still holding one hand, walking her three steps to the chairs by the window and putting her into one of them, talking the whole way, ordinary, quick, slightly rueful. "There. Sit. Your son is in nine, isn't he. What's his name?"

She was crying and could not speak.

"What's his name?" he said again, gently, and she said, "Guillaume," and he said, "Guillaume," and then he sat down in the chair beside her — not opposite, beside — and did not say anything else for about four minutes.

When she could talk she said the thing, the enormous thing, the whole apparatus of it, and he let her say all of it and did not correct one word.

And at the end of it he said: "I can't do what you're asking. I want to say that plainly to you because you'll be here every day and I'd rather you weren't waiting." A pause. "I'm going to move the chairs, and I'm going to be here till Thursday. That's what I've got."

"That is nothing," she said.

"Ja," said Yeshua — he had picked that up off Jakobus by then, and used it for the rest of his life, and there are people who think it is Aramaic. "It's nothing. It's what I've got, and it's yours."

She was in that family room every day for the rest of the visit, and on the last day she brought the entire ward a cake, badly made, and the staff still talk about it.

Guillaume did not get up. He has not got up. He is thirty-four and he is in a facility outside Lyon and his mother comes on Tuesdays and Fridays.

---

**The nine days produced nothing measurable** and Jakobus reported exactly that.

He wrote it himself, four pages, and refused three separate offers to have it written properly. Two hundred and eighty-four patients. Nine days. No change in the standard scales in two hundred and seventy-nine of them at the point of leaving. Four unclassified changes noted by staff within a fortnight, one of which reversed. One man who got up two months later and who says a man sat on the floor.

That was the whole finding, and he put the following paragraph at the end of it, which is the only piece of writing by Jakobus Swart that exists anywhere in the public record:

*I want to record that this was not a treatment and should not be developed into one, and that the two men I took with me both said so independently before I did. What I observed was two people who were prepared to be in a room where nothing was going to happen, for nine days, without needing it to be worth their time. I have spent my professional life around men who can do almost anything and I have never met anyone else who can do that. I am told this is a spiritual matter. I do not think it is. I think it is a skill, and that it is rare, and that we have built an entire civilisation in which nobody is ever required to learn it.*

The Porch took it apart for three evenings.

**Kant** objected — and was, as usual, the only one who saw the ugly thing early. "I want to raise something and I am aware it will be unpopular. You took two men into a ward as *instruments*. Did they consent? Yes. Were they asked in a house in which they are the only two people everyone treats as exceptional, by the man who binds this house, for a purpose everyone agrees is good?" He looked around. "That is not a free consent. That is the shape of consent. I raise it because it is the exact structure by which good men are used, and because I would like it in the minutes that somebody said so before it becomes a programme."

Jakobus said afterwards that it was the most useful objection anyone made in two years, and that he had not thought of it once, and that this was the point.

**Marx** wanted the staffing figures and got them, and observed that the ward had been two nurses short on that floor for fourteen months, and that a man from the first century had counted it at the desk in under four minutes, and asked the house to sit with the fact that the intervention which produced the most reliable improvement in that building was *an extra pair of hands.*

**Sartre** said the whole thing was bad faith of an extremely high order — that a man who cannot bear to be significant, doing an ordinary job in front of witnesses, is performing not-performing, and that he did not doubt the sincerity and that sincerity was not the question.

Yeshua, who had come to the Porch for that one evening only, said: "That's fair. I don't have an answer to it. I've never had one." And Sartre, who had been ready for anything except agreement, sat down.

And **Levinas** did not analyse it at all. He asked one question, of Jakobus, at the end of the third evening.

"The woman in the family room. When she went down. What did he do first?"

"He put the chair down."

"No. After that."

Jakobus thought about it.

"He pulled her up," he said. "Onto her feet. Hard enough that she had to catch her balance."

"Yes," said Levinas. "That is it. That is the whole of it and I have written six hundred pages that do not get to it." He was not looking at anybody. "She went down to *worship*, which is a way of putting another person infinitely far away and making them safe to need. And he would not have it. He put her back on her feet, which is the only place two people can be, and then — and this is the part I want the record to contain — **he sat down beside her and not opposite her.**"

Nobody said anything.

"Opposite is where you are examined," said Levinas. "Beside is where you are accompanied. Every person in this house has been examined for two years by a machine, by a planet, by each other, and by history. Not one of us has been accompanied." He looked up. "The colonel is right that it is a skill. He is wrong that it is rare because it is difficult. It is rare because it is *unrewarded*, and because a civilisation that cannot bill for it will not teach it, and because every one of us in this house is here on account of a lifetime of doing things that could be measured."

---

They drove back on the Thursday and it took forty minutes and nobody said much.

Jakobus watched the two of them in the mirror for a while: a barefoot man of thirty-five who had spent nine days sitting on a floor, and a carpenter of thirty who had spent nine days moving chairs and had a cut on his thumb from the second-floor tap.

"Can I ask you both something," he said. "And you can tell me to get stuffed."

"Ja," said Yeshua.

"You're the two everybody in that house is careful with. The two the world would tear the building down to get at." He watched the road. "You've both had — I don't know what to call it. Two thousand years of consequences. And you spent nine days on chairs and a floor."

Siddhārtha said nothing at all, which was itself an answer and which Jakobus had learned to hear.

"You've got it the wrong way round, Jakobus," Yeshua said, after a while. "You think the nine days was the small version of the thing." He was looking out of the window at the valley. "It's the other way. It was always the chairs. Everything else that got written down happened *because* of the chairs, and then people kept the writing and threw the chairs away."

The vehicle went up the valley toward the mountain and the machine under it and a house of the greatest people who ever lived, and a lottery that was, at that moment, sitting at eleven million names.

"You want to know the actual difference between us," Yeshua said, "since you've been trying to work it out for a year and you're too polite to ask."

"Ja," said Jakobus. "I do."

"He'd have let her go down." A nod at Siddhārtha, without heat, entirely without judgement. "Sat with her on the floor till she was finished, and it would have been the right thing to do, and she'd have got up when she was ready and she'd have been steadier than mine."

"And you pulled her up."

"I can't stand it," said Yeshua, simply. "That's the whole difference. Not a doctrine. I have never once been able to stand it, and I have been told by better men than me that this is my flaw, and they may well be right." He put his head back against the seat. "Two thousand years and about nineteen thousand books, Jakobus, and it comes down to that: one of us can watch a person kneel, and one of us can't."

Siddhārtha, in the back, with his eyes closed, said: "It is not a flaw."

"It's a bit of a flaw."

"It is not a flaw," said Siddhārtha again, mildly, "and I have thought about it for eleven months, and I would like it on the record before the colonel writes his report, because he writes down everything." A pause. "What I do is a *discipline*. It can be taught; I have taught it; they are teaching it in a hundred and forty buildings and it will outlast me by a long way." Another pause. "What he did in that room cannot be taught at all. I have watched it four times now and I cannot find the instruction in it. That is not a compliment, exactly. It is a difficulty."

Yeshua laughed — a short, ordinary, entirely unremarkable laugh, the laugh of a man in a car.

"Ja," he said. "It's been a difficulty from the start."
