# The Porch — the third casting

*G's list. He was asked, in a WhatsApp message on an ordinary Saturday, which philosophers had
actually shaped him, and he answered in Afrikaans, in no particular order, with the warning that
reading most of them directly is hard work and to get summaries. The Machine took the list as a
casting brief.*

> *"belangrikste filosofie is egter 420 code."* — G, before the list

The house has been asked a question — **who is it for** — and has no method for answering it. It has
the finest hands that ever lived, five physicists, four commanders, and two men who never wrote a word
down. What it does not have is anybody whose entire professional life was the business of asking what a
question *is*.

So: nineteen more. They arrive into a house that has already been asked. They are the only people in
the record who spent their whole lives on exactly that.

**The rule of this batch: every one of them speaks for himself, in his own grammar, and the book never
puts a refutation in a man's mouth that he did not earn.** Where two of them are opposed, both cases
are given at full strength. The reader is not told who won, because nobody did.

## The credos — each man states his own philosophy, in his own voice

*Written to be read aloud. This is the Porch: one round, one statement each, no interruptions. Gadamer
holds the round. What follows is what each of them said.*

---

**PLATO** — d. Athens, c. 347 BC, **aged c. 80**. Reached at **40**, the Academy newly founded.

> "You are all looking at the wall. Everything you have ever touched is a copy — the horse, the chair,
> the just act, your own bodies. Each of them fails at being the thing it is imitating, and you know
> this, and the knowing is the whole proof: you could not call a thing *imperfect* unless you were
> already, somehow, acquainted with the perfect. So the Forms are not a story I invented to comfort
> anybody. They are the only explanation for the fact that you can be *disappointed*. And if that is
> true then the work of a life is turning around — away from the wall, up the hard passage, into the
> light that hurts — and then, and this is the part nobody wants, **going back down into the cave** to
> the people still watching shadows, who will not thank you, and may kill you. They killed mine."

**ARISTOTLE** — d. Chalcis, 322 BC, **aged 62**. *(Already in the house since the second casting.)*

> "My teacher looked up. I looked *around*. There is no second world; there is this one, and it is
> knowable, and the way you know it is that you go and count things — I dissected four hundred and
> ninety-five kinds of animal and I was wrong about a great many of them and that is how it is supposed
> to work. Everything that exists is trying to be more fully what it is: the acorn at the oak, the boy
> at the man. A good life is not obedience to a rule. It is the excellent functioning of a thing at
> what it is *for* — and for us that is reasoning together in a city. Virtue is not a feeling and it is
> not a decision. It is a **habit**, built like a callus, by doing the thing badly until you do it well."

**ANSELM** — d. Canterbury, 21 April 1109, **aged c. 75**. Reached at **44**, the *Proslogion*.

> "I do not seek to understand in order that I may believe; I believe in order that I may understand.
> Now — take the thought of *that than which nothing greater can be conceived.* Even the fool who says
> in his heart there is no God has the thought; it is in his understanding, or he could not deny it. But
> a thing which exists in the understanding *and in reality* is greater than a thing which exists in the
> understanding alone. Therefore, if that-than-which-nothing-greater is only in the understanding, then
> a greater can be conceived — which is a contradiction in the very words. I have not proved that I am
> comfortable. I have shown you that the thought will not sit still."

**THOMAS AQUINAS** — d. Fossanova, 7 March 1274, **aged 49**. Reached at **40**, mid-*Summa*.

> "Anselm's argument moves from a word to a world and I do not permit that step. Begin instead with
> what any man can see from a window: things move, and nothing moves itself; things are caused, and
> nothing causes itself; things come to be and pass away, so there was a time each was not. Run the
> chain back — not backwards in *time*, which is the error everyone makes, but *downwards*, through
> what holds a thing in being at this instant — and either it is held by something that needs no
> holding, or nothing is held and there is nothing, and there is not nothing. *Et hoc omnes intelligunt
> Deum.* And I will say the rest of it plainly, since you have my whole life on your machine: at the
> end I stopped. Everything I have written is straw. I did not say it was *false*. I said it was straw."

**RENÉ DESCARTES** — d. Stockholm, 11 February 1650, **aged 53**. Reached at **41**, the *Meditations*.

> "I burned it all down. Every belief I had, I asked one question of: *could I be wrong about this?* —
> and if the answer was yes, even once, even in a dream, out it went. The senses lie. Mathematics could
> be a demon's trick. And at the bottom of the fire, when there was nothing left, there was one thing
> that could not be doubted, because doubting it performs it: **I am thinking.** Therefore I am. From
> that single stone I rebuilt — and I rebuilt badly in places, and you will spend three centuries
> telling me so. Say what you like about the pineal gland. But every one of you who came after is
> standing on a floor I cleared, and not one of you has put back a single thing I burned."

**BARUCH SPINOZA** — d. The Hague, 21 February 1677, **aged 44**. Reached at **42**, the *Ethics* complete and unpublished.

> "There is one substance. Not two, Descartes — one. Call it God or call it Nature; the two words point
> at the same thing and I use both to annoy both parties. It has infinite attributes; we know two,
> thought and extension, and they are not two *things* interacting, they are the one thing read two
> ways. Nothing in it is contingent. Free will is what a stone would call its own falling if it were
> conscious of the fall and ignorant of the push. This is not despair — it is the door. Blessedness is
> not the reward of virtue; it *is* virtue: the intellectual love of God, which is the mind
> understanding that it is a part and not an exception. They excommunicated me at twenty-three with a
> curse read aloud. I ground lenses. I was not unhappy."

**DAVID HUME** — d. Edinburgh, 25 August 1776, **aged 65**. Reached at **28**, the *Treatise* just published and ignored.

> "Look for the self. Go on — look. Every time I go looking for *me* I stumble on a perception: heat,
> cold, light, love. I never catch myself at any time without a perception, and never observe anything
> but the perception. There is no self in there; there is a bundle, moving very fast. And causation —
> you have never seen it. You have seen one thing, then another thing, over and over, and your mind has
> formed a *habit*, and you have called your habit a law of nature. I am not saying stop. I am saying
> know what you are doing. Reason is, and ought only to be, the slave of the passions. And no man has
> yet got from *is* to *ought* without a conjuring trick in the middle of the paragraph."

**IMMANUEL KANT** — d. Königsberg, 12 February 1804, **aged 79**. Reached at **57**, the first *Critique*.

> "Hume woke me from a dogmatic slumber and I have not slept since. He was right that you never see
> causation — and wrong about what follows. You do not find order in the world; **you bring it.** Space,
> time, cause: these are the spectacles you cannot take off, the conditions of there being an experience
> at all. So we know appearances, thoroughly, forever — and the thing as it is in itself is closed to
> us. Not mysterious. *Closed.* And then the second thing, which matters more: the starry heavens above
> and the moral law within. Act only on that maxim you could will to be universal. Treat humanity, in
> yourself and in every other, never merely as a means. Anselm — a word: existence is not a predicate.
> Your greatest conceivable being gains nothing by being real, because *being real* adds nothing to a
> concept. I am sorry. It took seven hundred years and it is still the answer."

**G. W. F. HEGEL** — d. Berlin, 14 November 1831, **aged 61**. Reached at **37**, the *Phenomenology*.

> "Kant drew a line and said: beyond here, nothing. I say: the drawing of the line is already the
> crossing of it, since you cannot mark a limit without standing, in thought, on both sides. Nothing
> true is static. A thing generates its own opposite, and the two are not resolved by choosing — they
> are *carried up*, both preserved and both cancelled, into a third thing that could not have existed
> without the fight. That is what thinking is. That is what history is: Spirit coming to know itself,
> slowly, expensively, through us, and it does not care whether we enjoy it. And the shape of it, if you
> want the human version — two self-consciousnesses meet, and each needs the other to *recognise* it,
> and one submits, and then the master finds he is dependent on a slave and the slave, who works, finds
> he is transforming the world. Everything after that is a footnote to who is holding whose life."

**KARL MARX** — d. London, 14 March 1883, **aged 64**. Reached at **49**, *Capital* volume one.

> "I found Hegel standing on his head and I set him on his feet. It is not Spirit that moves history; it
> is what people *do all day* — who owns the ground, who owns the machine, who has nothing to sell but
> the hours of their own body. Change that and the ideas change; the ideas have never once changed it
> first. And the specific injury is this: a man makes a thing, and the thing does not belong to him, and
> he does not recognise himself in it, and the more he makes the poorer he is — his own life confronting
> him as an alien power. Look at your house. You invented a currency in eleven days out of *chores*, and
> a man who lends appeared on the fourth day, and you were charmed by it. I am not charmed. Philosophers
> have only interpreted the world. The point is to change it."

**FRIEDRICH NIETZSCHE** — d. Weimar, 25 August 1900, **aged 55**. Reached at **43**, the *Genealogy*.

> "God is dead, and we have killed him, and I am not celebrating — I am telling you there is blood on
> your hands and you have not noticed. What comes after is the work. Ask where your morality came from:
> not from the sky. The strong called themselves good and called the weak bad, and the weak, who could
> not strike back, performed the most brilliant act in human history — they *revalued*, they called
> weakness humility and cowardice patience and impotence goodness, and they won, and you are all living
> inside their victory and calling it conscience. I do not say go back. I say: look at what is driving
> you, in the dark, under the reasons — the will to power, in the priest as much as the soldier. And
> then the only test I trust: if you had to live this exact life again, every pain, eternally,
> unchanged — could you say yes? **Amor fati.** Not resignation. Wanting it."

**EDMUND HUSSERL** — d. Freiburg, 27 April 1938, **aged 79**. Reached at **54**, the *Ideas*.

> "*Zu den Sachen selbst.* To the things themselves. Philosophy has spent three hundred years arguing
> about whether the world is out there, and it has never once looked carefully at what is actually
> given. So: **bracket it.** Suspend the question of existence — not deny it, *suspend* it — and describe
> what remains, which is everything: this cup, exactly as it presents itself, one side at a time, with
> the other sides intended and absent. Consciousness is not a box with pictures in it. It is always
> consciousness *of*. That is the whole finding and it took me forty years and it is rigorous science,
> not literature. My assistant took it and made poetry of Being out of it. He was the best mind I ever
> taught. In 1933 he signed the letter, and in 1936 my name was removed from the university, and I was
> forbidden the library where my own papers were kept."

**MARTIN HEIDEGGER** — d. Freiburg, 26 May 1976, **aged 86**. Reached at **37**, *Being and Time*.

> "Why is there something rather than nothing? Not as a riddle — as *the* question, which the whole of
> Western philosophy has been avoiding by talking about beings instead of Being. Start where you are:
> you did not choose to be here. You were *thrown* — into this body, this language, this century, this
> house — and mostly you cope by dissolving into what *one* does, what *they* say, which is a very
> comfortable way of not existing. What breaks that is your death. Not as a fact in the future; as the
> possibility that makes yours *yours*. Being-toward-death is not morbidity; it is the only door to
> being anything at all rather than an average of everyone. **And you all know what I did in 1933, and
> I will not perform an apology here to make this room comfortable. Ask my teacher. He is standing
> behind you.**"

**LUDWIG WITTGENSTEIN** — d. Cambridge, 29 April 1951, **aged 62**. Reached at **56** — and there is an argument in the control room about which one they got.

> "You have my first book in your archive. I wrote it in a trench and I thought I had finished
> philosophy, and I gave away a fortune and taught village children and built a house, and it was
> wrong. Not wrong in the details. Wrong in the *picture* — I thought a sentence was a picture of a
> fact and that meaning was that. It is not. Meaning is **use.** Look at how the word actually works in
> the life it lives in. There is no essence of 'game', only family resemblances; there is no private
> language, because a rule you alone can check is not a rule. And so most of what has been said in this
> room today is not false. It is a *cramp* — a knot produced by taking a word on holiday from the place
> where it does its work. Philosophy is not a body of doctrine. It is a therapy. When it works, the
> problem does not get answered. It **disappears**, and you notice you can move your hand again."

**MAURICE MERLEAU-PONTY** — d. Paris, 3 May 1961, **aged 53**. Reached at **37**, the *Phenomenology of Perception*.

> "Descartes: your error was a *posture*. You sat down. You sat down in a warm room, alone, and asked
> what you could be certain of, and of course you found a thinking thing, because that is all that was
> left in the room. But I do not have a body. **I am my body.** When I reach for the cup my hand knows
> where the cup is before I have located it; when I learn a skill it is not knowledge added to a mind,
> it is a new organ growing into the world. Perception is not a picture in the head that I check against
> reality — it is the way flesh opens onto flesh. There are twenty people in this building who know
> everything I spent my life trying to say and cannot say a word of it, and they have been saying it all
> morning, on a mat, with their hands. I would rather study them than any of you."

**HANS-GEORG GADAMER** — d. Heidelberg, 13 March 2002, **aged 102**. Reached at **60**, *Truth and Method*.

> "I will not add a system to this room; there are enough. I will tell you the one condition under
> which anything said here can be understood at all. You believe your prejudices are what stand between
> you and the truth. They are not. They are the *organ* by which you have anything at all — you always
> come from somewhere, and the somewhere is what gives you a question. Understanding is not one man
> emptying himself into another. It is two horizons, each limited, moving until they **fuse** — which
> changes both, which is why nobody who has really understood another person comes back the same. The
> model is not the method. The model is a **conversation** — the kind where you genuinely risk your own
> position and the thing that emerges belongs to neither of you. That is why I am hosting and not
> speaking. I know what my job is."

**JEAN-PAUL SARTRE** — d. Paris, 15 April 1980, **aged 74**. Reached at **38**, *Being and Nothingness*.

> "There is no human nature, because there is no God to have conceived one. **Existence precedes
> essence:** you turn up, and only afterwards are you anything, and what you are is the total of what
> you have done and nothing else — not your intentions, not your talent, not what you were going to get
> around to. This is unbearable and so everyone lies about it. *Bad faith* is the waiter playing at
> being a waiter, the coward saying it is his temperament — pretending you are a thing when you are a
> freedom. You are condemned to be free. Every one of you in this house was taken without being asked,
> and you have all decided that this makes you innocent. It does not. You chose what to do on the
> Tuesday. And hell is other people — not because they are unpleasant, but because under their look you
> become an object, and you cannot get behind their eyes to correct it."

**ALBERT CAMUS** — d. Villeblevin, 4 January 1960, **aged 46**, in a car. Reached at **34**, *The Myth of Sisyphus* and *The Plague*.

> "There is only one truly serious philosophical problem and that is suicide. Everything else — how many
> dimensions, what is Being — comes after you have answered whether life is worth living. The absurd is
> not the world and it is not us: it is the *confrontation* — a man who demands meaning, and a universe
> that is silent. And there are two ways to cheat. Kill yourself, which ends the confrontation. Or take
> the leap — call the silence God, or History, or Progress — which is philosophical suicide and it is
> more comfortable and it is still cheating. What is left is to hold both, and go on: revolt, freedom,
> passion. Sisyphus at the bottom of the hill, walking down to the stone, conscious, with no hope and
> without resignation. **One must imagine Sisyphus happy.** And I will say to this house what I said to
> a plague: there are more things to admire in men than to despise, and I am not being sentimental. I
> counted."

**EMMANUEL LEVINAS** — d. Paris, 25 December 1995, **aged 89**. Reached at **55**, *Totality and Infinity*. His wife and daughter were hidden in a monastery; his father, mother and brothers in Lithuania were murdered.

> "You have all been doing ontology. What is Being, what is the world, what am I — and every one of
> those questions begins with *me*, and grows outward, and swallows everything it meets into a totality
> where the other person is one more item in my inventory. I say ethics is **first philosophy**, before
> being, before knowing. And it does not begin in a thought. It begins in an encounter: the **face** of
> the other person — not their nose and eyes, the sheer nakedness of them turned toward you — which
> commands you before you have agreed to anything. *You shall not kill me.* You did not consent to that
> obligation. It arrived. It is infinite, and it is not symmetrical, and it does not wait to be
> reciprocated. I studied under Husserl. I admired Heidegger more than any living thinker, and I was in a
> camp for prisoners of war while my mother and father were killed, and I have spent fifty years asking
> how the century's greatest thinker of Being had nothing whatever to say about the *other person*." A
> pause. "Your colonel's greeting. *Sawubona.* Say it again for me, slowly. I have spent my whole life
> trying to write that word and his grandmother handed it to him before he could spell."

## The through-line

The house was asked **who is it for.** The physicists cannot answer it — it is not that kind of
question. The commanders answer it by reflex and are wrong. The fighters answer it with their hands.

The philosophers cannot answer it either. That is the point of the batch and the book must not cheat
it: nineteen of the greatest askers who ever lived, in one room, and no sentence comes out.

What comes out instead is the *practice* — the Porch itself, running three nights a week, broadcast
unedited to eight billion people who have nothing else to do and nowhere to put their attention, in
which nineteen men who cannot agree keep turning up to disagree with each other courteously, in
public, for months.

And the Monday number goes on falling.
