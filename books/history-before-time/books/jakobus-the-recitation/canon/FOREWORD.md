# Foreword

### *by Sandor Valenputers* †

I went into the Kalahari as a young man looking for the oldest people on earth, and I came out an old man understanding that I had been looking, all along, for the oldest *way* — the way of telling, the way of remembering, the way a people who own almost nothing can carry the whole of their history in the mouth, and hand it across a fire from one generation to the next without losing a single word.

This book understands that way. It is a small miracle that it does, because the way is nearly gone, and the men who knew how to write about it without sentimentalising it are nearly gone too, and I had rather assumed the door would close behind us. It has not. Here is a younger hand that has stood at a Kalahari fire and listened — really listened, which is the hardest thing a modern person can be asked to do — and has come away with the one truth that justifies the whole of my own long, flawed, well-meaning life's work: that the recitation *is* the inheritance. That a story, told right, is not entertainment laid over the world but the world itself, held in trust.

I must be honest, as the author is honest. I spent my life perhaps loving the San too much and seeing them too clearly through my own longing — making of them a mirror for what my own civilisation had lost. It is a tempting error and a kindly one and it is still an error, and I am glad to report that this book does not make it. Its San are not a symbol. They are people — funny, exact, unsentimental, in possession of a debt and a grace and a way of settling both that left me, an old believer, freshly humbled. The author has done what I tried and only sometimes managed: he has let them be *themselves*, and found that they are more than enough.

And he has set among them a stranger — a hard, quiet, capable man of the modern world — and watched what the recitation does to such a man when he is finally still enough to receive it. That is the engine of the book, and it is the truest thing in it: not the desert, not the danger, but the long slow opening of a closed man under the patient pressure of an older story than his own. I have seen that opening happen to real men around real fires. I never thought to see it caught on a page.

The Kalahari in here is the true Kalahari — the one that is not a wasteland but a fullness, the one the first people read the way you and I read a library. The author knows that the desert is not empty. He knows it is *written*, and that the people who can still read it are holding, in their failing hands, a text older than any scripture. He treats that knowledge with the reverence it is owed and not one ounce more, which is exactly right, because reverence past its proper weight becomes a kind of theft.

I am at the end of my road, and I have spent it telling the world that the oldest people had something we had thrown away and badly need back. Few believed me, and those who did often believed me for the wrong, soft reasons. This book makes the case better, and harder, and without me, and I lay down my own long argument beside it gratefully, the way an old man hands the telling, at last, to a younger and a steadier voice.

Sit down by the fire. Be still. Listen. The story is about to begin, and it is older than all of us, and it is true.

*— Sandor Valenputers*
