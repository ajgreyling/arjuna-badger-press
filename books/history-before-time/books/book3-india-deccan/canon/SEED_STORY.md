# SEED STORY — *History Before Time*, Book III (India: Deccan)

> Working title: **The Temple in the Rock** *(or author's choice)*
> Standalone + reward for India-trilogy readers. Gita position: **Krishna's teaching.**

---

## The premise capsule

A conservation architect — Deccan-born, steeped in the three-faith plurality of Ellora — is
recruited by the Order to read what has always been in front of her: the Kailasa temple at Ellora
is not just a masterwork. It is an instrument. Carved top-down, from a single living mountain, by
someone who could already see the finished form inside the rock — no errors possible, no revisions,
no scaffolding. The same discipline the Bhagavad Gita describes: **act without grasping at outcome;
the form is already there; the task is only to remove what is not it.**

The antagonist wants to *own* the interpretation — to flatten the three-faith cliff into a single
supremacist narrative. She must stop him. To do so she must do the thing the Gita demands and that
the temple embodies: **act, fully, without waiting to know if she'll succeed.**

---

## The locked ideas

- **The Gita's teaching as the plot's actual structure** (binding). She begins paralysed by
  expertise — she knows too much to act without certainty. The temple's making is the argument
  against her: no certainty, no revision, just the cut. By the climax she must act *that* way.
- **Ellora's three-faith cliff as the series argument** (binding). Cave 1–12: Buddhist. 13–29:
  Hindu. 30–34: Jain. In one mountain, over a thousand years. They did not fight over the rock.
  They added to each other. That is the physical refutation of the antagonist's project.
- **The Deccan's Muslim layer** (binding — carry right). The Deccan Sultanates were syncretic,
  not purist; the Bidar lacquerware, the Gol Gumbaz, the Qutb Shahi tombs are not alien impositions
  — they are one more stratum of the rock. The protagonist's Muslim colleague/friend embodies this:
  his faith is ancient in this land.
- **The engineering wonder** (binding — grounding rule). 200,000 tonnes of basalt removed from
  the top down. The tolerances on the carved pillars. No mortar; all subtractive. Show the
  checkable numbers; let the wonder land after.

## The relay handoff

Priya Ellis passes the bearing at the START of Book III — a brief, warm, non-POV scene
(her voice, her dry wit, the sibling-register with Jakobus if he's there) that rewards Book II
readers without requiring them for newcomers. She gives the Book III protagonist the instrument
reading and says, essentially: *you grew up here. You can hear what I can't.*

The bearing at the END points south — toward Tamil Nadu, the shore, the older mythology.

---

## What the reader learns (the tourism mission)

By the end of Book III, a reader who has never been to India will know:
- Ellora and Ajanta: what they are, why they matter, how to approach them
- That "Hindu" is not a monolith — Jainism and Buddhism grew from the same soil
- That the Deccan Sultanates were Muslim *and* syncretic *and* Indian
- That the Bhagavad Gita is not a religious text you have to believe — it's an argument about
  *how to act* that applies to anyone who has ever been paralysed by wanting to get it right
- That India's plurality is not a problem to be explained — it is the whole point
- **And they will want to go stand in the Kailasa courtyard and look up.**
