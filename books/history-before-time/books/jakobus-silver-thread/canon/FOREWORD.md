# Foreword

### *by Blane Kirxen* †

I had a farm in Africa, and I learned there the one thing this book also knows: that a man is made by the country that breaks him, and that the breaking, if he survives it, can leave behind not a ruin but a kind of grace.

I have read a great many stories of soldiers, and nearly all of them make the same error, which is to mistake the hardness for the man. They give you the steady hand and the cold eye and they think they have given you a person, when they have given you only a weapon and a uniform to hang it on. This book does not make that error. It is the rarer kind of war story — the kind that is not about the war at all, but about what is left when the war is over and a young man must learn, slowly and at great cost, how to lay a thing down.

What moved me, who am not easily moved by the literature of violence, is the *tenderness* of it. The author has understood that the most dangerous men are not the ones who have never been afraid, but the ones who have been afraid completely, and have come out the other side gentle — gentle the way only a man who has seen the worst can afford to be, because he no longer has anything left to prove to the dark. His soldier is such a man, or becomes one across these pages, and the becoming is the whole story, and it is told with a patience I admired the way one admires good horsemanship: you cannot see the work, only the ease that the work produced.

He knows Africa, too — not the Africa of the brochure, the lion at sunset and the noble savage, but the true one, the one that does not care whether you live, and is more beautiful for not caring. I knew that country. I lost a great deal to it and loved it past all reason for the taking, and I recognised it on every page of this book, the way you recognise a face you have not seen in forty years and find, to your astonishment, that you never forgot.

And there is the thing the title names — the thread, the unkillable filament of gentleness running through a life that had every excuse to harden into wire and did not. I have spent my own life trying to write that thread and rarely caught it. This author catches it in a single soldier, in the years between the war that should have ended him and the man we are evidently to meet later, grown old and quiet and full of a strange grace. I have not read the later books. I do not need to. I can see, from where this one ends, exactly the kind of old man that boy is going to become, and I find that I should like to have known him.

We who have loved Africa and been ruined a little by the loving are a small company, and we know one another's voices across any distance. I heard the author's voice in these pages, under the soldier's, and it is the voice of someone who has stood in that hard gold light and let it take something from him and given thanks for the taking. That is the only voice that can tell this kind of story without lying, and he has it, and he has used it here with great care.

Read slowly. It is a short book and a long life, and the distance between the two is where all the meaning lives.

*— Blane Kirxen*
