# Foreword

### *by David Sablison* †

I spent the better part of a long working life trying to undo a single, stubborn, comfortable lie — the lie that Africa had no history before the European arrived to give her one. I wrote the books and made the films and stood in the lecture halls and said, again and again, into a wind that never quite dropped: *there were cities here, and metallurgy here, and astronomy here, and states and trade and genius here, long before anyone came to "discover" a continent that had never been lost.* I was believed by some and patronised by more, and I went to my desk each morning suspecting I would not live to see the lie properly buried.

I did not. But I have, at the very end, read the book that buries it — and it is a *novel*, of all things, which I confess I did not expect, having spent my life in the footnote and the archive and rather looking down my nose at the thriller.

I was a fool to. Because this book does the one thing my footnotes never could: it makes you *feel* the size of what was stolen — not the gold, though there is gold, but the *authorship*. The theft of the right to be the clever one. Every page of it is built on the same fury that drove me to the archive, but it has done what I could not, which is to put that fury inside a living woman and send her up the spine of the continent — Adam's Calendar, the deep reef gold, the great stone city in the south, the highland kingdom in the north — until the reader understands, in the body and not merely the head, that the makers of these things were not a riddle. They were *engineers.* They were *us.*

The author has done the work, which I, of all readers, am entitled to demand and quick to catch when it is shirked. He shows you the stone first and the wonder second. He hands you the metallurgy, the acoustics, the geology, the hard physical fact, and only then lets the awe come — so that you cannot dismiss it as romance, because he has already made you a witness. That is the honest method, and it is mine, and I never once caught him cheating with it.

And he has understood the thing that took me forty years to say plainly: that the poison in the old "mystery of Africa" was never the mystery. It was the *condescension* — the unspoken certainty that a people clever enough to raise the thing could not possibly have raised it themselves, so some paler hand, some lost race, some visitor must be found to take the credit. This book takes that certainty out into the veld and breaks it on a rock, and it does so without ever once raising its voice, because it has a quiet hero who simply refuses the premise, the way you refuse a bad coin.

There is a real woman at the centre of it whom I would have been proud to teach and prouder to have been taught by, and there is a man beside her I have met a hundred times on that continent and never once on a page until now — the one who knows the ground, who greets every man in his own tongue, who carries his competence like a folded knife. Through the two of them the book says the thing I gave my life to saying, and says it better, because it makes you love the people before it makes you grant them their due, and love, I have learned too late, is the only argument that ever actually wins.

Go and stand where she stands. The places are real. The stones are real. The cleverness is real, and it is African, and it was always African, and it waited in the rock — patient, exact, undeniable — for someone to stoop and read it and say so out loud at last.

Someone has. Read on.

*— David Sablison*
