# The Rigveda — the oldest wonder

If you read only one Veda in your life, the tradition and the scholarship agree, for once, on which
one: the Rigveda. It is the oldest, the largest of the four in original material, and the one every
later text — the other three Vedas, the Upanishads, the epics, the Gita itself — leans back against.
Ten books (*mandalas*), 1,028 hymns (*suktas*), over ten thousand verses, composed across many
generations and finally arranged into the collection we have.

**Agni — the god who opens the book.** The very first hymn of the very first book is addressed not
to the sky, or the sun, or a warrior-god, but to fire.

> *Agni I laud, the household priest, the divine minister of sacrifice,*
> *The invoker, lavishest of wealth.*

**Plainly:** of everything in the world the poets could have opened with, they opened with the fire
in the hearth — because Agni is not only the god of physical flame but the *messenger* god, the one
who carries the offering upward from the human fire to the gods, present in every home and every
altar at once. Starting the Rigveda with Agni is like starting a hymnal with the light in the room
you are singing in. It is also, not incidentally, why this book is called *The First Fire*: Agni is
the Rigveda's first word, and — the tradition holds — the first thing the seers who received these
hymns actually saw.

**Indra — the storm who fights for rain.** After Agni, the god invoked most often is Indra, wielder
of the thunderbolt (*vajra*), who each year does battle with the serpent-demon Vritra — "the
encloser" — who has swallowed the world's waters and coiled around them. Indra's most celebrated
hymn (Rigveda 1.32) tells it as pure drama:

> *I will declare the manly deeds of Indra, the first that he achieved, the Thunder-wielder.*
> *He slew the Dragon, then disclosed the waters, and cleft the channels of the mountain torrents.*

**Plainly:** read as agriculture and read as cosmology at once — a storm-god cracking open a
drought — this is one of the oldest surviving versions of a story-shape (chaos-serpent coiled around
the waters of life, slain by a sky-god so the world can drink) that recurs, independently or not,
across an astonishing number of unrelated mythologies. Scholars call this a *chaoskampf* motif; this
book takes no side on whether it spread or arose independently, and simply notes how old and how
vivid the Rigveda's version of it is.

**The Nasadiya Sukta — the Hymn of Creation.** And then, near the very end of the collection
(Rigveda 10.129), the Rigveda does something almost no other ancient scripture of its era dares to
do: it asks how the universe began, and refuses to give itself a comfortable answer.

> *Then was not non-existent nor existent: there was no realm of air, no sky beyond it...*
> *Who verily knows and who can here declare it, whence it was born and whence comes this creation?*
> *The Gods are later than this world's production. Who knows then whence it first came into being?*
> *He, the first origin of this creation, whether he formed it all or did not form it,*
> *Whose eye controls this world in highest heaven, he verily knows it, or perhaps he knows it not.*

**Plainly:** this is not doubt as failure; it is doubt as the honest end of the longest possible
looking. A text composed perhaps three thousand years ago, in a culture that had every institutional
reason to hand down a tidy origin story, instead ends its own creation-hymn on an open question —
even the gods came *after*, so even they cannot be asked — and dares to suggest that perhaps not even
the highest being knows for certain. It is, by wide agreement among readers across traditions and no
tradition at all, one of the most remarkable single passages in the literature of the ancient world,
and it alone is reason enough to have written this book.

**The Purusha Sukta — the world as a sacrifice of a cosmic being.** A very different creation-hymn
(Rigveda 10.90) imagines the universe as arising from the ritual dismemberment of a primordial
being, Purusha — sky from his head, sun from his eye, the four social orders (*varnas*) from his
mouth, arms, thighs, and feet. **Scholarship note, held plainly:** this last detail — the origin
myth of the varna system, later hardened by other texts and centuries of social practice into caste
— is one of the most contested passages in the entire corpus, discussed and disputed by scholars and
reformers alike for how a cosmological image became a social one; this book names the hymn, names
the dispute honestly, and does not adjudicate it.

**The Gayatri Mantra — the verse most Hindus can recite by heart.** Rigveda 3.62.10, addressed to
Savitr, the sun as the vivifying divine light, is arguably the single most recited verse in the
whole of Hindu practice, chanted daily by observant Brahmins for millennia and known, at least in
part, to a very large share of practising Hindus of every background today:

> *Om bhur bhuvah svah, tat savitur varenyam,*
> *bhargo devasya dhimahi, dhiyo yo nah prachodayat.*

Griffith's English: *"May we attain that excellent glory of Savitar the god: So may he stimulate our
prayers."* A more common plain rendering: *"We meditate on the glorious light of the divine sun; may
it illumine our minds."* **Plainly:** it is, at its simplest, a prayer for clarity of thought,
addressed to the light that makes seeing possible in the first place — which may be the reason it
has survived, essentially unchanged, in daily use, for longer than most nations have existed.

The Rigveda is enormous, and this chapter has walked past a thousand hymns to stand in front of
four. That is the guest's honest limit. But if you take only these four away — the fire that opens
the book, the storm that frees the water, the hymn that dares to doubt its own creation, and the
verse still whispered at ten thousand sunrises this morning — you have stood, briefly, at the very
headwater of the whole tradition.
