# Going — where to read next, and where to listen

A guest, leaving a fire, owes his hosts one last courtesy: he points the people he brought toward
the door, so they can go in properly, by the real entrance, and meet the householders themselves.
This is that — and, this time, an invitation to *listen* as well as read, because at least one of
these four Vedas was never meant to live only on a page.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Read the Vedas themselves

**The free, public-domain translations (what this book quoted):** Ralph T. H. Griffith's English
translations of the *Rigveda* (1889), the White Yajurveda's *Vajasaneyi Samhita* (1899), and the
*Atharvaveda* (1895–96) are all long out of copyright and freely available — search Sacred-Texts.com
or [Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org) by title. Start here if you want to read further
tonight for nothing.

**Then go to the householders.** Griffith and I are both outsiders who loved these texts. To read
the Vedas as they live *inside* the tradition, reach for a translation and commentary by a scholar
or teacher within it:

- **Wendy Doniger, *The Rig Veda: An Anthology*** — a widely used modern scholarly selection with
  extensive notes, a kind first doorway into the Rigveda's range for a general reader.
- **Stephanie W. Jamison and Joel P. Brereton, *The Rigveda: The Earliest Religious Poetry of
  India*** — the complete Rigveda in a rigorous modern scholarly translation, prized for fidelity to
  the Sanskrit; the deep-water route for a serious reader.
- **Swami Vivekananda's and Swami Prabhavananda's writings on the Vedas and Vedanta** — for the
  living tradition's own voice on what these texts mean and how they are practised today.

There are hundreds more, across every school and generation. The point is only this: *do not stop at
the visitor's account.* Go in by the real door.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## A word on the schools (so you go in with open hands)

As with the Gita, you will quickly find that Hindus do not read "the Veda" as one flat, uniform
thing, and that this is a richness, not a problem. Some traditions emphasise the ritual layer this
book only glanced at; others hold the Upanishadic culmination as the Veda's true heart and the
earlier hymns as preparation for it; scholars, meanwhile, continue to debate dating, authorship, and
the historical relationship between the Vedic peoples and other ancient Indian cultures with real
and ongoing disagreement. This book deliberately took no side in any of that, because that was never
a guest's place. Listen to those who do take sides as you would listen to a family talking, with
love and long study, about the thing they hold most dear.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Go and listen

Because the Samaveda in particular resists the page, seek out a recording of Vedic chanting by a
recognised tradition or institution — many universities and cultural bodies specialising in Sanskrit
and Hindu studies host recordings of Rigvedic and Samavedic recitation by trained reciters. Hearing
even a few minutes of it will teach you something this whole book, on paper, could not.

And, as before — if what moved you here was the world of the *History Before Time* novels, go and
stand, when you can, in front of the stones those books are built on: the Kailasa temple at Ellora,
carved whole from the mountain; the shore temples of Mahabalipuram; the great stepwells of
Rajasthan. The Vedas came before any of that stone was cut. Standing in front of the stone, having
now heard something of the fire that came before it, is its own kind of offering — the leaf and the
water of an old hymn, given with your own two feet.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

The fire is still burning, four flames at once now instead of one. It does not need us to tend it;
it has been tended, syllable by syllable, for three thousand years, and will be tended long after.
But they let us sit, and warm our hands, and carry a little of the sound home.

Go in by the real door. And, if you can, go and listen to a hymn spoken exactly as it was meant to
be — aloud, by a voice trained for nothing else its whole life but getting it right.

*Om. What is heard, endures.*
