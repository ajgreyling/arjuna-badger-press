# One — The Lucky Fool

*Anchored in* Fooled by Randomness *(2001) — the first book of the* Incerto.

### The fire

Start with the first book, because everything after it grows from this root.

Picture a man on a trading floor who has made a great deal of money. He is confident, articulate, and
successful, and he has a theory of why: he is good at this. He sees patterns others miss. The market
rewards his insight. Everyone around him agrees, because the money is real and the money is the proof.

Taleb's first book is built to take that man apart — gently in its logic, mercilessly in its
conclusion. The argument, as he makes it in *Fooled by Randomness*, runs roughly like this. Markets are
noisy. Over any given stretch, some traders will do well and some badly, and a meaningful share of *who
ends up in which pile* is **luck** — not insight, not skill, just the coin landing their way enough
times in a row. Now add the cruel part: the lucky ones do not experience their luck as luck. They
experience it as *competence*. They build a story — a theory of their own genius — that fits the
outcome perfectly, because stories always fit the outcome once you know it. And the rest of us believe
them, because they are rich and confident and the money is real.

The deepest move in the book is about who we *count*. We look at the winners and study their habits,
their methods, their morning routines, and we try to copy them — and we never look at the larger crowd
of people who had the same habits, the same methods, the same routines, and lost anyway, because they
are gone and silent and not writing memoirs. Taleb's term for this is **survivorship bias**: the
graveyard does not give interviews. A whole industry of "how the successful think" is, on this view, the
careful study of lottery winners' lucky numbers.

He is not saying skill does not exist. He is saying that in domains thick with randomness, **a good
outcome is appallingly weak evidence of a good decision** — and that the human mind is built to
confuse the two, to read backward from "it worked" to "I was right," and to do it so automatically
that the lucky fool is usually the last person on earth who could tell you he got lucky.

### Where the smoke goes

The honest pushback comes from two directions, and it is worth naming both.

The first is empirical and friendly: *yes, but skill is real and sometimes detectable.* In domains
where the noise is lower and the sample is larger — surgery, chess, freethrow shooting — outcomes track
ability quite well, and a model that treats all success as luck would be as foolish as the one that
treats all success as genius. Taleb knows this; his fire is aimed at the *high-randomness* domains
(markets, careers, single ventures) where people *act* as if they were in low-randomness ones. But a
careless reader can walk away having learned "nothing is skill," which is its own kind of fooled.

The second pushback is about the man's tone, and it recurs across the whole *Incerto*, so let me say it
once here and lean on it later. Taleb is contemptuous of the lucky fool, and the contempt is part of the
argument's pleasure — but contempt is a poor instrument for telling the merely-lucky from the genuinely
skilled, because from the outside, in real time, **they look identical.** The book is sharpest as a
warning to *yourself* and weakest as a license to sneer at *others*, and the second use is the one the
internet kept.

### Plainly

**`plainly:`** Here is the test I actually use, on my own ground, since I build ventures and a press
where one good year could be talent and could just as easily be the coin.

Stop grading your decisions by how they turned out. Grade them by what you knew *when you made them*. A
decision was good if, with the information you had and the odds you faced, you'd make it again across a
hundred parallel lives — even the ones where it lost. A decision was bad if it only looks smart because
this particular life is the one where the coin came up your way. Outcome is the verdict of *one* trial;
quality is the verdict across *all the trials you didn't get to run.* The lucky fool grades on outcome,
which is why he learns nothing — the world keeps telling him he was right.

And the practical mercy in it: this is the idea that lets you survive a *good* result without being
ruined by it. If you can hold "I succeeded *and* I may have been lucky" in the same hand, you keep
checking your reasoning when everything is going well — which is the only time the checking is hard and
the only time it matters. Count the graveyard. Ask who had your exact plan and is not here to brag
about it. Then decide whether to trust the plan or just the survivor telling you about it.

### The line

Taleb's whole first book turns on a distinction he draws again and again — that we systematically
mistake *luck* for *skill*, and *noise* for *signal*, and then build proud theories on the mistake.

**`plainly:`** *A run of wins is not a proof of genius; it is, until shown otherwise, a run of wins.*
Go read it as he argues it — *Fooled by Randomness*, the book that starts the whole *Incerto*, and the
quietest of the five. It is where the bird in the next essay learns to fly.
