# Nine — The Cost of Being Wrong

*Anchored in* Skin in the Game *— where Taleb argues that those who make the rules should face the consequences.*

### The fire

The oldest wisdom is often the simplest: never take advice from someone who doesn't pay for being wrong. This is the spine of *Skin in the Game*, Taleb's book about risk-sharing and the moral architecture of decision-making. The phrase itself comes from Warren Buffett, but the principle is ancient — as old as the first builder who had to sleep under the bridge he designed.

Taleb's argument in the book runs deeper than "practice what you preach." He is making a claim about the fundamental asymmetry poisoning modern life: **the people who make decisions are increasingly not the people who bear the consequences.** The banker who gets a bonus for risks that won't explode until after he's gone. The foreign policy expert who advocates for wars he will never fight. The corporate consultant who recommends a strategy and leaves before it fails. They have the upside — fees, reputation, book deals — but not the downside. When the bill comes due, they are nowhere to be found.

Against this, Taleb sets an older principle, one he finds in everything from Hammurabi's code to traditional merchant law: **you eat your own cooking first.** In ancient Rome, engineers had to stand under the bridges they built when the scaffolding came down. In traditional societies, the healer who prescribed the cure had to take it himself if challenged. The oldest risk management system is not complex mathematics — it is the simple requirement that whoever makes the decision bears the results.

He calls people who violate this principle the **interventionistas** — those who intervene in complex systems without bearing any risk from their interventions. They are the ultimate fragilistas, making others fragile while keeping themselves safe. And he argues, across the book, that no system can survive when the people running it have no skin in its outcomes. It's not just unfair; it's unstable. Without skin in the game, the feedback loop between action and consequence is broken, and when that loop breaks, the system is already dying.

### Where the smoke goes

The sharpest criticism comes from those who note that *perfect symmetry is often impossible.* A doctor treating a patient cannot literally share the patient's cancer. A teacher cannot un-know math to experience the student's confusion. Some forms of expertise require precisely that the expert is *not* in the same position as the person they're helping. Demanding skin in the game everywhere would exclude genuine service.

There's also the charge that Taleb's framework can become a way to dismiss any expertise you dislike. Don't trust economists unless they're traders. Don't trust foreign policy experts unless they're soldiers. Don't trust anyone writing about risk unless they're currently taking it. This can slide from healthy skepticism into a kind of anti-intellectual populism where only practitioners can speak — forgetting that some of the best coaches never played the game at the highest level.

The deepest pushback is about feasibility: *modern systems may be too complex for clean alignment.* When a pharmaceutical company develops a drug, exactly whose children should test it first? When a city planner designs a neighborhood, should they be required to live in the worst unit? The principle is clean; the implementation gets murky fast.

### Plainly

**`plainly:`** I learned this lesson in the most expensive possible way — by violating it.

Years ago, I built software for a client without having to use it myself. I designed elegant systems for problems I understood only in theory. The architecture was clean, the code was proud, and when it failed — which it did, catastrophically — I was already on the next project. The client ate the cost. Their customers ate the frustration. I ate nothing but my fee.

Now I have a rule: I don't build anything I won't have to live with. If I design a system, I'm on call when it breaks at 3 AM. If I recommend a strategy, I invest in it first. If I write software, I'm the first user and the last one to abandon it when it fails. This isn't nobility — it's engineering. The feedback loop between decision and consequence is what teaches you to make better decisions. Cut that loop and you stay stupid forever, building bridges that will fall after you've crossed.

The practical test: when someone gives you advice, ask what they lose if they're wrong. Not what they've gained from being right — survivorship bias handles that — but what they *currently* have at risk if their advice fails. The portfolio manager who's fully in cash while telling you to buy. The professor of entrepreneurship who's never started anything. The foreign policy expert who's never been within a thousand miles of the war they advocate. They may be brilliant, they may even be right, but their advice is suspect because they've arranged to be gone when the bill arrives.

### The line

> "The curse of modernity is that we are increasingly populated by a class of people who are better at explaining than understanding."

> — Nassim Nicholas Taleb, *Skin in the Game* (2018)
