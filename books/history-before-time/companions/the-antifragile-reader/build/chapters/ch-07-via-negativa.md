# Seven — Subtraction

*Anchored in* Antifragile *— where Taleb argues that removing harmful things beats adding helpful ones.*

### The fire

There is a medical habit Taleb returns to throughout *Antifragile* that captures one of his deepest ideas. A doctor sees a patient with a mild problem — slightly elevated blood pressure, marginally high cholesterol, a touch of back pain. The doctor prescribes something: a pill, a procedure, a regimen. The patient now has the original problem plus whatever the intervention brings — side effects, complications, new dependencies. Often the cure is literally worse than the disease. Taleb has a name for this: **iatrogenics**, from the Greek for "caused by the healer." The harm done by the attempt to help.

But iatrogenics is just the entry point to a much larger idea, one Taleb calls **via negativa** — the negative way. In theology (where he borrows the term), you get closer to understanding God by saying what God is *not* than what God is. In life, you get better results by removing the harmful than by adding the beneficial. Stop smoking beats any supplement. Not being fooled beats being clever. Avoiding the toxic person improves your life more than finding the perfect mentor. Subtraction is knowledge; addition is often guesswork.

He pairs this with what might be his most practical heuristic: **the Lindy effect**. Named after a New York deli where comedians noticed that shows that had been running for years would probably run for years more, while new shows would probably close soon. The observation became a principle: for non-perishable things — ideas, books, technologies — every additional day of survival implies a longer life expectancy. What has been around for a thousand years will probably last another thousand. What was invented yesterday will probably be gone tomorrow. Time is the ultimate via negativa filter, removing the fragile and keeping only what works.

The combination is powerful. Want to know what to read? Read old books that are still in print — time has already done the filtering. Want to know how to eat? Eat things your great-grandmother would recognize as food — they've survived the Lindy test. Want to improve a system? First remove what's clearly harmful before dreaming up clever additions. The drunk doesn't need vitamins; he needs to stop drinking. The fragile complex system doesn't need more controls; it needs fewer interconnections. We overestimate addition and underestimate subtraction because addition feels like accomplishment and subtraction feels like giving up.

### Where the smoke goes

The empiricist's challenge is straightforward: *sometimes addition really is the answer.* The person with scurvy needs vitamin C, not the removal of something else. The bridge needs reinforcement, not the subtraction of weak parts. The child needs education, not just the absence of bad teaching. Taleb knows this — he's not advocating therapeutic nihilism — but readers can walk away thinking via negativa means never intervene, which becomes its own kind of dangerous.

The Lindy effect also gets shakier under examination. *Survival and quality are not identical.* Plenty of terrible things last — corruption is ancient, mosquitoes predate humans, bad ideas can persist for millennia if they serve power. Meanwhile, some innovations (antibiotics, the internet, refrigeration) are both new and transformative. Age predicts survival, not value. A pure Lindy heuristic would have you reading only ancient texts while dying of ancient diseases.

And there's a practical objection: *negative advice is harder to monetize, so you hear it less.* "Don't eat processed food" doesn't sell conferences. "Remove meetings" doesn't build consulting practices. The very fact that via negativa is undersold might mean we need the positive prescriptions too, just to compete with all the noise pushing addition.

### Plainly

**`plainly:`** The via negativa changed how I edit, how I build, how I live.

In writing, I now cut before I add. The chapter that isn't working doesn't need a clever new paragraph to save it — it needs the confused sections removed. The press's catalogue doesn't need twenty more titles to feel substantial — it needs the three weak ones pulled. This is painful. Those weak titles have authors, histories, sunk costs. But keeping them hurts every good book by association. Subtraction is harder than addition because you have to admit something you made is making things worse.

In life, the biggest gains came from stopping, not starting. I didn't need productivity systems; I needed to stop checking my phone before noon. The work didn't need more framework; it needed fewer meetings about frameworks. Every intervention carries hidden cost — not just time but complexity, maintenance, the chance of breaking what was working. When you add, you're betting you're smarter than time. When you subtract, you're letting time do the thinking.

The Lindy filter became my reading heuristic: if it's been in print for a century, start there. Not because old is automatically good, but because survival is expensive — books compete for shelf space, attention, reprinting budgets. What lasts has paid that tax for decades. Meanwhile, the business book published last month is statistically dead already, even if Amazon hasn't noticed yet.

### The line

Taleb's aphorism from *The Bed of Procrustes* catches it:

> "The best way to revenge yourself against those who provoke you is to reduce your wishes to nothing they can grant."

**`plainly:`** *Via negativa* in nine words: subtract what they have power over until they don't. But the full argument lives in *Antifragile*, where he shows why the negative way — removing fragility rather than adding complexity — is the only strategy robust to an uncertain future. Go read him make the case that less beats more, properly argued across a book that needed every page.
