# About this book {.unnumbered}

*The Walls of Uruk* retells the *Epic of Gilgamesh* plainly and reverently, so that a reader who
never studied Assyriology can finish it and know what the poem actually is — the tyrant, the friend,
the Cedar Forest, the Bull of Heaven, Enkidu's death, the flood, the stolen plant — and what each of
its twelve tablets asks of a human life.

**The names.** The Standard Babylonian poem uses Akkadian forms. This book keeps the familiar
English spellings most readers already know — **Gilgamesh**, **Enkidu**, **Uruk**, **Humbaba**
(also Huwawa), **Ishtar** (Inanna in the older Sumerian poems), **Utnapishtim** (Uta-napishti;
Atrahasis in the older flood tale), **Siduri**, **Urshanabi** — and notes variants where they matter.
Thompson's 1928 English sometimes spells *Gilgamish*; quoted passages keep his forms.

**A word on reverence and honesty.** The *Epic of Gilgamesh* is one of the foundation stones of
world literature — older than Homer by more than a thousand years — and was school-text and
royal library for a thousand years of Mesopotamian scribes. This book is written by an outsider who
loves the text and claims no authority over it — *a guest at someone else's fire.* It keeps three
things visibly separate: **(a)** what the poem says and what the tradition holds; **(b)** matters of
scholarship, where reasonable people differ; and **(c)** the author's own plain-language gloss
(always marked **plainly:**).

**The passages.** Quotations are from R. Campbell Thompson's *The Epic of Gilgamish* (1928), a
foundational English rendering of the Standard Babylonian twelve-tablet poem, always attributed.
Where Thompson's diction is too thick to learn from, a plainer modern line sits beside it and is
marked as the author's. For the living scholarly standard, see *Going*.
