# The Note — Gilgamesh in a Hand

Twelve tablets is a great deal to carry — and the poem knows it; that is why it ends not with immortality but with brick: a king who failed to keep youth forever, standing a friend at the walls he built and saying *look*. So before we close, let me do the thing this companion exists for — put the whole epic into a single hand. Not every variant; not the fine distinctions of scholarship; just *what the poem is about*, said plainly enough to carry out the door.

Here is the *Epic of Gilgamesh*, as honestly as one grateful guest can hand it on.

**It begins with walls — and a tyrant.** The poem praises the city Gilgamesh built, then shows why heaven intervenes. Power without a limit is a civic emergency. Enkidu is the gods' answer: a wild equal.

**Friendship civilises both men.** Enkidu loses the animals and gains bread, beer, and a brother. Gilgamesh meets the first person who can stop him. Their bond is the poem's engine until death cuts it.

**Adventure has a bill.** Cedar Forest, Humbaba, Ishtar's offer refused, the Bull of Heaven slain — glory that looks like triumph is already priced. Enkidu dies for what they did together.

**Grief will not stay in the house.** Gilgamesh laments like a lioness and a widow. He will not bury until the worm appears. Then he runs from death itself.

**Immortality is someone else's story.** Utnapishtim survived the Flood; Gilgamesh cannot even stay awake. The plant of youth is real enough to be stolen by a serpent. Failure is the point.

**What remains is work you can touch.** Climb the walls of Uruk. Inspect the brickwork. The poem's last wisdom is not escape from death but a city that outlasts a king.

**The gods are part of the weather.** They assemble, they flood the world when humans are too loud, they argue over survivors. You can read them as forces, as poetry, as the old religion the scribes believed. The mortals stay real underneath.

**Tablet XII is a hard appendix.** Enkidu in the underworld; the dead ranked by how they were mourned. Scholars dispute whether it belongs. The guest tells you what it says and does not pretend the family agrees.

---

That is Gilgamesh, in a hand.

If you take only one thing: **you cannot keep your life forever, but you can build something worth climbing, and you can love someone enough that their death teaches you what you are.** The poem opens with walls and closes with walls. Between them: a friend, a flood, a serpent, and a king who came home empty-handed and still had a city to show.

Now go read the old voice — or stand where Uruk's brick still rises from the plain, if you can, and put your hand on a wall.
