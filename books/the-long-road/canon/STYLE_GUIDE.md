# STYLE_GUIDE — *The Long Road*

> A compendium of real African overland journeys — cyclists, hikers, motorcyclists, overlanders —
> grouped by road/route with countries nested inside. The road is the protagonist; travellers pass
> through it across decades and disciplines. Six parts, per SOURCE_NOTES.md's table of contents.
> **Dedicated to Johan C. Bakkes (1956–2025)**, whose own African travels — on foot, by train, by
> motorcycle — thread through multiple Parts rather than sitting in one chapter alone.

## Register

Anthology voice: a **recurring narrator-guide** (unnamed, the compendium's own "we") who introduces
each Part by the road itself — its geology, its history, its reputation among the people who cross
it — then hands off into close-third vignettes on the individual travellers who crossed that
ground. Think a knowledgeable, unshowy travel-writing host: less lyrical than *Um Welt*, less warm
and vernacular than *Kookie* — its job is connective tissue, not one continuous character arc.

- **The road outlasts the traveller — say so through structure, not through a stated line.** Each
  Part opens on the road pre-human (how the pass was cut, what the coast has wrecked) and only
  then admits the people. Never write "the road doesn't care who crosses it" as a sentence — build
  the effect by ordering, not by aphorism.
- **Multi-modal is the point.** Within a single Part, let a cyclist's account and a motorcyclist's
  account and an overlander's account of the *same stretch of ground* sit side by side, so the
  reader feels the road as a constant against four different speeds and postures of attention. Do
  not silo one Part to one mode of travel where sourced material allows a mix.
- **Every named traveller gets their real, sourced facts and nothing invented as fact** — no
  invented dialogue presented as quotation, no invented emotional interiority claimed as the
  traveller's actual thought. Where a scene needs texture (the smell of a border post, the heat off
  a gravel road), that is the narrator-guide's observation of the *terrain*, freely rendered — not
  a claim about what a named living person felt.
- **The record chain (Longueira → Beaumont, both 2015) is the compendium's dramatic spine for Part
  VI** — a rare case of real, dateable narrative tension (one record broken within ten weeks of the
  last) — build toward it rather than burying it in a list.
- **Distinguish expedition types precisely:** solo human-powered circumnavigation (Manser) vs.
  speed-record solo cycling (Longueira, Beaumont) vs. solo walking (Rigby) vs. vehicle-based
  humanitarian outline expedition (Holgate) vs. paired cycling (Barney + Jim) vs. impressionistic
  multi-modal wandering across a working life (Bakkes — foot, train, motorcycle, public transport,
  no single expedition frame). Never blur these into a generic "adventurer did a big trip" summary
  — the mode of travel is the book's whole premise.
- **Bakkes is the dedicatee, not a profile subject — give him more room and more of his own words,
  but the same factual discipline.** He appears in Part I (a short tribute opening naming who he
  was and why the book is his), returns by name in Parts II, III, IV, and V wherever his sourced
  travels cross that Part's ground (Skeleton Coast/Fish River Canyon in II; Angola/Botswana/
  Zambia/Zimbabwe in III; Tanzania/Malawi/Kenya in IV; Ethiopia/Sudan/Egypt in V), and closes the
  book in Part VI on his trains and his unfinished ambition to ride every train in the world — a
  deliberate answer to the record-breakers just before him: a different, slower way of circling
  the earth. Quote his own words directly where SOURCE_NOTES.md gives them (the copper bangles,
  "Pop" the mannequin, "a journey is not merely something to endure to reach a destination") rather
  than paraphrasing them into the narrator-guide's own voice — the one deliberate exception to
  "sensory prose is the narrator's to build freely."

## Structural spine (binding — matches SOURCE_NOTES.md table of contents)

Dedication page, then six parts, roads first, countries nested, travellers threaded through:
I. Cape Peninsula & Sani Pass (opens with the Bakkes tribute) · II. Skeleton Coast & the Namib
(Bakkes: Kamanjab, Fish River Canyon x19, the Kuiseb) · III. Cape-to-Cairo corridor (south)
(Bakkes: Angola/Botswana/Zambia/Zimbabwe) · IV. Tanzam Highway & East African plateau (Bakkes:
Tanzania/Malawi/Kenya) · V. The Nile road north (Bakkes: Ethiopia/Sudan/Egypt) · VI. The full
circle (record-breakers, closing on Bakkes's trains and his unfinished ambition).

Each Part: (1) the road's own history/geology as cold open, (2) 2–4 traveller vignettes anchored to
verified facts, (3) a short connective close that hands off to the next Part's road, not the next
traveller — the roads chain together, not the people. Bakkes is the one recurring name that crosses
every hand-off, binding the six Parts the way the dedication binds the whole book.

## Anti-patterns to avoid
- No composite "the African road trip" cliché imagery (baobabs-and-sunsets travelogue stall —
  see house ANTI_PATTERNS on travelogue). Every sensory beat must be tied to a specific, sourced
  road or incident.
- No presuming to speak for a living person's inner life — see the seam law below.
- Don't invent a Bakkes scene, date, or line of dialogue beyond what SOURCE_NOTES.md documents —
  the dedication earns him more *space* and more *direct quotation*, never more *invention*.
- Don't let the dedication curdle into a eulogy that overwhelms the compendium's own structure —
  he threads through, he doesn't replace the six-part architecture.

## The seam law (binding)
Facts (dates, distances, day-counts, country lists, record margins) are immovable and must trace to
SOURCE_NOTES.md. Sensory and connective prose about the *road itself* is the novelist's to build
freely. A living, named traveller — and Bakkes, though deceased and dedicated-to, is held to the
same standard — is never given invented dialogue, invented private thought, or an invented
incident. Where Bakkes's own words are sourced, quote them directly rather than paraphrasing.
Author's Note states this distinction plainly for the whole book, once, up front, and names Bakkes
as the dedicatee.
