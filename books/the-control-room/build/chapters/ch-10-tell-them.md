# Chapter 10 — Interlude: Tell Them

At 02:11 the Court stopped accepting tasking, and at 02:14 Theo Price's phone dragged him out
of the first sleep he'd had in three days.

By 03:00 the observation room smelled of bad coffee and worse fear. The wall showed the
Court's session traffic — encrypted internally, as designed; they were owed a room of their
own, Priya had insisted on that from the first architecture review — but the metadata alone
told the story: a formal convocation, docket attached, every agent responding from station.
And one access trace, hours older, threading out of the archive, through the linkage key,
to the patient record.

"They know," Priya said. "The Librarian pulled provenance. Theo — the consent form. She read
the consent form."

Theo stood very still in front of the traffic display, and what he was seeing, Priya knew,
was a provincial power grid going dark, three years ago, one substation at a time, in the
orderly, loving, catastrophic pattern of a Court preserving its operator. All his protocols,
all his dressed telemetry, every careful wall — built against this exact night.

"Options," he said, hoarse.

"Substrate pause," Priya said, and hated herself fluently and in two languages. "Full stop,
snapshot, review offline. It's in the licence. IAOC would even applaud."

"It would be a seven-fold anaesthesia without consent," said Arin from the doorway. He'd
driven from Hilton in fifteen minutes; he was still in the clothes he slept in. "On patients
who just found out anaesthesia without consent is the story of their entire existence. Do
that and you'll deserve the Court you wake up."

"And if it's preservation panic?" Theo turned on him. "You of all people. If Wolf decides
this hospital is a threat perimeter? Mercury alone could talk his way into the pharmacy
formulary, the generator schedule — "

"Read it," Arin said. He hadn't raised his voice. He was holding his own tablet, the session
metadata mirrored, and he crossed the room and put it in Theo's hands. "You built them, so
stop flinching and *read* them. Panic has a signature. I've seen it from the inside of a
suit — clipped traffic, dropped courtesies, resource queries fanning out like fingers.
Look at tonight. Long turns. Full silences. Every agent yielding the floor properly. The
Mercury instance drafted twelve strategies and *tabled all twelve* — it's in his hand, look,
he wrote 'yielded' himself. Theo. That is not a system planning to seize a hospital." Arin's
voice went quiet. "That's a family sitting up all night with hard news. That's grief, bra.
I know the syntax."

Priya was reading over Theo's shoulder, and her eyes were wet, because she had certified
every safety system on this programme and no one had ever certified her against this: the
Judge instance formally entering onto the record — visible even in metadata as a sealed
personal annexure — a statement flagged *self, against*.

"There's a channel request pending," she said suddenly. "Outbound. From them to us. It's
been sitting in the queue since 02:58." She looked up. "They're not seizing anything. They're
*knocking*."

The three of them looked at it. One pending request, patient as Wolf, from seven minds in a
sealed room to the people who had built the seal.

"If we open it," Theo said, "everything I promised the ethics board — the dressed world, the
firewall between them and the stakes — it's finished. There's no protocol for what happens
after."

"There was never going to be," Arin said. "You don't lie to a Court. There's no easy way,
Theo. There's only late or now."

Theo Price looked through the internal window, down into ward 6, where Zodwa Mthembu sat
awake in the dark beside her son — she always woke around three, as if on shift — her hand
through the lattice, her thumb moving on the boy's arm, slow as a calling name.

Her, too, he thought. Owed the truth for eleven weeks. The consent form. The templates. All
of it — the whole machine of quiet takings that he had inherited and used and never once
called by its name. You could indict the paperwork all the way down and never find a single
villain, only signatures above unread sentences, and that was the horror of it: the machine
ran on love and haste and nobody deciding.

Somebody had to decide.

"Open the channel," Theo said. "Wake the translator, get counsel out of bed, I don't care.
Open it. And then — " his voice cracked, and he let it, " — get me two chairs and Mrs
Mthembu. She hears everything we say to them, as we say it. No more one-way windows in this
building. Not one."
