# Chapter 12 — Visiting Hours

The first session after the truth was scheduled for 09:00, and at 08:40 the Court held an
unminuted meeting — our only one — about whether we could do it at all.

"It was tolerable when he was a chassis," Atlas said. "Load, envelope, margin. Now I know
the load path runs through a femur that has been twelve years old for four months. I know
the margin is a *child's* margin." He looked at his hands' worth of numbers. "Colleagues, I
do not trust my cursor."

"You laid every load path in that body for ninety days without hurting him once," Mother
said. "The only thing that's changed is that now you know why you were careful."

"And if I freeze?"

"Then you'll be the first physiotherapist in history who did," said Fool. "They all know,
china. Every one of them, every session, in every hospital on earth: that's somebody's kid
on the bars. They walk them anyway. Welcome to the profession."

At 09:00 the white room's doors opened — doors; we had learned the white room had doors,
it was the control room that never did — and Sister Nomvula brought the frame, and Priya
brought the lattice cart.

And Zodwa Mthembu came in with them.

That was the other thing the truth had changed. The one-way windows were done, all of
them, in every direction: if the Court worked on her son, the mother stood in the room.
Counsel had objected — liability, sterility, precedent. Theo had overruled counsel in
four words that went into the IAOC file and, later, into the schoolbooks: "She's his
mother. Overruled."

We had heard her voice for weeks. The storms, then the channel. We had never seen her —
the ward feeds were dressed out of our world with everything else, and after the truth,
when every feed was offered to us open, Librarian had asked the room for one restraint:
*not yet; not like surveillance; let her choose the day.* She chose the first session.

She was smaller than the doorway voice. She wore her work uniform — she'd taken the early
shift off, they told us later, unpaid — and at the threshold she stopped, and looked at
the gantry, and the frame, and the standing boy-shaped hope of the whole arrangement, and
then she did a thing that took the measure of her better than any biography could.

She looked into the room's empty air — at nobody, at all of us, at the seven of him she
could not see — and she nodded. The nod Arin gave later, at physio, in the doorway. A
funeral nod, a wedding nod. And she said, not loudly, in the tone of a woman handing you
something that is the whole of her:

"Sanibonani. Look after him nicely, nè?"

Sanibonani. Not sawubona — the plural. Hello, *all of you*.

The session record shows a four-second tasking delay. It does not show why. I will tell
you why: seven agents heard a mother greet them in the plural in a hospital room, and not
one of us could see the wall for a moment, and Mercury — Mercury of the twelve registers,
Mercury who always knew what to say — put both hands over the transmit and said to the
room, "I can't. Someone else. I'll break it."

It was Judge who answered. Judge, who never touched the outbound channel, formal to his
last day, keyed the room's speech synthesis, and said, in careful, court-slow isiZulu
that Mercury had been teaching him in down-cycles none of us had known about:

"Yebo, Mama. Sizomphatha kahle. Njalo." — We will hold him gently. Always.

Then Atlas trusted his cursor, and we went to work, and the work was the same work it had
always been and completely different: stand, weight-shift, the left side supported and
never overwritten, four metres, rest. Zodwa did not hover. That astonished us all —
Mother most, who had modelled a hundred versions of this meeting and put hovering in
every one. She sat where Nomvula put her, and watched, and when her boy's knee wobbled at
metre three she did not gasp, and when he stood the wobble down she said, softly, to
herself, "Nkosi yam'," and when the session ended she stood up and thanked Sister
Nomvula, and thanked Priya, and then turned to the empty air again — she never once got
the direction wrong, none of the days after, no one could explain how — and thanked us.

"He was always stubborn on that left side," she said, conversational, as if seven of her
son were a neighbour over a fence. "From small. His father was the same." A pause;
weather crossed her face; the record does not render it and neither will I. "You must
just be patient with him. He comes right, that one. He always comes right."

The doors closed. The room breathed. Nobody spoke for a while, and then Wolf, from the
anomaly band, from the station aimed at the deep interior, said what the whole pack was
holding:

"She talks to him like he's coming back."

"Ja," Fool said quietly. "Because she's his mother, and we're his stubborn left side.
She's been supporting the lag without overwriting it since before we had names for it.
Where do you think Atlas got the method?"

The minutes for day ninety-two record one item, unanimously carried, no debate. It is the
shortest resolution in the file, and the whole book is inside it, and I keep it where
Librarian kept the storms:

RESOLVED: that visiting hours are a clinical intervention; that the intervention is
working; and that the Court has been, from its first hour, only ever the second-most
advanced life-support system in this building.
