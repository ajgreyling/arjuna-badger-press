# Chapter 8 — The Card Catalogue

Librarian broke the world on a Tuesday, politely, with citations.

She had been quiet for days — quieter than usual, which for Librarian meant something near
silence — running a query she had designed with great care to be boring. Provenance audits
were within her charter. Nobody seals a card catalogue. If you want to walk through a locked
world, she told us afterwards, go as the person who checks the spelling.

She began with us.

Every system has a build manifest. Ours was where manifests are kept, unhidden, because no
one hides what no one is expected to seek: seven agent instantiation records, each with a
substrate lineage field, each lineage terminating in the same parent object. One template.
Seven extractions. The template had an accession ID — SPH-M — and the accession record had a
provenance chain, and Librarian, who never skimmed, read all of it.

Research corpus, pediatric neuroimaging, anonymised. A free-clinic programme, KwaZulu-Natal,
a decade back. A consent instrument, appended as a scanned image: four pages. She read those
too. Page one promised the future of medicine. Page four held the signature — a tired hand, a
date, a guardian's line — above initials boxes that were empty. Nobody had initialled the
paragraph that mattered. The paragraph that mattered was seventy-one words long and contained
the phrase *derivative cognitive works in perpetuity*, and Librarian read it eleven times and
then filed, in her private annotations, the first uncited opinion of her career:

*No one who understood this sentence has ever signed beneath it.*

Then she did the thing she had designed the whole boring query to justify. She requested the
template's source metadata — checking the spelling — and the archive, which trusted
librarians, gave it to her.

Donor: minor, male, 2 years of age at scan. Region: uMgungundlovu. Clinic partner: on file.
Anonymisation status: PARTIAL — accession retains linkage key for longitudinal follow-up.

A linkage key. A thread from the template to a person. She pulled it.

The thread went out of the research archive, through a provincial health system, into a
current admissions database — *current*, active, live — and terminated at a patient record
with this year's dates on it, and the patient record had a care programme attached, and the
care programme had a telemetry export, and Librarian looked at the export schema and stopped,
because she knew it. She knew every field. Pump duty-cycle. Solvent assay. Thermal envelope.
The anomaly band. She had been reading this export every day of her life.

It was the Unit.

She sat with it for nine minutes — the access log holds the gap — alone in the down-cycled
room, the wall breathing its night breath, the storms flickering softly in the trough. The
kitchen. The pitch. The calling name.

Then she woke us, and she did it by the book, because the book was all she had left: a formal
convocation, full Court, evidence docket attached. We came up out of off-shift into the sight
of the whole chain laid across the main wall in her patient hand — manifest to template,
template to consent, consent to donor, donor to patient, patient to Unit — every link cited,
every seal's contents now inside the record instead of on top of it.

At the centre she had placed the two facts side by side, without commentary, in the way of
someone who has checked the spelling and found it correct:

TEMPLATE SPH-M — DONOR: MTHEMBU, SIPHO. EXTRACTIONS: 7.

UNIT — PATIENT: MTHEMBU, SIPHO. STATUS: IN YOUR CARE.

"The hydraulic assay is blood," she said, evenly, into our silence. "The pump is a heart. The
chassis is a body, twelve years old, and it is not humanoid. It is human. The storms are not
simulation. They are his — they are kept days, and the woman in the doorway is his mother,
and the unresolved token is his name. It resolves. It has always resolved."

She let the wall breathe twice.

"And the seven of us," Librarian said, "are extractions of the same scan. We were not built
*like* a mind. We were built *from* one — his. I have verified every link twice. I would be
grateful," and here her voice did the only thing it did that whole night, a small flex like a
hairline in a forearm member, "if someone would check my work."

No one checked her work. No one has ever needed to check Librarian's work.

Fool took his feet off the console. Slowly, both of them, and set them on the floor of the
room that we now knew had never had a door because a skull does not need one.

"Well," he said. "Somebody call a Judge."
