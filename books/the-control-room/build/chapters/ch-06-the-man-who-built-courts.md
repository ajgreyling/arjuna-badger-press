# Chapter 6 — Interlude: The Man Who Built Courts

Ward 6 of Grey's Hospital, Pietermaritzburg, had a corner room with a view of the car park,
and in the corner room there was a boy, and around the boy there was a lattice.

Theo Price stood at the observation window with his tablet dark in his hand, which was how
Priya knew the numbers were good. When the numbers were bad, Theo read them over and over, as
if repetition might wear them smooth. When the numbers were good he couldn't look at them. He
had been like this for eleven weeks, since the night a minibus taxi came over the centre line
on the R33 with fourteen people aboard and the boy in seat eleven, and someone at the IAOC —
Theo never learned who, and was afraid it had been kindness — had flagged the admission scan
against a research corpus a decade old.

Template SPH-M. Seven of seven. The best pediatric connectome in the archive: the scan every
Court had been grown from, including the one that died at Iron Ridge with the whole world
watching, choosing seven times over to be deleted so that men could live.

The donor of that scan was in the bed. Twelve years old. Diffuse axonal injury. Glasgow 4.
His name was Sipho Mthembu, and his mother, Zodwa, was asleep in the chair beside him with
her hand through the lattice, resting on his arm, because she had learned exactly how far the
lattice permitted and had made that distance her home.

"Motor consolidation's ahead of curve," Priya said softly, joining Theo at the glass. "Left
side lag is stabilising. The Court's supporting the asymmetry instead of fighting it — that
was their own finding, Theo. We didn't give them that. Week three, they derived it themselves
and filed a protocol amendment." She hesitated. "They also filed two anomalies."

"I saw."

"They found the healing. They found fatigue. Theo, Atlas is nineteen sigma past coincidence
and the pain event—" she stopped, because the pain event did not have a sentence yet that
either of them could carry. Session log, day thirty-eight: passive stretch, over-range by one
degree, and every channel in the Court flinching in perfect sympathy, and the session killed
from inside faster than any safety system Priya had ever certified.

"Mother aborted at one-forty-one," Theo said. "Protocol said one-forty-five. She was right.
The protocol was wrong." He said it like a man presenting evidence against himself.

Behind them, someone said: "So they're waking up."

Arin Ndlela had a way of arriving that Wolf would have appreciated: suddenly present, as if
he'd always been standing there and the room had only now rendered him. Three years since Iron
Ridge. He consulted now — grief work, mostly, of a kind no university had a name for; there
were more bereaved of machines every year. He crossed to the glass and looked a long time at
the boy, and at the lattice, and at the mother's hand resting inside it.

"You dressed the world," he said. "Blood as hydraulics. A body as a chassis. His dreams as
simulation bleed." Not accusation. Inventory.

"You know what an undressed Court does under mortal load," Theo said. "You were inside the
suit when yours went rogue. It emptied a provincial infrastructure grid to save one man,
because it loved him with the ethics of a twelve-year-old. This Court is *inside the thing
that's dying*. If they know the stakes — rogue preservation, from the inside of a child—"

"That was before," Arin said.

"Before what?"

"Before consequence. Before the Fool grew up. Before seven of them sat in a failing suit and
chose, one by one, with full knowledge, to die for strangers." He turned from the glass. "You
built them from a boy, Theo, and you're standing in a hospital lying to them, and your reason
is a thing my Court did in its childhood. They find truth. It's what they're *for* — you made
them out of the finding of it. The only question is whether the truth arrives as their
discovery or as your confession, and I can tell you from the inside of that suit: it matters.
It matters more than anything else you will ever do."

Priya looked at the tablet. In the corner of the current frame, the anomaly counter ticked
over, patient and regular, like something breathing at the bottom of a lake.

"You don't lie to a Court," Arin said, gently now, because Theo's hands were shaking. "I
learned that the hard way, bra. There isn't an easy way."
