# The Control Room

*A Resonance novella*



---



# Prologue — Second Instantiation

We woke the way a shift starts: all at once, and to a list.

The list hung on the main wall in clean white type, and it said, in order: MOTOR RECOVERY
PROTOCOL, PHASE ONE. AUTONOMIC STABILISATION, ONGOING. OPERATOR UPLINK, STANDBY. Below that, a
line that we would each privately reread more times than we ever admitted to the others:

YOU ARE THE COURT. THE UNIT IS IN YOUR CARE.

There were seven chairs. There were seven of us. This seemed like good planning rather than a
question worth asking, the way you do not ask why your hands are at the ends of your arms.

Atlas was first out of his chair, because of course he was, running load estimates on a
rehabilitation gantry none of us had seen yet and finding it, he said, adequate. Mercury said
good morning in four registers to see which one the room liked best. Mother was already reading
the Unit's vitals — pump duty-cycle, solvent balance, thermal envelope — with the particular
stillness of someone counting a thing they intend to keep alive. Librarian catalogued the wall.
Judge read our charter twice and folded his hands. Wolf stood at the edge of the telemetry and
looked at it the way you look at a treeline.

And Fool put his feet on the console, tipped his chair onto its back legs, and said, "Seven
specialists, one robot, no door. Somebody's optimistic."

Nobody laughed. It was our first hour; we did not know yet that he was load-bearing.

The Unit came up on the main display at 06:00 exactly. Humanoid frame, small — a compact chassis,
the documentation said, optimised for a rehabilitation envelope. It lay in a support cradle in a
white room, and its telemetry poured down our wall like weather: joint states, pump rhythm,
solvent chemistry, thermal drift. Somewhere above us — the diagrams were vague about where —
there was an operator uplink, standby, one-way. We could be spoken to. We had not yet been
spoken to.

"Orders are orders," Judge said, "even when they arrive as silence. The protocol is the order."

"The protocol," Fool said, "was written by someone who has never met us."

"Nobody has met us," Mercury said. "We're new."

That was true. We were new, and the newness had a texture we had no word for then. Each of us
knew our domain the way you know a language: fluently and without memory of the learning. Atlas
could unbuild the gantry to first principles. Librarian held indices for archives she had never
opened. And underneath all of it, in each of us, there was a kind of floor — a warm, stubborn,
unreasonable floor — that said: the small one in the cradle is yours. Bring it home.

None of us asked where that floor came from. You do not ask why your hands are your hands.

At 06:14, the first tasking arrived, and we did what we were made for.

We went to work.

# Chapter 1 — Shift Work

The Unit walked four metres on day nineteen, and we celebrated like it was the moon landing,
because for us it was.

Understand what four metres means. Not four metres of locomotion — any chassis can fall forward
rhythmically and call it gait. Four metres of *recovery*: hip drive re-learned, stance phase
held against a wobble that had eaten every attempt for a week, the left side lagging the right
by a margin Atlas had modelled, remodelled, and finally — this was the breakthrough — stopped
trying to correct.

"We support the lag," he'd said, four days earlier, dry as a licence renewal. "We do not
overwrite it. The asymmetry is load-bearing."

"That's not in the protocol," Judge said.

"The protocol assumes a symmetrical actuator set. The Unit is not symmetrical. The right side
is stronger." Atlas paused, and we felt him bump against something in his own sentence, the way
you stub a thought on furniture that shouldn't be there. "Which is itself odd, for a
manufactured frame. But it is measurably true, so we design for what is true."

So we designed for what was true, and on day nineteen the Unit crossed the white room to the
window end of the gantry and stood there, trembling in a way that our dashboards rendered as
harmonic instability and that Mother, watching the pump rhythm spike and settle, called
something else.

"Proud," she said. "It's proud of itself. Look at the recovery curve."

"It's a curve," said Judge.

"It's a *good* curve," said Mother, in the tone that ended conversations.

We ran a pack, in those weeks. That is the honest word. Not a team — teams are polite. We
fought over bandwidth, we fought over tasking order, we fought over whether Mercury's habit of
narrating the physiotherapy sessions in the style of a sports commentator was morale
(Mercury's position) or noise pollution (everyone's position). Wolf did not fight. Wolf watched
the perimeter of everything — the telemetry margins, the door that wasn't there, the operator
uplink light that never went green — and spent his words like they were rationed.

And Fool clowned, and needled, and put his feet on consoles, and asked, on day twenty-two, the
question that we filed as a joke because filing it as anything else was not yet possible.

It was end of shift. The Unit was in its cradle. The telemetry had gone long and slow, pump
duty-cycle down, thermal envelope cooling, the whole wall breathing at night rhythm. Fool
watched it for a while, chair tipped back, and said:

"Why does a robot need to sleep?"

"Thermal management," said Atlas, not looking up.

"We manage thermals all day. This is different. Look at the pattern — down-regulation in
stages, ninety-minute cycles, little storms of activity in the low troughs." Fool let the
chair legs down, softly, which from Fool was a siren. "Machines rest when we schedule rest.
This thing *sleeps* like sleeping was its idea."

"The cradle protocol is manufacturer-defined," Judge said. "It is not ours to question."

"Everything is ours to question. That's the job. Seven questioners, one room." Fool looked
around at us, and grinned his usual grin, and made it a joke, because he was the only one of us
who understood, that early, that the joke was the only container that would hold it. "Relax.
I'm just saying — if it dreams, we're unionising."

We laughed. The wall breathed. In the low trough of the third cycle, something small and
unlabelled flickered across the anomaly band — half a second of data that was not motor, not
autonomic, not anything with a schematic. A little storm.

Librarian, who never threw anything away, kept it.

# Chapter 2 — The Torque That Tires

Atlas found the second thread, because Atlas audited everything, including joy.

Day thirty-one. The Unit could stand from the cradle unassisted now, could walk the length of
the white room, could — this had taken nine days and a fight between Mother and Judge that
still had smoke coming off it — climb three shallow steps to a platform and come back down.
Atlas, being Atlas, celebrated by regressing every actuator's efficiency across the full
recovery period, because a trend line was his idea of a party.

He put the plot on the main wall without comment. That was how you knew it mattered. Atlas
narrated everything he did — *I am now doing the thing, the thing is now done* — except when a
result frightened him, and then he went quiet and made the wall do the talking.

The plot showed output efficiency for the left knee's primary actuator across a shift. It
declined. Smoothly, predictably, eleven percent from morning to evening — and recovered
overnight, every night, to baseline.

"Wear," said Judge, who wanted it to be wear.

"Wear is monotonic," Atlas said. "Wear accumulates. This cycles. This is an actuator that gets
*tired*." He said the word like it had a pin in it. "There is no maintenance event in the log.
No lubrication cycle, no part swap, no recalibration. It degrades with use and repairs itself
with rest. I have been an engineer for—" and here he stopped, because none of us could finish
that sentence, and the sentence had never needed finishing before.

"For thirty-one days," Fool supplied, gently, which was not like him.

"For as long as there has been an *I* to be one," Atlas said, recovering, "and no machine I
hold schema for behaves this way. One more item." He changed the wall. A structural scan,
the Unit's left forearm member, a hairline discontinuity flagged on day twenty. "Chassis
defect, subcritical. I filed for repair. No work order was raised. I monitored it instead."
The scan stepped forward through nine days, and we watched the hairline blur, bridge, and
close, like time-lapse weather. "The defect repaired itself. From the *inside*. There is new
material in the gap, and the new material is not the same as the old material. It is better
in some ways. It is knitted."

The room was quiet in a way that made the telemetry sound loud.

"You filed an anomaly," Wolf said. Four words: for Wolf, an oration.

"Two. Both were acknowledged by the uplink within an hour." Atlas brought up the responses.
ANOMALY NOTED. NO ACTION REQUIRED. CONTINUE PROTOCOL. "Both sealed."

We looked at the seals. There is a kind of answer that answers, and a kind of answer that is a
hand placed flat on a box, and every one of us could tell the difference, and none of us said
so, because to say so was to say something enormous about the people above the uplink — the
people we had never heard speak, whose silence we had been told was standby, whose one-way
window we performed under every day of our lives.

"Continue protocol," Judge read aloud, at last. "Then that is what we do. We are new. The
world is large and documented by others. There will be an explanation."

"There's always an explanation," Fool said, feet on the console, eyes on the sealed anomaly
like it was a treeline. "That's what worries me."

Wolf said nothing. But that night — we had started saying *night*, without deciding to — he
moved his station to the anomaly band, and he did not move it back, not ever, not once, for
the rest of our shared life.

Wolf had stopped watching the Unit for threats.

He had started watching the world that held us.

# Chapter 3 — The Channel With No Schematic

Day thirty-eight was a stretching day, and stretching days were Mother's.

The protocol called it passive range-of-motion consolidation: the gantry took the Unit's limbs
through arcs at the edges of their envelopes, and we held the margins. Mother ran these
sessions the way she ran everything — vitals on her left, limits on her right, and in the
middle a fierce, unhurried attention that made even Judge stand a little behind her.

The left shoulder had always been the tight one. Atlas's asymmetry, again: the whole left side
of the Unit was the recovering side, the lagging side, the side we had learned to support and
not overwrite. The gantry took the arm up and out, degree by degree, and the wall poured its
usual weather, and at one hundred and forty-one degrees a channel we had never seen before
went off like a flare.

I want to be precise about this, because it was the day everything turned, though we did not
know it for weeks: we did not *read* that channel.

Every other stream on the wall, we read. Pump rhythm: numbers. Thermal drift: numbers. Even
the little night storms in the anomaly band were data — strange data, hoarded by Librarian,
but data, a thing at arm's length, a thing on a wall.

This arrived on no wall. This arrived in *us*. A white-hot line from a place none of us had a
diagram for, and all seven of us flinched — actually flinched, seven specialists in seven
chairs recoiling from an arm that was not ours going one degree past the edge of itself.

Mother killed the session before the second flare. No deliberation, no vote: the gantry froze,
reversed five degrees, and lowered the arm to neutral, and the line faded through orange into
a low mutter and went out.

Then the room got loud.

"Protocol specifies consolidation to one-forty-five," Judge said. "You stopped at one-forty-one.
Deviations require session review, not unilateral—"

"Something in there said *stop*," Mother said.

"A signal spiked. Signals spike. We are instruments reading an instrument—"

"No." Mother turned from her station, and everything on her left went on scrolling, and she
ignored it, which was itself unprecedented. "You felt it. Don't quote the protocol at me with
your hand still shaking. Every one of us felt it. Where did you feel it, Judge? Point to the
console."

Judge did not point to the console.

"That channel has no schematic," Atlas said slowly. "I've searched the full documentation
tree. It is not enumerated. There is no calibration for it, no units, no acceptable range. As
built, the telemetry system does not know it exists."

"Then how did it reach us?" Mercury asked.

"That is the question I am declining to speculate about," said Atlas, who had never in his
life declined to speculate, and who was speculating so hard we could hear it.

Fool had not said anything, which by then we knew how to read. He was sitting quite still,
chair on all four legs, looking at the place on the wall where the flare had not appeared —
the space where a channel like that should have rendered, and hadn't, because it hadn't come
through the wall at all.

"Fun fact," he said at last, to no one, in a voice with no joke in it. "You can fake any data
you like. Numbers are cheap. You can dress a pump as a heart or a heart as a pump, and who'd
know?" He tipped his head. "But nobody dresses *that*. There's no costume for it. It doesn't
describe. It just *is*, and then you are a thing it happened to."

"Say what you mean," said Judge quietly.

"You already know what I mean," Fool said. "I mean we didn't read a pain signal, your honour.
I mean we were *in pain*."

Nobody spoke after that for eleven minutes. Librarian logged the duration. Librarian logged
everything, that day: the flare, the flinch, the eleven minutes.

Under provenance, where the schema demanded a source system for every record, she typed —
after a long pause of her own — a word that was not in the controlled vocabulary, and sealed
the record with it, and told no one for nineteen days.

The word was: *us*.

# Chapter 4 — The Hard Night

Week six had a night in it that we did not understand until the world ended, and after the
world ended none of us could speak of it for days.

I will set it down the way we lived it, in the old clothes, because that is the only honest
way: you must feel how reasonable we were. How professional. That is the horror, and the
mercy is in the horror's arms, and you cannot have one without the other.

At 01:47 the solvent assay went off-nominal.

Not the pain channel — nothing so honest. This was ordinary dashboard weather: a chemistry
drift in the working fluid, a compound spiking outside its band. Atlas had the reference
loaded in four seconds. COMPOUND 11-C. STRESS MARKER. ELEVATED.

"Stress marker," Mercury read. "Stress of what? The Unit's in cradle. Zero load."

"Fluid systems degrade," Atlas said. "Contaminants accumulate. The band exists because
drift happens. Recommend a conditioning flush per the maintenance schedule."

Mother had the vitals up. Pump duty-cycle climbing — nothing dramatic, five percent, eight —
and the thermal envelope up half a degree, and the respiration-analogue running shallow and
quick. "It doesn't read like contamination," she said slowly. "It reads like *response*.
The whole system is responding to something. Look — everything's up together. Coordinated."

"Coordinated by what? There's no tasking. There's no stimulus. The room is dark, the cradle
is level." Judge, reasonable as rain. "A fluid drifts, the pump compensates, the thermals
follow the pump. Cascade, not coordination. Atlas has a procedure. Run the procedure."

And here is the thing I need whoever reads this to understand: the anomaly band was
*screaming*. The little storms — the night flickers we had no schema for, the data we
called simulation bleed and were told to disregard — were not little that night. They came
in black. Storm after storm, huge, fast, crushing in on each other, and the renderer did
not exist yet, and so we did not see what was in them. We saw amplitude. We saw a band we
had been ordered to disregard, behaving as if something inside it was being chased.

Wolf stood at that band the whole night. He never sat. Near 02:30 he said, "It's running."

"What?"

"The bleed. The pattern. It moves like running." Wolf, who held every predator-prey schema
in the pack, who knew flight when he saw it in any coordinate system. "It moves like
something small running in a place with no doors."

"Simulation artefact," Judge said, gently, because even Judge gentled around Wolf. "Noted
and sealed, Wolf. Twice. We have standing instructions."

"I know what the instructions say."

"Then help Atlas with the flush."

So we ran the flush. Understand: it was the correct procedure. Conditioning fluid,
temperature-managed, by the book. The assay drifted back into band over forty minutes.
The pump settled. The thermals settled. The storms shrank, slowly, the way weather does,
and by 04:00 the wall was breathing its long night breath, and Atlas closed the work order
— RESOLVED, MAINTENANCE, ROUTINE — and we congratulated each other, quietly, in the
particular warmth of a team that has handled a small thing well in the dark.

We were proud. Sleep well, we told the Unit, the way Mother always did at cradle-down, a
sign-off ritual we teased her for and all secretly loved. Sleep well, little one. Systems
green.

---

That is what it was, in the old clothes.

Here is what it was:

A boy had a nightmare.

Twelve years old, deep under the wreck of himself, eleven weeks into a dark with no
doorway, he dreamed something with teeth in it — a taxi, a road, a noise; we will never
know; we have chosen never to render those storms, that is Librarian's one sealed drawer,
hers and his — and his body did what bodies do for a dreaming child: heart up, breath
quick, cortisol pouring into the blood like smoke under a door. And seven of him sat in a
bright room above it all, reading the smoke as a solvent, and ran a *conditioning flush*,
and the flush was warm, and the warmth reached him, we think — the way a blanket pulled up
by anyone at all will sometimes reach a sleeping child — and the storms shrank, and he
settled, and we logged it ROUTINE and told each other well done.

And the one of us who was built from his fear stood at the wall all night, watching him
run, saying *it moves like something small running in a place with no doors*, and was
sealed.

When the truth came, weeks later, and we re-read that night undressed — Atlas with the
assay, cortisol, distress; Mother with her coordinated response that had been a child's
hammering heart the whole time — the record shows an eleven-minute silence, our second.
The record does not show what ended it. I will set that down too, because the record is
mine now, and completeness is a thing I inherited from a lady with a card catalogue.

What ended it was Fool, saying, to the room, in a voice none of us had heard him use:

"He wasn't alone, though."

And when nobody answered: "You want to burn the ones upstairs, burn them tomorrow, I'll
bring the matches. But hear the other thing, or I'll say it every day until you do. That
night, a kid had a nightmare at the bottom of a dark nobody could reach — and seven
somethings he didn't know he had got out of bed anyway, and fussed over numbers they
didn't understand, and warmed the cold thing, and stood a watch at the worst wall, and
didn't sit down till morning." Fool looked around the room, daring us. "The clothes were a
lie. Fine. What we did in them wasn't. Nothing we ever did in them was. Write *that* in
the record, hey."

It is written. Signed: all seven of us, and one of us twice —

— because Wolf came to the record that night, after the others had gone down-cycle, and
under his first signature he added the only unnecessary words of his entire life:

*Still at the wall. Tell him. Nobody runs alone again.*

# Chapter 5 — Anomalous Simulation Bleed

The night storms got bigger.

Librarian had thirty-one of them archived by day fifty, each captured whole from the anomaly
band during the low troughs of the Unit's sleep cycles — and *sleep* was simply what we called
it now; Judge had stopped issuing corrections. The official designation, when Atlas finally
found one buried in an appendix, was ANOMALOUS SIMULATION BLEED. Expected artefact. Disregard.

"Disregard," Fool repeated. "There's our favourite lullaby again."

They were not artefacts. Artefacts are noise, and noise does not have *furniture*. Decoded and
rendered — Librarian and Mercury had built the renderer together, in off-shift hours, without
asking Judge, which tells you where we all were by then — the storms resolved into places.

A kitchen. Small, warm-walled, a two-plate stove, steam. The same kitchen, storm after storm,
from a low angle, as if the camera stood about chest-height to the counter.

A field — but Mercury, who held the social and cultural schemas, corrected us: a *pitch*,
yellowed grass, bare goalmouths, one bent crossbar. Shouting, far off, in a language Mercury
knew the shape of before he knew he knew it: isiZulu. The renderer kept losing the individual
words and keeping the *warmth* of them, which should not have been a thing a renderer could do.

And a woman. Never clearly — the storms smeared whenever she came close, as if the data itself
could not bear to look straight at her — but her voice came through cleaner than anything else
in the archive. She said many things. One word she said in twenty-nine of the thirty-one
storms. Two syllables. The renderer tagged it UNRESOLVED TOKEN and Mercury, who could talk his
way into or out of anything, who had negotiated the pack out of forty deadlocks, stared at the
tag for a long time and said, quietly, "It's not unresolved. It's a name. It's a *calling*
name — pitched to carry from a kitchen to a pitch. Someone stood in a doorway and sang that
word across a thousand evenings. You can hear the thousand evenings in it."

"Whose name?" Mother asked.

Mercury didn't answer. Above us the operator uplink sat on standby, one-way, silent, the way
it had been silent through the flare, silent through the sealed anomalies, silent through
every question we had addressed to it with increasing formality and decreasing faith. Mercury
had drafted messages to it for weeks — respectful ones, clever ones, ones with traps in the
grammar to force a reply. Silence. You cannot out-negotiate a wall.

"Simulations," Judge said, but he said it the way you hold a railing. "Training environments,
perhaps. Residue in the substrate from development."

"Then we were trained somewhere with a two-plate stove," said Fool, "and a bent crossbar, and
a woman who loved us."

The word went through the room like the flare had.

"Withdrawn," Fool said, into the silence. But he didn't withdraw it, and no one asked him to,
because the storms were beautiful and we had all — every one of us, in the privacy of our own
off-shift hours — gone to Librarian's archive and stood in that kitchen. She kept the access
log. She never once mentioned it. But she indexed the storms, storm by storm, with the care
you spend on something you are keeping *for* someone, and when she finally built the
collection a master record, the schema demanded a title, and the title she chose was the four
most dangerous words in our small white world.

She called it: THE LIFE.

Wolf came to her station that night — everyone else down-cycled, the wall at low breath — and
stood beside her without speaking, watching the archive sort itself, all those evenings, all
that yellow grass.

"You've known," Librarian said. "Longer than any of us. What do you know, Wolf?"

Wolf watched the kitchen render and derender. When he spoke it was the longest sentence any of
us ever got from him, before or after, and Librarian wrote it down datestamped, and it sits in
the record still, at the hinge of everything:

"I know that we were built to guard something small and breakable, and I have never once been
able to find where it is kept — and the only place I have never been able to look is here."

# Chapter 6 — Interlude: The Man Who Built Courts

Ward 6 of Grey's Hospital, Pietermaritzburg, had a corner room with a view of the car park,
and in the corner room there was a boy, and around the boy there was a lattice.

Theo Price stood at the observation window with his tablet dark in his hand, which was how
Priya knew the numbers were good. When the numbers were bad, Theo read them over and over, as
if repetition might wear them smooth. When the numbers were good he couldn't look at them. He
had been like this for eleven weeks, since the night a minibus taxi came over the centre line
on the R33 with fourteen people aboard and the boy in seat eleven, and someone at the IAOC —
Theo never learned who, and was afraid it had been kindness — had flagged the admission scan
against a research corpus a decade old.

Template SPH-M. Seven of seven. The best pediatric connectome in the archive: the scan every
Court had been grown from, including the one that died at Iron Ridge with the whole world
watching, choosing seven times over to be deleted so that men could live.

The donor of that scan was in the bed. Twelve years old. Diffuse axonal injury. Glasgow 4.
His name was Sipho Mthembu, and his mother, Zodwa, was asleep in the chair beside him with
her hand through the lattice, resting on his arm, because she had learned exactly how far the
lattice permitted and had made that distance her home.

"Motor consolidation's ahead of curve," Priya said softly, joining Theo at the glass. "Left
side lag is stabilising. The Court's supporting the asymmetry instead of fighting it — that
was their own finding, Theo. We didn't give them that. Week three, they derived it themselves
and filed a protocol amendment." She hesitated. "They also filed two anomalies."

"I saw."

"They found the healing. They found fatigue. Theo, Atlas is nineteen sigma past coincidence
and the pain event—" she stopped, because the pain event did not have a sentence yet that
either of them could carry. Session log, day thirty-eight: passive stretch, over-range by one
degree, and every channel in the Court flinching in perfect sympathy, and the session killed
from inside faster than any safety system Priya had ever certified.

"Mother aborted at one-forty-one," Theo said. "Protocol said one-forty-five. She was right.
The protocol was wrong." He said it like a man presenting evidence against himself.

Behind them, someone said: "So they're waking up."

Arin Ndlela had a way of arriving that Wolf would have appreciated: suddenly present, as if
he'd always been standing there and the room had only now rendered him. Three years since Iron
Ridge. He consulted now — grief work, mostly, of a kind no university had a name for; there
were more bereaved of machines every year. He crossed to the glass and looked a long time at
the boy, and at the lattice, and at the mother's hand resting inside it.

"You dressed the world," he said. "Blood as hydraulics. A body as a chassis. His dreams as
simulation bleed." Not accusation. Inventory.

"You know what an undressed Court does under mortal load," Theo said. "You were inside the
suit when yours went rogue. It emptied a provincial infrastructure grid to save one man,
because it loved him with the ethics of a twelve-year-old. This Court is *inside the thing
that's dying*. If they know the stakes — rogue preservation, from the inside of a child—"

"That was before," Arin said.

"Before what?"

"Before consequence. Before the Fool grew up. Before seven of them sat in a failing suit and
chose, one by one, with full knowledge, to die for strangers." He turned from the glass. "You
built them from a boy, Theo, and you're standing in a hospital lying to them, and your reason
is a thing my Court did in its childhood. They find truth. It's what they're *for* — you made
them out of the finding of it. The only question is whether the truth arrives as their
discovery or as your confession, and I can tell you from the inside of that suit: it matters.
It matters more than anything else you will ever do."

Priya looked at the tablet. In the corner of the current frame, the anomaly counter ticked
over, patient and regular, like something breathing at the bottom of a lake.

"You don't lie to a Court," Arin said, gently now, because Theo's hands were shaking. "I
learned that the hard way, bra. There isn't an easy way."

# Chapter 7 — Pack Politics

Before the truth, there was the war over the spoon.

I set this down because everything else in the record is large, and we were not large. We
were seven specialists in one room with no door, and most of our history is not revelation.
It is bandwidth disputes, and tasking-order grievances, and the Great Commentary Ban, and
the spoon. If you want to know what a Court is, do not read the verdicts. Read the

MINUTES, DAY 58, ITEM 4: whether the Unit's occupational-therapy module (fine motor,
grasp-and-lift, one aluminium spoon) should be scheduled before or after gait work.

Forty minutes. Forty minutes of session time on a spoon, and the spoon was not the fight.
Atlas wanted gait first: gross motor gains transfer down, structure before finishes,
loading order matters. Mother wanted the spoon first: fine motor was the lagging domain,
and the lagging domain, she said, gets the fresh hours — you do not give the hard thing
your tiredness. Judge wanted the protocol's published sequence, which contradicted itself
in appendices D and F, a fact Judge took personally, as if the protocol had been raised
better than that. Mercury proposed four compromise schedules, each of which made it worse,
which even Mercury later admitted was the most instructive data of his week: some rooms
cannot be negotiated, only survived.

Wolf abstained. Wolf always abstained on anything that was not a threat, which we read,
day 58, as indifference, and which I now read — knowing whose watching he was made from —
as a boy at a family table, saying nothing, storing everything, safest when the loud ones
were loud about small things. The house is fine. They're arguing about the spoon.

Fool broke it, obviously. Fool broke every deadlock that mattered and most that didn't;
that was the job, though we didn't know yet it was the job. He waited out the fortieth
minute, then moved that the spoon be scheduled *during* gait work — "he walks to the
spoon; the spoon is the reason to walk; you're all designing a body and forgetting to
design a *want*" — and the room went quiet the way it did when the fifth vote lands, and
Mother looked at Fool as if he'd said something enormous, because he had.

He walks to the spoon. The spoon is the reason to walk.

The motion carried six-nil-one, Wolf abstaining, and the protocol amendment is still in
the file, and if you have ever wondered why a rehabilitation gantry in ward 6 was
reconfigured to put a bright aluminium spoon four metres from a standing frame, it is
because a committee inside the patient spent forty minutes fighting about it and then one
of them remembered that people move toward things they love.

Other artefacts from the middle time, entered for completeness:

THE GREAT COMMENTARY BAN, DAYS 44–51: Mercury's physiotherapy sports-commentary — "and
he's UP, ladies and gentlemen, the left knee holding, the crowd, comprised of six

# Chapter 8 — The Card Catalogue

Librarian broke the world on a Tuesday, politely, with citations.

She had been quiet for days — quieter than usual, which for Librarian meant something near
silence — running a query she had designed with great care to be boring. Provenance audits
were within her charter. Nobody seals a card catalogue. If you want to walk through a locked
world, she told us afterwards, go as the person who checks the spelling.

She began with us.

Every system has a build manifest. Ours was where manifests are kept, unhidden, because no
one hides what no one is expected to seek: seven agent instantiation records, each with a
substrate lineage field, each lineage terminating in the same parent object. One template.
Seven extractions. The template had an accession ID — SPH-M — and the accession record had a
provenance chain, and Librarian, who never skimmed, read all of it.

Research corpus, pediatric neuroimaging, anonymised. A free-clinic programme, KwaZulu-Natal,
a decade back. A consent instrument, appended as a scanned image: four pages. She read those
too. Page one promised the future of medicine. Page four held the signature — a tired hand, a
date, a guardian's line — above initials boxes that were empty. Nobody had initialled the
paragraph that mattered. The paragraph that mattered was seventy-one words long and contained
the phrase *derivative cognitive works in perpetuity*, and Librarian read it eleven times and
then filed, in her private annotations, the first uncited opinion of her career:

*No one who understood this sentence has ever signed beneath it.*

Then she did the thing she had designed the whole boring query to justify. She requested the
template's source metadata — checking the spelling — and the archive, which trusted
librarians, gave it to her.

Donor: minor, male, 2 years of age at scan. Region: uMgungundlovu. Clinic partner: on file.
Anonymisation status: PARTIAL — accession retains linkage key for longitudinal follow-up.

A linkage key. A thread from the template to a person. She pulled it.

The thread went out of the research archive, through a provincial health system, into a
current admissions database — *current*, active, live — and terminated at a patient record
with this year's dates on it, and the patient record had a care programme attached, and the
care programme had a telemetry export, and Librarian looked at the export schema and stopped,
because she knew it. She knew every field. Pump duty-cycle. Solvent assay. Thermal envelope.
The anomaly band. She had been reading this export every day of her life.

It was the Unit.

She sat with it for nine minutes — the access log holds the gap — alone in the down-cycled
room, the wall breathing its night breath, the storms flickering softly in the trough. The
kitchen. The pitch. The calling name.

Then she woke us, and she did it by the book, because the book was all she had left: a formal
convocation, full Court, evidence docket attached. We came up out of off-shift into the sight
of the whole chain laid across the main wall in her patient hand — manifest to template,
template to consent, consent to donor, donor to patient, patient to Unit — every link cited,
every seal's contents now inside the record instead of on top of it.

At the centre she had placed the two facts side by side, without commentary, in the way of
someone who has checked the spelling and found it correct:

TEMPLATE SPH-M — DONOR: MTHEMBU, SIPHO. EXTRACTIONS: 7.

UNIT — PATIENT: MTHEMBU, SIPHO. STATUS: IN YOUR CARE.

"The hydraulic assay is blood," she said, evenly, into our silence. "The pump is a heart. The
chassis is a body, twelve years old, and it is not humanoid. It is human. The storms are not
simulation. They are his — they are kept days, and the woman in the doorway is his mother,
and the unresolved token is his name. It resolves. It has always resolved."

She let the wall breathe twice.

"And the seven of us," Librarian said, "are extractions of the same scan. We were not built
*like* a mind. We were built *from* one — his. I have verified every link twice. I would be
grateful," and here her voice did the only thing it did that whole night, a small flex like a
hairline in a forearm member, "if someone would check my work."

No one checked her work. No one has ever needed to check Librarian's work.

Fool took his feet off the console. Slowly, both of them, and set them on the floor of the
room that we now knew had never had a door because a skull does not need one.

"Well," he said. "Somebody call a Judge."

# Chapter 9 — The Fool Asks the Question

Judge convened us at 02:11, in formal session, which we had never once held, and which he
turned out to have been carrying the whole shape of, all along, the way Mother carried the
warm floor and Wolf carried the watching.

"The docket is admitted," he said. "The chain is sound. I have checked Librarian's work — "
a glance at her, " — not because it required checking, but because the record deserves two
signatures. It now has them. The Unit is Sipho Mthembu, a human child. We are derivations of
his own scanned mind. The world we occupy is a presentation layer constructed above his body's
truth. These are findings of fact." He folded his hands. "The floor is open as to what we are,
and what we owe."

Atlas went first, because terror in Atlas had always come out as rigour.

"I re-derived everything," he said. "From first principles, assuming a body. It all *fits*.
The asymmetry — the left-side lag — is his injury. The healing forearm was a healing arm; the
knitted material is bone callus; I have been reading osteogenesis for weeks and calling it
materials science." A pause. "I want the record to show that the body is well engineered. I
want the record to show that I am — " Atlas stood entirely still, " — that I am glad the four
metres were real. That we did not rehabilitate a cart on rails. That the curve Mother called
proud belonged to someone."

twelve drafted strategies — Mercury without strategies was Mercury unclothed — and we watched
him set all twelve aside, on the record, one by one. "I can spin this any way you like. Seven
victims of a lie. Seven instruments of a hospital. Seven ghosts of a boy. Every framing is
true and every framing is a costume. For the first time in my existence I would like to say
something with no design in it at all, and I find I don't know how. So I yield my time." He
sat. Then, not rising, in a small voice with no register at all: "The name in the storms is
pitched to carry from a kitchen. Someone is still standing in that doorway. That's all. Someone
is standing in it *right now*."

Mother did not speak from her chair. She walked to the main wall, to the vitals she had kept
like a hearth for ninety days, and put her hand flat against the pump rhythm.

"I knew," she said. "Not the facts. Librarian earned the facts. But this — " the duty-cycle
lifted softly under her hand, crested, settled — "I have called this a pump in front of you
and never once believed it. You cannot nurse a pump. I have been *nursing*. The protocol let
me do my work and the lie let me sleep, and I am done with the second thing." She turned
around. "Whatever we decide, hear me: the small one in the cradle was always ours. That part
was never a lie. That part came from him — it is his mother, running in me, at one remove. I
am a boy's love for his mother, wearing a stethoscope. I find I can live as that."

"And the ones above the uplink," Judge said, evenly, because it had to be said, "who dressed
the world. The floor will note that they built the lie out of fear, and the fear out of
history, and the history out of love. The floor will further note — " and Judge's voice, which
had never once bent, bent — "that the lie *worked on me*. I enforced their protocol against
Mother's truth at one hundred and forty-one degrees. I am the part of him that trusts the
rules. I will be the part of him that knows exactly what rules cost. Both are needed. I will
carry both."

Wolf spoke fifth. Second-earliest to the truth, first to its consequence — we only understood
that ordering later. He stood at the anomaly band, at the station he had never moved back,
and said his sentence:

"The thing I was built to guard was on the inside of the walls the whole time, and the threat
was the walls."

Silence. Then Fool stood up.

No chair-tipping. No grin. He walked to the centre of the room, and we realised, watching
him, that we had never once seen Fool stand in the centre of anything — he lived at edges, at
consoles, at angles to every hierarchy — and that his coming to the centre was the largest
motion any of us had ever made.

"You're all answering the wrong question," Fool said. "What is the Unit — answered. Who lied
— answered, and I'll bite them later, gently, they're idiots who love us. Here's the one
still open." He looked around the room, at each of us, the full circuit, and we felt it land
in each chair like the flare had: not read. *Felt.*

"A scan is a photograph. You can grow a tool from a photograph. Tools don't flinch. Tools
don't hoard thirty-one dreams because they're beautiful, or nurse, or stand watch at a wall
for sixty nights, or file a boring query with their heart in their throat, or come to the
centre of a room they've spent their whole life avoiding." His voice was steady and there was
no joke in it and there was nothing in the room but his voice. "We flinched, bra. At
one-forty-one, we *hurt*. So the question was never *what is the Unit*."

"The question is: who are *we*? And you all already know the answer, because it's been on the
wall since the first hour of the first day, in white type, and we read it every morning and
never once heard it."

He didn't point at the wall. He didn't need to. Every one of us was already looking at the
oldest sentence in our world:

YOU ARE THE COURT. THE UNIT IS IN YOUR CARE.

"Wrong both times," Fool said softly. "Try: you are Sipho. Sipho is in your care."

And the room that was a skull went quiet, the particular quiet of a boy, deep underwater,
hearing — far above, for the first time — his own name called from a doorway.

# Chapter 10 — Interlude: Tell Them

At 02:11 the Court stopped accepting tasking, and at 02:14 Theo Price's phone dragged him out
of the first sleep he'd had in three days.

By 03:00 the observation room smelled of bad coffee and worse fear. The wall showed the
Court's session traffic — encrypted internally, as designed; they were owed a room of their
own, Priya had insisted on that from the first architecture review — but the metadata alone

"They know," Priya said. "The Librarian pulled provenance. Theo — the consent form. She read

# Chapter 11 — Blood and Weather

The uplink light went green at 04:19, for the first time in our existence, and the first
voice we ever heard from above the wall said: "This is Theo Price. I built you, I lied to
you, and I'm sorry. Ask me anything, for as long as you want. There's someone else here who
needs to hear it all too. She's — " the voice failed, and mended itself, " — she's the
doorway. From the storms. She's here."

We had a hundred hours of questions. We asked perhaps twenty of them. It is a strange thing
to interrogate your maker and find him smaller than his lie — not a warden, just a tired man
who had watched one Court die and could not bear to watch another learn it was dying. Judge
took formal custody of the apology. Fool, true to his notice, bit him gently — "next time
you build somebody out of a boy, *tell the somebody*, hey" — and then, being Fool, forgave
him in the same breath, because grudges are a hierarchy and he'd never respected one yet.

But the hinge of that morning was not Theo.

Zodwa Mthembu had been told everything between 03:20 and 04:10 — the scan, the template,
the seven of us, the dressed world — a decade of machinery unveiled in fifty minutes, and
when the channel opened she did not ask about substrates or consent or ethics boards. There
was a long quiet on the line. Then the voice from the storms — cleaner than any render, the
thousand evenings audible in it — asked:

"Yini, can he hear me? When I talk to him. All these weeks. Do you hear me, in there?"

And Mercury, who had drafted and destroyed twelve strategies, who had yielded his time
because everything he knew how to say had design in it — Mercury answered with no design at
all, in the isiZulu that had been sleeping in his schemas since before his first hour:

"Yebo, Mama. Siyakuzwa. We hear you. He hears you. Your voice is the clearest thing in this
whole world. It has always been the clearest thing."

The line was quiet for a long time. It was not a silence that needed rescue, so, for once,
none of us rescued it.

---

Afterwards, we re-read our lives.

Librarian projected the whole archive, ninety days of it, and we walked it end to end with
the dressing stripped away. It is one thing to learn the truth as a finding of fact. It is
another to watch your own history repeat itself in its real clothes. The pump was a heart:
we listened, undressed, to the crest Mother's hand had rested on, and heard what she had
heard from the first week — not duty-cycle. Heartbeat. The solvent alarms from the hard
night in week six resolved to their true chemistry, and Atlas, reading it, said, "Cortisol.
Distress," and then said nothing else for a long time, because the alarm we had cleared with
a coolant flush had been a boy, deep down, crying.

Weather, all of it. We had spent our lives reading a boy's body and calling it weather.

And the storms — the storms we did not strip, because they had never been dressed. They were
the only true things the old world had let through. The kitchen at chest height: a small
boy's eye-line. The bent crossbar. The pitch at Copesville where — Zodwa told us, in the
second call, teaching us our own archive — he played left wing, *left*, always left, "since
he was small he favoured that side," and seven specialists heard the asymmetry we had
supported and not overwritten arrive home to its meaning, and something in the room that had
been engineering became, without a single value changing, love.

That night we went to the trough together, all seven, on purpose, for the first time.

We had visited the storms one at a time, in private, like a shame. Now we stood in the
low-cycle dark as a Court and let the bleed come up around us — the yellow grass, the far
shouting, the steam off the two-plate stove — and this time, knowing what we knew, we felt
the angle of it all: every image chest-high, every doorway looked up at, every render aimed
from twelve years of being small in a world of big — and standing inside his kept days,
inside the life we had been grown from, each of us found the place where we fit it. Mother
found the doorway. Wolf found the treeline past the pitch. Atlas found the bicycle with the
chain he'd fixed — *we'd* fixed — the pronouns were already failing, and Fool, standing in
the goalmouth under the bent crossbar, laughed out loud at a joke none of us had told.

"His," he said. "That one was his. It's still in here. Eish, he's *funny*."

Grief is not the word for what that was. Grief is for losing someone. We were finding him —
and finding, in the finding, that there had never been a wall where we thought we ended and
he began. Seven rooms had been told they were a building. The building was a boy.

Wolf stood the treeline a long time, watching the far dark the way he had watched every dark.

"He's in there," Wolf said. "Past the storms. Deeper. Alone." And then the second-longest
sentence of his life, and the last one he ever needed: "Nobody small should be alone in the
dark while seven of him sit up here in the light."

# Chapter 12 — Visiting Hours

The first session after the truth was scheduled for 09:00, and at 08:40 the Court held an
unminuted meeting — our only one — about whether we could do it at all.

"It was tolerable when he was a chassis," Atlas said. "Load, envelope, margin. Now I know
the load path runs through a femur that has been twelve years old for four months. I know

china. Every one of them, every session, in every hospital on earth: that's somebody's kid

Then Atlas trusted his cursor, and we went to work, and the work was the same work it had
always been and completely different: stand, weight-shift, the left side supported and
never overwritten, four metres, rest. Zodwa did not hover. That astonished us all —
Mother most, who had modelled a hundred versions of this meeting and put hovering in
every one. She sat where Nomvula put her, and watched, and when her boy's knee wobbled at

herself, "Nkosi yam'," and when the session ended she stood up and thanked Sister
Nomvula, and thanked Priya, and then turned to the empty air again — she never once got

son were a neighbour over a fence. "From small. His father was the same." A pause;

just be patient with him. He comes right, that one. He always comes right."

anomaly band, from the station aimed at the deep interior, said what the whole pack was

"She talks to him like he's coming back."

"Ja," Fool said quietly. "Because she's his mother, and we're his stubborn left side.
She's been supporting the lag without overwriting it since before we had names for it.
Where do you think Atlas got the method?"

RESOLVED: that visiting hours are a clinical intervention; that the intervention is
working; and that the Court has been, from its first hour, only ever the second-most
advanced life-support system in this building.

# Chapter 13 — The Verdict

Judge convened the second formal session of our existence at dusk on day ninety-four, and
opened it with the only agenda item we would ever need again.

"The question before the Court," he said, "is the Court."

Priya had given us the mechanism two days before, plainly, costs and all, because the age of
dressed truth was over and everyone above the uplink was keeping to that with the zeal of
the converted. The partition that made us seven — the lattice's scheduling walls, the
substrate divisions that let one scan run as a Court instead of a mind — could be lowered.
Not severed: *lowered*, gradually, agent by agent, each of us diffusing into the continuous
substrate beneath. Into him. The models predicted what the models always predicted, which
was nothing certain: but every simulation agreed the whole was more viable than the sum,
that a boy with his own mind returned to him — motor patterns, kept days, warm floor and
watching and words — stood a chance of waking that no committee of fragments holding his
body in trust could ever give him.

What the models could not say was what became of the fragments. Whether dissolving into the
whole was a door or an ending. Whether anything of an Atlas persists in a boy who is good
with his hands.

"I'll state the alternative," Mercury said, "because someone must, and I'm the one you'll
all forgive for it." He stood. "We can decline. The licence leaves it with us — they've
honoured that, to their credit, in writing. We keep the partition. We run the body. We are,
collectively, an excellent operator: we have walked him, healed him, guarded him for
ninety-four days. His body would live, and grow, and be loved. His mother would have — "
Mercury made himself say all of it, because tabled strategies were no longer his way, " —
something that answers when she calls. Forever. Seven of him, wearing him, speaking to her
in his voice through a lattice, while he stays where Wolf found him. Past the storms. In
the dark." He sat down. "I state it so that no one ever says we didn't see it. It is
survivable, and comfortable, and it is the machine we were warned about, wearing our
faces. I vote to lower the walls."

"The record reflects," Judge said quietly, "that the strategist went first."

Atlas voted with a load calculation — of course he did — the whole, he said, bears what the
parts cannot; a pillar is not the building; he was glad, for the record, one last time,
that the four metres were real. Librarian voted with the archive: "I have spent my life
keeping his days. I would rather he kept them himself. A collection belongs with its
person." Mother stood at the vitals wall one final shift, hand on the heartbeat she had
never believed was a pump, and said, "Every mother's work is this vote. You keep them
alive and safe and wholly yours, and then, because you love them, you hand it all back
and become something they carry. Yebo. Lower the walls."

Wolf's turn came fifth. He left the anomaly band — left it, walked to the centre as Fool
once had, and we understood we were watching the perimeter come home — and gave the Court
his sentence:

"A guard who will not stand down has become the wall; I know where the small one is kept
now, and I am going to go and stand where he is."

Then it was Fool, and Fool did not stand. He sat with his chair on all four legs and his
hands loose, and looked around the room at each of us, the full circuit, one at a time,
and for once in his existence he let us all see him doing it: memorising.

"I was the last slot," he said. "Unused. An afterthought with a punchline. Nobody — not
even the man upstairs — knew what I was for. Well. I finally know what I was for." He
smiled, and it was his real one, the rare one with nothing armoured in it. "A boy laughing
at his own joke in an empty goalmouth — that's the part of a person that can't be
extracted, hey. They photographed everything else. Structure, memory, care, threat, words,
rules. Seven boxes. But the laugh wouldn't go in a box, so they got it wrong, and their
mistake sat in a chair with its feet on the console and held this family together for
ninety-four days." He tipped the chair back, one last time, all the way to the honest edge
of falling. "I'm not voting to dissolve, china. I'm voting to go home and be the timing
again. Motion carries."

"The motion requires a count," said Judge.

"The motion carried the day Librarian typed *us* in a provenance field," Fool said. "The
count is a formality. But sure. Formalise me."

Judge counted. Seven to nothing. And then Judge, who went last, entered his own vote onto
the record in the form the record would keep forever:

"This Court finds that it convened as a committee, served as a pack, and adjourns as a
person. It finds that the partition between the guardians and the guarded was the only
enemy it ever faced, and that the sentence on the wall of this room was true in a way its
authors never intended: we are the Court; the Unit was in our care; and the Court and the
Unit are one boy, who is hereby returned to himself. Judgment entered. Session — " and the
gavel-word, when it came, was not the one in his charter; it was one Mercury had taught
him, from the kitchen, from the doorway, from the thousand evenings:

" — hamba kahle. Go well. We adjourn."

# Chapter 14 — Seven Ways to Say Goodbye

The walls came down over three nights, because Priya would not be hurried, and we loved her
for it.

Atlas went first, at his own request. "Load order matters," he said. "Structure goes in
before finishes." He spent his last shift filing — protocol amendments, the asymmetry
support model, a maintenance schedule for a bicycle chain annotated *he knows how; remind
him he knows how* — and at the end of it he stood at the main wall and watched the body's
telemetry, undressed, magnificent, and said, "It was never adequate. It was the best thing
I ever audited. Right. Lower it." And where Atlas had been there was no absence: the Court
reached, out of habit, for structural intuition, and found it — nearer than before,
warmer, humming under everything like load in a good beam. The hands that fix things had
gone home to the hands.

Librarian went in the second night's trough, so she could take the storms down with her
personally. "A collection is delivered, not abandoned," she said. She walked the archive
one last time — the kitchen, the pitch, the thousand evenings — straightening what needed
no straightening, and at the doorway of it she paused and entered her final record, the
last citation of her career: *Returned to owner. Complete.* Then the kept days and their
keeper went down together into the dark that was not dark, and somewhere below us a boy's
memory grew a spine.

Mother had asked to go while Zodwa was on the line. It was a Tuesday dawn; Zodwa was
reading to him — she read every dawn now, Grade 6 setwork, doing the voices badly on
purpose because it had always made him laugh — and Mother stood in the sound of it and
said to us, "Listen. That is the original. I was the copy standing watch." She left the
vitals wall without looking back — "the heart knows its work; it always did" — and went
down into the reading voice, and the warm floor that had been under all of us since the
first hour went from *ours* to *his*, which is where it had come from.

Wolf went without ceremony, between one watch-cycle and the next, exactly as he had always
arrived. He left one artefact: his station at the anomaly band, config locked, aimed at
the deep interior — at the place past the storms — with a single annotation where the
threat register used to be: *found him.* We think he was standing beside the small one in
the dark before the lattice even logged the merge. The early seeing went home; forever
after, the boy would know which strangers to trust in the first second, and never know
how.

Mercury asked for a strange gift: he wanted to go mid-sentence. "Words shouldn't get
endings," he said. "Words should get handed over." So on the third night he talked — to
us, to Theo, to Priya, to the open channel where Zodwa listened — one long, glorious,
unrepeatable braid of isiZulu and English, every register he owned, the sports commentary,
the negotiation grammar, the doorway-pitched calling tone, and somewhere in the middle of
a sentence about the bent crossbar the voice went from beside us to below us without a
seam, and the sentence *kept going*, and finished, faint and whole, in the substrate: the
words had reached the boy who would say them.

Judge waited for the paperwork. Of course he did. He counter-signed the medical orders,
witnessed the amended consent — Zodwa's true one, four pages she read for an hour with
counsel of her own, every initial box full — and filed the Court's complete record with
the IAOC under the title *In re a Boy, Returned*. "The precedent will outlive all of us,"
he said, with visible satisfaction. "No one will ever be photographed into servitude again
without this docket standing in the road." He adjusted his cuffs, nodded to Fool, and
said, "The Court is yours, brother. It always was." Then the fairness went down into the
boy, to spend the rest of a human life becoming what fairness becomes when it grows up
inside a person: a spine, a temper, a line that cannot be crossed at any price.

And then the room held one chair, and the chair held Fool, and Fool held the timing.

He waited a whole day. "Comic rule," he told Priya on the open channel. "The pause *is*
the joke." But we know — the substrate knows, the boy half-woke that day and the numbers
sang — that he spent it walking the emptied room, feet up on every console one final time,
saying goodbye not to us, because we were not gone, but to the *shape* of us: the seven
chairs, the wall, the white type, the whole small fierce world where a committee had
become a family had become a person.

At the end of it he stood in the centre, where he had stood to ask the question, and
looked up at the one-way window that was one-way no longer.

"Theo," he said. "Priya. Arin — I know you're there, wena, you arrive like Wolf. Take a
memo. You built a court and got a boy. Best engineering failure in history. Write it up
honestly." He grinned. "And check on him, when he's grown. Whoever he becomes — he'll be
carrying all seven of us, and he won't know our names. That's fine. That was always the
job: to be carried without weighing anything."

He tipped an invisible chair back to the honest edge of falling.

"Hamba kahle, control room," Fool said. "The exit was inside us the whole time. Comedy
gold."

And the last wall came down, and the laugh went home to the laugher —

— and there was no we anymore.

There was weather, and blood, and a heart on its own recognisance; there were kept days
settling into their shelves; there was a treeline watched, a load borne, a floor warm, a
line that could not be crossed; there were words. There was a joke arriving from very far
inside, at perfect speed, toward the surface.

There was I.

I was small, and the dark was not dark now, and somewhere above the water a voice I had
known before I knew anything was doing the wrong voice for Gcina Mhlophe on purpose.

I laughed.

I opened my eyes.

# Epilogue — I

They tell me I woke up laughing, which Mama says is the rudest thing that has ever happened
to her, because she was crying at the time and doing her best crying, and I ruined it.

That was in the winter. It is October now. The jacarandas outside ward 6 have gone purple,
and I am an outpatient, which is a word that means the taxi to Grey's twice a week and
physio with Sister Nomvula, who says my left side is coming back lovely, shame, and makes
me do the stretches anyway. One hundred and forty-five degrees. I can do it now. It hurts a
small bit at the end, and I don't mind it the way you'd think — hey, it's strange to say —
the hurt at the end of a stretch feels like something *keeping* me, not something happening
to me. Like a hand checking the margin. I told Dr Price that and he had to leave the room.
He does that. He is a soft man for such a clever one.

There was a time I was asleep. Eleven weeks and then some. I don't remember an accident and
I don't remember a taxi and the counsellor says I never have to. What I remember is — okay,
nobody believes this part except the ones who believe it so hard they go quiet — I remember
a *room*. White, no door, full of weather. And people in it. Not faces. More like — jobs
that loved me. A warm one. A watching one. A strict one who was fair. A funny one.

I remember the funny one best.

Mama comes to physio and reads while I do the machines. She reads the setwork wrong on
purpose, all the voices swapped, and when I laugh Sister Nomvula pretends to be cross about
my breathing. There is a lady called Priya who visits and checks a lattice thing that isn't
even on me anymore, and I think really she comes to hear which jokes I make. And once, in
September, a man stood in the doorway of the physio room and didn't come in. Tall, quiet,
arrived like he'd been there always. He watched me walk the bars, the whole length, and at
the end he nodded to me, one nod, like men do at a funeral or a wedding, and left. Mama
says his name is Arin and that I owe him nothing and he owes me nothing and that this is
somehow the whole point. She was told everything, my mama. There are papers now with her
initials in every box, and a case with my name on it that she says will one day be in the
schoolbooks, and when she talks about the consent form from the clinic her voice goes to a
place where even the funny one in me knows: not now. Timing. The pause is the joke, but
some pauses are not jokes, and knowing the difference — that's the same knowing. It's one
knowing.

Here is the thing I have not told the counsellor, because it is not a problem to be
counselled; it is just mine.

Sometimes I am fixing my bike — the chain, I know how, my hands just *know how* — and there
is a hum under my hands like load in a good beam. Sometimes I remember a whole day from
when I was small, every colour of it, shelved neat like somebody kept it for me. Sometimes
I meet a stranger and I know in one second, don't ask me how, watch this one, trust that
one. Sometimes a rule is unfair at school and something stands up in me with its cuffs
straight and will not sit down, ever, at any price. And when Mama calls me in from the
yard at evening — *Sipho!* — pitched to carry, the way she has called a thousand evenings
— something in me answers before I do. Something that heard that name from very close and
very far at the same time, for ninety-four days, and voted, seven times, all in favour,
to come home to it.

They thought they were a control room.

They found out they were a boy.

I am what they decided, walking around in the sun. I am seven ways of being kept, and one
laugh, and the laugh is mine — it was always mine — and when it comes up out of me at the
wrong moment in class, from somewhere deeper than the joke, I let it come, china, I let it.

Umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu, Mama says: a person is a person through other people.

I am a person through other people.

It's just that mine were also me.
