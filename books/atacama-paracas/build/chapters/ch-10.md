# Chapter 10 — The Official Story: Tridents and Mysteries

The boat smelled of diesel and dried anchovy, and Marisol Quispe-Ortega had not said a word in eleven minutes.

We knew it was eleven because Theo was counting. Theo counts when the room goes quiet — not to be rude, he'd told me once, but because a silence with a shape to it is information, and you don't throw information away. He sat in the bow with his knees up and his notebook braced against his shins, and his pen had not moved, and the line of foam off the hull was the only thing on the whole bright bay that was in a hurry.

Behind us the Candelabra hung on the slope of the peninsula like a watermark pressed into the world. Three prongs. A central spine. A hundred and seventy metres of trench cut into a hillside the colour of old bone, and from out here — a kilometre, two — you could see the whole of it the way you're meant to. Not from above. Not from a drone. From the deck of a small boat with salt in your teeth, looking back at the land the way a man coming home from a season of fishing would look at it.

"Right," said Esme, and spread the folder on the bench between us. The Lens had her camera in her lap, lens cap on, which for Esme was a kind of restraint, a held breath. "Theories. Let's lay them all out and be fair to every one of them, because some of them are going to be true and I want to know which."

"That's not how it works," said Theo, without looking up.

"Humour me."

I read routes for a living — the logic of how a thing connects to another thing, why a path bends where it bends, what a sign is *answering*. So I take the catalogue of theories the way I take a junction I don't know yet: lay them all down flat, no favourites, and watch which ones the land agrees with.

Esme started at the top.

"One. Paracas culture. The locals' culture, the one that buried its dead out here wrapped in those impossible textiles, two hundred BC give or take. Pottery in the Paracas style turns up not far from the base of the slope. Ergo: the Candelabra is Paracas. Their sign, their hand."

She let that sit. It's a good theory. It's the careful one, the one museums put on the placard, and it has the great virtue of being *near*. Pottery you can hold. Pottery you can date. The thermoluminescence on Paracas ware lands around two centuries before the Christian era and it does not waver much, and there it is, broken and scattered on the ground beneath the largest sign anyone ever carved at the sea.

Theo finally moved his pen. He drew two dots and a line that *didn't connect them*, and he held the notebook up so we could see the gap.

"The pot was near the sign," he said. "That's the whole evidence. A pot was near a sign. I left a coffee cup near the Mona Lisa once. Doesn't mean I painted it."

"You weren't allowed near the Mona Lisa."

"I was near it from across the room. That's still near. Nearness isn't authorship." He set the notebook down. "Nobody has ever dated the Candelabra. Not the trench. Not the stones in the trench. The figure itself floats. We are dating a thing by the litter around it, and the litter could be from anyone who walked past in eleven hundred years."

*The man drops his cup and it becomes a footnote*, said the fish — the small mean voice I keep for the parts of my own head that won't shut up, the one that swims along the gunwale heckling everybody including me. *Soon they'll be dating you by the wrappers in your bag. Carbon says: snack-forward, late Anthropocene.*

I said none of this out loud. Marisol was watching the water.

"Two," Esme went on. "Nazca. Cousin of the Nazca lines, two hundred kilometres south, same broad cultural neighbourhood, same impulse — enormous figures scraped into the desert floor for reasons we argue about endlessly. The Candelabra as a coastal Nazca, basically. A relative."

This is the one the documentaries love, because Nazca is famous and the Candelabra is not, and fame is contagious. And it isn't *wrong*, exactly. There's a family resemblance in the gesture: the human animal, looking at a vast flat surface, deciding to *write on it*. The same species, the same itch.

But.

"The Nazca lines are scratches," Theo said. "Centimetres. You sweep the dark stones off the pale ground and there's your line, and the desert's so dead and windless it stays. The Candelabra is a *cut*." He held two fingers apart. "Two feet down. Into salt-hardened ground. Somebody dug a trench you could lose a child in, the whole length of a city block, and then — this is the part — the salt crust *heals* over it and holds the edges and the wind can't get a grip. The Nazca people were drawing. Whoever did this was *engineering*. Different verb."

He's right about the verb. I felt it when we stood at the lip of it that morning, before the boat — the depth of the thing, the way it doesn't read like art when you're standing in it. It reads like a road cut. Like someone solving a problem about *staying visible* and refusing to lose to the weather.

The Lens lifted her camera then, half a motion, and stopped. Lowered it. She does this — the war taught her hands to reach for the shutter when a moment gets too large, and the years since have been her trying to teach them to wait. The Candelabra was getting larger behind us as the light shifted. She wanted it. She didn't take it. I watched her not take it, which is its own kind of photograph.

"Three," said Esme. "Ritual symbol. It's a sacred sign — the *chonta* staff of Viracocha, the lightning, a stylised plant. Religion. We carved it for the gods or to mark the gods' ground, and asking what it's *for* in a practical sense is like asking what a cathedral spire is *for*. It's for heaven. It's a prayer you can see from a boat."

I like this one more than Theo does. The trouble with us route-readers is we want everything to be infrastructure, and the world is full of people who built enormous things for no reason a satellite could ever recover, who did it because a god told them to and that was sufficient. To assume function is its own kind of blindness. The pampa and the bay are full of marks that mean *we were here and we believed something*, and that's a complete sentence.

"It's aimed at the sea, though," Theo said quietly. "That's the problem with prayer."

"Why's that a problem."

"Because a prayer you carve into a hillside, you'd aim at the temple. Or the mountain. Or the sky, like Nazca, if you think the gods are up. This one's aimed at *the horizon you sail in on*." He turned his hand flat, like a deck. "You don't pray to the ocean by facing the ocean. You signal to it. A god doesn't need a line of sight from nineteen kilometres out. A *sailor* does."

The fish went quiet. The fish goes quiet when Theo's right.

Nineteen kilometres. That's the number that won't leave you alone once you've heard it. The Candelabra is sited — *sited*, the way you'd site a beacon, the way you'd site a runway light — to be legible from far out on the open water. Somebody chose that slope, that pitch, that orientation, so that a figure on a hill would clear the curve of the world and hang there for a man at sea to find. You can stand on the land and you cannot see it the way it was built to be seen. The land is the back of the sign. The front of the sign faces the deep.

"Four," Esme said, and her voice changed a half-step, because here came the famous one. "Lighthouse. Navigation. A maritime mark — exactly what Theo's circling. A coast with no good landmarks, fog, a treacherous bay, and somebody puts up the biggest possible *here* so the boats can find the harbour. Pure infrastructure. A road sign for the ocean."

"Yes," Theo said.

"You're meant to argue with the others, not agree."

"I agree with this one. I'm allowed."

I had to argue with it, then, because that's the job — you don't let the theory you *want* walk through unsearched. "Then date it as a lighthouse," I said. "Lighthouses serve a traffic. Show me the traffic. Show me the harbour it's lighting, the route it's gating, the season the boats ran. A beacon implies a network — a thing on the other end that needed finding. We have the sign. Where's the rest of the sentence?"

He nodded slowly, which is Theo for *that's a fair hit and I'm going to sit in it.* "We don't have it yet," he said. "But absence of the network isn't absence of the function. The crust kept the sign and lost the boats. Wood rots. Salt keeps stone." He tapped the trench-line he'd drawn. "We're reading a road by the one milestone that survived."

"Five," said Esme, gentle now, because she'd saved the romantic one for the end the way you save a dessert. "San Martín. Eighteen-twenty. The liberator lands at Paracas to free Peru from the Spanish, and the story goes the Candelabra is *his* — a Masonic emblem, the trident of liberty, cut by his men or pointed out by them as a sign that the revolution had a blessing carved into the coast." She smiled. "It's a beautiful story. The independence of a continent, written on a hill."

It is beautiful. And it is the kind of beautiful that tells you immediately it's been *added* — a thing too good, too convenient, draped over an older bone. The Candelabra was here long before San Martín's sails. The freedom-fighters didn't make it; they *found* it, and found it useful, the way every passing century has found it useful, the way we were finding it useful right now, hanging it with our own meanings like coats on a hook that was there before any of us.

That's the thing about an undated sign. Everyone gets to claim it, because nothing it carries can be sent back stamped *return to sender, wrong era.* The pottery says two hundred BC and the pottery isn't talking. The salt holds the edges and the salt doesn't sign its work. A hundred and seventy metres of certainty about *that something is there* sitting on top of a total blank about *who, when, why, and for whom* — and into that blank pours alien antennae and Freemasons and homing beacons for sailors drunk on datura and the entire glorious zoo of the human need to *finish the sentence.*

We laid all five out on the bench, fair to each, and not one of them could close its hand around the figure. Each had a place where the land said *not quite.*

The Lens had her camera up again. This time she didn't lower it. The afternoon had gone gold and slantwise, the way it does on that coast in the last hour, and the light was raking *across* the Candelabra now instead of flat onto it, and the three prongs threw their own thin shadows down into the trenches so the whole sign stood up off the hill in relief, deep and certain and lit from the side like something underlined. From the boat. Only from the boat. She held the shutter and didn't press it, and held it, and the boat rocked, and then she let it down without a frame, and I understood she'd decided the moment was Marisol's, not hers.

Because the whole time, the archaeologist had said nothing.

Eleven minutes had become forty. Marisol Quispe-Ortega had heard us lay out Paracas and Nazca, ritual and lighthouse and the general from history, had heard us be careful and be fair and find each one's flaw, had heard Theo draw his gap between the pot and the sign, and she had watched the water and the slope and the light coming sideways onto the trident, and she had let us run the whole catalogue out to its end without a single correction.

That's not silence because she has nothing. I've sat with enough wayfinders now to know the difference between a quiet that's empty and a quiet that's *full and choosing.* This was the second kind. She knew which of our five was foolish and which had a bone in it; she knew, I was nearly sure, things that weren't on any of the five lists, things you couldn't get from a pot or a satellite, that came from a grandfather's hand pointing at a hill from a boat exactly like this one. She had all of it. She just hadn't decided yet whether we'd earned it, or whether earning it was even the right frame, or whether the consortium's people prepping their tidy interpretive signage up the coast had already poisoned the well for any outsider with a folder full of theories.

"You've left one out," she said finally. Her first words in three-quarters of an hour.

Esme's pen went up, hungry. "Which?"

Marisol looked at the Candelabra, lit and deep and aimed past us at the open water, and she didn't answer. She let the line sit there in the diesel air — *you've left one out* — and she turned her face back to the sea, to the horizon the sign was built to address, the one none of us could stand on.

The fish, for once, said nothing at all.

We had five good theories and a real hole in each, and a woman in the stern who knew the sixth and hadn't given it. The official story was intact, every piece of it respected, none of it closed. And the boat carried us in toward the slope where the trident waited, patient as a milestone on a road whose other end is two thousand years downstream and underwater, still asking — of us, of the consortium, of every boat that ever came home this way — *who are you, and can you read me.*

I wrote nothing down. Some doors you don't sketch. You just note that they're open, and that the keeper is standing in front of them, and you wait.
