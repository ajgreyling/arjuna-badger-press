# Chapter 6 — What Is It For: Read by Walking

The thing about a map you have to walk is that it doesn't care whether you believe in it.

Rosa Mamani started before dawn, which is to say she started while the rest of us were still arguing about whether to start. She wore the same windbreaker she'd worn for three days and carried nothing — no pack, no water bottle clipped to her hip, no GPS unit blinking its little blue lie of certainty. She had a stick. She had her knees. She walked into the pampa like she was stepping off a curb she'd stepped off ten thousand times, and we scrambled after her with our four hundred euros of hydration systems sloshing.

"Path's not where you think," she said, not turning around.

I thought it was right under our feet. There was a track — a pale braid in the dark gravel, the famous caravan trace, the one that shows up in the survey photos like a vein. I'd memorised it. I'd loaded it into my head the way I load everything, the route a glowing thread I could pull from memory and lay over the real world. That's the wiring. People say it like a diagnosis; I've used it to cross cities I'd landed in an hour before. I knew where the path was.

"You're walking the photograph," Rosa said. "Not the road."

The official story, and let me give it to you straight, because it deserves straight: the Atacama Giant is a god. Or a shaman. Or a lord — an authority figure, hands at his sides, head crowned with rays, scraped and piled into a hillside at Cerro Unitas a thousand years ago and change, sometime between AD 1000 and 1400, by the Andean peoples who moved goods and llamas through the driest non-polar desert on this planet. He is 119 metres tall. He is the largest known prehistoric depiction of a human being anywhere on Earth, and he is one of roughly five thousand figures the people here carved, scraped, and stacked across the pampa — birds, geometries, llama trains, men. The consensus, hard-won and reasonable and held by serious people who have given their lives to this ground, is that the Giant is a *symbol*. A presence. Something that meant power, or the divine, or both, to a culture watching over its routes.

I love that story. I want to be clear, because what comes next is not a sneer. The people who built the consensus walked this pampa too, with theodolites and patience and grant cycles that ran out, and they were right about almost everything. The Giant is a symbol.

He is also, Rosa was about to show us, a thing you read with your feet.

---

We tried, the day before, to make our own.

This is the part I'd cut from a braver edit, except that cutting it would be a lie. We had a survey-grade drone. We had Tomas — our systems thinker, who can hold a three-dimensional figure in his head and rotate it like a man turning a key in a lock, and who had spent the night before laying out a forty-metre geoglyph in software, every coordinate clean. We had stakes and string. We had a *plan*, which is the most dangerous thing an outsider can carry into a desert.

We made a man. Sort of. From the ground he looked like a man who had been described to someone over a bad phone connection. The proportions drifted; the right arm came out longer because the slope cheated us and we didn't read the cheat. Tomas stood at the bottom of our little hill and looked up at our little figure and was quiet for a long time, which from Tomas is a paragraph.

"The math is right," he said finally. "The ground isn't."

The wind took half of it overnight. Just the scraped lines — the additive parts, the piled stones, those held, but the pale extractive lines blurred at the edges like chalk in rain, except there's no rain, just air moving across the most patient surface on Earth, lifting grain by grain. By noon our man had a smudge for a face.

The Giant has had a thousand years of that wind. He still has his face.

That's the potato, and I'll say it plainly so nobody thinks the trick is hidden: the easy version is not easy. You cannot just *decide* to carve a 119-metre man on a hillside and have him be legible, symmetrical, and durable. Symmetry over a slope is a nightmare of foreshortening — the figure has to be *distorted* in construction so it reads correct from the angle people actually see it. The builders solved that. With sticks and knees and a road. We solved it with software and made a smudge.

"You measured down," Rosa said, when I told her, half-defeated. She found this very funny. "You measured down at the ground. Nobody saw it from up there. They saw it from over there." She pointed across the pampa, at nothing, at distance.

"From the path."

"From the path. Walking." She looked at me with something that wasn't unkindness but wasn't going to do my work for me either. "You make a thing for the eye that will see it. You don't have that eye yet."

---

So we walked to find the eye.

The LENS came with us and didn't lift the camera for the first hour, which I noticed and didn't say anything about. He'd been bad in Iquique — a market, a sudden noise, the way his shoulders went and his hands went up with the body's old instinct to put glass between himself and the world. The camera is a shield as much as a tool, and he knows it, and the knowing is the slow work of him. This morning he just walked, hands empty, watching Rosa's feet.

"She's not going straight," he said.

"No."

"She's reading something."

She was. The path Rosa walked was not the path in the photograph. It bent — out toward a low rise that the survey trace ignored, then back, then held a line I couldn't account for until the sun came up enough to throw shadow into the gravel and I saw it: the ground itself was sorted. Slightly. Darker grit here, paler there, a grain change you'd never see from above and never see standing still, but walking it, over hours, your eye built it into a road. The pampa had a texture that was a sentence, and Rosa was reading it aloud with her body.

This is the thing my wiring is *for*, if it's for anything. Not solving. Reading movement. I fell into her rhythm without deciding to, and somewhere in the second hour the route stopped being a thread I'd memorised and became a thing I was *inside of*, the way a melody stops being notes. I could feel where it wanted to go before it went. I started to walk it right.

"Now look up," Rosa said.

I'd been so deep in the ground I'd forgotten the Giant.

He was *there*. He had arrived sometime in the last twenty minutes without my noticing, risen up the flank of Cerro Unitas, and from this point on the path — exactly this point, this stride, where the sorted gravel held a particular bend — he was not a smudge or a distortion. He was a man. Symmetrical. Crowned. Looking, it seemed, directly down the road at us, the way a face in a doorway looks at whoever's coming up the walk.

"Three steps back," Rosa said.

I stepped back and he tilted, the proportions sliding, the crown skewing.

"Forward."

Forward and he resolved, perfect, *correct*, and I understood — felt, in the legs, not the head — that the figure had been carved to be right from *here*, from this segment of the moving road, distorted on the slope precisely so the walking eye would unbend it. The Giant doesn't stand in a place. He stands in a *sightline*. He is aimed at people in motion.

Tomas, behind me, made a sound I'd describe as a small religious experience. "The distortion's the engineering," he said. "It's not error. It's correction for the angle. They built the *wrongness* in so it'd come out right." He turned in a slow circle, the figure resolving and dissolving as he moved. "It only works if you're going somewhere."

---

The official story is a god. I want to keep holding it, because here is where a lesser book would drop it to grab something shinier, and I won't. He may well be a god. A god you meet at the right step on the right road is not less a god. But Rosa had something else to show us, and she showed it the way she did everything, sideways and without a thesis statement.

She crouched and pointed up at the rays. The crown. The "antennae," if you've been on the parts of the internet where the Giant has antennae and a spacecraft and a backstory written by people who never came here. I won't pretend those readings don't exist; the rays are real, jutting from the head like spokes, and an honest book says plainly that the alien gloss is a gloss — a story laid over real lines by people who'd rather the desert be from somewhere else than be *this*, which is harder and better.

What's harder and better is checkable, and it's this. In 2006, in *Antiquity*, a peer-reviewed journal, the archaeologist Luis Briones published the hypothesis that the rays may be more than crown. That their angles — the directions they point — may track the setting positions of the moon across the year, and that those positions mattered because they could mark the turn of the season. The rare season. The one this desert waits for the way a body waits for breath. The rain.

"When the moon sets *there*—" Rosa pointed along one ray, off toward a notch in the far hills, "—old people said it was time to watch the sky for water. Not every year. But the years it came." She shrugged, a small precise motion. "My grandmother said it. I don't know if the man built it for that. Nobody knows. You can't ask him."

I wanted, badly, to close it. To say *yes, it's a lunar gnomon, the rays are a calendar, write it down.* The wiring that reads routes also wants resolution; the same gift that finds the pattern aches to nail it shut. I felt the ache and I felt Rosa watching me feel it.

"It's checkable," I said instead. "The alignment. Someone could measure whether the ray actually points to the lunar set position."

"Someone published that it might," Rosa said. "Might isn't is."

The fish, were the fish here, would have said: *so the giant antenna man might be a moon clock, or he might be a moon clock the way my horoscope is a calendar — technically tracking something celestial, mostly tracking what you brought to it.* And the fish would not be wrong, and the fish would also not be the one who walked the road right, so the fish can mind the boat.

"You see why I don't say," Rosa said. Not to me. To the LENS, who had finally raised the camera and then, watching her, lowered it again. "If I say *it's a moon marker* — done. They put a sign. They put a fence. The grant people up there—" a tip of her chin north, toward where the consortium's white trucks sat parked at the access road like patient gulls, "—they want a sentence they can print. *The Atacama Giant tracked the moon to predict the rains.* Then it's finished. Then it's a thing that already happened." She looked back up at him, crowned, watching the road. "It's not finished. People still walk past him. He still tells you where you are."

---

That was Jakobus's whole contribution, and he made it the way he makes everything, which is to take up almost no room.

He'd come up the road last, slow, reading the ground the way Rosa read it — he reads *made things*, that's his whole strange gift, he can stand in front of a wall or a wheel or a 119-metre man and ask the only question he ever asks, which isn't *who* and isn't *what does it mean*. He waited until Rosa had said her piece and the silence had settled and then he said it, low, to nobody, to the pampa:

"What's it for."

Rosa didn't answer. It wasn't hers to hand him, and he knew it wasn't, and the not-asking-harder was the respect.

But she'd already shown us, hadn't she. She'd shown us with her feet for three hours. The Giant is not a monument. A monument is a thing you arrive at — you stand in front of it, you look up, you leave. This is a thing you *pass*. You are going somewhere across an open desert with no other landmark and animals to keep alive and water you can't see, and he rises up at the right stride to tell you: you are on the road. You are still on the road. And maybe — checkable, unproven, published, open — the crown over his head tells you something about *when*: which moon, which season, whether to watch the sky for the water that comes the years it comes.

A waymark. A calendar you walk into. A thing for people in motion, in a world where the land and the sky were the only maps and you read them with your body or you died.

The LENS still hadn't taken the shot. He stood there with the camera at his chest, the figure perfect over his shoulder, the light going long and gold and *correct*.

"You can take it," I told him.

"I know." He didn't. "I keep thinking — if I shoot him from here, he's right. Symmetrical. And then anyone who sees the photo thinks he's *standing* somewhere. They won't know you have to walk to find him." He lowered his hands all the way. "I don't think there's a picture of this. The this is the walking."

Behind us, on the access road, an engine turned over. A white truck, pulling out, headed back up to whatever brief the consortium was assembling — the sentence they wanted to print, the fence they wanted to plant, the question they wanted, gently and with funding and the best intentions in the world, to *answer.*

Rosa watched the truck go. Then she turned north herself, the direction we were all going next, up the coast to a peninsula and a sign cut into a hill that faces the wrong way — that faces the sea, that no one on land could ever stand to read.

"You think this one's hard," she said. "Wait until you meet a road that runs on water."

She started walking before we did. We scrambled after her.
