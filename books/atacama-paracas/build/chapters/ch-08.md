# Chapter 8 — North by the Sea Road

The Panamericana north of Iquique runs like a ruled line, and for a while that's all it is — a single dark stroke laid across the pale subsoil, the same trick the old people used, scrape away the desert varnish and the ground beneath comes up bone-coloured. Mara watched it out the passenger window of the rented Hilux and felt the old itch start at the back of her skull, the one that meant *this is a route and routes have grammar.*

"Stop doing the face," Esi said from the back.

"I'm not doing a face."

"You're doing the face. The wayfinding face. You look like the road is talking to you and you're too polite to interrupt."

Mara hadn't been aware her face did anything. That was the thing nobody told you about a brain that read the world all day — you forgot you were broadcasting. "The Atacama has about five thousand geoglyphs," she said, because facts were a place to put the broadcasting. "Most of them aren't gods or shamans. Most of them are *here's water, this way, eight days to the next quebrada.* Llamas, fish, men, abstract lines. Signposts. The whole desert is a manuscript and the caravans were the readers."

"And you," said Esi, "are the kind of person who'd read a stop sign for fun."

"Stop signs are *fascinating.* Octagon so you can tell it from the back, in the dark, half-buried in snow—"

"Oh god."

Jakobus, driving, said nothing, which was how Jakobus said most things. His hands sat on the wheel the way a man's hands sit on a tool he's used so long he's stopped seeing it. Every so often his eyes went off the tarmac and out into the pampa, and they snagged on something, and they let it go.

---

The LENS rode with the window cracked because closed cars did things to him he didn't discuss. His name on the manifest said Theo. He thought of himself, mostly, as the thing behind the camera, and the camera was on his knee, capped, which was its own small cowardice and he knew it.

He shot the desert anyway, through the gap, on instinct. Burn of white. A vicuña standing exactly where a vicuña should stand, which is to say at the precise point where two invisible lines of grazing crossed. A wind-collapsed shrine at the roadside, a cross made of two femurs of corrugated iron, plastic flowers gone to chalk. He didn't lift the camera to his eye. He shot from the hip, blind, the way you do when you've spent fifteen years learning that the eye to the viewfinder is the eye that doesn't flinch, and that the not-flinching is the thing that breaks you in the end.

"There," Mara said suddenly, and Theo's whole body braced before he understood it wasn't *that* kind of *there.*

She was pointing at nothing. A faint braid of disturbance in the desert floor, running off perpendicular to the highway and vanishing toward the coastal cordillera.

Jakobus slowed without being asked.

"Caravan track," Mara said. "Look at it. The modern road took the easy line, the engineer's line, the gentlest gradient. But the old track ignores it. It goes *that* way — harder, steeper — because it's not going where the road's going. It's going to water, and then to the next water, and the water decides everything." She had her hands up now, framing it, the way Theo framed things, and he noticed she did it too, and felt a small ridiculous warmth at that. "The road is a line drawn by money. The track is a line drawn by thirst."

"Write that down," Esi said. "That's a chapter title."

"It's not subtle enough," said Mara. "Why Files doesn't do *thirst.* Why Files does *here is the boring true thing, and here is the one place it stops being boring.*"

---

The boring true thing, for the record, held up.

They'd spent three days under the Giant at Cerro Unitas and the Giant had been everything the literature promised and harder than the literature let on. A hundred and nineteen metres of human figure laid up the side of a lonely hill, the largest prehistoric anthropomorph anyone's ever found, built somewhere in the long window between AD 1000 and 1400 by people whose names did not survive the building. Part of it scraped down to the bright subsoil. Part of it built up, dark stones carried and laid by hand to draw the dark parts dark. From the base of the hill it didn't look like much — foreshortened, a smear, a rumour of a man. You had to back off across the pampa, you had to get the angle the old caravans got, and then it stood up out of the ground and looked at you, and the rays came off its head like a crown or a headdress or — well. That was the whole argument, wasn't it. *Or.*

They had tried to make one. That was the rule of the show: you don't get to call a thing easy until you've failed to do it.

They'd surveyed a patch of pampa with the drone — and the drone, bless it, had spent the first morning being slapped sideways by a wind that came off nothing and went to nowhere and never once stopped. Esi had flown it like a woman defusing something. They'd staked a modest figure, forty metres, a fifth of the Giant, and scraped their lines with rakes and string and a theodolite-app on a phone that kept overheating in the sun.

Their man came out wobbly. The legs disagreed about where the hips were. One arm had a kink in it like a broken antenna. And then the wind, which had been their enemy all along, did the thing the desert does to everything it isn't fond of — it came in the night and feathered the edges, and by the second dawn their forty-metre man was already going soft and grey, dissolving back into the ground that had loaned him out for a day.

Whereas the Giant had stood for seven hundred years.

"That," Esi had said, standing over the ruin of their man with a rake in her hand and grit in her teeth, "is the potato. That right there. They make a god you can read across the desert for centuries. We make a stick figure the wind eats before breakfast."

---

So now they drove north, up the sea road, and Mara built the corridor in her head the way other people hum.

It went like this. The desert geoglyphs were infrastructure — that word again, Jakobus's word, the one he'd offered at Cerro Unitas like a man laying a single card on the table and then taking his hand away. *Infrastructure.* Not art, not only. Signs. And signs come in systems. A stop sign is meaningless alone; it means *stop* because there's a *go* and a *yield* and a whole grammar of the road behind it. So if the Giant was a sign, what was it part of?

She'd been mapping the answer for two thousand kilometres. The Atacama geoglyphs clustered on caravan routes — the dry highways that linked the coast, where you got fish and guano and the cool sea fog, to the highland valleys, where you got maize and water and people. Llamas carried the trade. The geoglyphs marked the way. And the routes didn't stop at a modern border. They ran north, the same logic, the same thirst, the same need to call across distance — up into what was now Peru, up toward the river valleys, up toward a peninsula that stuck out into the Pacific like a thumb.

"It's one corridor," she said aloud, somewhere past Arica, with the sea grey on her left and the dunes climbing tan on her right. "Chile, Peru, it doesn't matter, the border's two hundred years old and the road's two thousand. It's one long sign-system. The Giant's at the desert end of it. And up the coast—"

"—is the Candelabra," said Jakobus.

It was the most words he'd said in an hour. Everybody waited, because Jakobus saying a thing twice was Jakobus underlining.

"Two signs," he said, and his eyes were out the windscreen, off the road, on the long grey horizon where the water went on past anything. "Same coast. One faces the land. One faces the sea." He let it sit. "A sign tells you which way to go. So." A pause you could have driven the truck through. "Where's the traffic going that needs both?"

---

Theo lifted the camera then. He didn't decide to. The hands did it, the way the hands had done it for fifteen years, and he caught Jakobus in profile against the bright window — the weathered face, the eyes that read made things — and then he caught himself doing it and almost lowered it again, the old argument, *whose is this, am I taking or am I keeping,* and then he thought: this one's allowed. A man looking at a horizon is allowed. He clicked once and put the camera down in his lap and felt, for once, that he'd taken nothing that wasn't given.

"There's a problem with your corridor," he said to Mara, because someone had to be the fish.

"There's always a problem. That's the job."

"You can't read the Candelabra from the corridor." He gestured at the land, the dunes, the road. "The Giant — fine, you stand on the pampa, you get the angle, the caravan reads it, beautiful, infrastructure, I buy it. But the Candelabra faces the *water.* A hundred and seventy metres, cut two feet deep into the hill so the wind can't have it, and the literature says you can see it from nineteen kilometres out — *to sea.* So it's not for your caravans. It's not for anybody on your corridor. It's for somebody in a boat." He spread his hands. "Who's in the boat, Mara? You've built me a beautiful land map and the last sign points the wrong way."

Mara opened her mouth and shut it.

That was the hole, and she knew it was the hole, and the thing about the real holes is that you can feel them as a small cold draught coming through a wall you thought was solid. The Giant had a hole — Briones, in *Antiquity,* 2006, the peer-reviewed idea that the rays were a working lunar gnomon, that they pointed at where the moon set at the turn of the season, that a desert farmer could read the rains off a man's stone crown. Checkable. Unproven. A draught you could feel in the room.

But the Candelabra's hole was worse, because the Candelabra had no *floor.* Nobody had dated the thing. Not once. The only number anyone had was the pottery in the dirt nearby — Paracas style, around 200 BC — and there was no chain, no proof, no rivet connecting that pottery to the hand that cut the sign. A hundred and seventy metres of trident aimed at the open ocean, undated, unsigned, its makers unknown and its message addressed to *the sea,* and the only people who could read it were people you couldn't ask, in boats you couldn't find, going somewhere the land road didn't go.

"I don't know who's in the boat," Mara admitted.

"Good," said Jakobus.

She turned around in her seat. "Good?"

"You don't know." He didn't smile, exactly. The corner of his mouth conceded something. "That's the right amount of knowing. The wrong amount is when you decide." He looked back at the road. "Somebody up there has already decided."

---

He meant Pisco. He meant the news Esi had been reading off her phone for the last hour and saving up, because Esi saved bad news the way some people save wine, for when it would land.

"Right," Esi said now, taking the cue. "So. The consortium. The heritage-plus-tourism people. They've moved up the coast ahead of us. Filed for the Paracas reserve. Restricted-access research permit, the whole apparatus." She scrolled. "And they've got a line, and the line is good, that's the horrible part. *Securing the meaning of the Candelabra for the Peruvian people.* Interpretive centre. Boardwalk. A plaque." She looked up. "A plaque, Mara. They want to put a plaque on a sign that nobody has ever read."

"What does the plaque say?"

"That's the thing. They haven't decided. There's a committee. The committee's going to *fix* it. Pick the meaning. Datura beacon, or Viracocha, or trident-of-the-storm-god, whatever scans best for the brochure, and then carve it in bronze and bolt it down and that's what it means now, forever, the end."

Theo watched Mara's face do the wayfinding thing, and then do something else, something tighter, and he recognised it because he'd worn it himself: the look of a person who wants very badly to be the one who decides, who knows they'd decide *well,* and who has just understood that the wanting is the trap.

"We could get there first," Mara said quietly. "We could read it first. We could—"

"Could what," said Jakobus. Not unkind. Just the question, set down flat on the table, his hand taken away.

Mara didn't answer that one either. Out the window the sea went on, grey on grey, past the edge of every map she'd ever built, and somewhere up the coast a hundred and seventy metres of someone's answer faced the water and waited for a reader it had been waiting two thousand years for, while a committee with a good heart and a bronze plaque drove the same road, faster, with more money, toward the same hill.

"Drive," Mara said.

Jakobus drove. The line of the road ran out ahead of them, the engineer's line, the easy gradient, the route drawn by money — and somewhere under it, or beside it, or older than it, the other line ran too, the one drawn by thirst and the sea and the long human need to call across a distance to someone you can't see.

The corridor was a map. The map had two signs. One faced the land and one faced the water, and the people racing them to the second one had already chosen what it said.

The crew had not. That was the whole of what they had. They went north to keep it.
