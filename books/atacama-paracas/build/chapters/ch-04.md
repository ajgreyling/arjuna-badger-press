# Chapter 4 — The Hole: The Rays and the Moon

The thing about a 119-metre man is that you cannot photograph him from your own height. You can stand at his feet — and we did, in the blue hour before the wind came up — and look along the run of him up the hillside, and he refuses to assemble. From the ground the Giant is a topographic rumour: a darker patch here, a paler shoulder there, a leg that goes on too long because the slope eats perspective. He is built to be read from somewhere you are not standing.

Mira found the somewhere first. She always does.

"You're in his armpit," she said, not unkindly, from forty metres downslope and to the left. "Come stand in mine."

I went and stood in hers. And there he was — the whole of him, the rectangular head, the stepped torso, the legs apart in that pose every reconstruction calls *authority figure* and every five-year-old calls *robot* — and the rays.

Four off one side of the head. A spray of them. Antennae, if you've been online too long; a crown, if you read the museum placard; a headdress, if you've read the slightly better placard. They radiate from the skull at angles that look, from Mira's armpit, deliberate to the point of fussiness. Somebody cared about those angles. Somebody walked this hillside a thousand years back and decided the rays went *there*, and not three metres over, and the wind has been trying to vote on the matter ever since and losing.

"Briones counted them," Mira said. She had the *Antiquity* paper printed and folded into eighths in her jacket, soft at the creases from being read in the truck. Mira is our systems person — autistic, allergic to the word *intuition*, fond of the word *grid*. She reads a landscape the way other people read a spreadsheet that's lying to them. "Two thousand six. He looked at the rays as a set of azimuths. As bearings."

"Bearings to what."

"That's the whole paper." She handed it to me. "Bearings to where the moon sets. At the right time of year."

---

Let me give you the official story, straight, because it deserves better than the gloss.

The Atacama is the driest non-polar desert on Earth. Some weather stations here have measurement records and no rain in them — not *little* rain, *no* rain, a column of zeroes going back further than the instrument's warranty. People lived here anyway. Caravans of llamas moved salt and maize and dried fish and copper through it along routes you can still see scratched into the pampa: faint parallel grooves, hundreds of metres of them, the negative of a thousand years of hooves and feet.

And along those routes, the people who walked them left signs. Something like five thousand geoglyphs across the Atacama — llamas, men, birds, suns, abstract grids — and a great many of them sit exactly where a caravan would want a landmark. A turn. A water source. A pass. They are not, mostly, decoration. They are infrastructure. They are the desert's signage, painted by scraping the dark desert varnish off the pale ground beneath, or by piling dark stones onto light, and they have lasted because here nothing washes anything away.

The Giant at Cerro Unitas is the king of them. Hundred and nineteen metres. Largest prehistoric human figure known on the planet. Built by Andean cultures sometime around AD 1000 to 1400 — the date is a span, not a point, because nobody scratched the year into his shoulder. The standard reading: a deity, a shaman, a lord. A figure of authority presiding over the route, the way a saint presides over a crossroads. That reading is sober, sourced, and almost certainly partly true.

Almost.

---

The LENS came back from the ridge with his camera down at his thigh, which is how you know he's been thinking instead of shooting.

"I can't get him," he said. "Not the whole of him with the rays sharp. I get the body or I get the rays. There's no station where both come good at once."

"There's Mira's armpit."

"For your eye. Not for a lens at this focal length without going up in the —" He tipped his head at the drone case, which we did not open, because the consortium's access filing had a no-fly clause in it and we were, that week, being scrupulously legal in a way that cost us things. "The geometry's not built for a camera. It's built for a person standing in a particular place."

"Which place."

He didn't answer, because Cira had come up the path.

---

Cira is the wayfinder. Aymara on her mother's side, and the reason we had permission to be on the hill at all was that she had decided, provisionally, that we were not the consortium and might not become it. She wore a wind-cracked jacket and the particular stillness of someone who has spent a lifetime in places where moving wrong costs you. She had watched us for two days the way you'd watch a dog you hadn't decided about yet.

"You found the standing place," she said to Mira. Not a question.

"His left armpit. About forty metres down."

"That's *a* standing place." Cira let that sit. Then, to me, because I'd been pacing the ray-angles off with a compass app like a tourist: "Put the phone away. It's lying to you about the slope."

It was. The phone's compass doesn't know the hillside tilts the rays toward you; it gives you flat azimuths for a thing that lives on a tilt. Briones's whole method had to correct for that — for the fact that a ray painted on a 20-degree slope and read from below points somewhere the flat math won't tell you. Mira had a column of corrected figures. They didn't match my phone. Of course they didn't.

Cira watched me put the phone away, which I think was the test.

---

Here is the hole, and I want to set it down with cold hands, because it is the kind of thing that gets oversold the instant you stop being careful.

In 2006, in the journal *Antiquity* — peer-reviewed, the real thing, footnotes and all — Luis Briones published a study of the Atacama geoglyphs that included a proposal about the Giant. The rays from the head, he suggested, might not be only a crown or only an antenna of the spirit. Read as bearings, certain of them point toward where the moon sets at particular phases — and the moon's setting position swings north and south across the year and across its longer cycle, and if you knew how to read that swing, you would know where you were in the year. You would know, in a land with no rain to warn you and no green to read, when the brief possible season of water was near.

A lunar gnomon. A calendar built into a god's head. The rays as the shadow-stick of a clock whose hand is the moon, and whose face is the desert horizon.

That is the hypothesis. Published. Citable. Real.

It is also unproven. Briones proposed it; the field did not rise up and confirm it. The alignments are suggestive, not nailed; with enough rays and enough lunar setting positions across a complex cycle, a careful skeptic will tell you — correctly — that you can find a match for almost anything if you go looking, and that the burden is on the claimant. Nobody has stood on that hill across a full set of seasons with the instruments and the patience to either close it or kill it. It floats. It has floated for nearly twenty years, peer-reviewed and unresolved, which is a strange and honest place for a fact to live.

"So is it true?" the LENS asked Mira. He asks the cleanest questions of any of us.

"It's *checkable*," Mira said. "That's not the same thing. Checkable means somebody could go up there with a theodolite and a lunar ephemeris and stand here for fourteen months and find out. Nobody has. So it sits in the paper being possible." She refolded the printout to its eighths. "I hate it. It's my favourite kind of fact."

---

The fish, who travels with us in spirit and in the form of a sticker on the drone case, would like to point out that *a god whose haircut is also a clock* is exactly the sort of claim that ought to set off every alarm in your house, and that the phrase *if you know how to read the swing* is doing an Olympic amount of work, and that the moon sets in a *lot* of places over its eighteen-year wander, and that pointing four rays at the sky and finding the moon is approximately as hard as pointing four fingers at a beach and finding sand.

Noted. The fish is not wrong. The fish is also not the one who built a hundred-and-nineteen-metre man on a hillside positioned for one viewing angle and then bothered to get the ray-angles *fussy*.

Both things sit in the room. That's the job.

---

We tried, the day before, to make our own line. This is the part of the work I least like to write down.

The cost — the potato, as Jakobus calls it, the moment the easy story turns out to be a quiet monster of difficulty — had come the previous afternoon, downslope on a patch of pampa Cira said we could disturb because nothing was there. We laid out a figure. Just a man. Forty metres, a tenth of the Giant. We had survey pegs, a measuring wheel, a line-laser, two phones, and four people who'd planned it on graph paper in the truck.

We scraped the dark crust off in the shape of a man.

From the ground it looked like a man. From Mira's standing place, forty metres back, it looked like a drunk had described a man to another drunk. The legs splayed wrong. One arm was longer because the slope had eaten our judgement exactly the way it eats the phone's compass. We hadn't corrected for the tilt. The Giant's makers had. That's not mysticism; that's craft, accumulated, taught, the difference between a person who walks the hill every season and a person who flew in with a line-laser.

And in the morning the wind had been at it. Our man had a smeared edge already, the scraped crust filling back with blown grit. Give it a week. Give it the season. Gone.

The Giant has been up there a thousand years, his ray-angles still fussy, because somebody knew the wind and built against it, and because the Atacama, having no rain, has no eraser. We made a smear. They made a clock. Maybe a clock. The gap between our smear and their possible-clock is the gap this whole book lives in.

---

Cira waited until the light went long and the rays' shadows lengthened on the hillside before she walked us down to the caravan path.

You can feel the old route under your boots before you see it — the ground packed harder, the parallel grooves a darker line in the raking light. People walked here for centuries. She put us on it, single file, facing the way a caravan coming up from the coast would face, and she walked us north until she stopped.

"Here," she said. "Now look back up."

From the caravan path — not from Mira's armpit, not from the LENS's ridge, from the *path*, the place a tired walker with salt to sell would actually stand — the Giant came up over the slope, and the rays stood off his head against the open western sky. The sky where the moon would set.

"This is the place to read him," Cira said. "Not the place for your camera. The place for a person who has walked four days and needs to know how long until the water comes." She didn't point at any one ray. She didn't say *that one marks the moon*. She let us stand there and feel the line of sight do its own work — head, rays, horizon, the seam where they all meet — and she watched our faces instead of the hill.

"Briones says the rays mark the moon," I tried.

"Briones wrote a paper," Cira said. "I'm showing you the path. From the path, the rays are where the sky is. You can decide what that means." A pause that had a smile somewhere far back inside it. "Or you can leave it open. That's allowed too. We've left it open a long time. It hasn't hurt anyone."

---

So here is where the chapter has to end, and it ends on a thing standing up rather than lying down.

The official story is true as far as it goes: a deity, a lord, a route-marker for the caravans, a sober and well-sourced reading that explains the placement and most of the figure. It explains the *where*. It does not, quite, explain the rays — why they are angled with such care, why they spray off only the one side, why they stand precisely against the western horizon when you read them from the path a real traveller used.

And the one extra thing — the lunar reading, peer-reviewed and twenty years unconfirmed — the official story doesn't close. It can't. Nobody has gone up that hill with the moon and the instruments and the seasons and made it surrender. The gap is real, and citable, and it is shaped exactly like a question someone declined to answer.

Cira walked back up the path ahead of us, into the last of the light, and did not look back to see whether we'd understood. It wasn't ours to understand. It was hers to show, and ours to carry north — to a coast where a different sign waits, cut deeper, undated, aimed not at the moon but at the sea.

The fish, for the record, is still muttering about the haircut.

I am still standing in the wind, looking at the rays against the horizon, deciding nothing.
