# Chapter 19 — Departure: The Question Remains

The trucks were packed wrong. That was the first thing Sefi noticed, and the noticing was a small, familiar relief — a problem with a shape, edges she could hold. The drone cases had gone in before the water, which meant somebody would have to unpack the water at the first checkpoint, which meant the gear would shift, which meant — she stopped herself. Not her truck. Not her job anymore. The job was done, or the part of it that was hers to do.

The bay at Paracas did the thing it did every morning before the wind came up: it lay flat and grey and patient, the colour of a thing waiting to be told what it was. Out beyond the point, a single fishing boat sat with its engine off, and Sefi watched it the way she'd been watching boats for eleven days now — to see whether the man at the tiller would look up at the hill.

He looked up at the hill.

Everyone here looked up at the hill. It was not a thing you decided to do.

"You're going to miss it," Mara said, coming up beside her with two cups of coffee made from the last of the good stuff and the rest from the terrible stuff, and Sefi could tell which was which by the colour and took the good one without asking, which was a freedom you earned across eleven days. Mara had her camera over her shoulder still, but the cap was on the lens. She'd been doing that more. Cap on. Letting the morning be a morning and not a frame.

"Miss what," Sefi said.

"The not-knowing." Mara nodded up the slope, to where the Candelabra cut its three deep arms into the saline crust of the hillside, 170 metres of trident aimed out past them at a horizon nobody could stand on. "You like it. The hole. You've been happier all week than I've seen you in a year and it's because nobody can close the lid."

It was true and Sefi didn't say so, which was its own kind of saying so.

---

Let's be fair to the people leaving, and fair to the thing they were leaving. Because the official story is decent and the people who hold it are not fools.

The Candelabra is loosely Paracas. That's the honest version of the attribution, with *loosely* doing real work. Down the coast at Nazca the lines fan out across the pampa in their hummingbirds and spiders and impossible straightaways, and the Candelabra has always been read as their coastal cousin — a ritual sign, a sacred mark, kin to that great catalogue of figures scraped into the earth by people who knew exactly what they were doing. Pottery in the Paracas style sits in the soil nearby, dated to around 200 BC, and that's a real date, measured by real people, and it's the firmest thing anyone has.

It is also not a date for the geoglyph. It is a date for some pots.

This is the part the consortium hated to write on its placards and the part that is simply, checkably true: nobody has ever dated the Candelabra itself. There is no carbon in a scrape. You cannot run a mass spectrometer over the *absence* of dirt. The pots are nearby, the way a coffee cup is nearby a mountain. They might be the makers' cups. They might be a tourist's, two thousand years early.

So you have a 170-metre sign, cut two feet deep into hardened, salty ground — much deeper than the Nazca lines, deep enough that the crust seals the trench and the wind that erased everything Sefi's crew had tried to draw all week sails clean over the top of it without taking a grain. A sign engineered to last. A sign engineered to be *seen* — not from the land behind it, but from the water in front of it, nineteen kilometres out, where the only audience is somebody on a deck.

Maker unknown. Date unknown. Purpose unknown. Built to be read by people on boats, by a coastal culture, and aimed, with deliberate care, at the one place its makers could never have stood to check their work.

That is not a gap you can talk your way across. That is the hole, and it does not close because you're tired of standing next to it.

---

Daniela came down the track with the consortium's site manager, and the two of them weren't arguing, which after eleven days felt like its own small monument.

The site manager — Reyes, a careful man with a careful folder and the haunted patience of someone who genuinely loved the thing he'd been hired to fence off — had stopped trying to get the crew's data. That fight was over. The crew had said no, and the no had cost something, and Reyes had gone away and come back two days later not with a better offer but with a worse mood and, eventually, with the beginning of an idea Daniela had planted and let him think was his.

"They're keeping the lower path open," Daniela told them, falling into step. "Reyes argued for it. The fishermen keep their sightline. No fence between the village and the water."

"He argued for it," Sefi repeated.

"He argued for it." Daniela's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. She was the custodian here, the archaeologist who'd been suspicious of them on day one and had every reason to be, and she had let them in exactly as far as she chose and not a metre further, and the thing she'd given them in the end was not an answer. It was a boat. She'd put them on a fishing boat and let them watch a man use the Candelabra to find his way home, and she had said nothing about what the trident *meant*, because the meaning was not the point and never had been, and she'd watched to see whether they understood that the meaning was not the point.

They had. Eventually. That was the whole education of the trip.

"You'll write it up," Daniela said. It wasn't a question, but it had a question folded inside it.

"We'll write what we saw," Sefi said. "The depth. The crust. The line of sight. The pottery's a pot, not a date. We'll write that you didn't tell us what it's for."

"Good," Daniela said. "Because I didn't."

---

Here is the thing the crew had learned the hard way, with their hands, which is the only way Sefi ever learned anything that stayed.

They had tried to make one. Early on, before the humility set in, they had marked out a modest figure on a flat stretch the rangers let them use — nothing sacred, nothing protected, a practice patch. They had a survey rig and a drone and two people who'd built geoglyph-replication into actual planning documents, and they had laid lines with stakes and string and good intentions.

Within a day the wind had taken the shallow ones. Within three the whole figure had gone soft at the edges, the way a chalk drawing goes soft in rain, except there was no rain, only the patient sandpaper of the air. From above, the drone showed them something that looked less like a sign and more like a wound that was healing badly. Wobbly. Off-axis. Apologetic.

Two feet deep, the Candelabra said back to them, from up on its hill. *Two feet deep into salt-locked ground, and aimed at a horizon I can't see.* You children scratched the surface and let the wind erase your homework. I was cut by people who understood that a sign you can't maintain has to be a sign that doesn't need maintaining, and who solved that problem so completely that I am still here arguing with you, and you don't even know my name.

The potato is not a metaphor. The potato is the blisters on Toma's hands and the data on the drone's card and the simple, humbling, *physical* fact that the easy story — *they just dug a trench* — gets harder the longer you stand in the trench yourself.

---

They drove north out of Paracas with the windows down because the air conditioning had died at Cerro Unitas and nobody had fixed it, and the desert came up around them, the driest non-polar desert on the planet, where it does not rain in any sense a wet country would recognise the word, and somewhere far behind them and far south the Atacama Giant stood 119 metres tall on his bare hill with his crown of rays.

Sefi had not stopped thinking about the rays.

That was the other hole, the one she'd carried out of Chile, and it itched worse than the Candelabra's because it was *almost* a thing you could test. Luis Briones had published it in *Antiquity* in 2006 — peer-reviewed, sober, no aliens, no antennae, no broadcast to the stars: the suggestion that the rays radiating from the Giant's head might function as a lunar marker, the moon setting against particular rays at particular times, tied to the rhythm that mattered most in a place with no rain — the turn of the season, the moment to plant, the moment the meltwater might come.

A gnomon. A clock built of dirt and a hill and the sky.

It was the most beautiful idea Sefi had ever been handed and she could not have it. Because it was unproven. Briones had floated it carefully, the way you float a thing you believe and cannot yet land, and twenty years on it was still floating, still a hypothesis, still checkable in principle and unconfirmed in fact, and the wayfinder up at Cerro Unitas — old Mamani, who read the pampa the way Sefi read a transit timetable — had watched her get excited about it and had not told her she was right.

He had not told her she was wrong, either.

He had taken her out to the old caravan path, the line the llama trains had walked for centuries between water and water, and he had shown her where you stood so that the Giant resolved out of the hillside clutter into a *figure*, sudden and total, the way a face appears in a wallpaper pattern and then will not un-appear. From the path. Only from the path. The Giant was placed for the walker, the trader, the person moving across the world with a load and a memory and the sky.

"He's a waymark," Sefi had said, because that part was solid, that part was infrastructure, one of five thousand geoglyphs in the Atacama and many of them strung like beads along the caravan routes, saying *here, this way, water ahead, you are not lost*.

"He's a waymark," Mamani had agreed.

"And the rays?"

And Mamani had looked at the rays, and looked at the moon, which was up in the afternoon sky pale as a thumbprint, and he had not answered, and the not-answering had been the most generous thing anyone gave her on the whole trip. Because he could have closed it. He had the standing to close it either way — to say *yes, the old people read the moon there* or to say *no, that's a foreigner's story scratched onto our grandfather*. He had the right. He'd just declined to use it. He'd left the door open and let her stand in the doorway and feel the wind come through.

That was the gift. The maybe was the gift.

---

Jakobus rode in the second truck and said almost nothing, which was how Jakobus rode in every truck. He'd come and gone across both sites like weather, never out front, always handing the moment back — to Daniela on the boat, to Mamani on the path, to Sefi when she was ready to take it. He read made things for one thing only: what they were *for*. And on the last evening in the Atacama, beneath the Giant, with Mamani beside him and the crew arranged on the cooling ground, he'd said the only sentence anyone would remember him for.

*The question isn't who made it. It's who it was made for.*

And then he'd shut up and let Mamani decide whether to answer, and Mamani had answered by pointing at the path. Not at the sky. At the path. The people moving. The Giant was for *them* — for the walker who needed to know the way and the season both, the map and the calendar fused into one enormous patient figure on a hill, a call across distance to anyone who knew how to read it.

A function. Grounded, physical, a tool in human hands. And the wonder didn't close when Jakobus named it. It opened wider. Because a thing that is *for* something is a thing with a story still running through it, and the story ran out past the edge of what anyone could prove, into the dark where the moon set against a particular ray, maybe, for somebody, once.

---

They stopped at a high point near dusk to let the radiator breathe, and Sefi got out and walked a little way off the road, where the desert was just desert, no Giant, no sign, no audience.

She felt the pull to go back. It was a specific feeling, located somewhere behind the sternum, the same place that lit up when a route resolved or a language clicked into a grammar — the pull of an unsolved thing, the *come back, sit with me, you almost had it*. She could spend a career on the rays. She could chase the lunar alignment until it confessed or refused, run the numbers, plot the settings, fund the survey, *close it*.

And she stood in the wind and chose not to.

Not because she couldn't. Because the closing wasn't hers. It was Mamani's, and Daniela's, and the wind's, and maybe nobody's, and a maybe that belongs to nobody is the rarest thing left in a world that fences and dates and explains. The crew had come down the coast to keep two doors from being nailed shut, and they'd done it, and the price of doing it was that the doors stayed open for *her* too. She didn't get to walk through. She got to stand in the draft.

Mara came and stood beside her, cap on the lens, looking at the same empty distance.

"You're not going to write the answer," Mara said.

"No."

"Even the bit you think you know."

"Especially that bit."

Behind them the radiator hissed and ticked. Far south, on a hill she couldn't see, a 119-metre figure waited for the moon. Far behind that, on another hill, a trident aimed itself patiently at an ocean, undated, unclaimed, sealed in salt against a wind that would outlast all of them and erase nothing.

Sefi looked at the horizon they couldn't stand on, and let it stay a question, and got back in the truck.
