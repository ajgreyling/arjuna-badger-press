A book-length chase down the Pacific coast, where a neurodiverse crew — out-read at every turn by local wayfinders — races to decode two monumental geoglyphs that point not to the sky, but to the sea: the Atacama Giant, a city-block-tall figure whose radiating "antennae" may have tracked the moon to time the rare desert rains, and the Paracas Candelabra, a 170-metre trident cut deep enough to outlast the wind, visible only to those at sea, its origin and meaning unclaimed by history.

**The interesting middle path:**  
- *Official story:* Atacama Giant is a deity or shaman, a marker for camelid caravans; Candelabra is a coastal cousin of the Nazca lines, loosely Paracas, a ritual or symbolic sign.  
- *The Potato:* Modern attempts at giant ground art, even with planning and survey tools, yield wobbly figures or lines erased by wind; the Candelabra's depth and the Giant's perfect symmetry from ground level remain stubbornly hard to replicate.  
- *The Hole:* For the Giant, Briones' peer-reviewed hypothesis: the rays as a working lunar gnomon tied to the rainy season, checkable but unproven. For the Candelabra, the gaping attribution gap: no one has ever dated it, and the only evidence is pottery nearby — a 170-metre sign, undated, its makers unknown, its message aimed at the ocean.  
- *The Wink:* Did the Atacama Giant's rays really mark the moon for desert farmers, or is that a story read back into the lines? Who was the Candelabra for — and how do you aim a sign at a horizon you can't stand on?  
- *What is it for:* Jakobus, standing with the local wayfinder beneath the Giant, finds the answer is not who made it, but for whom it was made: these are waymarks — signals for travelers and sailors, infrastructure for a world mapped by memory and sky. The function is a map, a calendar, and a call across distances, re-opening the wonder instead of closing it.

**The chase:**  
The crew — led by the ND hyperpolyglot, whose wiring makes them a natural at reading the logic of routes and signs — tries to document and "read" the geoglyphs before a well-funded, ideologically-driven heritage consortium can lock down access and turn the sites into untouchable relics. The adversary wants to fix the meaning (for tourism, for national pride, for grants); the crew wants to keep the question open, to let the land's own readers — the local archaeologists and Andean wayfinders — have the last word.

**The local who out-reads the crew:**  
A Peruvian coastal archaeologist (for the Candelabra) and a Chilean desert wayfinder (for the Giant) — each holds knowledge the crew can't Google or measure, and each decides how much to give. The answer, when it comes, is theirs.

**ND crew lead:**  
The hyperpolyglot/wayfinding specialist, whose neurodivergence is their edge — they see the pattern in routes, signs, and the logic of navigation.

**Jakobus's made-thing read:**  
He reads the geoglyphs not as art or myth, but as tools: infrastructure for navigation, orientation, and timing in a world where the land and sky are the only maps.

**Back-matter promise:**  
Every measurement, alignment, and open question in the story is real and checkable; the lunar-alignment hypothesis and the undated Candelabra are current scholarly gaps; all speculative claims are flagged as such. The reader leaves with the wonder intact: these are real marks, their purpose not fully known, the maybe left open for you to stand in the wind and decide.
