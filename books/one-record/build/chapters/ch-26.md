# 26 — What Would Kill It

> **[G]** · Act II-A · Technopark, Stellenbosch

G had never once asked the machine a question about his own work, and he wanted it minuted that the first time he did, he did it in front of five people on purpose.

"Not because I am frightened of the answer," he said. "Because I am frightened of *me*, alone, with the answer. Sit down, all of you. Dumisani, you are running it. I am not touching the keyboard."

"What am I asking it?"

"You are asking it whether the case data supports the claim that Record establishes the direction of time."

The room went quiet in a particular way.

Lwazi said, carefully, "That's the foundation."

"That is the foundation, yes."

"Why would you—"

"Because it has been eleven months since Antarctica," G said, "and a file was written before the thing it described, and I have spent eleven months telling all of you that this is not a violation of anything, and I would like to find out whether I believe myself."

---

The first return took four hours and came back:

**INSUFFICIENT GROUNDED RECORD.**

Dumisani read it out and looked up. "It's refusing."

"Read the whole thing. It never simply refuses; that is the point of it."

The full return was two pages. It cited what it had: the derivation, the frozen expressions, the measurement history, the seven-month bounded forecasting campaign, the Antarctic anomaly with its elimination log attached. It stated, in the flat generated prose that nobody had ever managed to make less irritating, which of the claim's components were supported by the cited material and to what degree.

Then it stated what the citations did not reach.

*The proposition requires a general relation between actualised events and directional structure. The cited material establishes a relation for the observed class of systems within the observed interval. Extension to the general case is not supported by grounded record. No refusal is made regarding the proposition's truth.*

"It's saying it doesn't know," Dumisani said.

"It is saying something much more precise than that and you should learn to read it." G leaned forward. "It is saying: your evidence covers a smaller region than your claim. That is not a machine failing to answer. That is a machine declining to launder a generalisation."

He had them narrow it. Twice.

He had them offer it the derivation directly — the whole thing, in his own notation, the eleven years — as cited material.

It read the derivation. It reported what the derivation assumed. It noted that three of the assumptions were not themselves grounded in the record and were, in its phrasing, *stipulated by the source*.

Which was correct. It was completely correct. G had stipulated them. He had said so, in print, in 2026, in a footnote he had spent two days on.

**INSUFFICIENT GROUNDED RECORD.**

---

Nobody in the room said anything for a while. Lwazi had gone a bit grey.

"It's telling *you* no," Dumisani said. "You wrote the corpus it runs on."

"I wrote a corpus. It runs on the record." G sat back, and the thing on his face was not what any of them had braced for. "Good."

"...Good?"

"Good. Look at what would have to be true for it to have said yes." He counted on his fingers, which he did when he was pleased and pretending not to be. "One: that a system built to compute over grounded record had somewhere acquired a weighting for the authority of the person who originated its framework. Two: that this weighting had survived every audit any of us has ever run. Three: that I had built, over eleven years, without noticing, a machine that agrees with me."

Silence.

"Half this building," G said, "has been treating this instrument as my opinion with better hardware. I have heard it. I have heard it in the corridor and in reviews and once, memorably, from a journalist who thought she was being kind. It is the single most dangerous thing anyone believes about this project, and I have never had a way to disprove it."

He nodded at the screen.

"Now I do. Minute it. Date it. Put the full return in the public record with the query and the narrowings, exactly as run."

Dumisani hesitated. "It's going to get quoted. *Machine rejects creator's central claim.* That's the headline."

"That is a much better headline than the alternative," G said, "and it has the additional advantage of being true."

---

Afterwards, in the corridor, Lwazi caught him.

"Can I ask you something that isn't scientific?"

"You may ask. I may decline."

"Did it hurt?"

G stopped.

He considered it seriously, which Lwazi had not entirely expected, and which he would think about for years.

"Yes," G said. "For about ninety seconds, and not for the reason you think. Not because it disagreed. Because for those ninety seconds I could feel myself constructing the argument for why the machine was being too strict."

"That's not unreasonable. It might be too strict."

"It might be. That is a legitimate technical question and somebody should investigate it, and it must not be me, and it must not be this month." He started walking again. "That is the whole discipline, Lwazi. Not never being wrong. Noticing the exact moment your reasoning starts working for you instead of for the question. Ninety seconds. I have been doing this for thirty years and it still took me ninety seconds."

---

He wrote a sealed note that night. It was, at eleven words, the shortest he ever filed:

*It refused me in public. This is the best day in years.*

Four years later, in a room in Stellenbosch, a woman from a commission would ask him why the consortium had continued to trust a system it could not fully audit, and he would not talk about architecture or provenance or the Witness Protocol.

He would ask them to bring up the return from that afternoon, and the narrowings, and the two pages of flat generated prose declining to support the central claim of his own life's work, and he would say:

"Because on the day it could have flattered me, in front of witnesses, with everything to gain, it declined to. Nothing else in this file is evidence. That is evidence."
