# Foreword

### *by Neil Crowlands* †

There is a hill in the south-east of Turkey, above the plain where the two rivers begin, that I have walked
more times than I can honestly count, and every time I climb it I have to teach myself the same lesson over
again, because the mind will not keep it. The lesson is this: *the strangest thing about Göbekli Tepe is not
in dispute.* It is in the textbook. And almost nobody — not the believer who wants it to be Atlantis, not the
debunker who wants it to be nothing — can bear to leave it there, plain and enormous, exactly where the
evidence put it.

Let me say the plain enormous thing. Eleven and a half thousand years ago, before anyone on Earth had sown a
field of wheat or fired a clay pot or fitted a wheel to an axle, people on that hill quarried limestone
pillars the height of two men and the weight of a small lorry, carved them into the shapes of *human beings*
— look at the arms, the long fingers folded over the belt, and tell me that is not a person — stood them in
rings, surrounded them with foxes and vultures and scorpions cut into the stone, and gathered there. *Then,*
later, came the farms. Then the villages. Then everything we are pleased to call civilisation. The temple did
not crown the city. The temple came first, on an empty hill, built by people we had filed under "wandering
hunters who could not possibly," and it is at least an open and serious question — asked not by cranks but by
the archaeologists who dug it — whether the gathering to build it is *what taught us to settle down at all.*

I have spent my life in the borderland between what is settled and what people insist on still seeing, and I
have learned one hard rule that almost nobody, on either side, will obey: *you have to hold the rigour and
the wonder in the same hand, and most minds can only hold one.* The believer holds the wonder and drops the
rigour, and reaches for a lost super-race to explain a thing that needs no explaining beyond the staggering
fact of *people.* The debunker holds the rigour and drops the wonder, and says "Neolithic ritual complex" in
a tone meant to make you stop looking, as though a label were the same as an understanding. Both feel, to
themselves, like the sober one. Both are half-blind. And the author of this book is that rarest third thing —
the one who can stand on the hill and say, with total seriousness, *this is exactly as old as the careful men
say it is, and it overturns the order we thought our whole story ran in, and I am not going to flee that into
aliens and I am not going to flee it into a shrug.*

He gives the diggers their full due, and more — he names them, he reveres the radiocarbon, he treats the
patient unglamorous science as the wonder it actually is, and he is *right* to. He does not need a single
thing from beyond the sky; the truth is stranger than the sky. But then he does the thing the careful men are
sometimes too careful to do out loud: he stands in the draught of the real, unclosed questions and does not
flinch. *Did they bury their own temple on purpose, switch it off and cover it over — or did the hillside do
that for them?* The honest answer, today, is that we argued ourselves into one story and are now arguing our
way back out, and the difference between *we know* and *we thought we knew* is the whole of the matter, and he
makes you feel it in your spine. There is a measure — a unit, a grammar of building — that seems to repeat
across hills that should never have shared one, and he shows it to you, and he does *not* tell you what it
means, because nobody honestly can, and to pretend otherwise would be to break the one promise this kind of
book exists to keep.

For there is a thriller here too — a real one, with clean hands and a glossy promise and a foundation that
would very much like the world to agree the hill is *solved,* catalogued, finished, safe in their keeping —
because a solved thing can be quietly emptied, and a mystery the public still loves cannot. And there is a
quiet, unplaceable man who reads a made thing the way I have spent my life trying to read the unexplained,
and reads it *better,* because he asks the question I learned too late to ask. Not *who made it.* Not *is it
real.* But *what is it for.* I have watched clever people spend careers on the first two and never think to
ask the third, and the third is the master key to the whole borderland, and this book turns it, and a door
opens in that hill that I did not know was there.

You may finish it certain the old order is overthrown. You may finish it sure we still know far less than we
pretend. If the author has done his work — and he has — you will finish it something better and harder than
either: a person willing to stand on a bare hill where the impossible plainly happened, and to say *I don't
know,* and to find in those three words not a defeat but the oldest feeling we have, the one that very likely
built the place — the one that makes a person look up from the field, and put down the seed, and go and help
raise the stones.

Go and stand on the hill. Decide for yourself. The answer is allowed to stay a maybe. That it is allowed to
is the whole gift of this book, and the whole labour of my life, and I am grateful, at the end of it, to have
been handed both by a younger and a clearer hand.

*— Neil Crowlands*
