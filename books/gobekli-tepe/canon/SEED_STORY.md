## The one-line seed
> *The oldest temple on Earth was built by people who hadn't invented farming, pottery, the wheel, or
> writing — and that's not a fringe claim, it's the textbook. So the only honest question left is the one
> nobody can answer: **why?***

## The frame — the interesting middle path (LOCKED)
The book does **not** say "aliens built Göbekli Tepe." It does **not** say "it's just a Neolithic
gathering-site, mystery solved, move along." Both miss the actual, *mainstream-accepted* shock. The book
lives in the gap:

- **The decoy = Stonehenge & the Pyramids** (the rhyme with Book 1's "Stonehenge is misdirection"). The
  world's eye is trained on the famous monuments — and Göbekli Tepe is **~6,000 years older than either,**
  sitting on a bare hill in southeastern Turkey, and most people have never heard of it. The famous thing
  draws the eye; the *real* thing is older, stranger, and was *deliberately buried.*
- **The official solve = "hunter-gatherer ritual centre."** **TRUE.** Excavated from 1995 by **Klaus
  Schmidt** (German Archaeological Institute); secure radiocarbon dates the oldest enclosures to **~9,500
  BCE.** Built by **foragers** — before agriculture, before pots, before metal. The book *reveres* this. It
  is real, hard, peer-reviewed archaeology, and it is *astonishing on its own terms.* (Then: "…and yet.")
- **THE CRACK (the real residue, and it's *mainstream*, which is what makes it lethal):** Göbekli Tepe
  **inverts the order of civilisation.** The textbook said you need farming → surplus → towns → *then* you
  can organise monumental architecture. Here the **monuments come first** — and the live, unresolved
  question, asked by the archaeologists themselves, is whether **gathering to build this drove the invention
  of farming**, not the other way round. That is not von Däniken. That is the consensus, and **it means the
  story of how we became us is genuinely open.** No aliens required; the truth is weirder.
- **The second crack (genuinely *disputed*, perfect for the maybe):** Schmidt argued they **deliberately
  buried their own temple** — ritually "switched it off" with backfill carried in from elsewhere. **Since
  his death in 2014, that's fallen out of favour** — newer evidence points to landslide and slope-wash. So
  the book gets a *real* live scholarly fight: *did they bury it on purpose, or did the hill bury it?* The
  narrator never settles it. The fish wink.
- **The real target = the ~90% still in the ground.** Geophysics shows **~20 enclosures, ~200 T-pillars** —
  and **only ~10% is excavated.** Göbekli Tepe is not a solved site; it is the **flagship of a whole buried
  tradition** (Karahan Tepe, Nevalı Çori, the Taş Tepeler / "Stone Hills"). The thing the world calls a
  finished mystery is **mostly unopened.** Somebody wants in before the people whose ground it is.

## Real-world load-bearing facts (the *Why Files* "here's the hole" beats)
*(All sourced in [`../../../canon/SITE_DOSSIERS.md`](../../../canon/SITE_DOSSIERS.md) §D.12 — binding for
real-vs-claimed.)*
- **~9,500 BCE**, pre-pottery / pre-agriculture / pre-metal — **~11,500 years old**, ~6,000 before
  Stonehenge. Excavated from 1995 by **Klaus Schmidt**. Real, secure radiocarbon. USE IT straight.
- **Enclosure D's two central T-pillars: ~5.5 m tall, ~8–10 tonnes each** — and they are **stylised
  humans** (Pillars 18 & 31 carry low-relief **arms bending at the elbow, hands with individual fingers
  meeting over a belt/loincloth**; **Pillar 18 wears a fox-pelt loincloth and cradles a fox**). The
  pillars are *people.* That detail is checkable and uncanny — hang a scene on it.
- **Monuments-before-agriculture is the real, mainstream textbook-overturner** — the strongest *non-fringe*
  "the experts themselves don't fully understand this" in the whole series. Lean on it hardest.
- **The deliberate-burial claim is genuinely contested** (Schmidt's intentional-backfill vs the current
  landslide/slope-wash reading). A real scholarly fight to stage — don't resolve it.
- **The labour is *also* contested** — against "hundreds of workers," **E. B. Banning** argues **7–14
  people** could move a pillar with ropes + a lubricated track. (So the book *resists* the "impossible
  workforce" over-reach — Naila/Jakobus do the arithmetic; it's astonishing without being "impossible.")
- **~20 enclosures / ~200 pillars / ~8 hectares — only ~10% dug.** The **Taş Tepeler** sister-sites share
  the T-pillar DNA — a whole regional tradition, ~90% still buried.
- **Accuracy caveat (honour it):** newer work found domestic structures, heavy cereal processing, and a
  water system on site — softening Schmidt's "temple of nomads, nobody lived here." The *monuments-or-
  farming* astonishment survives; the "purely a sanctuary" line is being revised. The book uses the *honest
  current* picture, not the romantic old one.

## The chase (the propulsion — Book 1's template, ported)
A **clean-handed adversary** races the crew to *take* what they're racing to *read*: a
plausible-deniability outfit — a **"heritage foundation" / private antiquities concern / survey company** —
buying up excavation access, geophysics data, and the **unopened-90%** before the local team and community
can protect it. They weaponise the *official story* (the rhyme with Book 1's "the debunk is the cover"):
here, **"it's a solved site, just a gathering-place"** is the cover that lets them quietly strip the
*unexcavated* enclosures. Give the adversary a **mortal want and clean hands**, never a moustache
(masterpiece-campaign doctrine). The thing they want out of the ground is *provenance itself* — the oldest
made things on Earth — and the crew + the local stand between them and it.

## The "what is it for" (the grounded turn — re-opens wonder, never closes it)
The crew can establish *who* (foragers), *how* (ropes + a lubricated track + 7–14 people, the Banning
arithmetic), and *is-it-real* (yes, secure radiocarbon). The book turns on **what it's for** — and the
answer is a *function*, grounded and physical, that **re-opens** the mystery: the T-pillars are **people**
(the arms, the hands, the belt), and the enclosures are a **place the living gathered to stand with the
raised dead / the watching ancestors** — and the act of *gathering to build* is, plausibly, **the thing
that made them settle, and farm, and become us.** The monument didn't follow civilisation; the monument may
have *caused* it. The local hands the crew that reading (it's hers); the narrator never declares it proven.
The wonder gets *larger,* not smaller. (And the buried-or-not question stays a maybe to the last page.)
