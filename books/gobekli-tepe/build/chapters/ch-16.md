# Chapter 16 — The Measure

The drone came up out of the southwest a little after four, while the worst of the heat was still sitting on the plain like something that had been left there to die.

Frik heard it before he saw it. He always heard the machines first — the brain that couldn't filter the world into background and foreground had its uses, and one of them was that a high small whine on the edge of the wind arrived in him fully formed, demanding, while everyone else was still pouring tea. He set his glass down on the low table under Cemal's awning. His left shoulder jumped twice, the old hitch, the one his physiotherapist in Bloemfontein had called *the gun-cock*. He let it.

"Up there," he said, and his hand went up and pointed, steady, because the pointing was a deliberate thing and the deliberate things he could mostly hold. "Two o'clock. Coming over the wadi."

Mara was already moving. She had the long lens up off her knee and against her eye before the rest of them had found the dot, tracking it across the white sky, her thumb on the focus ring, and her whole body had gone into the stillness that came over her when she worked, the stillness that was the opposite of calm, that was a held breath dressed as professionalism.

"It's theirs," she said. "Trust livery. White, the little crest on the boom." A pause while she rode it. "Survey pattern. Lawnmower lines. They're not done with this hill."

"Eight days," Eylül said. She didn't look up. She was peeling an apple for Cemal's grandson with a small black-handled knife, one long unbroken curl of skin, and the boy was watching it like it was the only thing happening in the world, which for him it was. "Eight days they're gone, and they leave the eyes behind." She handed the boy the apple. She said something to him in Kurdish, low, and he laughed and ran off toward the goats with the curl of skin trailing from his fist like a streamer.

"They're allowed?" Frik asked. "Just — to fly that. Over the dig."

"They have *page eleven*," Eylül said, and the way she said it the words had quotation marks you could hang washing on. "Page eleven says they have a data-sharing interest. Page eleven is a clean white room with no doors in it. The directorate is fighting page eleven in an office in Ankara with the air conditioning broken." She set the knife down. "So yes. They are allowed to look. Looking is free. Looking is what they are best at."

Cemal said something then, leaning forward off his cushion, his hand moving toward the hill, and Eylül translated without being asked, the way she'd been doing all afternoon, the two voices braided together so easily you forgot one of them was for the foreigners.

"My uncle says: they have been flying over this plain since before the foreigner — the German, Schmidt — since before he ever came. Surveyors. The state, the companies, the oil people who found no oil. He says the plain is very used to being measured." Eylül's mouth did something complicated. "He says it does not feel measured."

---

Naila was not watching the drone.

She had clocked it — she'd have to be dead not to, the whine cutting a clean diagonal across the ambient field of goat-bell and generator and the wind moving in the awning's loose corner — but she'd assigned it and moved on. A surveying UAV running a boustrophedon raster at, call it, sixty metres altitude, ground sample distance fine enough to count the courses of stone but nothing the directorate's own photogrammetry hadn't logged in 2019. It would learn nothing. The hole in the hill was not a thing you could photograph from above. She'd said as much to Mara two valleys ago and Mara hadn't argued.

She was watching the numbers. She'd been watching them all afternoon, in the good way, the way that felt like cold water going down: the page from the museum library that Eylül had let her copy out longhand because the photocopier was broken and had been broken, by the look of the dust on it, since roughly the late Holocene. Pillar heights. Enclosure diameters. The spacing of the ring-walls. The recurring interval she'd started calling, privately, in the part of her head that named things, *the unit*.

It kept showing up. That was the whole of it and it was enormous. The central pair in Enclosure D — the two great Ts, five and a half metres, the People, the ones with arms — sat on a baseline. Measure the baseline. Measure the gap to the inner ring. Measure the modules at Karahan, twenty-odd kilometres east, a different hill, a different ridge, in the photographs and the published plans Eylül had laid out for her on the awning's plank table, weighted at the corners with tea glasses. The same interval. Not exactly the same — nothing eleven and a half thousand years old was *exactly* anything, the stone slumped, the slope crept, the German's trenches and the new Turkish trenches sampled different parts of a thing that was mostly still in the ground. But within the error. Within the kind of error you got from people, not from coincidence.

A hand's-width. A forearm. Some reach of the body, repeated, carried.

"It's not nothing," she said, to no one, which was how she said most of the things she most meant. "The measure. It's not nothing. It's under the noise but it's there. You'd need every plan, properly. Total-station, not the old tape data. But it's there."

"There you go again," Frik said, gently, the way you'd say it to someone you liked, "with your *it's there.*"

"It's there," she said.

"Ja. Okay." He was smiling. The smile cost him a fast double-blink and a click of the jaw, the tics riding the warmth out the way they rode everything out, and nobody at the table so much as flickered, and that — the not-flickering — was the thing Frik had come four thousand kilometres and into a war over a hill to find. A table where his body was just weather. "So what does it *mean*, the measure. Same ruler, two hills."

Naila looked at the hill. The drone scratched its quiet lines across the sky above it.

"I don't know what it means," she said. "I know it's not random. That's a different sentence from knowing what it means. People want those to be the same sentence and they're not." She put her finger on the longhand column. "This is where it goes wrong. This is exactly where someone stands up and says *a lost civilisation* or *a master race* or — the other one, the worse one —"

"Aliens," Frik supplied, and a muscle by his eye jumped on the word, and it was not a joke, it just arrived.

"Aliens," Naila agreed. "Because a shared measure across sites that shouldn't share one *feels* like it needs a big answer. And it doesn't. The boring answer is the staggering one. The boring answer is: the same people. The same *culture*. A way of building you learned from your mother's mother, a length you carried in your own arm, that travelled the twenty kilometres between hills the way songs travel, the way a recipe travels, because someone walked it." She stopped. "I can prove the measure's real. I can't prove who carried it or why. So I won't say. I'll say the true small thing and shut up about the big one."

"That's the most boring superpower I've ever heard," Frik said, and meant it as the highest possible compliment, and Naila knew it, and the corner of her mouth moved.

Eylül had been listening. She had a way of listening that didn't look like listening, that looked like apple-peeling and tea-pouring and watching her uncle's hands, and then she'd say one thing that proved she'd had the whole conversation in her hands the entire time.

"You're afraid of saying the wrong big thing," she said to Naila, "so you say no big thing. That's a good fear. Keep it." She turned a photograph around on the table so it faced them — a glossy thing, a frame from the Trust's film, the one that had been pulled. *Cradle: The First Temple.* Anton Reyes had handed copies around the village like sweets, in the first days, before any of them understood what the foundation was. In the frame, the central pillars of Enclosure D glowed honey-gold under lighting that had been hauled up the hill at considerable expense, and a sonorous caption hung beneath them: *AT THE DAWN OF TIME, A LOST PEOPLE BUILT A MIRACLE THEY COULD NOT EXPLAIN.*

"This," Eylül said, "is the wrong big thing. Two wrong big things, actually, in one sentence, which is efficient of them." She tapped *lost*. "Not lost. We know who. Hunter-gatherers, here, on this ground, the people who became the people in that village over there pouring you tea — there isn't a clean wall between them and us, that's a thing the film wants you to feel, the *lostness*, so you'll think it needs rescuing." She tapped *could not explain*. "And this. This is the one that does the work. *Could not explain.* Make the builders into mysteries and you make their hill into a mystery, and a mystery has no owner, a mystery belongs to whoever can fund the helicopter." She sat back. "They built it. They could explain it perfectly. We're the ones who can't, and we've decided to call that *their* mystery instead of our ignorance, because it sells better and it costs us nothing."

The drone changed its note. They all heard it. It had reached the end of its raster and it banked, the white speck tilting, and it came back over the hill on the return line, lower now, and Mara tracked it the whole way with the long lens to her eye and her breath held and her finger nowhere near the shutter.

---

Here is what Mara could see, and what the drone could see, and the difference between them, which was the whole of her work:

The drone saw the hill. Ground sample distance fine enough to count stones. It would go home tonight to a server in a building she'd never enter and it would be stitched into an orthomosaic, a flat true god's-eye map of every exposed pillar, accurate to the centimetre, completely correct, and it would be a lie the way an orthomosaic is always a lie, because it would have no light in it that meant anything and no person in it at all.

Mara saw the table. She saw Frik's shoulder hitch and settle, hitch and settle, a metronome no one had asked for that the table had absorbed into its weather. She saw Naila's finger on the longhand column, the stillness of someone for whom the numbers were not cold but warm, were the warmest thing, were the place the noise stopped. She saw Eylül's hands, which were the most eloquent thing in the frame and which Mara would not photograph, because Eylül hadn't said she could, and the asking and the not-photographing had become, over these weeks, the actual content of Mara's days — the long slow undoing of the reflex that had made her name on other people's worst hours, the reflex she'd come here precisely because she could no longer obey.

She lowered the lens. Her hands were steady. They hadn't always been; there were mornings the camera still felt like the thing she'd been holding the day she stopped being able to hold it, in a different country, with a different smell. Today they were steady. She set the camera in her lap and looked at the table with her own eyes, which was its own discipline, the hardest one.

"He's not in any of it," she said.

"Who's not," Frik said.

"Jakobus." She nodded at the hill, at the drone, at the air. "They've flown that thing over us — what, four times now, since they left? And the day they did their big survey flyover, the real one, the calibrated one, the day they brought the second drone and ran it for three hours — " she stopped. "That was the day Cemal had him under here. The whole day. Tea and the long table and that thing they did with the — " she gestured, helpless, at the memory of two men who didn't share fifty words trying to argue about engines. "He was off every lens for the only hour it mattered. And I tried, after. I've tried for weeks. I have a thousand frames of this trip and I have him in maybe nine of them, and in all nine he's turned, or it's his back, or there's dust, or the focus walked, or he's exactly behind the awning post." A short breath that was nearly a laugh. "I'm a professional. I made my living being the person who got the shot. And I cannot get a clean shot of that man to save my life."

"He doesn't like cameras," Eylül said.

"It's not that he doesn't like them." Mara was working it as she said it, the way she'd learned to from these three, saying the true small thing and not reaching past it. "I've photographed a hundred men who don't like cameras. You get them anyway, the ones who don't like it, you get them best, the not-liking is *in* the picture. This is different. It's like —" and she stopped, because she'd been about to caption it, to put a sentence under it that named it, and that was exactly the thing she didn't do anymore. She left it. "I don't have a word for it. I just keep not having the shot."

Across the yard, by the low wall where the goats came through, the man himself was crouched on his heels in the late gold light, doing something with his hands that none of them could quite see — the field-kit spread on a cloth, the little tin he carried everywhere, the cayenne he swore by for reasons that had made sense in a desert two continents away and made no sense here and worked anyway, the always-clean white hanky folded into a square that no amount of this red dust seemed able to dirty. He had the water bag off the Beast's wing mirror and he was hanging it from a nail in the shade so it could weep itself cool the way it did, the way it had no business doing in this dry, and Cemal was watching him do it with the frank delight of a farmer watching a stranger's trick that he intends to steal.

Mara looked at the two of them. The light was perfect. It was the kind of light she'd flown into war zones to chase, low and gold and forgiving, raking across the wall, the two crouched men, the weeping bag, the goats coming through. It was the photograph. It would have been a beautiful photograph and it would have said something true about how a man carries his home across the world in a tin, and it would have belonged to nobody who'd consented to it, and she lifted the camera and put it to her eye and framed it and the focus walked.

It just walked. The lens hunted, the way good glass doesn't, racked past him and came back and sat down a half-metre short, on the wall, on a goat, on the bright nothing of the dust, and by the time she'd thumbed it back the light had moved a degree and the men had shifted and the picture was gone.

She lowered the camera.

"See," she said, to no one, and there was no fear in it, only a kind of laughter she didn't have to explain to this table. "Nine frames. Nine, in a thousand."

Naila looked up from her numbers. "It's not a thing," she said, mildly, because she could not help saying it, because she heard the shape of where the table's mood wanted to go and she put her small cold true hand on it before it could leave the ground. "The focus. It's not a thing. It's dust, and heat shimmer off the wall, and a long lens at the worst hour for it. There's no — there's nothing *in* it. It's nine out of a thousand because a thousand is a lot of frames and nine is exactly the number of accidents you'd expect." She said it to protect them. She said it because the worst big thing wears the costume of the best small mystery, and she would not let it in even here, even for a friend, even for a man she loved. "He's just a guy who's always behind the post."

"Ja," Frik said, and his jaw clicked, and he was grinning into his tea. "He's just a guy who's always behind the post." His shoulder jumped. "*Always behind the post,*" he said again, the echo riding out of him unbidden, and then, in the flat helpless honest voice that was his other body, the one that said the thing the room had agreed not to say — "but you can't photograph him."

It hung there. Nobody picked it up. Naila let it sit because it was, narrowly, true — Mara *couldn't* — and Naila only ever fought the false thing. Eylül smiled into her glass. The drone whined off toward the southwest, its lawnmower done, going home to its server to make its perfect lying map of every stone it could see and none of the ones it couldn't.

And out by the wall, the man no camera could hold stood up out of his crouch, and turned the water bag a quarter so it would weep on the other face, and said something short to Cemal in the four words of the local tongue he'd troubled himself to learn — a greeting, the one that meant *I see you,* the one he carried from home and laid down wherever he went like a coin on a counter — and Cemal laughed and clapped him on the arm, and the two of them stood looking not at each other and not at the camera but out at the hill, at the low round buried nine-tenths of it, the way men stand when they're looking at a thing one of them has made and the other one understands.

Because that was the question. Mara watched the back of him, the only angle she'd ever get, and thought it without saying it. He read made things. He'd walked up the hill the first day and stood in front of the great central pair, the five-and-a-half-metre People with their relief arms bent at the elbow and their long fingers meeting over the belt, the one in the fox-pelt with the fox held against him like a thing he loved, and he hadn't said *who* and he hadn't said *how* and he hadn't said *aliens* and he hadn't, mercifully, said *miracle.* He'd put his shades up on his head and looked at the stone people a long time and asked the only question that had ever interested him about anything anyone had ever built.

*What is it for.*

And the answer to that one wasn't his. It wasn't Naila's, who could prove the measure but not read it. It wasn't Mara's, who could frame the hole but couldn't fill it. It wasn't Frik's, whose body could say the true thing but couldn't tell you what the true thing was *for.* It belonged to the woman peeling apples for her uncle's grandson on the ground her family had farmed since before the German came, and she had been carrying it under the awning all afternoon, turning it over, deciding whether the foreigners had earned it yet.

"Eylül," Frik said. He'd felt it too — he felt everything, it was the curse and it was the gift. "You keep nearly telling us. The measure. The People with the arms. What it's *for.* You keep getting to the edge of it and then you peel another apple."

Eylül set down the knife. She looked at the hill, and then she looked at the four of them, the photographer who'd stopped consuming, the man whose body couldn't lie, the woman who'd say the small true thing and starve the big false one, and the road who'd gathered them and was standing out by the wall with his back to all of it, watching her hill go gold.

"Another time," she said — and then she heard herself, and a laugh came up out of her, real, the wariness going off her shoulders for the length of one breath. "No. No. I keep saying that. Cemal says I'm worse than the German." She wiped her hands on the cloth.

"Pour the tea," she said. "I'll tell you what it's for."

The drone was a dot now, and then it was gone, and the hill kept the only ninety percent that mattered.
