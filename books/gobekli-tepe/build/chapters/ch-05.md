# Chapter 5 — The Anatolia Heritage Trust

The brochure was on glossy stock, the kind that wants to be held.

Naila held it because Eylül had put it in her hands at the awning that morning and said *tell me what you see,* and because paper that good has a tell. The grain on the cover was a drone shot of Enclosure D at golden hour, the two central pillars throwing long shadows that did not exist at that latitude on the date the photo claimed in its caption corner. Somebody had composited the shadows. Somebody had wanted them longer.

She did not say that yet. She turned the page.

THE ANATOLIA HERITAGE TRUST. *Stewardship for what cannot speak for itself.* Beneath it, a man's photograph — open collar, no tie, the deliberate informality of money that has stopped needing to prove it has money. Anton Reyes, Director of Acquisitions and Conservation. Behind him, soft-focus, a conservation lab, a row of fragments laid on grey foam in numbered trays. The trays were the thing. Naila counted them without deciding to. Eleven. The eleventh was empty.

"He's coming up at noon," Eylül said. She was sitting on the upturned crate she always sat on, peeling the foil off a fresh brick of tea for Cemal, who was asleep in the shade with his cap over his eyes. "The provincial office set it. A *courtesy meeting.* The Trust is a registered partner on three sites in the Tas Tepeler. They have a research-access application sitting in Ankara with the museum's name on the cover sheet, which is how they'd like it to look. The museum doesn't love it. The museum can't stop it either."

"Why noon," Frik said. He was crouched on his heels at the awning's edge, watching a line of ants carry a dead beetle uphill, and his head jerked twice to the left, the small clean tic he carried like a comma. "Hot at noon. Bad light. Why would the camera guy pick the worst light."

Mara, who had not lifted her camera since the sun cleared the ridge, looked up from where she sat with the lens cap on in her lap. "Because he's not the camera guy. He brings the camera guy."

"Two of them," Naila said. She had reached the back page. Partners and Patrons, set in a typeface that cost more than the tea brick. She read the names down the column and stopped on one near the bottom and read it again, and the small machine behind her sternum that never quite switched off went *click.* "Mara. The drone shot on the cover. The shadows are wrong for the date. Somebody added them in post."

"Shows you what they want it to feel like," Mara said. "Older. Lonelier. Empty."

"Empty," Naila said, "is the whole document."

She laid the brochure flat on the table and the table rocked, because the table always rocked, and she had stopped trying to fix it on day one and started using it as a metronome. She put her finger on a line of body copy halfway down a page that was otherwise photographs of light through dust.

*Following decades of distinguished excavation, the central enclosures of Göbekli Tepe are among the most thoroughly understood monuments of the ancient world. The story is, in essence, told. The Trust's mission is to secure what remains — the unexcavated periphery — against the twin threats of neglect and unregulated tourism, and to ensure that the chapter, now substantially closed, is preserved for all humankind.*

"Read it out," Eylül said quietly. "The middle bit."

Naila read it out. The story is, in essence, told. The chapter, now substantially closed.

For a moment the only sound was the ants, and a generator coughing two terraces down, and the wind doing the thing the wind did here, coming off the plain in a long even press with no edge to it, the kind of wind that had been blowing across this exact ground for eleven and a half thousand years and would not be hurried by anyone's noon meeting.

"They've solved it," Mara said.

"They've *closed* it," Eylül said. "Different word." She set the tea brick down. "If it's solved, there's nothing left to find. If there's nothing left to find, there's nothing left to protect. If there's nothing left to protect —" She looked uphill, past the two of them, toward the long modern roof that sheltered the excavated enclosures, and past that to the bare brown swell of the rest of the hill, the ninety percent of it that no spade had ever touched. "— then who's going to make a fuss when somebody quietly buys the right to dig the rest of it on a *conservation permit* and ship whatever comes up to a lab in a country with friendlier export law. Nobody. Because the story's *told.* You can't loot a place that's already empty."

Naila looked at the eleven trays again. The empty eleventh.

"Ten percent," she said. "That's what's been dug. Schmidt's enclosures, the central ones, the famous ones. A through F, more or less. Ninety percent of this hill is still in the ground. And that's just this hill. There are — " she did the count she'd been doing for two days, the count that wouldn't leave her alone "— what, twenty enclosures here. Two hundred pillars, give or take, when you add the ones still buried. And it's not one hill. Karahan Tepe, Sayburç, the others. The whole — what did your survey call it."

"Tas Tepeler," Eylül said. "Stone Hills. A region. A *tradition.* Not a site."

"Right." Naila pressed her finger on the brochure where it said *the chapter, now substantially closed.* "You can't close a chapter when you've read one paragraph of one page of one book in a library nobody's catalogued. The arithmetic doesn't let you. It's not an opinion. It's not even close." She looked up. "Somebody who knows numbers wrote this. They wrote it *because* they know the numbers. You don't say *substantially closed* by accident over ninety percent. You say it on purpose, to a reader who won't check."

Frik's head jerked. "Lie," he said. Not loud. Just out, the way it came out of him, ahead of the part of him that decided things. "It's a lie." And then, quieter, almost to the ants: "Sorry. It's a lie though."

"You don't have to be sorry for being right," Eylül said, without looking round, and Frik's shoulders came down a centimetre, and Naila watched that happen and said nothing about it because saying something about it would have ruined it.

---

Reyes came up the walkway at ten to twelve, ahead of his noon, which Naila noted, because people who control rooms arrive early to own them before anyone else does.

He was not what the brochure photo had been. The photo had been informal-money. The man was tired-money — a good linen shirt gone soft with travel, a watch you'd miss if you weren't her, dust on his good shoes that he had decided not to mind. He came up the long boardwalk with one hand trailing the rail and he stopped, twice, the way people stopped who actually felt the place, and on the second stop he stood for a long moment looking at the central pillars in their roofed enclosure with his head tilted back, and his face when he didn't think he was being filmed was not a salesman's face. It was the face of a man looking at something he loved.

That was the part Naila hadn't expected and didn't like, because it didn't fit the arithmetic. The arithmetic said *liar.* The face said *believer.* Both could be true. Both being true was worse.

Behind him came the camera guy, exactly as Mara had said, a young man with a gimbal rig and a cargo vest, and behind *him* a drone in a padded case, and Mara's whole body changed when she saw the drone case — a stillness Naila recognised because she had her own version of it, the body bracing before the mind had named the thing. Mara set her own camera, capped, deliberately on the table. Made it a stone among stones. Took her hands off it.

"You must be the team," Reyes said, and his voice was warm and his English was the frictionless English of a man who had four languages and used the one in front of him. He looked at Eylül first, which surprised Naila a second time. "Doctor Demir. They told me in Ankara you were the one who actually knows this ground. I'm grateful you'd see me." He put out his hand and Eylül took it, once, and let go.

"You said courtesy meeting," Eylül said.

"It is. I genuinely mean it as one." He looked at the awning, at Cemal stirring under his cap, at the tea things. "May I?"

"Sit," Eylül said. It was not warm. It was correct. There is a difference and everyone at the table could hear it.

He sat. The cameraman hung back at the rail, and the gimbal swung, and Frik said, "He's filming the table," flat, and the cameraman, who clearly had not been told this team had a man who said the true thing out loud before anyone could pretend it wasn't happening, lowered the rig half a foot and looked at Reyes.

"Sani," Reyes said, without turning. "Not the people. The stones are public. The people aren't ours to take." He said it like a thing he believed, and the cameraman lowered the rig the rest of the way, and Mara looked at Reyes then, hard, the first full look she'd given him, because he had just said her own rule back to the air with no idea it was her rule, and Naila watched the cost of that move across Mara's face — the small disorientation of hearing your principle in the mouth of the person you'd come ready to fight.

Eylül poured him tea. The hospitality was the territory. He understood that; he wrapped his fingers round the little glass and did not gulp it, which was the right thing to do, and Naila hated, in a quiet bookkeeping way, how many right things he kept doing.

"I'll be plain," Reyes said. "Because you've read our materials and you've decided what you think of us, and you're probably most of the way right." He turned the glass a quarter-turn on the table, a small grounding gesture, and Naila clocked it as the same kind of thing she did with the rocking table. "I run a foundation that buys access. I won't pretty it up. We pay provincial governments, we pay landowners, we pay for geophysics surveys that the state can't fund and the universities can't afford to run at scale, and in exchange we get rights — to data, to selected excavation, to a portion of what comes up. People hear *private antiquities concern* and they picture a man in a back room with a stolen frieze. I understand the picture. I'd like to be measured against the actual alternative."

"Which is," Eylül said.

"Which is this." He gestured, gently, at the bare ninety percent of the hill above them, browned and patient in the noon glare. "Nothing happening. For years. Underfunded, understaffed, the most important archaeological site on Earth and there's a hosepipe leak in the conservation shelter that's been on the work order since spring. You know this better than I do. The site gets ten million dollars of attention and a hundred thousand dollars of actual money, and meanwhile the periphery sits there unmapped, unprotected, and every farmer's plough and every flash flood and every freelance metal-detector man with a Bulgarian buyer takes a little more before anyone with a permit ever sees it." He set the glass down. "I don't take it from the ground. I get to it first. I document it. I conserve it. And yes — I keep some. I keep enough to fund getting to the next thing first. That's the engine. I won't insult you by hiding the engine."

It was a good speech. Naila knew it was a good speech because parts of it were true, and the true parts were carrying the false part the way good cargo hides bad. She set the brochure down on the table between them, face up, the cover with the wrong shadows, and she put her finger on the line.

"*The chapter, now substantially closed,*" she read.

Reyes looked at it. Looked at her. There was a flicker, brief, of a man recalculating which kind of opponent he had.

"Marketing," he said. "Honestly. The donor deck writes itself in a register that —"

"Ninety percent," Naila said. "You've dug ten. You've read the introduction and you wrote *the story is, in essence, told* on the cover of a fundraising document. You know it's not. The number's in your own survey appendix. Page forty. You commissioned the geophysics that proves there are at least nine more enclosures under that slope you just gestured at, and then you printed *substantially closed* on the front." She wasn't angry. Anger blurred the count. She was just laying it out, the way you lay out the figures and let the gap speak. "You didn't write that for the museum. The museum can read page forty. You wrote it for the people who decide whether there's anything left worth protecting. You wrote *empty* on the door so nobody asks what's behind it."

The wind pressed. Cemal had stopped pretending to sleep; his cap was tipped back and he was watching Reyes with the flat ancient patience of a man whose family had farmed this exact plain since before the word foundation meant a charity.

Reyes did not bluster. That was the third thing Naila hadn't expected. He sat with it. He looked, for a second, genuinely caught, and then — worse — genuinely sad.

"You think I don't love this," he said. "You think because I have an engine I don't —" He stopped. He looked up at the hill. "I started in a museum basement in a country that lost half its heritage to a war I was too young to vote against. We catalogued what we could. We watched the rest go out the back of trucks while men in suits debated jurisdiction. I decided I'd rather be the truck than the debate." He turned the glass again. "If I'm in the ground first, with a camera and a conservator and a numbered tray, then whatever I keep, at least its *provenance* survives. At least we know where it stood and what it stood next to. The man with the Bulgarian buyer doesn't write that down. I do. That's not nothing. In this business that's nearly everything."

"Provenance," Eylül said.

"Provenance," Reyes agreed. "The story of where a thing comes from. Lose it and the object's still beautiful. But it's mute. It can't testify anymore." He almost smiled. "You and I want the same thing, Doctor Demir. I'm just willing to pay for it."

And there it was, Naila thought, the cleanest version of the trap, said by a man who half-believed it: *we want the same thing.* It was nearly true. That was its whole power. He wanted the provenance preserved. He just wanted to *own* it. He'd save the story of where everything stood, and then the story would be his to license, his to lend, his to lock in a friendly-jurisdiction lab and release in the shape that funded the next acquisition. The wonder, catalogued and shelved and *substantially closed.*

Frik, who had been very still, whose head had not jerked in a full minute, which was its own kind of warning, said suddenly, with no run-up, in a voice that came up out of him like water finding a crack: "He believes it. He's lying and he believes it."

Reyes turned to him. Not offended. Curious.

"That's a hell of a trick," Reyes said, gently. "Both at once."

"Ja," Frik said, and his shoulder hitched, and he looked away. "I know."

---

After, when Reyes had gone back down the walkway with his cameraman and his caged drone, Naila stayed at the rocking table and turned the brochure over and over until the gloss came off warm on her thumbs.

"He's better than I wanted him to be," she said.

"He's worse," Eylül said. She was clearing the glasses again, the ordinary motion. "The cartoon villain you can fight in front of the museum board. *Look, he wants to loot it.* This one shows up with a conservator and a numbered tray and a sad story about a war, and the board thinks, well, at least someone's *doing* something, the state isn't, the university isn't, and the next thing you know there's a partnership agreement and a portion clause and a man with a key to the ninety percent." She stacked the last glass. "He'll get the geophysics first. He probably already has it — he commissioned half of it. He'll know what's down there before we do. And the law lets him, because the law was written before there was a thing like this site to protect, and the people who'd rewrite the law have been told the chapter's closed."

Mara had picked her camera back up. She hadn't shot anything. She turned it in her hands, looking at the dark uphill swell where nine more enclosures slept under the dirt, and Naila watched her not lift it to her eye.

"There's a frame," Mara said slowly. "Reyes, the empty trays, the drone in the cage, the wrong shadows on the cover. I could make that the most-shared archaeology photo of the year. *The man who's closing Göbekli Tepe.* It'd run everywhere." She set the camera down again. "And it'd make him the story. It'd make the *fight* the story. And the second this is a story about a fight, it stops being a story about the hill, and then somebody asks *well what's even down there, is it worth fighting over,* and the only answer anyone in the room has is — " she nodded at the brochure " — *the chapter's substantially closed.* His sentence wins. Even when I'm using it to attack him, his sentence wins."

Naila looked at her. This was the thing about Mara that the war had given her and taken from her in the same motion: she could see the frame, and she could see what the frame would *cost,* and the cost lived in her hands now where the shot used to.

"So we don't make it about him," Eylül said. She straightened up with the tray. "We make it about what's *down there.* We make the ninety percent so loud, so specific, so undeniably *not* closed, that nobody with a vote can pretend it's empty. We don't fight the man. We fill the room he wants empty."

"With what," Frik said.

Eylül looked at all of them, the system-reader and the truth-teller and the lens-reader, and up the slope, briefly, past the roofed enclosures to the top of the walkway where a man in dark shades stood apart from everything, separate as he'd been all morning, not in the meeting, not in any frame, a shape the cameraman's gimbal had swung right past as if there were nothing there to catch — and then she looked back at her own people.

"With the truth," she said. "Which has the advantage of being real. He's selling *solved.* We have the one thing solved can't survive. We have the hole." She set the tray down for the last time and the table didn't rock, because at some point in the last hour Naila had reached down without noticing and folded the brochure into a wedge and shimmed the leg. "Tomorrow you stop reading the brochure," Eylül said, "and you come down into the dig. The real one. Mud, diesel, the broken hosepipe, the local crew, my uncle's tea. And I show you what *substantially closed* actually looks like under a trowel. Then we find out how loud the hole can get."

She picked up the wedge of brochure to throw it, and stopped, and looked at it — the wrong shadows, the eleven trays, the empty eleventh — and put it in her pocket instead.

"Provenance," she said, to no one, the way you repeat a word you intend to take back from someone. "He thinks it's his to keep."

She went up the slope. The race was on, and it was the quiet kind, the deniable kind, no shots, no shouting, two parties walking toward the same buried ground from opposite directions — and the prize was not gold and not glory but the story of where everything stood, and who got to tell it, and whether it stayed a question worth asking at all.

Naila put her thumb back on the shimmed leg. Steady now. She left it there a moment, just to feel the thing hold.
