# Chapter 3 — The Man Who Reads the Made Thing

The Beast had crossed two seas to be here and it had not enjoyed the trip.

Frik stood in the hotel's back lot at six in the morning watching Jakobus open the bonnet of the thing the way a man opens a letter he's been dreading, and the dawn light came in flat off the plain and turned all the dust gold, which was a lie the dust told every morning, because by ten it would just be dust again, in your teeth, up your nose, ground into the creases of your hands like you'd been handling old money. The Land Cruiser had come overland from the port at Mersin on a flatbed, and it had arrived with a film over everything, a salt-and-grease film off the Mediterranean crossing, and Jakobus was wiping it from the air intake with the hanky.

The hanky. *Eish*, the hanky.

It was always clean. That was the thing about it Frik couldn't get past, had been unable to get past since the first week. The man drove a truck the colour of a riverbed through countries that produced dust as their primary national export and the hanky was always, impossibly, white. He never seemed to wash it where anyone could see. It just was. Folded into a square. Came out. Did a job. Went back. Frik's left shoulder jumped — a hard hitch up toward his ear, twice, the morning tic, the one that liked the cold — and he let it, because there was nobody here to manage it for, just the truck and the man and the gold lie of the light.

"It ran rough on the test," Jakobus said. Not to Frik especially. To the engine, maybe. "Bad fuel somewhere. Cape diesel and this diesel, different animal."

"Different animal," Frik said. He hadn't meant to. The words came back out of him the way they did, repeated, polished, handed back. Echolalia, the doctors called it, when they were being kind, and the other thing when they weren't. He'd long ago stopped flinching at himself for it. Mostly. "Sorry. Carry on."

Jakobus didn't look up. "Nothing to be sorry for."

That was the whole strange country of the crew, right there, in three words said to a carburettor. *Nothing to be sorry for.* Frik had been thrown out of more rooms than he could count — out of a chemistry exam, out of a funeral, once out of a cinema by a man who genuinely believed Frik was barking the words *back* at the screen on purpose, as a comment, as criticism — and here was this enormous quiet South African with the sun-creased eyes who had said, in the first hour they met, that Frik's mouth was the most honest instrument he'd ever found, and that he intended to point it at some lies, and would that be all right.

It had been all right.

It was the most all right thing that had ever happened to Frik Basson, and he didn't have a single tic for that, no word at all, which was its own kind of telling.

---

This was the part Frik kept, the way other people kept photographs: how they'd come to be standing on the wrong continent.

It had started with chalk. A school's worth of chalk, a year and a country ago, on a board Edie Vane had filled and filled and not been able to stop filling — holes in the ground, all over the map, holes that weren't supposed to know about each other. Frik hadn't understood most of it. He understood that Jakobus had stood very still in front of that board for a long time and then taken off his sunglasses, which was the man's entire vocabulary for *this matters*, and had said: it's not one. It's a pattern. And you don't get a pattern from accident.

And then he'd gone and found them. One at a time. The way you'd build a tool when the tool you needed didn't exist yet.

Naila, who read the system underneath the thing — who could look at a too-clean number and feel it the way you feel a step that isn't there in the dark. Mara, who read the lens, who'd put a camera down at a war and walked away from the only thing she was famous for. And him. Frik, who read the lie — not cleverly, not by deduction, God knew, but with his actual body, which had the appalling and lifelong habit of saying the true thing out loud at the worst possible second, in the worst possible room, where it couldn't be unsaid.

*The anti-paintover*, Jakobus had called him once, and Frik had had to look it up. A paintover was when you painted over the crack instead of fixing the wall. Frik was what happened when the wall couldn't keep a secret.

He thought about that a lot, here, in a place that was — by every account — built on a very large and very old and very *polite* version of painting over the crack.

---

They drove out to the hill before the heat, the four of them, on Eylül's say-so and under the project's grace, which was a thing made of permit numbers and a stamped letter in the glovebox and Eylül's name spoken to a guard at a gate, and not at all of money, because the money in this story was the wrong money and belonged to the wrong people.

The site sat under a great pale tensile roof now, a modern shelter spread over the enclosures like a held breath, and the walkways kept you off the stones, up above them, looking down, which was correct and which Frik hated, a little, the way you hate being told not to touch the dog. From the walkway Enclosure D opened below them, the great oval of it, the wall of smaller pillars ringing it, and in the centre the two big ones.

The two big ones.

Frik's first time, two days ago, his mouth had gone off like a fire alarm. He'd stood on the walkway and his throat had done the thing it did when something was *too much*, when the dial went past where the body could hold it quiet — a rising series, *hah, hah, HAH* — and then a word, loud, into the German tourists' reverent hush, a word that was just the truth coming up sideways out of him before he could swallow it: *"People."*

Heads had turned. Mara had stepped half in front of him, casual, blocking sightlines without making a show of it, the way she always did, a body between him and the staring, and Naila had kept looking at the pillars as if nothing had happened because to Naila nothing had, and Eylül — Eylül had turned and looked at him with an expression he was still chewing on, two days later. Not annoyance. Not pity. Something closer to: *yes, and?*

Because of course they were people. Anyone could see it. Klaus Schmidt had seen it in the nineteen-nineties when he started digging, the German archaeologist, Deutsches Archäologisches Institut, the man who'd given his whole life to this hill and then died of it more or less, of a heart attack, in 2014, and Frik thought of him often, the way you think of someone who built the house you're sleeping in. Schmidt had looked at these T-shaped megaliths — and they were *megaliths*, that was the right and unhysterical word, eleven and a half thousand years old, made by people who had no pottery, no metal, no wheel, no writing, no farms — and he had seen that the T wasn't a T. It was a body seen flat. The crossbar at the top was a head turned to face you. And on the great central pair, carved in low relief down the long shaft of each, were arms.

Arms that bent at the elbow. Arms that came round to the front. And at the belt, where the belt was carved — actual fingers. Individual fingers, the two hands almost meeting over the buckle, the way a man stands when he's at ease and somebody's about to take his portrait.

Pillar 18 wore a belt. Pillar 18 wore, carved across the front of him, a fox-pelt loincloth, and cradled against the side of him, in low relief, a fox.

They were people. Schmidt knew. The fish knew. *Frik's body had known so hard it shouted.* And the only question worth a damn — the question Jakobus had been turning over since the chalkboard, the question that wasn't *who* and wasn't *is it real* and wasn't, God help us, *was it aliens* — was the one nobody could carbon-date.

*What is it for.*

---

"Banning says seven," Naila said.

She was on the walkway rail, both hands flat on it, rocking very slightly, heel to toe, which Frik had learned not to read as nervous because it wasn't, it was thinking, it was her metronome the way Jakobus had his. She'd been at the labour arithmetic for two days now and she'd taken it apart like a watch.

"Seven what," Mara said. She had the camera up — the small one, the one she used for *looking* rather than *taking* — and she was sighting on the far pillars, not pressing anything, just framing, the way she warmed up.

"To move one. Seven to fourteen people. E. B. Banning ran it — ropes, a lubricated track, the weight's eight to ten tonnes, you don't need a magic army, you need a dozen people who aren't idiots and a wet log." Naila's heel came down hard, once. "Everyone wants it to be impossible. It isn't impossible. That's the *boring* part and the boring part is *true* and people hate that, they'd rather have a miracle than a wet log." She turned her head, not quite to Frik, eyes on his collarbone the way she aimed. "It being possible is the whole problem."

"Because they'll say so," Frik said. His shoulder hitched. "The Trust."

"Because they'll *say so*." She put a little weight on it, glad to be understood. "It's solved, it's just a workforce, hunter-gatherers got together for a barn-raising, nothing left here to protect, sign the access agreement, let the nice foundation take the unexcavated ninety per cent off your hands for safekeeping." She said *safekeeping* like it had a hook in it. "I proved the easy version is feasible and I handed them the knife."

"You didn't hand them anything," Mara said, low, still framing. "You did the maths. The maths is just true."

"That's what a knife is."

Down on the walkway behind them, near the far end where it bent toward the slope, Jakobus had stopped.

Frik watched him do it. That was Frik's specialty when it came to Jakobus — watching, because watching was all anybody got. The man could not be had any closer than the outside. Mara had said it best, weeks ago, half a bottle in: she'd tried to photograph him for a year and never got him clean. He came out blurred, or turned, or eaten by glare, or just somehow *not in the frame he should have been in*, and she'd stopped calling it bad luck. Even the Trust's little drone the other day, the one that had hummed over the dig fence like a fat metal bee and that Eylül had glared at until it left — even that had a hard drive somewhere full of everyone but him.

So Frik watched him from the outside, which was the only telescope there was.

Jakobus had stopped at the rail. He took off the sunglasses. He did it slowly, hooking one arm, folding them, and the bare eyes went down into the enclosure and stayed there a long time on the two central pillars, on the arms, the fingers, the fox, and his face did the nothing it did, the great patient nothing of a man reading a made thing for its purpose, and then he put the glasses back on, which was him filing it, and he said, to the air, in his way, not loud:

"Sawubona."

*I see you.* The Zulu greeting. The one he carried from home and aimed, sometimes, at things that weren't people, except these were, that was the whole point, these were people, eleven thousand years dead and standing up straight in the ground, and the big man had just said good morning to them.

Frik's throat moved. He let the word come because it was true and there was nobody here it would cost. "*Sawubona*," he said back, to the pillars, and it didn't feel like echolalia. It felt like answering.

---

Eylül arrived at the slope-edge in a field shirt the colour of the dust with a clipboard under her arm, and the gate-talk and the permit-number weight came off the day the second she was on the ground, because this was her ground and you could feel the air change.

"You're up early," she said, to all of them, and to Naila in particular, because she'd clocked which one was the engine. "Cemal sent tea. My uncle. It's in the truck and it's better than the hotel's, so don't insult me by saying no."

"We won't say no," Mara said.

"Good. The Trust's people were here yesterday." Eylül said it lightly and it wasn't light at all. "Reyes. Mr. Anton Reyes, of the Anatolia Heritage Trust. Lovely man. Genuinely, that's the trouble, he's a lovely man. He brought a slideshow." She made the word *slideshow* sound like a soft and terrible weapon, which Frik supposed it was. "It had drone footage and a sad piano and a sentence about *neglect.* The provincial budget is real, you understand. The roof you're standing under has a maintenance cost. He stands in the gap between the money we don't have and the heritage somebody has to *save,* and he stands there with very clean hands and he is not lying, that's the genius of it, he believes he's the hero, and the official story —" she gestured down at the enclosure, at the whole solved, dated, radiocarbon-secure, beautifully-explained wonder of it — "the official story is his best slide. *It's a gathering place. We know what it is. There's nothing left to find. Let the grown-ups take care of it.*"

Naila had gone very still on the rail. "It's not solved," she said.

"I know," said Eylül.

"No, but it's *not.* People think it's solved because the headline got solved. Schmidt cracked the headline — monuments before farming, the order's inverted, you didn't need a town to build a temple, you maybe needed a temple to build a town, *that's* secure, that's the part that's actually true and actually huge." She was rocking again, hard, the maths coming up through her. "But that's the famous part. The famous part being solved makes everyone stop looking, and under the famous part —"

"Under the famous part," Eylül said, and she was smiling now, the first real one, because here was an outsider who'd found the trapdoor under the floor, "there's the part nobody put on a slide."

---

The tea came out of Cemal's flask, and it was better, and they drank it on the walkway with the heat starting to lean in, and that was when Naila laid the two pages on the rail, weighted with a stone, and Frik leaned in to read over her shoulder because his eye liked her handwriting, which was small and even and ran perfectly level without a line.

The first page was the labour arithmetic, the wet-log math, the dozen-not-a-miracle, all of it, ruled and totalled and *killed*, the over-reach lying dead on the page where she'd shot it.

The second page had two numbers on it and, under them, one line of words.

The numbers were spans. A measure. The same measure, she'd written, twice — once for here, once, in a smaller hand, for *Karahan.*

Karahan Tepe. The other hill. One of the Taş Tepeler, the Stone Hills, the whole spread of them across this plain, twenty enclosures maybe, two hundred pillars at Göbekli alone and only a tenth of it dug, ninety per cent of it still down there in the dark under the dirt, asleep. A whole *tradition.* Not one weird hill. A region of them, talking to each other across ground a person would walk for two days.

And the span was the same.

"It's probably nothing," Naila said, before anyone could speak, fast, the way she headed off a thing she didn't trust herself near. "It's probably convergence. The arm. The reach of an arm makes a natural unit, you lay out a circle with a rope and a peg, you get human-scale numbers everywhere, of course you do, it's the most boring explanation and the boring explanation is *usually* the —" She stopped. Her heel came down. "I keep wanting it to be the boring one."

"Why," said Mara, gently.

"Because the boring one doesn't keep me up."

Frik's body moved before his mind caught it. The hitch, the throat, the rising *hah, hah,* and then — out, before he could hold it, dropped into the middle of the quiet like a coin into a well — the true thing, the thing nobody at the rail had said out loud yet, the thing under the thing:

"Somebody *taught* them."

Silence.

His face went hot. He waited for the room to do what rooms did. To go careful. To go *managed.*

It didn't.

Eylül looked at him for a long second with that *yes, and?* face, the one from two days ago, and then she said, slowly, like she was being fair to a possibility she didn't owe anything: "That's the question the maths puts on the table, and I'll tell you honestly, I don't know the answer, and I'll tell you something else." She tapped the second page, the measure. "Don't fall in love with it. That's how the woo gets you. You see one shared number and next thing you've got a lost super-race and a master plan and little grey men with a tape measure, and you've made the whole thing *smaller,* not bigger, you've taken the actual miracle — that ordinary people, hunters, with stone tools and rope and their own backs, *invented all of this from nothing* — and you've handed it to imaginary strangers because you couldn't bear that human beings did it." Her voice had heat in it now, the protective heat of someone whose ancestors were on the wrong end of *humans-couldn't-have.* "They could bear it. They *did* it. The same measure might be a teacher walking between two hills. Or it might be a man's arm and a piece of rope. I'm not going to pretend I know which, and you" — she looked at Frik, dead level, full dignity, the first person in his whole life to do it about a tic — "your mouth's right that it's a real question. It's just not a solved one. Hold it open."

"Hold it open," Frik said. And then, because it was true, and because there was nobody here it would cost: "Thank you."

---

The handoff came without anyone calling it that.

It came in the angle of things. Naila's pages stayed on the rail, weighted with the stone, but she didn't pick them back up, and when Mara raised the camera at last — toward the enclosure, toward the two great pillar-people with their bent arms and their patient fingers and the small fox at the belt — she didn't press the shutter. She lowered it again. She turned to Eylül.

"Can I," she said. "The pillars I'll shoot — they're under a public roof, they're on every postcard in town, that's nobody's secret. But the *meaning.* The thing it is to *you.* I'm not putting that in a frame and selling it." She said it plainly, no performance in it. "So I'm asking. What do you want seen, and what do you want left alone."

Eylül looked at her the way you look at someone who's just declined the thing everyone else takes.

And Jakobus, at the far end of the walkway, with his back half to them and his sunglasses on and the desert behind him that wasn't his desert, the wrong continent under his boots, the Beast washed of two seas of salt down in the lot, the cayenne tin somewhere in his pack against a snakebite that the snakes here didn't deliver the same way — Jakobus did the thing he did, the only thing, the thing he'd built this whole crew to be able to do without him.

He stepped back. He let the rail be theirs. He turned his face to the plain and the buried ninety per cent of it and he stood guard over a question that wasn't his to answer, in a place that wasn't his to own, and he said nothing at all, which was him handing it across.

Naila looked at her two pages on the rail. The dead arithmetic. The open question.

*Who taught both hills the same length.*

And the woman whose ground it was set down her uncle's flask, and looked at the three of them, the system-reader and the lens-reader and the man whose body could not lie, and she said:

"You want to know what it's for."

It wasn't a question.

"Then come down off the walkway," said Eylül Demir. "And I'll tell you what my grandmother told me. But not up here. Not over the heads of the dead."
