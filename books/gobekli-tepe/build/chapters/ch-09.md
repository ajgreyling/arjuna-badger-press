# Chapter 9 — Did They Bury It

The meeting was in the dig-house common room, which was a portable cabin the colour of old teeth with a window-unit air conditioner that did not so much cool the air as redistribute its misery. Frik counted the people coming in. He always counted. Twenty-two chairs, somebody had said, plastic ones stacked in the corner of the equipment shed and carried over two at a time, and there were going to be more than twenty-two bodies, which meant some would stand at the back by the urn of tea, and standing-room-only was a thing that lived in Frik's shoulders like a current, because in a packed room your body was a sound nobody had asked for and there were always more ears.

He took a chair at the end of the second row, on the aisle, near the door. Near the door was a thing he did without deciding to. Naila took the chair beside him and turned it a few degrees so it pointed at the wall rather than the front, and nobody told her not to. Mara sat on his other side with her camera in her lap, lens cap on, hands folded over it like a person keeping a small animal calm.

"Lots of people," Naila said. Not anxious. Inventorying.

"Twenty-six and counting," Frik said, and his neck pulled, a quick hard tug to the left and back, the kind that made the muscle ache by evening. He let it pull. Fighting it cost more than the tic did.

Eylül was up front with her uncle Cemal, who had brought a tray of glasses and was setting them out on the trestle table with the gravity of a man laying out something that mattered, which it was, because the tea was the ground here, the tea was the thing that said *you are in our house even if the house is a German foundation's prefab.* Cemal poured. The steam went up. Outside the one window the light was going long and gold over the plain, the same plain the carved people on the hill had been looking at for eleven and a half thousand years, and a generator somewhere chugged under everything like a heart that needed work.

The Trust had brought their own water. Bottles. Branded. *Anatolia Heritage Trust,* a clean sans-serif and a little gold ibex, on a side table all their own, and Frik looked at the water and his stomach did the thing it did near a polished lie.

Anton Reyes came in last, which was a choice, and shook hands on the way, which was also a choice, and the handshakes were good — warm, two-handed, the left coming up to cup the elbow — and Frik watched him do it and understood that the man meant them. That was the part nobody believed when Frik said it later: Reyes meant the handshakes. He wasn't a snake oiling people. He was a man who genuinely loved this. That was worse.

"Friends," Reyes said, when he was at the front, and he had a voice like a good radio, low and unhurried. "Thank you for coming. I know this is your supper hour."

Cemal said something in Kurdish to the man beside him and the man laughed quietly. Eylül did not laugh. She stood near her uncle with her arms folded and her field clothes still grey at the knees from the trench, and Frik watched the line of her jaw and thought, *she has done this before, this exact meeting, with a different glossy man.*

"I'll be brief, because I respect that you've all read more about this hill than I ever will." Reyes smiled. Self-deprecation was a tool and he used it cleanly. "The Trust isn't here to dig. Let me say that first and plainly, because rumours travel faster than facts on this plain — I'd know, I'm a rumour myself by now." A ripple of real laughter. He had them. "We're here to *protect.* That's the whole of it. There are enclosures out under that slope that no spade has touched and no roof has covered, and every winter the rain takes a little more of them, and the local team — and I will say this in front of Eylül, who knows it better than anyone — the local team does not have the budget to save what's already cracking, let alone what's still buried."

Frik felt the tic build in his diaphragm, a pressure, a held breath wanting to become a sound. He breathed it down. Not yet. There was nothing yet. The man hadn't said the thing yet.

"And here's the good news," Reyes went on, and now he opened his hands, generous, *I am about to give you something.* "The science is largely settled on what this place is. Professor Schmidt's team established it, and the work since has confirmed it. This is a gathering-place. A ritual site. People came, they raised their pillars, they feasted, and crucially — crucially — when they were done with each enclosure, they *buried it themselves.* They decommissioned it. They filled it in deliberately and walked away."

He let that sit.

"Which means," Reyes said, gently, like a man laying a baby down, "the unexcavated ninety percent isn't a treasure-house waiting to be looted. It's the same. It's more of the known. Beautiful, sacred, and *finished.* It was finished by the people who made it. So when the Trust steps in to stabilise the slope, to put a membrane over the unexcavated ground — we are not opening a tomb. We are honouring a burial those people performed themselves. We're keeping a closed thing closed."

And there it was.

Frik's whole body knew it before his mouth did. It was always like that — the truth came up through the floor of him, through the gut and the chest and the throat, on its own track, and by the time it reached his mouth it had the weight of a thing already decided. People thought the tics and the blurts were chaos. They weren't. They were the most orderly thing about him. They were the part that wouldn't be edited.

He looked at Naila. Naila was looking at the wall, but her hands had gone flat on her knees, very still, and he knew that stillness. She'd heard it too. The wrong number in the ledger.

"That's a hell of a load-bearing word," Mara said quietly, beside him, not loud enough for the front, "*buried.*"

"Mm," Frik said. The pressure climbed his throat. *Not yet. Not yet.*

Up front, somebody from the provincial office — a tidy man with a lanyard and a folder — asked a question about the membrane, about drainage, about who held liability if the slope went anyway, and Reyes answered it beautifully, and Cemal poured more tea, and the air conditioner died and restarted, and through all of it Frik could feel the word sitting in the room like a stone somebody had thrown into still water and was now pretending hadn't made a ring.

*Buried.* The whole pitch hung off it. *They buried it themselves, so it's finished, so let us seal the rest, so the provenance of ninety percent quietly stops being anybody's problem before the local team can get a roof over it.* Frik wasn't a clever man the way Naila was clever. He couldn't have walked you through the argument. But his body could feel where the floor was rotten, and the floor was rotten right under that word, and the man was standing on it like it was concrete.

Eylül unfolded her arms.

"It's contested," she said.

The room turned to her. Reyes turned to her, and his face did a kind, attentive thing, *of course, tell us,* and Frik watched it and thought: he is not afraid of her saying this. He has an answer for this. This is the part he rehearsed.

"The burial," Eylül said. She didn't raise her voice. She had her uncle's gift for filling a room by lowering it. "Schmidt read the backfill as intentional. He believed they decommissioned each enclosure — packed it with rubble and animal bone and broken tools, and closed it on purpose. That's the famous reading. *They buried their own temple.* It's a beautiful idea and a lot of people built careers on it, including some who are dear to me."

"But," Reyes said, encouraging.

"But the fill has been re-read since," Eylül said. "There's a strong case — published, peer-reviewed, not fringe — that a great deal of what looks like deliberate backfill is *slope-wash.* Hillside coming down. Erosion and landslide over millennia, sediment moving downslope and filling the enclosures the way water fills a bowl, with no human deciding anything at all. The bone and the flint are in there because there was *occupation debris* on this hill — because people lived around here, which we also now know, because we found the houses and the grinding stones and the water channels Schmidt didn't think were here." She paused. "Both readings are live. Neither is closed. Anyone who tells you the burial question is *settled* is telling you a story they want you to act on, not a fact you can check."

It was the cleanest thing Frik had ever heard a person do. She hadn't called Reyes a liar. She'd just put the rot in the floor under a lamp and let everyone see the colour of it.

And Reyes — and this was the thing, this was the part that made Frik's hands cold — Reyes *nodded.* Warmly. Like she'd helped him.

"Eylül, this is exactly why I wanted the local team in the room," he said, and he meant it, and that was the horror of him. "You're right that the scholarship has nuance. Of course it does. But I'd put it to everyone here that the *nuance* is precisely the argument for sealing rather than digging. If we don't even know whether the fill is sacred or accidental — then opening it risks destroying something irreplaceable to answer an academic question that may never resolve. The cautious thing, the *respectful* thing, is to leave it closed. Whether they buried it or the hill did. Closed is closed."

And he smiled, and it was a good smile, and Frik watched the room start to move toward it, the lanyard man nodding, the provincial people nodding, because it sounded like wisdom, it sounded like humility, it had the shape of the right answer —

— and the pressure in Frik's throat became a sound.

He didn't decide it. That was the thing he would try, and fail, to explain to Mara that night on the dig-house step. You don't decide it any more than you decide a sneeze. It comes up the track and the track is older than your manners and it arrives with its own weight and it will *out,* and the only question your will gets a vote on is whether you spend your whole life clenched around it, swallowing, going red, being the strange quiet man in the corner who must not be let near important meetings — or whether you let your body say the true thing it has decided to say.

His head pulled left, hard. His shoulder jumped.

"He's not saying *closed,*" Frik said. Loud. Flat. Certain. The voice that wasn't quite his and was the truest part of him. "He's saying *yours.*"

The room stopped.

Frik's hand came up and pressed his own sternum, the old grounding, palm flat. The next part came whether he liked it or not, and it came in the cadence the truth always came in, blunt and unhedged, because the editing centre that softens a thing for a room — that centre was the part of him that didn't work, and it was also the part of him the crew had stopped asking him to fix.

"*Leave it closed* means leave it *unrecorded.*" His leg bounced. "No provenance. No roof from the local team. No protection on paper. He puts a membrane on the ninety percent and the membrane's not a lid, it's a *deed.* And the burial — " his throat clicked, the word fought him and lost — "the burial's the *excuse.* He needs the burial to be real so the closing's holy. If they buried it, then closing it's respect. If the *hill* buried it — " the tic snapped his head again, his hand pressed harder, and the last of it came out of him like a stone leaving a sling — "then it's just *dirt over the thing he wants and nobody watching while he takes it.*"

Silence. The air conditioner laboured. Somewhere outside a sheep complained.

Frik sat with his palm on his chest, breathing, the heat coming up his neck and into his ears, the old waiting for the old thing — *get him out, who let him in, is he all right, can someone manage him.* He'd lived in that waiting his whole life. The door was right there. Near the door, always, because near the door was the only place a body that says the unsayable can ever fully relax.

Nobody moved to the door.

Cemal set the teapot down on the trestle, slowly, and the small sound of it was very loud.

Naila said, into the quiet, not loudly, "That's correct."

She wasn't looking at the room. She was looking at the wall, hands flat. "I worked the ledger this morning. The Trust's stabilisation proposal references the unexcavated enclosures by survey grid, not by name, and assigns them a single heritage classification — *decommissioned ritual deposit* — across all of them. All of them. That's not a thing the science supports for ground nobody has opened. You can't classify the fill of an enclosure you haven't sampled. The only way you write *decommissioned* across ninety percent you've never touched is if you need the word to do legal work the trench can't do yet." She tilted her head a fraction. "He needs *buried* to mean *finished.* It doesn't. Frik's right. It's load-bearing and it's hollow."

Mara had not lifted the camera.

Frik registered that, distantly, through the roar in his ears — that her hands were still folded over the lens cap, that the best photograph in the room was him, palm on sternum, the foundation's branded water glowing on its little table behind him, the whole rotten beautiful machine of it caught in one frame that would make her name on three continents, *neurodivergent activist names the trick at the heritage meeting,* and she had not moved her finger. She was looking at him and not through a lens, and she gave him a small nod, the nod that meant *I have you, and I am not going to make you the picture.*

Reyes recovered. Of course he did. He was very good.

"That's a serious accusation," he said, and the warmth was still there but it had a floor under it now, a coolness coming up. "Made, I'd say, in a — forgive me — a somewhat unguarded way." A beat. A choice. The kindness turned, just slightly, into a blade so soft you could almost miss it. "I understand the gentleman may not have full control over—"

"He has full control over the truth," Eylül said. "That's the only thing he can't lie about. Which is more than most of us in this room can say."

And she let that hang too.

It was the second time she'd done it — taken the thing the powerful man reached for and turned it in her hand so its edge faced him. Reyes had reached for Frik's neurology the way men like him always did, the soft *the poor fellow can't help himself,* the move that turns a true thing into a symptom so the room can stop hearing it. And Eylül had simply refused the trade. *Yes. He can't help it. That's why you should listen.*

Frik's breathing came down. His hand stayed on his chest a moment longer and then dropped to his knee. The heat in his ears stayed. It always stayed a while. But under it, for the first time he could remember in a room this full, was a thing he had no good word for, a settling, the absence of the door's pull — because for once the door was not where he needed to be. He needed to be in the chair. In the second row. On the aisle. *Inside* the room, with the others, where he'd said the unsayable thing and the people he'd come with had not flinched and had not managed him and had picked the true thing up off the floor where he'd dropped it and held it up so it couldn't be put back down.

That was new. He sat very still inside it.

Reyes was talking again — to the provincial officials now, the lanyard man, low and reasonable, *clearly there are strong feelings, perhaps we table the membrane discussion, reconvene with documents* — and it was a retreat dressed as procedure, and everyone in the room could feel it was a retreat, and that was the thing about a true word said where it can't be taken back: it doesn't win the argument. The argument grinds on. The membrane will come up again. The Trust has lawyers and the local team has a portable cabin with a dying air conditioner. Frik knew all that. The blurt didn't fix the world.

But the room could not un-hear it.

You could watch it move through the people. The lanyard man wrote something in his folder and underlined it twice. Two of the village men were talking to Cemal in low fast Kurdish and one of them looked over at the branded water with a new flatness in his face. The word *deed* had been said out loud in a room where Reyes had wanted only the word *respect,* and you cannot take a word back out of a room. It stays in the walls. It gets carried home and said over supper. By morning it would be on the plain.

Through the one window, the long gold light had gone to copper, and the plain was the colour of a thing about to be dark, and out at the edge of the dig-house yard a figure stood by the Beast with its olive paint and its slow-weeping canvas water bag swinging under the bumper, a man in dark glasses he had no business wearing this near to dusk, his back to the cabin, watching the road. He hadn't come in. He never came into the rooms. He'd stood the whole meeting out there in the failing light with his hands loose at his sides, and Frik understood, the way you understand a thing about someone without being told, that the man had stood out there *so that the room would be theirs* — Eylül's, Cemal's, the crew's — and that he was watching the one road in because that was the only part of this he could carry for them, and he meant to carry it.

Cemal began, quietly, to refill the glasses. The meeting was breaking into the murmur that follows a meeting, the scrape of plastic chairs, somebody's phone, and under it the older sound, the sound that had been on this hill before any of the rest of it, the wind coming up over the lip of the plateau with the dark behind it.

Eylül crossed to where the three of them sat. She didn't say *well done.* She wouldn't insult them with it. She crouched in the aisle so she was at Frik's level, and she looked at him, plainly, the way you'd look at a man and not at a condition.

"They don't believe their own *buried* line," she said, low, just for them. "I'll tell you how I know. There's a sister-site east of here. Karahan. The Trust filed for the same membrane there, same grid-classification, before this hill was even on the table." She glanced toward the door, toward the dark coming on, toward the figure by the truck and the one road. "I've been wanting someone to come stand in that trench with me and tell me whether I'm seeing what I think I'm seeing in the stone."

Naila's hands came off her knees.

"What are you seeing," she said.

Eylül stood. She looked at all of them, and at her uncle pouring the tea, and out at the darkening plain that had held its measure under the dirt for eleven and a half thousand years.

"The same measure," she said. "Where there's no reason there should be."

And outside, the wind came over the lip of the plateau and went out across the buried fields toward Karahan, carrying the word the room could not un-hear, and nobody in the cabin said whether the hill had been closed by hands or by rain, because nobody knew, because nobody had ever known, and the carved people up on the ridge stood in the last of the copper light with their fingered hands resting on their belts, facing east, waiting, easy, for whatever the living were going to do next.
