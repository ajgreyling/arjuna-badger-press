# Chapter 8 — Vultures

The name hung in the truck for a second longer than a name should, the way a thing does when nobody knows yet whether it's a key or a grenade, and then Eylül said, quietly, "Tomorrow. We go up tomorrow. Not tonight," and started the engine, and the plain swallowed the rest of it into dust and headlights and the long flat dark of the drive back.

So Mara did not sleep, and in the morning she went up the hill with the laptop closed in her bag like a held breath and the cameras around her neck like the tools they were supposed to be.

—

You came up to Göbekli Tepe and the first thing the place did was refuse to be impressive. That was the part nobody put in the foundation's promo film, with its drone sweeps and its swelling strings and the slow gold light it must have cost somebody a permit and three good mornings to steal. The real thing was a low rise on a treeless plateau, the colour of a biscuit, with a tensioned-fabric roof slung over it on steel legs to keep the rain off the stones, and the roof made the whole site look like a regional airport that had given up. You climbed the boardwalk. You smelled hot dust and diesel from the generator and the specific sun-baked plastic of the canopy. You heard the wind and a Turkish radio somewhere down by the visitor centre playing something with too much synth in it.

And then you looked down into the enclosure, and the place stopped pretending.

Mara had stood at the edge of a lot of holes in the world. She knew the difference between a thing that performed depth and a thing that had it. This one had it. Two pillars stood in the centre of the great oval ring, taller than three men, the colour of old bone, and even from up on the walkway, even before Eylül said a single word, you could see they were not pillars. The crossbar at the top sat like a head turned away. The shaft was a body. Down the long front of one of them ran an arm in low relief, bent at the elbow exactly where an elbow bends, the forearm coming round the side, and a hand at the end of it with fingers — five of them, carved separate, the way a child draws a hand and the way a hand actually is — spread across the stone belt at the waist as if the figure were resting its palm there, the way you'd rest your hand on your own belt, the way a man stands when he is waiting and easy and very old.

"Pillar 31," Eylül said. She'd come up beside Mara without sound. "And the other is 18. The two in the middle of every enclosure, the big pair, they face the same way. East. They are the ones the whole ring is built to attend. Around the inside wall there are more, smaller, standing in toward them. Like —" She stopped, and chose. "Like a gathering, turned to face the two who matter."

"They're people," Mara said.

"They are people," Eylül said. "Schmidt saw it early. The arms, the hands, the belt. Eighteen wears a fox-pelt at the loincloth, carved, and it cradles a fox here, in the crook of the arm. Not a god with a name we can recover. Not a king. But — people. Big ones. The dead, maybe. The ancestors. We don't agree on which. We agree that they are *somebody*."

Frik had come to the rail on Mara's other side and was holding it, and his head ducked once, and he said, low, not to anyone, "Hello." Then again, softer, an echo of his own word, "hello, hello," the tic running the greeting out like a stone skipping, and nobody turned to look at him, and Mara understood that this was correct, that you greeted them, that the body that couldn't lie had simply said the only true thing there was to say to two carved humans eleven thousand five hundred years dead, which was hello.

—

Naila had not spoken. She stood a little apart with her hands loose and her eyes moving along the ring of stones the way Mara's eyes moved along a contact sheet, fast, then stopping, then going back. After a while she said, to the air, "The spacing isn't random."

"No," Eylül said.

"The two centre pillars, the gap between them, that distance repeats. The smaller pillars on the wall, they're set off that same measure. And —" Naila tilted her head, and her hands came up and made a small shape, a width, the unit she carried in her body the way some people carry a tune. "It's the same proportion they used at Karahan Tepe. I read the survey on the flight. Different hill. Different enclosure. Same belt. Same elbow. Same gap. People who never met, who were born and died centuries apart, on hills you can't see one from the other, used the *same ruler*."

Eylül looked at her for a moment, and Mara saw the look, because reading looks was the job — it was the look of an expert hearing an outsider get something genuinely right and deciding, in real time, how much to give back. "The Taş Tepeler," Eylül said. "The Stone Hills. There are maybe twenty enclosures here at Göbekli alone. Two hundred pillars. And we have excavated —" she held up a hand and pinched a sliver of air "— that much. Ten percent. The rest is under the ground we're standing on. And there are more hills. Karahan. Sayburç. A dozen we've only walked over with instruments. A whole — country of this. We've barely knocked."

"And they share a measure," Naila said.

"Some of them seem to. Yes."

"Across sites that shouldn't share one."

"Naila," Eylül said, gently, and it was a full sentence, that *Naila*. It meant: I see the door you are opening, and it is a real door, and I am not going to let you walk through it into the place where outsiders go when a thing is too big, the place with the spaceships in it. "We don't know how the measure travelled. People walked. People remembered. People taught. That is already strange enough to keep me up at night. Leave it strange. Don't make it into magic."

Naila's jaw worked. Then she nodded, once, the nod she gave when an argument had actually landed and not just bounced off her. "It's strange enough," she agreed, and let it go, and Mara watched her let it go and thought it cost her something, the letting-go, and thought it was the bravest small thing she'd see all day, and was wrong about that, but only just.

—

They went down into the enclosure proper, on the inner walkway, close enough now to see the carving on the stone, and this was where the place reached up and took you by the throat.

Because the pillars were not bare. The big quiet humans wore animals. And not the animals you'd put on a thing if you wanted it loved — no lambs, no does, nothing soft. Boars with their tusks out and their hackles up. A lion coming down a shaft head-first, claws spread. Snakes, dozens of them, poured down the stone in waves. A scorpion, tail cocked. An aurochs, that vanished wild ox, twice the bull we have now, head lowered to charge. The whole bestiary of everything on that plain that could open you up and kill you.

And the birds.

"Vultures," Eylül said.

There were two on the one she pointed to, big-winged, the unmistakable bald-headed hunch of them, and on a famous slab Mara had seen in books and which they did not let you near now, there was a vulture with one wing held out flat, and balanced on that outstretched wing, a small ball. A circle. And beneath the bird, a human figure with no head.

"That panel's not in this enclosure," Eylül said, watching Mara's face find it in memory. "But you know it. Everyone knows it. The vulture, the wing, the round thing — a head? A sun? — and the man without one. And people look at that and they want it to *say something*. They want a sentence."

"Excarnation," Naila said.

"That's the careful word for the idea, yes." Eylül crouched at the rail so she was level with the carved boar, and the four of them came down with her, instinctively, the way you lower your voice in a particular kind of building. "Sky burial. In a lot of places, across a lot of human history, you don't bury your dead in the ground straight away. You put them out. On a platform, a high place. And the birds come. The vultures take the flesh, and what they leave is clean, and the bone is what you keep, and bury, and carry, and stand before. And vultures —" she looked up at the carved bird, the patient terrible bald head of it "— a vulture isn't a horror, if that's your world. A vulture is the one who carries the dead up. Who turns the body back into sky. The ferryman."

Frik's shoulder pulled. "Carries them up," he said, flat, the echo. Then, lower, the involuntary engine of him finding the true thing under the sentence and saying it before he could stop it: "*we eat the dead too*." His face did the thing it did after, the small private bracing for the room to turn on him. Nobody turned. Mara watched Eylül take the blurted truth in and *use* it, the way the good ones did.

"That's exactly the thought the carving won't let you off the hook of," Eylül said to him, evenly, as if he'd said it in a seminar and meant to. "Yes. The birds eat the dead. And these people carved the birds, big, in the most important room they ever made, and stood their giant ancestors in the middle of it. So whatever death was to them, it wasn't a thing to hide. It was the centre."

She stood. She put her hand on the rail, near the carved boar, not touching the stone — you never touched the stone — and she said the thing Mara would carry down off the hill and into the rest of her life.

"We don't know," Eylül said. "We don't get the sentence. The man with no head — is it a real practice they did here? A myth they told? A particular dead person, somebody they all knew, the founder, the killed one? Is the round thing on the wing his soul going up, or is it just how you carve a man losing his head, or is it the sun? I have read forty papers that each pick one and build a castle on it. I'll read forty more. The honest answer is the one nobody publishes loudly, because it doesn't fund a dig." She looked at the headless figure across the enclosure, in the bright dust, under the airport roof. "We know they put the birds at the centre. We know the centre is people. We know they did it before farming, before pots, before the wheel, before writing, before any of the things we tell our children you need before you can build something this size. The rest is theirs. They took it with them."

—

This was the part the foundation's film would have ruined, and Mara knew it, because the film would have put a voice over it saying *but what if*, and a graphic of a star-map, and a man in a good shirt asking why mainstream archaeology was *afraid* of the truth, and the whole enormous real thing — that human beings with flint and patience had quarried a ten-tonne person out of bedrock and stood it up to face the dawn six thousand years before anyone built the pyramids — would have been made smaller, made into a riddle for the man in the good shirt to be cleverer than.

It didn't need him. That was the thing. The hole was real without him.

"Show me how they raised it," she said, because she had to ask one true question to hold herself together, and Naila answered before Eylül could, because Naila had clearly been waiting for someone to be wrong about it.

"People say it's impossible," Naila said. "Ten tonnes, no machines, no draft animals — they want it to be impossible, because impossible is exciting and impossible needs a saviour with a spaceship. It's not impossible. Banning ran the numbers. With ropes and a track you lubricate, and a lever crew, you're talking seven to fourteen people to move one. Fourteen. You could field that from one big family." She looked down at the pillar, at the bent arm, the spread fingers. "That's not the hard part. The hard part is the carving. The hard part is that fourteen people had to *agree* — to stop hunting, to feed each other while nobody hunted, to haul this thing and dress this stone for weeks, for a thing that doesn't feed anyone, that they couldn't eat, in a world where everyone was one bad week from starving. The labour's possible. The *wanting it that much* — that's the impossible thing. And they did it anyway."

"Try it, then," Frik said, and it wasn't a tic this time, it was Frik, dry, the actual man. "Get fourteen okes from the visitor centre and a rope and a nice slippery plank. Have a go. Daylight. Good plan. Modern boots." His shoulder jumped. "*Couldn't do it.*"

"We couldn't," Eylül agreed, and almost smiled. "People have tried, you know. Replicas. Smaller ones. With a crew that knows what's coming and wants the experiment to work, and a forklift on standby, and lunch. It is *brutally* hard. And then you stand under the real one, which they made the worst possible way, the first way anyone ever made anything this big, with no example to copy because there was no example, anywhere on Earth —"

She didn't finish it. She didn't have to. Mara had her hand on the camera. She had had her hand on the camera for some time.

—

Here was the frame.

Mara saw it whole, the way she used to see them, the way that had once made her name and then, in a white room in another country, made her put the cameras down for a year: Eylül crouched at the rail with the carved vulture rising behind her on the bone-coloured stone, the bald head of the bird hanging over the head of the living woman, the headless figure in the dust beyond, the light coming low and gold under the lip of the roof exactly the way the foundation paid for and could not get. Eylül's face open and unguarded, mid-sentence, talking to her dead. The Kurdish archaeologist and the eleven-thousand-year-old vulture and the man with no head, in one column of light.

It would run everywhere. She knew exactly how it would run. It would run with a headline that said something about the ancients and the birds and the secret of death, and it would be on the cover of the kind of magazine that had once flown her to the worst places on Earth, and it would have her name in nine-point type along the gutter, *Voss*, the way her name used to live along the gutter of images of things much worse than this. It would make this real to a million people. It would make this *hers*.

And it would make Eylül into a face on the cover of a death-secret. It would make her grief — because that crouch was grief, that gentleness toward the headless man was grief, Mara had photographed enough grief to know it without a translator — into content. It would put the vulture and the soft open face together in a frame that *said a sentence*, the one Eylül had just spent twenty minutes refusing to say, the one the place didn't get to have. It would caption these people. It would hand the man in the good shirt his riddle. It would take the *we don't know* and sell it as a *we found out*, and the foundation — the soft-handed man whose name was now a grenade in her bag — the foundation would love it most of all, because *mystery solved, move along, nothing left to protect* was the exact lie they were buying the unopened ninety percent with.

The shot that made her name would help them strip the hill.

Mara's finger was on the shutter. Her hands were very steady. Her hands were always steady; that was the trouble, that was what the white room had been about, the steadiness that didn't stop when it should, the body that could keep lifting the camera after the soul had walked out of the room and left it.

She thought about the bazaar yesterday, Naila against the old wall, the unbearable photograph she had not taken.

"Eylül," she said. Her voice came out level. "Can I make a picture of the pillar? The bird, the carving. Not you. The stone."

Eylül looked up at her, and something moved across her face — surprise, then a recognition, then a kind of thanks she didn't say out loud, because to say it out loud would have made the not-asking the normal thing and the asking the favour, and she would never let it sit that way round on her own hill. "Yes," she said simply. "The stone. Of course."

So Mara stood, and stepped left, and let the living woman fall out of the frame entirely, and waited for Eylül to rise and move so that the crouch and the open face and the grief went with her, out of the light, back into the privacy of the woman doing her work. And then it was only the stone. The vulture. The bent arm and the fingered hand on the belt. The man without his head, eleven and a half thousand years patient in the dust. She made the photograph small and exact and true, the carving and only the carving, the thing that documented the hole without stealing the people standing at the edge of it. She made it for the hill and not for the gutter. It would not be on any cover. It would not have her name down the side.

It was the best decision she had ever made with a camera, and she knew it the way you know a thing in the body before you know it in the head, and the knowing came without relief and without applause, just the small clean click of a door she could finally close.

She lowered the camera.

Down the boardwalk, at the far rail where the wind came over the lip of the plateau, a man in dark glasses stood with his back to all of them, very still, watching the road below — the one road in, the dust of it, a car a long way off coming up from the plain — and the low gold light was on him and Mara's eye found him out of habit and framed him out of habit and her finger never moved, because some part of her had already understood, days ago, in another country, that there was no use lifting it for that one; the camera simply would not hold him; he was the kind of thing that left no clean frame.

"Whose car," Naila said, beside her, reading the dust the way she read the stones.

Frik's head ducked. The truth came up through him before the question had even finished, flat and certain, a name surfacing for the second time in two days, and this time nobody flinched, because this time they'd been waiting for it.

"Reyes," he said.

And down on the hill, in the centre of the great oval ring, the two carved people stood and faced the east and the coming dust the way they had faced it through every sunrise since before the first seed was ever planted on Earth — waiting, easy, hands resting on their belts — for whatever the living were going to do next.
