# Chapter 14 — Clean Hands

Thursday came in white. The plain held its heat from before dawn, and by the time the convoy raised its dust on the access road — two glossy hire SUVs and a third car behind, lower, official plates — the light had already gone flat and merciless, the kind of light Naila hated because it killed shadow and shadow was how she read depth. Without shadow the pillars looked drawn rather than carved. She stood at the rail at the head of the boards and counted the vehicles and counted the men getting out of them and the numbers settled her the way numbers did. Three cars. Four men in shirts that had been ironed somewhere with electricity. Two women with lanyards. One man in a linen jacket who waited by the lead car until the dust laid itself down before he moved, because his shoes were pale and good.

That was Reyes. She knew him from the brochure before she knew him from the man.

"He waited for the dust," she said.

Beside her, Frik bounced once on his toes, a settling motion, his hand loose now — Devran had been taken back to the village before sunup, Eylül firm about that, *not today, not with lawyers* — and he echoed it back to her under his breath, *waited for the dust, waited for the dust,* the way he held a true thing in his mouth to keep it. Then, louder, not to anyone: "Clean shoes."

"Frik," said Mara, low.

"It's not — I'm not —" His shoulder jerked. "It's just true."

"I know it's true," Mara said. "Keep it for later."

Eylül didn't look round at any of them. She stood at the top of the boards with a clipboard she did not need and a thermos of her uncle's tea she did, and she watched the linen jacket come up the slope with the ironed shirts fanning slightly behind him, and Naila watched her watch, and learned more from Eylül's stillness than from all three cars.

Reyes stopped a courteous two metres off and put out his hand. "Dr. Demir."

"Mr. Reyes."

"Anton, please." He had the handshake of a man who'd been told his handshake was good. He turned the warmth of it on the rest of them like a lamp finding a corner. "And these must be the famous internet people. I watch, you know. I'm not embarrassed to say it. The episode on the chalk figures — the woman, Edie Vane —" he pressed a hand briefly to his own sternum, a man locating his sincerity, "— it stayed with me. You found a person under the data. Most people just find the data."

"We found a person," Mara said. Her camera was at her hip, capped. She had not lifted it. Naila clocked the cap and read the choice in it.

"That's the gift, isn't it." Reyes turned, took in the ring of pillars below, the canopy, the boards, the buried slope behind it all going up brown and patient toward the crown of the hill. He let himself be moved by it, and the thing Naila could not get past — the thing that made her arithmetic itch — was that he wasn't faking it. The brochure's prose had read like a lie. The man in front of it did not. He looked at the giants the way you'd look at someone in a hospital bed.

"Eleven and a half thousand years," he said. "Before the wheel. Before pottery. Schmidt pulls it out of a slope in the nineties and the whole story of us has to be rewritten — monuments first, then the farm, then the city, then everything. And it's *here*." He said *here* like a wound. "In a province that can't keep a museum's air-conditioning running through August. I've seen the budget lines, Dr. Demir. I've seen what reaches the dig and what doesn't. The geophysics says there are twenty enclosures under that hill. You've opened, what —"

"You know what we've opened."

"Two. You've opened two, properly. The rest is sitting in the ground while the slope moves and the water table does what water tables do and the funding gets voted on in Ankara by men who've never held a flint." He spread his hands, clean, open, nothing in them. "I'm not here to take anything. I'm here to keep it from being lost by neglect. The Trust puts a roof — a real roof, climate-controlled, the conservation it deserves — over the unexcavated enclosures *before* the next collapse, not after. We fund the dig you can't fund. The finds stay catalogued, stay studied, stay —" a small smile, "— stay *yours*, on paper. We just hold the door open. For all humanity. That's all heritage is. A door somebody pays to keep open."

It was a good speech. Naila could feel how good it was, the way you can feel a tune is in tune without being able to say why. Every line of it was a true line. The air conditioning did fail in August. The funding was voted on by men who'd never held a flint. The slope did move. And under all those true lines, like the dressed face of a pillar over the rough back you weren't meant to see, sat the thing that wasn't said, and Naila's whole body bent toward the unsaid thing the way it always did, the gap in the ledger, the entry that should be there and wasn't.

"For all humanity," Eylül repeated. Flat. "Whose roof."

"The Trust's, in the build phase, then —"

"Whose name on the access agreement. For how long. With what clause about the catalogue if the funding chain breaks." She didn't raise her voice. She had her uncle's tea in one hand. "I read the draft, Mr. Reyes. Page eleven. The rights to the *unexcavated material* — the ninety percent still in the ground, the stuff nobody's recorded yet, the stuff that has no provenance because it's never been touched — those convey to the Trust on signature. Not the finds we've already catalogued. The *unknown* ones. The ones where nobody can ever check what came out of which enclosure, because there's no record yet, because they're still in the dirt."

A small pause. The ironed shirts had stopped fanning.

"That's standard salvage language," Reyes said, and his voice didn't change, and that was the tell, Naila thought; a man defending a clean thing gets warmer, a man defending a clean-*looking* thing keeps very level. "Material without secure context is —"

"Is everything that's left," Eylül said. "That's the point. The ten percent that's dug, that's published, that has Schmidt's name and mine and the radiocarbon attached — you don't want that. You can't sell that, it's witnessed. You want the ninety percent that's never been seen, the part where a pillar can come out of enclosure C and surface in a private collection in Geneva labelled *Anatolia, ca. 9000 BCE, ex-Trust conservation programme,* and there is no slope diagram, no find number, no me, to say it's a lie."

The plain was very quiet. A bird did something far off. One of the lanyard women looked at her shoes.

Reyes did not look at his shoes. He looked at Eylül with what Naila could only read as genuine hurt, and it frightened her more than a sneer would have, because a sneer she could have done the arithmetic on.

"You think I'm a thief," he said quietly.

"I think you believe you're a rescuer," Eylül said. "I think that's worse, because it means you'll do it with a clear conscience and a press release."

---

Mara had the camera off her hip now. Naila watched her bring it up to chest height, not to her eye, the lens still capped, holding it the way you hold a tool you haven't decided to use. There was a drone up — Naila had heard it before she'd seen it, a wasp note over the canopy, one of the Trust's, a little white quadcopter making its slow pretty arc over the enclosure to gather the footage that would become the promo, the *for all humanity* sizzle reel, the empty cathedral set to strings. It hung over Pillar 18 and his fox and his fingered hands and it would have him for the brochure by lunchtime.

"They're filming," Mara said, not loud. To Eylül. Asking.

"Let them," Eylül said. "It's a public site under permit. They can film the stones." A beat. "They can't film the people without consent. That's the law and it's also the line, and Devran's home for a reason." She glanced at Mara. "Same goes for you."

"I know," Mara said. And she did the thing then that Naila had watched her not-quite-do for a week — she lowered the camera back to her hip and left the cap on, and stood with empty hands in front of the best frame of her professional life, the lawyers and the giants and the linen jacket and the lie, the photograph that would have gone everywhere with her name on it, and she let it go past her ungrabbed. Naila felt the cost of it come off her like heat off a stone. Witnessing without consuming. The discipline of the not-taken shot.

"Page eleven won't stop him," Mara said under her breath. "He'll argue salvage language till the funding window closes and then it's signed and it's done. He's not wrong that the slope's moving. That's the horrible part. He's *not wrong about the slope.*"

And that was the hook in it, Naila understood — not a hook anyone had set, just the true geometry of the thing. Reyes had built his grab out of true facts and pointed them at a real wound. You couldn't break him by calling him a liar, because the bricks were honest. You had to find the one place where the honest bricks didn't add up.

So she did the arithmetic.

She'd been doing it under everything else, the way she always did, the numbers running like a tap she couldn't turn off, and now she let them come to the front. The Trust's pitch rested on a single load-bearing claim, repeated in the brochure and twice this morning in the linen jacket's own mouth: *neglect.* That the site was being lost. That the part in the ground was perishing faster than the underfunded local team could save it, and therefore needed rescue, and therefore the rescuer's clauses were mercy.

She pulled the geophysics in her head. She'd read the published survey three times on the flight, the magnetometry, the ground-penetrating radar grids, the ~20 enclosures, the ~200 pillars at this hill alone, the famous ~10% opened and ~90% sleeping. The numbers were beautiful and they were stable. And stability was the thing.

"How fast is the slope moving," Naila said.

Everyone looked at her. She didn't like everyone looking at her and she kept going anyway because the number mattered more than the looking.

Reyes recovered first, gracious. "Centimetres a year, in the worst sectors. Over a decade —"

"Centimetres a year." She had it now, clean, and the cleanness of it was the only comfortable thing in the white morning. "You said the slope's moving and the water table's doing what water tables do and the next collapse is coming and that's why the ninety percent has to be rescued *now*, this funding window, this signature. But the survey's been run three times in twenty-five years. Schmidt's grid, the DAI grid, the 2019 grid. And the enclosures haven't moved. The magnetometry overlays. The anomalies sit in the same place to within the instrument error. The thing that's been holding them for eleven and a half thousand years —" she nodded down at the buried slope, at whatever it was that had put the dirt over the giants, the backfill they fought about, the landslide they fought about, the argument nobody on earth had won, "— whatever buried them, intentional or a slope-wash, it's the same dirt that's been protecting them since the Neolithic. It's not racing the collapse. It's the *reason* there's anything left to find. The ground is the conservation. It's been the conservation for ninety-six centuries. Your roof isn't saving it from neglect. There's no neglect. There's just dirt that's slower than your funding cycle."

She ran out of breath. Her hands were doing the thing where they pressed flat against her thighs, hard, and she let them, because nobody was making her stop.

"That's —" Reyes began.

"The urgency is invented," Naila said. "Not the slope. The slope's real, you were right about the slope. The *urgency* is invented. You need it to be perishing this quarter so the clause that hands you the unrecorded ninety percent looks like mercy instead of a buy. But the radiocarbon's secure and the survey overlays and the dirt's slower than you. There's no clock. You built a clock."

The drone wasp-noted over them. Somewhere a vehicle ticked, cooling.

"It's a great deal of confidence," Reyes said, and now there was an edge under the warmth, the first one, fine as a flint flake, "from a woman who read a survey on a plane."

"I read it three times," Naila said, because that was the relevant fact.

---

That was the moment, and afterward Naila could reconstruct it exactly, the way she could reconstruct anything, because it had a clean shape — the relay, no one of them able to carry it alone, the thing that only worked because it went hand to hand.

Naila had the arithmetic. *There's no clock; the ground is the conservation.* It killed the *neglect* claim cold. But arithmetic doesn't move a room; it only loads the chamber. The room was full of men in ironed shirts who could take a true number and bury it under salvage language and slope diagrams until the white light moved off the hill and the moment passed and the brochure went out anyway, *for all humanity,* strings rising.

Mara had the lens. She'd just declined the photograph that would have made her — and now she lifted the camera at last, off her hip, to her eye, and Naila heard the cap come off, that small mechanical sound, and Reyes turned toward it on reflex the way a man with good shoes turns toward a camera, his face arranging itself into the sincere wound, the rescuer's grief. And Mara held him there, not even shooting, just *aimed*, holding the linen jacket in the frame in front of his own drone, and the holding said: *I see which picture this is. I know the difference between the frame that documents you and the frame that helps you. I'm choosing.* He didn't know which she'd take. That uncertainty was a leash and she had him on it for one second, two.

And in that held second, with Reyes's face turned and arranged and the room gone still around the unanswered number, Frik Basson's body did the thing it had done his whole life in every room they'd thrown him out of. His shoulder jerked. His head pulled left. And the truth came up out of him the way it always came, involuntary, exquisitely timed, landing in the silence where it could not be taken back:

"You already have a buyer."

Nobody breathed.

Frik's hand flew to his own mouth, the old reflex, the apology that had lived in his body since he was six. "I didn't — I'm not —" The shoulder again. "Sorry. *Sorry.* It just —"

But it was out. And Naila watched Anton Reyes's clean-handed face do, for less than a second, the one thing the brochure could never do: it confirmed. Not in words. The brochure would never use words. It was the flick — the involuntary glance, fast as Frik's tic and just as honest, toward the third car, the low one, the official plates, where a man had stayed inside with the windows up the whole morning, a man nobody had introduced, the man you bring when the deal is already done and you're here to witness the signature. The flick said *how does he know.* And the *how does he know* said *there's a he to know about.*

Mara's shutter fired. Once. Naila heard it. Not the empty-cathedral frame, not the giants and the strings. The frame of a man in a linen jacket glancing at the car that held the buyer, in the half-second after a stranger's involuntary mouth had named the thing the salvage language was built to hide.

That was the one. That was the photograph that documented the hole instead of decorating it.

"That's enough," Reyes said. The warmth was gone now. Not into a sneer — he was too good for a sneer — but into the flat administrative cold of a man recalculating exposure. "Dr. Demir, I think we've —"

"Eylül," said Eylül. "If we're past *Anton*, we're past *Dr. Demir.* And no. We're not done. *I'm* not done." She set the thermos down on the rail, deliberate, the way you put down a thing you've finished holding so your hands are free. "You came here Thursday because Thursday's when the provincial committee meets in Urfa and you wanted the access agreement on the table before they sat. You wanted my signature this morning so it'd read as the local archaeologist's blessing." She took her phone out. "The committee's in session in forty minutes. The Director of the Museum is on it. Cemal Demir's cousin's on it — that's how a poor province that can't keep the air-con running actually works, Mr. Reyes, it works on cousins, it's the densest provenance network you'll ever fail to buy. I'm sending three things to that committee right now. The survey overlay that says there's no collapse clock — that's hers." A nod at Naila, brief, exact, *hers,* and Naila felt the credit land where it should, the relay acknowledged. "Page eleven, highlighted, the unrecorded-material conveyance. And one photograph." She didn't look at Mara. "If she'll license it to the site and not to a wire."

"It's the site's," Mara said. "It was always going to be the site's."

"Then it's evidence," Eylül said, "not a scoop. Send it to me."

The phone made its sound. Off it went — the number, the clause, the flicked glance — over the white plain toward a committee room in Sanliurfa where men who'd held flints would read it with cousins' eyes.

Reyes stood very still in his clean shoes. The drone, up over Pillar 18, kept making its pretty arc, gathering footage for a promo that had just, somewhere in the last ninety seconds, become unusable — because you cannot cut *for all humanity* over a deal the local committee voted down on Thursday morning with the archaeologist's number in their hands and the buyer's car in the photograph.

"You'll lose the funding," he said. It came out almost gentle. Almost a warning offered in good faith, and Naila believed it was, half of it; the slope was real, the air-con did fail, the men in Ankara had never held a flint. "Whatever you think I am. The roof was real. The conservation was real. You're choosing the slope over the science because you won't take a clean man's hand."

"I'm choosing the ground that's held it for ninety-six centuries," Eylül said, "over a man who built a clock to scare me out of it. The slope's slower than you. She did the arithmetic." A breath. "Go to your committee meeting, Mr. Reyes. We'll be at ours."

---

He went down the boards the way he'd come up them, unhurried, a man who would survive this and try the next province, and his ironed shirts went down after him, and the low car with the windows up turned around without anybody getting out of it, and the drone peeled off the sky and went home to its case. Naila watched the dust raise itself and lay itself back down. Three cars out. The number was satisfying. The number was the same as the number coming in and that was correct.

Down by the rope at the head of the boards, near where Reyes had paused for his shoes that morning, a folded square of white cotton sat weighted under a stone, very clean, exactly where it had been left, and a shape that might have been standing near it for the last hour was, when Naila looked properly, not there, and the dust by the rail held no clean print of any boot. Eylül looked at the white square and didn't say anything about it, which Naila had learned was its own kind of saying.

The pillars stood in their ring, fingered hands meeting over their belts, foxes at their loincloths, watching the cars go the way they'd watched everything go for eleven and a half thousand years, and the ninety percent slept under the slope behind them, unrecorded, unprovenanced, unbought, slower than any clock a clean-handed man could build.

"For now," Eylül said. She picked her thermos back up off the rail. "It holds for now."

She poured tea into the cap of it, her uncle's tea, dark and oversweet, and handed it not to Naila and not to Mara but to Frik Basson, who had said the true thing in the room where it couldn't be taken back, the way his body had said true things in every room that had ever shown him the door — except this one.

"Drink," she said. "You did a thing."

And Frik took the cup in both hands, and his shoulder jerked once, settling, and he didn't apologise for it.
