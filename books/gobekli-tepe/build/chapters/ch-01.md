# Chapter 1 — The World's First Temple, Solved

Naila counted the cones first. There were forty-one orange cones laid in a line along the cordon, and the line wanted to be straight and wasn't, and that was the first wrong thing.

She let it sit. A cordon line bending six degrees off true was nobody's crime. But the line was supposed to read as *control* — the whole production read as control — and the people who'd laid it had no idea it announced the opposite to anyone who counted. The cones were too far apart at the western end, where the camera wouldn't go, and clustered tight at the eastern end, where it would. Forty-one cones. A budget for forty. Someone had grabbed one extra from the van and not bothered to even it out.

"You're doing the cones," Mara said beside her. Not a question. Mara had her camera down at her hip, not up, and she was watching Naila watch.

"They didn't have enough to do it properly," Naila said. "So they did it where it films."

"That's the whole company in one sentence." Mara lifted the camera, then didn't. Lowered it. Let it hang.

They stood at the edge of the world's first temple, which is to say they stood on a low ridge of pale, baked, broken ground above Şanlıurfa, with the dust coming up gold in the slant light and the diesel smell of three generators laid under everything, and below them a man in a linen jacket the colour of clean sand was being filmed telling the truth in a way designed to make a lie.

His name was Anton Reyes. The banner behind him said ANATOLIA HERITAGE TRUST in a serif so restrained it cost money. He had a lavalier mic and a way of pausing before the important words that meant he'd done this a hundred times and the pauses tested clean.

"Eleven and a half thousand years," Reyes was saying, to the lens, to the future, to whoever the Trust's video would land in front of. "Six thousand years before Stonehenge. Built — and I want to be careful here, because this is the part people get wrong — built by *hunter-gatherers*. People with no farming. No pottery. No wheel. No writing." He let that breathe. "And they raised stone pillars five and a half metres tall and carved them with foxes and vultures and *lions*. Before the plough. Before the village."

It was all true. That was the thing Naila kept having to hold. Every fact out of the man's mouth was a real fact, and the radiocarbon under it was good, and the wonder he was rationing out in those tested pauses was an actual wonder she felt in her own sternum even now, with the generators going and the cones lying crooked. Klaus Schmidt had dug here from 1995, German Archaeological Institute, and what he'd pulled out of this hill had bent the spine of the whole story — monuments first, then the village, possibly the monuments *making* the village. She'd read the papers. She'd read the papers twice.

Reyes turned, lovely, toward the enclosures behind him — the famous oval rings, the T-pillars standing in them grey and patient under their shelter roof.

"And the Trust is proud," he said, "to have helped bring this site to the world. Catalogued. Mapped. Secured." The pause. "Solved. The mystery of Göbekli Tepe — answered. A gathering place. A meeting ground. The first place our ancestors came together." Pause. "And safe. In careful hands."

"Liar," Frik said.

He said it quietly, into his own collar, the way he said most of them lately, head ducked, jaw working before and after the word like it was a stone he'd had to spit. He'd been good for almost a minute. Naila had been half-counting that too without meaning to.

"Sorry," he added. "Sorry. Solved. *Solved.*" The echo came off him three times, softer each time, his hand pressing flat against his own thigh. "Not my — that one's mine but it's also just *true*, hey."

"It's true," Naila agreed.

"He didn't lie once and the whole thing's a lie. How does he *do* that." Frik blinked hard, twice, a tic riding through, and breathed out long through his nose. "Sorry."

"Stop saying sorry," Mara said, easy, not looking at him, eyes on Reyes. "You're allowed to hate him out loud. We all are, we're just being quiet about it like cowards."

Jakobus stood a little apart from the three of them, further along the ridge, where the cordon line bent. He had his shades on against the gold light and a flat cap and the worn tan jacket, and he was not looking at Reyes at all. He was looking at the pillars. He had been looking at the pillars since they'd arrived, in the particular still way he had, the way that made the production assistants who passed him glance twice and then decide he was hired and then decide he wasn't and then decide not to ask. He'd brought them here. He'd flown them across the world and shipped the Beast across a sea and stood them on this ridge, and now he stood at the edge of it the way he stood at the edge of everything, and let them be the ones looking at the thing.

"He keeps saying *our*," Naila said.

"Mm?"

"*Our* ancestors. *Helped bring this site to the world.* *Our hands.*" She watched the man's clean jacket. "He's from a private antiquities concern. The dig is a Turkish state project — Taş Tepeler, the whole regional programme, Şanlıurfa museum, the ministry. He keeps standing where the dig is so the *our* lands on the dig. But the sentences are about the Trust."

Mara made a low sound. "That's the steal."

"That's the steal," Naila said.

She knew the shape of it because she'd seen the shape of it in the paperwork before they'd seen the man. Jakobus had a way of getting paperwork. The Anatolia Heritage Trust was buying things — access, geophysics data, the *rights to publish* on enclosures that hadn't been opened yet. And the argument that let them buy it, the argument under the linen jacket, was the word *solved*. If it's solved, there's nothing left to protect. If it's a gathering place, just a meeting ground, mystery answered, then the ninety percent of this hill still under the dirt is a parking lot. You don't fight for a parking lot. You sell the parking lot to the careful hands.

Two hundred pillars, at Göbekli alone. Around twenty enclosures. About one in ten dug. The rest still down there in the dark, holding whatever they held.

"Where's the local," Frik said. "The one Jakobus said. The — Eylül."

Naila scanned. There was a cluster of project people in dust-coloured field clothes standing well back from the Trust's setup, arms folded, not part of it, watching it happen to their site with the specific patience of people who have watched a great many things happen to their site. One of them, a woman maybe a decade older than Naila, dark hair under a faded cap, was not folding her arms. She had a clipboard against her hip and she wasn't watching Reyes. She was watching the *other* thing — the thing the Trust had set up forty metres downslope, inside the eastern cluster of cones, where the camera could swing to it on a track.

"There," Naila said.

The other thing was the reason they'd come.

The Trust had built a set. A flat pad of compacted earth, raked. A length of timber track, two parallel beams. A block of stone the size of a small car — pale limestone, freshly cut, a rough rectangle that wanted, you could see it wanted, to become a T. Around it, eight people in matching grey coveralls with ANATOLIA HERITAGE TRUST stitched on the back, hard hats, gloves, water bottles in a neat row. A drone hovering. A second camera on a tripod. A man with a tablet who was clearly the experimental-archaeology consultant, hired in, hard hat too new.

"They're going to do the easy version," Frik said. "On film. To show it's easy."

"Yes," Naila said.

"Oh, *no*," Frik said, with real feeling, and grinned for the first time all morning. "Oh, this is going to be *kak*."

Naila looked at her watch. It was 9:14.

She started counting.

---

The consultant — his name carried up the slope as *Devrim*, and he had the harried look of a man who'd told someone the timeline was wrong and been overruled — got his eight people on the ropes by 9:22. That was eight minutes to organise eight people. Naila noted it. The plan, as far as she could read it from above, was sound on paper: drag the block along the lubricated timber track to the upright position, lever it into a prepared socket, raise it with ropes and an A-frame, then carve the relief while it stood. One day. Daylight, modern crew, modern kit, a plan. The Trust's whole pitch.

It was a good plan. That was what nobody filming it understood. E. B. Banning had run the arithmetic for real, in a real journal — seven to fourteen people could move a pillar this size with ropes and a greased track. Not hundreds. Not thousands. Not, Naila thought, with the particular cold pleasure of killing a bad number, an *impossible workforce*. The over-reach people loved — *it would take ten thousand slaves, it's impossible, therefore* — that over-reach was just wrong, and you could feel how wrong it was by watching eight people in coveralls actually try.

Because the plan was sound and they still couldn't do it.

The block came off its blocks and onto the track at 9:31 and immediately did the first thing the plan hadn't accounted for: it twisted. Not much. A few degrees. But a few degrees of an eight-tonne block put the whole load onto one beam, and the beam complained, a long woody groan that carried up the ridge over the generators, and four of the eight let go to grab it, which put the rest of the load onto two people who very sensibly also let go.

"Eight minutes to load," Naila said. "Off the track in under thirty seconds."

"They're laughing," Mara said. She had the camera up now, low, careful, framing not the people but the *block* — the patient stupid weight of the thing. "Down there. They think it's funny."

"It is funny," Frik said. "It's funny until you've got to do it twenty-six more times for a whole enclosure and then a roof and then come back in spring and do another one." His hand was tapping his thigh, four-beat, steady, a metronome Naila had come to read as *Frik thinking*, not *Frik struggling*. "Hey. They've got the block wrong way up."

Naila looked. He was right. The grain of the limestone — she squinted — the bedding planes ran the wrong way for the shape they wanted. To carve a T-pillar you wanted the flat broad faces to take the relief, and the way they'd quarried this block, the strongest plane to spall along was exactly where the head of the T had to cantilever out. The first hard knock at the neck of the T and it would shear.

"They don't know that yet," she said.

"They'll know it at lunch," Frik said, happily.

It took them until 10:40 to get the block to the foot of the socket. An hour and nine minutes, four people, then six, then all eight, then a break, then a different rope configuration that the consultant clearly hadn't wanted and the director clearly had, because the director wanted the *raising* on camera before the light went flat. The drone kept dropping in close for the hero shot and the rotor wash kept lifting fine pale dust off the raked pad into everyone's eyes, and at one point a woman in coveralls waved it off with both arms and the drone retreated and you could read, even from the ridge, the small mutiny in her shoulders.

Down among the project people, the woman with the clipboard — Eylül — had drifted closer to the set, and she was watching the block with an expression Naila wanted to photograph and couldn't, because it wasn't an expression you could photograph. It wasn't contempt. The Trust people would have read contempt and felt better. It was closer to the look you'd give a stranger handling your grandmother's hands roughly while explaining your grandmother to you.

"She knows something we don't," Naila said.

"She knows everything we don't," Mara said. "We're the tourists, Nai."

The raise started at 11:50. The A-frame went up, the ropes went taut, the eight became eleven — they'd pulled in three of the camera grips, which Naila also noted, *eleven now, not eight, the number keeps climbing* — and the block came off the ground at one end, beautifully, a metre, a metre and a half, the crowd of production people making the involuntary *ooh* a crowd makes, and the drone diving, and Reyes himself drifting down from his banner to be filmed near the triumph.

At a metre and a half the block found out about its own centre of mass.

It didn't fall. Naila wanted to be fair about that, later, when she wrote it up — it didn't crash, nobody was hurt, it was never *dangerous*, exactly, because they had modern rope and modern sense. What it did was worse, for them. It *settled*. It found the place it wanted to be, which was not the place the plan wanted it, and it leaned into the ropes with the whole patient argument of eight tonnes, and the eleven people leaning back against it discovered they were not raising it anymore. They were *holding* it. And you can raise a thing in bursts, with your whole body, in glory. You cannot hold a thing. Holding is just losing slowly with your feet planted.

You could see the exact second they understood. It moved through the eleven of them like a temperature change.

"There it is," Frik said, very softly, and there was no cruelty in it at all now. He'd stopped grinning. "Ag, man. *There it is.*"

Naila had stopped counting the people. She was counting something else now, and her chest had gone quiet and clean the way it did when the numbers lined up, that good cold quiet that other people didn't seem to have a word for and called *cold* when they noticed it on her face.

Because here was the arithmetic, and the arithmetic was the whole thing.

Eleven people. Modern rope. A steel A-frame. A greased timber track they hadn't had to fell, shape, and haul themselves. A pre-dug socket they hadn't had to cut into bedrock with stone tools. Tea and water bottles and a van and lunch coming. Daylight. A plan, on a tablet, written by a consultant with a degree. Every advantage. And they were standing in the dust holding a block they could not finish, and the block had not even been *carved* yet — no fox, no vulture, no arms bending at the elbow, no fingered hands meeting over a belt, none of the part that made it a *person* instead of a stone.

And the people who'd actually done this, who'd done it for real, eleven and a half thousand years ago, had done it without the rope and without the steel and without the metal to cut the stone with and without the village to feed the crew and without — this was the part that went all the way down — *without anyone before them having done it.* No precedent. No tablet. Nobody to copy. They'd done it the way you do a thing the first time anything has ever been done.

It was not impossible. That was what she'd write. She wanted to be exact, because the over-reachers got everything by being inexact, and the surest way to lose an argument to the woo was to overstate it and get caught. Seven to fourteen people. Ropes. A track. It was *possible.* Banning was right and the man on the documentary about the alien stonemasons was wrong, and she would prove that cold, on paper, with her own hands, because that was the work.

It was possible. It just wasn't *easy*. And the Trust had bet the whole site on *easy*.

"Cut it," someone called down on the set. The director. Sharp. "Cut. Cut, cut, *kapatın şunu* — kill the cameras, kill the drone."

The drone hung a second too long.

"*Kill it.*"

The drone retreated. The tripod camera swung away, deliberately, toward the enclosures, toward the safe finished famous standing pillars, so that whatever the edit was it would not have this in it. And the block stayed up there at its tired angle in the white noon, held by eleven people who could not put it down and could not raise it, with Anton Reyes standing in his clean linen at the edge of the raked pad with his hand half-lifted toward a triumph that wasn't going to arrive, and the awful thing, the thing Naila found she couldn't look away from, was that his face wasn't a villain's face. It was the face of a man watching his good intentions fail in front of the people he'd promised. He believed it. That was the part nobody warned you about. He genuinely thought the careful hands were his.

Up on the ridge nobody said anything for a moment.

Then Frik laughed — one short bark, surprised out of him, more than half horror in it. "They just showed everybody," he said. "On film. They set up a camera to prove it's solved and they just — they showed *everybody* it isn't."

"They cut the camera," Mara said.

"They cut *that* camera," Naila said.

Because there were other cameras. There were the project people's phones, half of them up. There was Mara, who had not been told to cut anything and who had, Naila saw, taken exactly one frame the whole morning — not of the failure, not of the eleven sweating people, not the cheap shot — but of the *block itself*, off the ground at its tired angle, alone, the patient impossible-not-impossible weight of it filling the frame with the cordon's crooked cone-line just visible behind, and nothing in it that would shame a single person who'd pulled on those ropes.

"Ask first," Mara said, to herself, the way she did, and lowered the camera. "Going to ask her if I can use even that."

Down the slope, Eylül Demir turned away from the set. She'd seen enough. She walked back up toward the project cluster, and her path took her along the bent part of the cordon, near where Jakobus stood, and Naila watched the two of them pass within a few feet of each other without speaking — the woman with the clipboard and the still man in the shades — and watched Eylül glance at him once, the way everyone glanced at him once, and then keep walking, because she had a hill to protect and he was just another stranger on it, for now.

Jakobus took off his shades. He looked up the slope at the three of them, and he tipped his head a few degrees toward the standing block — the failed, held, un-carved block — and then toward the finished pillars in their shelter, the real ones, the ones with the arms bending at the elbow and the fingered hands and the fox cradled at the belt.

"What's it *for*," he said. Not loud. He didn't repeat it. He put the shades back on and went back to watching the hill, and left the question lying there on the ridge for them to pick up or not.

Naila looked at her watch. 12:03.

She had the start time. She had the load-out, the twist, the wrong-grain block, the climbing headcount, the held angle. She had a notebook full of a thing the Trust would spend real money pretending hadn't happened. She had Banning's number, seven to fourteen, the number that killed the *impossible*, the number that was on her side.

And underneath it, where the clean cold quiet usually closed over and let her rest, something would not close.

It wasn't the weight. The weight she could do. The weight was *possible* and she would prove it.

It was the order of it. Foragers. Before the pots. Before the seed went in the ground.

She'd carry that down the hill, and it would keep her up that night in the room in Urfa with the call to prayer coming over the rooftops, and in the morning she'd open the file and start with the part she could win — the workforce, the ropes, the lubricated track — and find, the way you find a stone you can't lift in a field you thought you'd cleared, that the part she could win wasn't the part that mattered.
