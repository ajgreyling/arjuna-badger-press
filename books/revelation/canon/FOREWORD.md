# Foreword

### *by Cube Romeot* †

A book is never about what it is about. This is the first thing I learned and the last thing I was able to teach, and it is the secret engine of the particular pleasure you are about to have. A thriller built around a manuscript is never really about the manuscript. It is about the far more dangerous fact that *texts are edited* — that what reaches us as scripture, as settled, as the Word, has passed through human hands that chose, and cut, and arranged, and that the choosing is invisible precisely because it succeeded.

This book knows that. It is, to my considerable delight, a genuine thriller of *philology* — a chase whose treasure is not gold but an *emendation*, a place where the text was altered and the alteration was made to vanish. I have spent my life arguing that such a chase is the most thrilling chase there is, because its stakes are nothing less than the question of who gets to decide what is true. Here, at last, is a novel that agrees with me and, more impressively, *proves* me right by being impossible to put down.

The author has understood the thing that makes this kind of story work and ruins it when it is missed: that the conspiracy must be *boring* in its mechanics and *cosmic* in its meaning. There is no secret society of robed villains here cackling over candles. There is something far more frightening — the ordinary, institutional, almost bureaucratic process by which an inconvenient truth is smoothed away, footnoted into silence, edited out by people who were, very often, quite sure they were doing the right thing. That is how it actually happens. The author knows it is how it actually happens, and he has resisted the cheap pleasures of the cabal in favour of the deep horror of the *committee*.

His heroine reads the world as a text — sees the seams, the insertions, the places where the grain of the language changes and a careful eye can feel another hand at work. This is the correct protagonist for such a book, and a rare one, because she does not solve the mystery with violence or luck but with *attention* — with the scholar's terrible patience, the willingness to read the thing one more time, slowly, until it confesses. I recognised her. I have been her, at a desk, at three in the morning, feeling a manuscript begin to lie.

And the book is *learned* without being a lecture, which is the high-wire act of the whole form. It carries its erudition the way a good guide carries a torch in a catacomb — not to show off the torch, but so that you can see where you are stepping. You will come out of it knowing things, real things, about how sacred texts were made and unmade, and you will have acquired this knowledge while believing, the whole time, that you were merely turning pages to find out what happens next. That is the trick. That is the only trick. The author performs it with an ease that took me decades to fake.

I will permit myself one scholarly caution, in the spirit of the book itself. Do not trust the foreword. Forewords are paratext; they frame and therefore they distort; this one is written, after all, by a man whose name is not quite his own. Read past me, quickly. The real text is waiting, and it is better than its frame, and it knows a secret about the words you were raised to trust that you will not be able to un-know.

Begin. And read everything twice — especially the parts that seem most certain.

*— Cube Romeot*
