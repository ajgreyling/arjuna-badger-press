# Foreword — AFRIKA 2100 (draft)

> Author's foreword for the published edition. House convention: honest about what is real,
> what is imagined, and what is owed respect. Draft — finalise after the manuscript.

---

This is a work of fiction, and it is the fourth time I have walked this ground.

*RESONANCE*, *REVELATION*, and *RELIC* told the story of a machine beneath Africa and the
people who had to decide what to do about it. This book is not their sequel so much as their
consequence: it asks what the world looks like seventy years after a handful of engineers
chose understanding over power — and what happens when the question they answered gets asked
again, larger, by someone standing politely on the tarmac.

Some of this book's furniture is real. The physics of a Carrington-class solar flare is real,
and the vulnerability of long high-latitude power grids to geomagnetically induced currents
is real — as is the relative shelter of southern latitudes. The Bushveld Complex really does
hold most of the world's platinum. The Witwatersrand's gold really is older than almost
anything human hands have touched. The Amish and Old Order communities really do hold, intact
and living, the complete craft of a life that does not need the grid — and the imagined
partnership in these pages is written in admiration of that competence, not as costume. Where
the book touches Plain faith and practice, it does so with respect and with the help of
readers who know it from inside; the errors that remain are mine.

The rest — the visitors, the instrument, the Fold, the port at eGoli, and the engineer-saint
whose copyleft stack carries this imagined century — is fiction, built the way this house
builds: every miracle a machine, every faith honoured as meaning, both sides of every
contested thing given their best argument.

South Africa is at the centre of this book because it is at the centre of the trilogy, and
the trilogy put it there on the evidence: the oldest scar, the deepest gold, the longest
human story. If the twenty-second century in these pages runs through Johannesburg, that is
not a fantasy of the future. It is a memory of how long this ground has already carried us.

We are, all of us, more alike than we are different.

— Andries J. Greyling
