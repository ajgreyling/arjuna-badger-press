# Landing Day — 2071, the texture

> Street-level world for the first-contact set-piece (C-18, [`CH_FIRST_CONTACT.md`](CH_FIRST_CONTACT.md)).
> Texture bible, not plot. Grounded register per C-10: no invasion, no panic-movie.

## The governing fact: it was an appointment, not an ambush

The craft has been **visible on approach since 2066** (C-02). Five years. Long enough to be
diarised, argued about in parliament, taught in schools, and finally to become boring. A whole
grade of children has never known a sky without it.

**So the emotional key of Landing Day is not terror. It is the unbearable ordinariness of a
scheduled miracle** — the same nerve as a funeral you've known about for a year and still
aren't ready for. People took leave. People couldn't get leave. Some worked the whole thing
through a shift and heard about it afterwards.

Anyone writing panic in this chapter has misread the book.

## Where the country is, seven years after the Flare

2064 the grids died and the South held. **2064–2069 were the hunger years.** 2071 is *two years
into not being hungry* — which is its own specific feeling: new fat on children, food still
counted, nobody yet trusting the counting to stop. Recovery is younger than the approach.

South Africa in 2071 is not triumphant. It is **indispensable and exhausted** — the country that
kept its lights on and has been feeding, meshing and repairing a hemisphere ever since, with
the resentment and pride that earns.

## What was on the radio

**Radio wins the day, and it matters that it does.** The mesh (LEGACY_TECH) is store-and-forward,
low-bandwidth, brilliant for text and voice and useless for pictures at scale. Radio is
one-to-many, cheap, and survived everything.

- **isiZulu and Sesotho talk radio carries the country** — phone-ins from 04:00, the presenters
  doing what SA presenters have always done: half sermon, half comedy, entirely refusing to
  pretend. Callers argue about whether to go to work.
- **The commentary problem:** nobody knows the register. Sports commentators get drafted because
  they're the only people trained to narrate a thing arriving slowly. This is remembered
  afterwards as either the most human or the most embarrassing decision of the century,
  depending who you ask. **It is both, and the chapter should let it be both.**
- **Church broadcast** all morning on the religious stations, and not one message agrees with
  another.
- **The North hears it and does not see it.** Compact zones, Europe, the whole rebuilt-meshed
  hemisphere: audio only, relayed. For most of humanity, first contact is **a sound.** A voice
  from Johannesburg describing a thing landing on the Witwatersrand.

## What was on television

**Television is a southern privilege in 2071**, and everyone knows it. SA broadcast exists
because SA has power. The signal goes out to a world that mostly can't render it and listens
instead.

- Split screens, a countdown clock nobody asked for, and a panel of experts who have been
  booked for five years and have run out of things to say.
- Long stretches of **nothing happening**, which is the truest thing television did that day.
- The camera positions become iconic. The footage is still being shown in 2100 — worn, quoted,
  fossilised into liturgy. This is the footage in the transcript scene the Act II/III beat uses.

## What was on the mesh

Text, voice notes, and **phone-to-phone gossip moving faster than any broadcast** — the mesh's
native behaviour (BadgerNet ancestry: store-and-forward epidemic gossip).

- Rumour outruns fact all day and is mostly wrong in ways that are later funny.
- Every family thread is alive. People message relatives in the North to say *are you hearing
  this*, and the replies come back late because the mesh is doing its patient thing.
- The ledger-coin jokes start here and never stop.

## The street, the township, the kitchen

- **Rooftops.** Soweto, Alexandra, Tembisa, Katlehong — anywhere with a view west toward the
  reef. Chairs carried up. Neighbours who don't speak, speaking.
- **Taxis stop running by mid-morning**, which does more to halt the country than any
  government instruction. The rank is where the arguing is best.
- **Spaza shops sell out of everything** — not looting, stocking. The instinct of people who
  remember 2064: whatever this is, be indoors with food.
- **Church halls, mosques, temples full on a weekday.** Also: full car parks outside them, of
  people who wanted to be near a building rather than in one.
- **Braai smoke by afternoon**, because it is South Africa and the day is long and people have
  to eat. The most-quoted line of the century is somebody's uncle, on air, being asked what he
  feels and answering that the meat won't cook itself.
- **The people who worked.** Nurses, mesh technicians, water crews. The chapter should hold at
  least one person who missed it because a pump needed fixing — and does not regret it.
- **Children treat it as a public holiday**, which is unbearable and correct.

## The weathers being born

Landing Day is **genesis day** for things the 2100 novel treats as ancient:

- **Abokuqala / the Firstborn** is born in this decade (WORLD §Firstborn, C-13). Somewhere in
  this crowd is the first person to say aloud that *they came here, to us, and that means
  something about us.* It is said on Landing Day as **joy**, not doctrine. The chapter should
  plant it in a mouth that means no harm — the reader who knows what it becomes will feel the
  floor tilt. **Do not have anyone argue it down. Nobody argued it down.**
- **Saint-hunger:** G is thirteen years dead (2058). His face is already on things. Somebody in
  the crowd says *he should have seen this* and somebody else says *he did*, meaning the maths,
  and neither is wrong.
- **Receipt-faith:** the pedants are out too — people reading the published telemetry aloud on
  the radio because the numbers are the only thing not lying yet.
- **The Fold** is mid-assembly (2068–2072). On Landing Day it is still a rumour of a practice,
  not a church.

## Register rules

- **Grounded. No invasion, no sirens, no fighter jets, no president at a lectern saving anyone.**
- Specific, ordinary, South African. Argument as the national love language.
- Funny where it would really be funny. The day was funny in places and pretending otherwise is
  a lie the book doesn't tell.
- **The biggest emotional beat is anticlimax**, and Gogo — who is working, who is survey — feels
  it before anyone: the thing lands, and then there is admin.
- No alcohol as the engine of any scene.
