# The Custodial Brake

*eGoli Spaceport · five weeks to Renewal*

The Commissioner's office sent a car, which Naledi declined, and a follow-up note saying the
Commissioner entirely understood and would she prefer the port canteen, which was how she
learned the first true thing about Dr. Sefako Marais: he did his homework, and his homework
told him she'd rather meet where the tea was bad and the ground was hers.

He was waiting at a corner table at oh-seven-hundred with two cups and no aides, a broad grey
man in a suit the colour of good concrete, and he stood to shake her hand like she was the
appointment he'd been looking forward to, which, she reminded herself, was a skill.

"Engineer Mokoena. Thank you for the hour. I know what shift-change costs." He sat, nudged
the better cup toward her, and put a printout of her ledger application on the table between
them, face up, unbothered, like a man laying down a card he respected. "This is fine work."

"It's an application, Commissioner. The fine work is sealed."

"Ha." It was a real laugh, brief. "So it is. Let me do this the way you'd want it done, then
— no fog." He turned the printout to face her and tapped the grounds paragraph. "One: your
curve is probably real. My office ran your public numbers past two people I trust; they
couldn't break it. Two: your application will be denied." He said it exactly as evenly as
everything else. "Not by me — the Stewards hold the key, and the Council moves at the speed
of stone. But the Port Authority countersign comes through this office, and I will not be
providing it before the Renewal. I wanted you to hear that from my mouth, with my reasons,
instead of from a form letter. You've earned the reasons."

"Which are?"

"Arithmetic." He wrapped both hands around his cup. "You know what I did before this? I ran
disaster logistics. Blackout Decade. I moved grain on a continent with no fuel and sixty
governments' worth of paperwork, and the thing that job burns into you is that being right
about a question is worth nothing — *nothing*, Engineer — next to being ready for its
answer. You have found a real question. I believe you. Now walk it forward with me: I
countersign, the Council deliberates in its own good time, and meanwhile the fact of the
request — which is already on your ledger for the world to read, and they are reading it,
my office counts newsfeed reads too — becomes the Renewal's centrepiece. Forty delegations
arrive not to sign a treaty but to relitigate twenty-nine years of consignments. The Keep
bloc gets a flag. The Give bloc gets a panic. And the counterparty, who have never once
answered a question we had no standing to ask, get asked it with the whole species watching
— six weeks before the only signature that matters." He turned his cup a quarter-turn. "We
can be curious for the next twenty-seven years, Engineer Mokoena. We can be *sovereign* for
the next six weeks. I know how that sentence sounds. I've had thirty years to get
comfortable with how my sentences sound. It's still the right arithmetic."

The canteen filled and emptied around them, grey coats and freight tabards. Naledi drank her
bad tea and gave herself a moment, the way the interview had taught her long ago: *read the
tolerance before you read the intent.*

"Your arithmetic has a suppressed term," she said.

"Show me."

"Volume." She set her cup down. "The amendment on the Renewal table raises consignment
volume. Everyone knows it; it's the whole agenda. So the choice isn't *ask now or ask in
twenty-seven years*. The choice is: ask while the question is small, or sign first and
triple the flow of the thing we didn't ask about — and then ask, from underneath it. Waiting
isn't neutral, Commissioner. Waiting is a position. It's the Give position, wearing
patience as a coat."

Marais looked at her for a long moment, and something in his face rearranged — not
displeasure; inventory. It occurred to her that people did not often show him their working.

"You'd have done well in grain," he said. He gathered the printout, squared it, and stood.
"The countersign answer is still no. But I'm going to do two things a form letter wouldn't.
First: after signature, I will co-file your disclosure application myself — this office,
your name and mine together, and the Council can move at the speed of stone with a
commissioner sitting on the stone. My word, on the ledger by end of day, where you keep
your things." He meant it, she could see, which was somehow the most alarming part of the
whole hour. "Second: a Renewal season needs its best people visible. I'm recommending you
to Dlamini as tuning liaison for the American consignment when it berths. Where the
delegations can watch South African verification at work."

*Where everyone can watch the flag risk stand in the light with her hands full,* Naledi
translated, and said, "Thank you, Commissioner," and meant about a third of it.

He paused at the door, because men like him always had a door line. "For what it's worth,
Engineer — I hope you're wrong about the margin. But I've read your grounds paragraph six
times now. *Good faith deserves good measurement.*" He tapped the doorframe once. "If the
world were run by verification techs, we'd deserve it sooner. Good morning."

He was gone before she could decide whether she'd been complimented, recruited, or
outflanked, and by mid-shift she knew the answer was all three, which was her first full
lesson in why nobody had ever beaten Sefako Marais at anything.

—

The second visitor found her at end of shift, in the refinery row, and made no appointment
at all.

She noticed him because he was the only stillness on the catwalk: a young man in the
Stewards' undyed robe, too young for it by every association the robe carried, standing at
the rail above the evening pour exactly where Baba Sipho had stood a week ago. Watching not
the gold. Her terminal, dark under her arm. Then her.

"Engineer Mokoena." He said her name like a man returning something she'd dropped. "Tefo. I
won't hold you. The Council received your application."

"And will move at the speed of stone. I've had the weather report."

"Mm. Stone moves," Tefo said. "Ask the Vredefort dome." Below them the pour ran gold light
up the walls, and he watched it with an expression she couldn't file: not reverence, exactly.
Grief's younger cousin. "I'm not here from the Council. If anyone asks, I'm not here at all —
you'd be surprised how good the robe is for that; people record the robe and forget the man.
I came because of the deposit note."

Her stomach went quiet. "You've read my access trail."

"No. I read the same note you did, four years ago, and I've been waiting since to see whose
name showed up next to it." He turned from the rail and faced her properly, and up close he
was her own age, with the eyes of a man who had been losing an argument politely for a long
time. "*D. Mokoena attending.* The survey engineer. The Council speaks of her the way they
speak of the founding generation — which is to say, in the past tense, and carefully. She
carried the series in, Engineer. Twelve hundred hours of the deep record, everything the
waking was, sealed on her survey handover — and there is a thing about that seal that even
your very complete application doesn't know to ask." He glanced along the catwalk, an
unhurried habit of a glance, and lowered his voice not at all, which she would later
understand was the tradecraft of a man who knew that whispering is what recordings listen
for. "Everyone assumes the Stewards sealed it. Look again at the countersign order, if your
grade shows you metadata. The Authority sealed it *at the surveyor's written insistence*.
Condition of handover. Her hand, her clause. The Council keeps the key because your
grandmother would not hand the series over any other way."

The pour ended. The moulds ticked. Somewhere below, a foreman called numbers in isiZulu, and
the great room went on being the world.

"Why," Naledi said, and found her voice had gone down to bearings and datum points, "would a
surveyor seal her own survey away from her own guild?"

"That," said Tefo, "is the first question I've heard anyone at this port ask in four years
that the Renewal can't answer." He drew the robe's hood up, which somehow made him more
anonymous rather than less, exactly as he'd said. "The Council will deny you, Engineer, this
season and probably next. The order is old and frightened and right about more than it can
say. But orders are made of people, and some of us think two hundred years of silence has
begun to protect the silence instead of the thing." He was three steps down the catwalk when
he turned, and gave her the rest the way you hand over a load — squarely, watching her take
the weight.

"What did she leave off the map, do you think?"

Then the robe went down the stairs into the shift-change crowd and was, as advertised,
nothing but a robe among coats, and Naledi Mokoena stood above the cooling gold with her
dark terminal under her arm, aware that the day had handed her three doors: one closed
kindly, one closed politely, and one, somewhere four kilometres under her family's own
signed and public and incomplete maps, that had never been closed at all — because it had
never been drawn.
