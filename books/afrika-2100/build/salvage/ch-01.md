# The Tolerances

*eGoli Spaceport, Gauteng · six weeks to Renewal*

> Read the tolerance before you read the intent. The maker is in the margin.
>
> — Tuning Guild founding manual, vol. I, epigraph. Author unrecorded.

The hull came down the last hundred metres the way everything about the visitors moved: as if
hurry were a concept it had examined and declined.

Naledi Mokoena watched it on the board, not through the glass. Juniors watched through the
glass. The glass showed you a shape the size of a grain silo lowering itself out of a highveld
morning with no sound except the wind it displaced, and the shape was so far outside what eyes
were built for that eyes just gave up and reported *large*. The board told the truth. The board
showed eleven hundred tonnes of something that read to radar like a question mark, riding down
a column of nothing anyone could name, at nine centimetres per second, dead on the centreline
of Berth Six.

"Coupling in forty," said Mothusi, on her left. "Rails are singing."

"They're not singing, they're drifting," Naledi said. "Bring your reference up before it
couples, or you'll be tuning the port to the ship instead of the ship to the port."

"Yebo, yes." He brought it up. The drift on his channel flattened out.

The berth rails were gold. Not plated — worked. Four hundred metres of conductor refined and
formed to tolerances the old bullion trade never dreamed of, laid into the apron like the
strings of an instrument the size of a city block, because that was what they were. When a hull
that size sat down, it did not land; it *coupled* — its own resonance let down gently into the
port's, matched note against note by the people in this room until the two systems agreed to
hold one standing wave between them. The guild called the room the console line. The rest of
the port called it the choir, and the guild had stopped fighting the name around the time
Naledi was born.

She had been on the line six years. Her grandmother had surveyed the ground it stood on before
the first berth was poured, back when it was still the old mine belt — headgear and slimes dams
and a century's holes in the earth — and there was a brass plaque with her survey team's names
on it by the lifts, which Naledi passed every morning and touched, some mornings, when the
shift was going to be long.

This shift was going to be long. Six weeks out from the Great Renewal, the schedule had
thickened until the berths never cooled: consignments outbound, delegations inbound, and
everything at eGoli moving with the particular tension of a household before a wedding nobody
was completely sure about.

"Coupling," Mothusi said.

The hull sat down onto its column of nothing and the board lit from apron to gallery as the
standing wave took. Naledi read it left to right, the way you read anything: power first — the
platcap banks under the apron taking the coupling surge, thirty seconds of load that would have
browned out the Johannesburg of her great-grandmother's day, sunk into platinum-alloy cells and
already bleeding back out to the port grid. Structure next — the piezo struts in the berth
frame drinking the vibration nobody wanted and turning it into the lights over her console,
which was the oldest joke in the guild: *the visitors pay for their own tea.* Then the wave
itself, the long braid of it, human port and visitor hull holding one note together, and every
harmonic in its lane.

Almost every harmonic in its lane.

"Berth Six is green," Mothusi announced, to the room's general grunt. "Choir's done. Freight
can have it."

"Freight can have it," Naledi agreed, and did not move.

Because the consignment calibration was still running on her second screen, and the
consignment calibration was doing the thing again.

Outbound consignments were the other half of the guild's work, the half that never made the
newsfeeds: gold, worked gold, refined past nine-nines purity and formed in the port's own
refinery row into the geometries the manifests specified, verified by the guild before loading
because a mistuned component was a rejected component, and a rejected component was a
diplomatic month nobody wanted. The verification was routine. It was the most routine thing in
the port. You keyed the consignment's resonance profile against the manifest's declared
purpose — *maintenance, network, repair schedule 9* was what the manifests had said, with minor
variations, for twenty-nine years — and the profile matched, and you signed, and eleven hundred
tonnes of patience carried it away to wherever the network's failing pieces were.

The profile on her screen matched. That was the thing. It matched at every declared line, sat
sweetly inside every tolerance the manifest named. If she signed, no gate would refuse her, and
no auditor would blink.

But the manifest's tolerances were wide, the way a doorway is wide, and inside that doorway
the actual signature stood off-centre — and it stood off-centre the way it had stood off-centre
on the last consignment she'd verified, and the one before that, and, she was increasingly sure,
though she'd need the archive to prove it, on consignments going back a lot further than her
sign-off history.

Repair looked a certain way. She had spent six years learning exactly what it looked like. When
you patched a resonant system you saw broadband compensation — energy spread to shore up
whatever was sagging, the acoustic equivalent of a splint. What she was looking at was narrow.
Narrow, coherent, repeatable across consignments, and pointed. Energy disciplined toward a
single spectral line, the way you disciplined a system when you were not mending it but
*tuning* it — bringing something, somewhere, to a pitch.

The pitch was always the same pitch.

She sat with it for a minute, which on the console line was a long time. Around her the room
ran its ordinary business: the choir chirping between channels, freight bickering with
scheduling, somebody's tea going cold, the enormous quiet presence at Berth Six ticking as its
skin equalised, if skin was even the word. Through the west glass, far across the apron, the
rotor-anomaly testbed was running one of its endless charge cycles, a crystalline flywheel the
size of a small room hanging a handspan off its cradle in a way that four hundred years of
physics could describe to nine decimal places and not explain. She had grown up with all of it.
The visitors, the port, the anomaly, the folded Sundays, the whole patient furniture of the
century — it was just the world. Her world, in the specific way it was nobody else's: third
generation on this ground, guild since twenty-two, her name on a console the way her
grandmother's was on a plaque.

Which was, she would think later, exactly why she couldn't leave it alone. You could lie to a
stranger. The port was not a stranger.

She opened a discrepancy log.

The form was old, older than the port — inherited from mine practice through the guild's
founding generation, and it had the founding generation's fingerprints all over it: field
after field demanding the *cue*. Not what you felt. What you measured, where, with what, at
what confidence, referenced to which baseline. Somewhere in the guild's manuals — she'd
noticed it as a cadet and it still snagged her, some shifts — there was a voice underneath the
procedure, dry and exact and never named, an engineer from before the Flare who had apparently
believed you could teach pattern-reading the way you taught arc welding, and had nearly been
right. *Read the tolerance before you read the intent,* the epigraph said. *The maker is in
the margin.*

The maker was in the margin. Something was.

She keyed it in plainly, the way the form wanted. *Consignment 100-417, outbound verification.
Signature conforms to manifest MNT/NW/RS-9 within declared tolerances. Sustained coherent
offset from declared repair profile: +2.94 sigma, spectral line 441-something* — she typed the
full designation — *consistent with offsets logged informally on 100-409 and 100-412.
Assessment: profile indicates disciplined tuning toward a common reference, not broadband
compensation. Repair manifest may be incomplete as a description of use. Recommend: archive
correlation against waking-note baseline, 2064, custodial series.*

Her cursor sat on the last line for a while.

The 2064 logs were sealed. Everyone knew the 2064 logs were sealed, the way everyone knew the
Stewards had halted two consignments in twenty-nine years and never said why, the way everyone
knew which questions were freight and which were not. A verification tech asking to norm her
curve against the custodial series was not doing anything wrong. She was just doing something
that had, as far as Naledi knew, never once been done.

Down on the apron, the freight gantries were already walking their loads toward Berth Six, an
e-grav frame under the big platcap bank shedding a third of the weight in ninety-second sips
so the rails wouldn't groan. The morning was bright and enormous. Somewhere north of the
refinery row a bird was giving the port its opinion, which was the same opinion birds had
given the mine before it and the veld before that.

*It's not wrong,* she thought. *It's undeclared.* And the whole shape of her life afterward
would hang on the difference, though all she knew at the time was that her grandmother had
gone down into the old workings for three years for the Accord survey and come up with maps
everyone trusted and a silence nobody questioned, and that Naledi had inherited the trust and
the silence both, and had room left over for exactly one of them.

She signed the verification, because the verification was true.

Then she filed the discrepancy log, timestamped, onto the guild record, where things could be
closed but never uncreated — and went back to work, because the board was filling again, and
at eGoli the sky was always coming down slowly toward somebody.

It was the ninth of September, 2100, six weeks to the Renewal, and the first domino went over
making no sound at all.
