# The Crossing

*South Atlantic, aboard* Karoo Dawn *· four weeks to Renewal*

The sea kept its own ledger.

Ruth learned the ship's rhythms inside three days, the way you learn any post: the wings
trimming themselves along the wind with a sound like a barn settling, the wave-flex skin
running its slow shivers down the hull — the mate called it *the shrug* — every swell that
struck them paying a toll into the cells, storm chop worth more than fair weather, so that
the crew, in the oldest sailor grammar there was, had come to speak of weather as money.
"Rich night coming," the bosun would say, tapping the glass, and mean eleven metres and a
gale.

The gold rode amidships in a hold with three seal systems and one Army cot, and the cot was
ceremonial — Amos had inspected the hold, pronounced it tighter than any granary in
Lancaster County, and moved the watch rotation to the door, where a man could at least see
weather. Ruth stood her own watches. It wasn't required. It was what she had instead of the
count coming out even.

On the sixth night, somewhere over the mid-Atlantic ridge with the aurora faint and green to
the south — you could see it most clear nights now, the sun still restless, the sky's old
threat turned almanac — Amos brought two mugs to the hold door and set one on the coaming at
her elbow and stood drinking his in the dark, which was Beiler for *I'll listen if it wants
out.*

It had been wanting out for nineteen years.

"Bottomland harvest, eighty-one," Ruth said, to the sea more than to him. "You'd have been —
what. Twelve."

"Eleven. I remember it, though. Everyone in the districts remembers it." He blew on his tea.
"You'd know better than the stories."

"I was the stories." She wrapped both hands around the mug the way Marais of eGoli, whom she
had never met, wrapped his — the habit of people who have learned to give their hands
something to do that isn't shaking. "Second lieutenant, first posting, Susquehanna flood
detail. We had four thousand acres of winter grain in the bottomland and a warm week coming
off the mountains, and the Corps model said the levees held and the window was nine days.
Move the harvest convoys in, lift the grain, everybody home dry. The model had satellite
weather, river gauges, sixty years of hydrology. It was a good model." The shrug ran down
the hull, long and slow, and paid its toll. "And there was an elder at the staging barn —
Yoder, white beard to his belt, farmed that bottomland fifty years — and he stood in front
of my convoy with his hat in his hands, polite as a funeral, and said the ground didn't
agree. Said the frost was out of the far bank too early and the ice meadows upstream were
going, and when they went, they went all at once, and nine days was a city man's number.
Asked me — didn't tell me, *asked* me — to hold the convoys on the high road one more day
while his boys walked the banks."

"And the model said nine days," Amos said quietly.

"The model said nine days, and my captain said the model said nine days, and I had the
order in my hand." She drank. The tea had gone the temperature of the sea. "I gave Yoder
every respect. I remember being proud of how much respect I gave him — parked half the
convoy on the shoulder of the high road, kept the loading crews staged instead of camped.
Split the difference. You know what splitting the difference is, Sergeant, when one side of
the difference is water?"

Amos said nothing, which was the right answer.

"Ice dam let go up at Conowago in the small hours. The river came up eleven feet in forty
minutes. We got everyone off the flats that we could raise on the mesh — and the mesh
worked, mesh always works, that's the bitter miracle of it, every soul we lost heard the
alert just fine. They were forty minutes from high ground with grain trucks axle-deep in a
field going to porridge." She set the mug down on the coaming, carefully, dead level.
"Eleven souls, Amos. Six of them mine to move. Board of inquiry called the order sound
based on available modelling, called the loss weather, commended the evacuation. My
captain made major on schedule. And Elder Yoder came to the memorial — came to *me*, at
the memorial, hat in his hands the exact same way — and you know what he said?"

"I'd guess he said it wasn't yours."

"He said, *'The river was going to do what the river did. But child — next time, believe
somebody.'*" Her voice didn't break, which she'd had nineteen years of practice at. "Not
*believe me*. Believe *somebody*. Like the failure wasn't picking the wrong knowledge. It
was thinking the choosing was mine to skip."

The aurora breathed, far south. The ship shrugged, and earned.

"Everyone at home thinks I stayed in the Army because I left the faith," Ruth said. "I
stayed in because of the opposite thing. Because there's always going to be an order and a
Yoder, and there ought to be one soul in the room who's stood where both of them stand.
Somebody who knows what wrong orders cost should be the one carrying them. That's the whole
of my theology, Sergeant. The Fold got it down to two sentences and I still needed nineteen
years and a flood."

Amos was quiet for a proper while, Beiler-quiet, the kind with work going on inside it.

"You know what this run is, then," he said at last.

"Say it anyway."

"It's the bottomland again, writ big as the world." He nodded forward, at the hold, at the
boxes that did not look like anything. "There's a model — twenty-nine years of manifests
that never once lied, that's a good model, Captain, best mankind ever had of a stranger.
And somewhere down south there'll be a Yoder. Some soul who's walked the banks and says
the ground doesn't agree. And the whole species is going to be standing where you stood,
with the order in its hand and nine days on the clock." He drained his mug and turned it
upside down on the coaming, done. "Be a mercy on everybody if the officer in the room's
already made that mistake once, and cheap."

"Cheap," Ruth said. "Eleven souls."

"Against the world, Captain," said Amos Beiler, gently, "that's cheap. Which is a hateful
kind of sum, and why quartermasters keep the books and God keeps the accounts." He
straightened off the bulkhead. "Deck watch says a rich night coming. I'd sleep now if I
were you."

She didn't sleep. She stood the rest of the watch at the hold door doing the thing the Fold
called folding it in: taking the day's weight and finding where it sat in the two
sentences. It sat, like most things, in the gap between them — *don't be a cunt* was easy,
nineteen years easy; it was *be kind* that had teeth, because kind wasn't soft, kind was
Yoder holding his hat, kind was telling a lieutenant the truth at a memorial, kind was
whatever it was going to cost some tuning engineer at eGoli — she did not yet know the
name — to stand in front of the species' nine-day window and say the ground didn't agree.

Toward the end of the watch the storm came up out of the southwest, and the *Karoo Dawn*
leaned into it and began, in her patient centennial way, to get rich. Ruth Stoltzfus stood
in the noise with her hand on her breast pocket and let the sea have the rest of the
count.

Eight days later, at dawn, the lookout's whistle went, and she came up on deck into cold
clean light and saw it lifted over the horizon like the lid of the world: Table Mountain,
and the green land under it that had never once gone dark — and, hanging silver and vast in
the high blue north of it, too far to be anything and too still to be nothing, the first
visitor hull of her life, riding down the sky toward the interior slow as a lowered flag.

Around her the crew kept working. It was just the world.

"Well," said Amos at the rail, hat in his hands, in the voice his people saved for weather
and Scripture. "Now we find out."
