# Reasonable Men

*eGoli Spaceport · three weeks to Renewal*

The record went onto the ledger at sixteen forty-one. The world had read it by supper.

By morning it had a name — the newsfeeds, with the infallible instinct of their trade, had
found the four warmest words in nine pages of assessment language and led with them:
**THE GROUND DOES NOT AGREE.** By the second morning it had a market reaction, two
parliamentary questions in Pretoria and one in Canberra, a Keep-bloc communiqué "noting
with appreciation the integrity of the eGoli verification process," a Give-bloc editorial
calling it "a three-sigma tantrum at a species wedding," and a mesh-hymn — someone in the
Compact zones had set the second sentence to a tune already, *recommend we believe
somebody*, four-part, the way that century made everything four-part.

What it did not have, anywhere in it, was a lie, and Naledi came to understand over those
days that this was precisely the problem she had created for reasonable men. A lie could
be corrected. A provocation could be condemned. A true, procedurally impeccable treaty
document with two flags on it could only be *managed*, and the port began to manage it
around her with a smoothness that would have been beautiful to watch happening to someone
else.

Her night access to the archive annex was "rationalised" — a Renewal-season security
posture, the notice said, applying to all non-essential grades, and when she checked, it
did apply to all non-essential grades; they had simply redrawn essential around her like a
chalk outline. The consignment verification records adjacent to her curve — 409, 412, 417
— were "administratively sequestered pending conference review," which meant nothing was
deleted and everything was behind a door with a committee for a handle. Her protocol
sessions with Sela continued, line-perfect; a second guild observer now attended each one,
young, apologetic, and never the same twice, and their notes went somewhere hers didn't.

Nobody raised a voice. Nobody threatened. Files just closed ahead of her wherever she
walked, softly, like doors in a house where someone was sleeping.

"You have to admire it," Mothusi said, low, at the console line. "It's not even malice.
It's craft." He had copied her closed discrepancy flag to his own log the day the record
went public — so had one other tuner, Ayanda from the night choir, both of them without a
word to her — three small acts of professional witness sitting on the guild record now
like stones on a coffin lid, and all three flags had been closed by the same abbreviated
signature within the hour. Baba Sipho's wax thumbprint, three times.

She went to him. She owed him that, and she was angry enough to pay it.

She found him where the guild always found him in a bad week, in the choir loft above
Berth Six, listening to the resident hull tick, and he heard her out — the accesses, the
sequestrations, the chalk outline — with his hands folded on the rail, an old man in a
grey coat under eleven hundred tonnes of patience.

"Everything you have described," he said when she finished, "crossed my desk, and most of
it carries my signature." He said it the way he'd said *it was my hand*: no shelter
taken. "You put a treaty document on the world ledger without bringing it to your
guild-master first, my child. Not because you feared I'd forbid it. Because you knew I'd
be obliged to." He looked at her sideways. "That was correctly reasoned, by the way. I
would have been."

"And now?"

"Now I sign the paperwork that manages you, because if my hand is on it, other hands
aren't, and the other hands available this season have fewer scruples about young
engineers than mine." The hull ticked. Far below, freight went about the world's
business. "You've read the old manuals. The founding voice — the epigraph hand. There's a
line of hers the cadet syllabus doesn't include. *When the system protects itself instead
of the work, the discrepancy has moved up a level.*" He let that sit exactly long enough.
"Note the diagnosis, Naledi. Then note that she wrote *the system*, not *the people*. The
people are your colleagues who copied your flag, and a commissioner who has slept four
hours a night since the grain years so that other people could be idealists inside a fed
perimeter, and an old man who is trying to keep his best tuner attached to the guild that
will still need her the morning after the treaty. Do not let the newsfeeds teach you that
the door-closers are a species. It's the season leaning on everyone, and everyone
creaking where they're weakest."

"And where do you creak, Baba?"

"Loyalty," said Baba Sipho Dlamini, without hesitating, and put his hands back on the
rail.

The delegations' reception that evening was held in the concourse under the mesh-lattice
ceiling, forty flags and a thousand lanyards, and Naledi attended because liaison staff
attended, and stood at the edge of it with a glass of wine she didn't drink, watching the
species negotiate with itself. She had expected to despise it and found she couldn't
manage anything so clean. The Give bloc was not a slogan up close; it was the delegate
from Bangladesh, whose grandmother's coastline the platcap seawalls had held, saying
*whatever they are, the relationship is the only power grid we have* — and it was the
Chinese delegation's quiet, ferocious lead negotiator, who had rebuilt a sixth of her
country on licensed cells, remarking to a circle of small nations that goodwill from a
superior power was the only security that had ever existed, ask anyone who had lived
beside anyone. The Keep bloc was the Chilean custodial minister with the burn-line
tattooed on his forearm — his own grid's date of death — saying *we kept the lights on by
owning our own ground, and we will not lease the planet's basement to a courteous
stranger*. Everyone was right. That was the sickening physics of it. Everyone was
standing on a true thing, gesturing at a dark thing, and the dark thing did not gesture
back; it berthed at Six and paid its freight and waited, patient as procedure.

She was leaving when she saw the old man.

He was at the far margin of the reception where the catering met the freight corridor, in
a suit that had been very good two decades ago, arguing with a gate clerk in the low
furious voice of a man rationing his dignity — something about a pass, a list, a name
that should have been on it. Seventy-something, straight-backed, West African by his
accent's bones. The clerk's face had the fixed patience of a man following a script, and
the script was winning; the old man gathered himself, turned from the desk, and for one
second his eyes crossed Naledi's across the concourse — took in the guild grey, the
liaison tag — and she watched him *recognise* her, the way people had started to, since
the ledger.

He didn't approach. That was the thing she carried home. Every other stranger that week
had approached. The old man in the twenty-year-old suit looked at her, and something went
across his face like grief remembering its manners — and he inclined his head, once, the
smallest bow in the world, one professional to another, and walked away toward the
transit hall with his refused pass in his fist.

"Who was that?" she asked the clerk, on her way out.

The clerk checked his script, and evidently found the name harmless enough to share with
a lanyard. "Nobody, Engineer. Retired. He applies every conference — wants access to the
delegates' technical annex. Says he has material for the record." He shrugged, boxing up
the evening. "Okonkwo Diri. Used to be one of the space men. You know. From *before*."

Her terminal, in her pocket, chimed once against her hip as she stepped out under the
winter stars — the message header dark to her grade, the text one line long, and the
first thing in three weeks to make her stand still on the concourse steps and smile,
slowly, like a tuner hearing a note come true.

*The Council will see you. Bring the curve, and wear boots. — T.*
