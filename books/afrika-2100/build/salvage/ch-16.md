# The Formal Answer

*eGoli Spaceport · one and a half weeks to Renewal*

The Commissioner received them standing at his window, which Ruth understood by now was
the local dialect for *no cameras*, and the first thing he did was pour three cups of the
canteen's bad tea from a flask on his desk, which she understood was an apology, in the
same dialect.

"Engineer. Captain." Marais handed the cups. He looked like a man who had stopped
sleeping in shifts and started not sleeping at all. "You'll want it plain, so: the audit
finding stands, my sequestrations are lifted, your recall is rescinded — the American
desk received my secretariat's *revised* observation this morning, Captain, to the effect
that the witness of record's presence is indispensable to conference integrity. I have
spent three weeks working against you both with every procedural tool I own, and I would
do it again on the same information, and I am not here to apologise. I am here because
the information changed, and a man who can't change with it should be running a museum."
He drank his own bad tea, standing. "You've assumed I'm Give bloc. Everyone does. Let me
show you what I actually am."

He keyed the wall, and the window opaqued, and became the Blackout Decade.

Not the newsfeed version. The logistics version — his version, the one he had lived at
some desk in Geneva while the North froze: supply networks rendered as rivers of light,
and then the Flare, and then the rivers going out. Not all at once. In order. Fuel, then
food, then medicine, then order itself, each system's failure loading the next, cascade
after cascade, thirty-one months of it, a civilisation's nervous system dying of
sequence.

"Nine hundred million people, the demographers' consensus, decade total," Marais said,
flatly, to the map. "I sat in the room where we triaged continents, Engineer. I know to
four decimal places what a system failing *in order* costs, and I have spent twenty-nine
years watching this port because this port is the same diagram." He turned. "You think I
fear offending the visitors. Grow up, both of you — they are the *counterparty*; fear of
offence is for courtiers. What I fear has been the same shape since the grain years."
He tapped the dark map. "Dependency without understanding. We have rebuilt the species'
entire energy economy around platcaps whose key step is licensed technique we cannot
re-derive. Our heavy lift is an anomaly we cannot explain. And your finding — your
*correct* finding — says the ground of it all, the machine under my apron, is degrading
on a curve, serviced by consignments we don't comprehend, on a schedule we don't
control. You've proven the species is a tenant farmer on its own planet, Engineer. My
whole career has been about never letting anyone find that out *the cascade way*." He
sat, at last, heavily. "So. The amendment triples the flow, and I was for it, because
flow was the only relationship we had, and starving the relationship looked like
starving the farm. Your description changes the arithmetic. I need the rest of it.
What happens at the bottom of the degradation curve, and what happens if the
consignments stop — and there is exactly one party who can answer either question, and
this afternoon, Captain, Engineer, for the first time in twenty-nine years, the Accord's
own machinery is going to *make* it answer. Article Nine. A ledgered audit finding
touching consignment use obliges formal response at protocol level." Something crossed
the grey face that had not been there in the canteen three weeks ago: the look of a man
who has decided to find out. "I've called the session for fifteen hundred. I will ask
the question myself. You will both attend — and Engineer, you will watch the answer the
way you watch a coupling. I want your reading before the floor gets its version."

—

The Article Nine session convened in the protocol hall at fifteen hundred exactly, and
Ruth, from the attendance benches, watched the species' entire relationship with the
sky fit itself into one rectangular room: three flags, a treaty crest, a stenography
ledger, and at the far side of the long table, robed in the fabric that behaved like
fabric until it didn't, the liaison.

She had been in rooms with Sela four times now. She had never yet seen it asked a
question it was obliged to answer.

Marais read the finding's operative paragraphs into the record, laid the treaty text
beside them, and asked, in the same flat voice he'd used for nine hundred million:
"Under Article Nine: the audited telemetry shows consignment gold employed in
disciplined tuning against the terrestrial node, whose idle degrades consistent with
unserviced operation. The Accord requests formal statement of consignment use, the
node's condition, and the consequence to that node should consignments cease."

The pause was eleven seconds. Ruth counted, because the engineer beside her was
counting; she had learned whose habits to borrow.

"The consignments perform maintenance within manifest scope," Sela said. The doubled
voice was even, unhurried, word-perfect. "The network requires retuning. The
terrestrial node is the network's reference. Maintenance of a reference is performed
*through* the network it references: scope, therefore, is a matter of network
architecture. Network architecture is a matter for the Renewal. The node's condition
is: serviceable. The consequence of cessation is: the node would no longer be
serviced."

Silence. The stenography ledger ticked.

It was, Ruth thought, watching Marais's knuckles whiten on the treaty text, a
masterpiece. Every sentence true. Every sentence responsive. The whole of it, together,
a door painted on a wall — *the reference is serviced through the network; the network
is the Renewal; sign, and the description you seek becomes the architecture you
signed.* Twenty-nine years of manifests that never once lied, and here at last was the
grammar of it: they did not withhold the truth. They enclosed it, the way a bearing
encloses a race, so that it turned and turned and never came out.

Marais asked two follow-ups and got two more perfect enclosures. The session moved to
close. And then Ruth Stoltzfus — who had no standing to speak, and knew it, and had
spent three weeks learning from a tuning engineer that standing was a thing you could
sometimes simply *hold* — stood up at the attendance benches, in the turned-earth dress
uniform of a country that had shipped two hundred and forty tonnes of its good faith
into this room, and asked the question that was not in the treaty's grammar at all.

"Ma'am," she said — the protocol officers half-rose; Marais, watching, lifted two
fingers off the table, and they sat — "or sir, or neither, and no disrespect in any
direction. Ruth Stoltzfus. I signed for the American gold. Where I come from we ask a
thing plainly when it matters, so, plainly: all this that you're doing — the gold, the
tuning, the reference, the patience — twenty-nine years of it. Are you being *kind* to
us?" A beat. "Or careful with us?"

The pause was not eleven seconds. It was longer than any pause in the port's protocol
record; the stenography ledger would fix it, to the world's later and permanent
fascination, at forty-one seconds — and it did not read as computation. That was the
thing Ruth would swear to, to the general, to the floor, to her own Sunday silence, for
the rest of her life. She had sat opposite enough horse traders and enough bishops to
know the difference between a mind calculating an answer and a mind deciding whether it
is *allowed* to give one.

And the room's answer, when it came, was two words where every previous answer had
been architecture, and the doubled voice, for the first time in anyone's protocol
record — the engineers would fight over the acoustics for years — came a full
quarter-tone *apart*, the second voice no longer quite underneath the first, as if,
for one utterance, whatever spoke had stopped speaking in unison with its own
discipline:

"We are trying."

Then the liaison rose, inclined its great grey head to the human benches — to Ruth,
directly, unmistakably, the stenography record says so — and left the hall at the pace
the hulls came down: as if hurry were a concept it had examined, and declined, and
regretted the declining all the way to the door.
