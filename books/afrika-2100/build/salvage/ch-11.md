# The Survey Books

*Johannesburg · two and a half weeks to Renewal*

The box was smaller than a life had any right to be.

Naledi collected it from the estate agents in Braamfontein at nine, signed the release
with the pen chained to the counter — her grandmother would have had a professional
opinion about a firm that chained its pens — and carried it home to the flat in Florida
on her lap, on the transit, like something that might need comforting.

Gogo Dineo had died two years ago, in her sleep, at eighty-eight, in the same Qwaqwa house
she'd been born in, and the family had done the funeral and the year of small
observances and the slow distribution of a household — and then the lawyers had written
about *the professional effects*, held separately under some instruction of the will
that had taken two years and a fee to discharge. Naledi had assumed medals. Theodolite
certificates. The kind of paper that outlives its meanings.

She cut the tape at her kitchen table with the winter morning coming through the window,
and unpacked, one by one, onto the wood:

The theodolite itself, boxed in oiled hardwood, the old optical Wild her grandmother had
carried when the survey went places electronics were not invited. A tobacco tin of plumb
bobs. A photograph she'd never seen: five people in mine coveralls at a shaft collar,
young, laughing at something to the left of the camera, her grandmother second from the
right with a coil of tape over her shoulder, and on the print's white margin, in pencil,
just: *Handover week, '73.* No names.

And the books.

Eleven of them. Field survey books, mine-standard, vellum-paper, quarter-bound, their
covers rubbed soft, numbered on the spine in her grandmother's mine-shorthand — the angular
private script that Naledi had grown up thinking of as *Gogo's other alphabet*, taught to
her in pieces at the kitchen table the way other grandmothers taught recipes. Books one
through ten ran 2071 to 2074: the Accord survey, the deep galleries, the work that was on
the plaque. Traverse tables, reduced levels, gallery sections in a draughtsman's hand so
clean it looked printed. All of it matched the published record; she knew the published
sheets by heart before she was twenty.

Book eleven was the same as the others, and wrong.

It took her until the afternoon to see it, because the wrongness was a surveyor's
wrongness, buried in the arithmetic where only family would go. Book eleven covered the
final season, the handover season — and its page numbering was continuous, but its
traverse numbering was not. Traverse 118 closed on a station named in the margin *K-9
collar*. Traverse 119 opened from a station named *K-9 collar* — and its opening backsight
did not reduce. She ran the numbers four times, tea going cold, the old alphabet crawling
up out of childhood into her professional eye. The closing coordinates of 118 and the
opening coordinates of 119 differed by a whole leg's worth of easting and eleven metres of
depth. Between the last line of one page and the first line of the next, her grandmother
had walked a traverse that the book did not contain — and had then, meticulously,
carried its error forward through every subsequent reduction, so that the published
network closed perfectly, seamlessly, around a hole.

You could not do that by accident. You could barely do it on purpose. It was the
surveyor's equivalent of a scar sewn shut from the inside.

And in the margin of the gap, in the other alphabet, in pencil gone silver with
twenty-six years, four lines. Naledi translated them out loud, alone in her kitchen, her
finger under the symbols the way her finger had gone under them at eight years old:

*Bearing 214 from K-9 collar, 380 on the tape, then down.
The room is true. The room was always true.
They asked me for the map. I gave them the map.
Nobody asked me for this.*

She sat for a long time with her hand flat on the page.

*They asked me for the map. I gave them the map.* Every sheet published, every gallery
honest, the Accord survey a monument of professional integrity — and one room folded out
of the world along a dotted line of her own arithmetic, sealed not by the Stewards, not
by the Authority, but by an old woman's private grammar, in the one archive no council
could subpoena: a kitchen alphabet and a granddaughter.

*Nobody asked me for this.* Twenty-six years of Sunday lunches, and Naledi understood at
last that the silence had never been secrecy. It had been custody. Held the way her
people held things — until asked, by the right asker, in the right language. The books
had been willed to *next of kin, professional effects*, two years' worth of lawyer-proof
patience deep, to whichever of the family's engineers came looking with the training to
read a backsight that didn't reduce.

"You could have just told me, Gogo," she said to the kitchen, and heard the answer in
the old woman's own voice, dry as the highveld winter: *Telling isn't teaching, my
child. Now you know it the way I knew it.*

She photographed nothing. She wrote nothing to the ledger. She was learning.

Bearing 214 from the K-9 collar. She didn't need the published sheets to place it; she
had walked the modern version of that ground a hundred times, on catwalks, under
lights. Two-one-four from the old K-9 shaft collar ran south-southwest under the whole
working port — under the refinery row, under the apron, under Berth Six itself — and
three hundred and eighty metres on the tape put you *past* the deep coupling galleries,
past everything on any map she'd ever verified against, out into ground the Accord
survey showed as competent rock.

Then down.

On the axis, she realised — standing now, the winter light gone long across the table,
holding the book against her chest like a hymnal — the axis the sealed 2064 series would
norm to. The bearing every consignment's undeclared pitch pointed along, if you drew it
on the ground instead of the spectrum. Her grandmother's un-drawn room sat where the
mathematics had been quietly gesturing for twenty-nine years, four kilometres under the
place where the species shook hands with the sky every working day, and to reach it,
Council or no Council, boots or no boots, they were going to have to go down her
family's own arithmetic into ground the world had agreed did not exist.

Her terminal chimed on the table. Tefo, headerless as ever, as if the day had been
listening:

*Council convenes at the deep house, day after tomorrow, first shift. Bring your
witness. The Elder asks one thing in advance — she says you'll know by then what it
means: bring the eleventh book.*

Naledi stood in her kitchen with the hair going up on her arms, doing the arithmetic
she was bred to. The lawyers had released the box this week because the will's
conditions had taken two years to discharge. The Elder Steward had known the box
existed — had known which book mattered — for, at minimum, as long as the will had.

The Stewards hadn't been keeping the room from her grandmother.

They'd been keeping it *with* her, and waiting, all these years, for the family to
finish the handover.
