# The Blessing of the Load

*Lancaster Compact zone, Pennsylvania · six weeks to Renewal*

The barn had been a church for thirty-six years, which was longer than it had ever been a barn.

Ruth Stoltzfus sat in the fourth row on the visitors' side, in uniform, with her cover on her
knee, and let the singing come up around her like water finding its level. The hymn was High
German, four hundred years old, unaccompanied, led from the middle of the congregation by
whoever the note found first — and when it ended, the reader stood up with the folded lectionary
and gave out the day's passages in English, because the Fold's book belonged to everybody and
Deitsch was for the hymns.

"From the Psalms," the reader said. He was a Beiler, one of the young ones, with hay dust still
on his shoulders from the morning feed. "Then from the Dhammapada. Then from the sayings of the
Nguni, collected. One inheritance."

*One inheritance,* the congregation answered, and the reading began.

Ruth had been six years old when the sky burned. What she remembered was not fear. She had
been asleep, and then her mother was carrying her into the yard in a quilt, and the whole
bowl of the night was green and rose and moving, aurora down over Pennsylvania like the Lord
had hung washing across every star at once, and her father had said, very quietly, to no one,
*"Now we find out."* She was too small to know what he meant. She knew now. The neighbours'
farms — the English farms, as her people had still said then — had gone dark and stayed dark,
and the milk had died in the tanks, and the cars had worked but the fuel pumps hadn't, and the
phones in everyone's pockets had become mirrors, and within a month the roads were full of
people walking out of the cities carrying the wrong things.

Her community's farms had not gone dark, because they had never been lit. That was the whole
of it. Thirty-six years of theology and policy and the Fold's careful liturgies had grown up
over that fact like sod over a foundation, but the fact stayed simple: when the grid died, her
people did not notice for three days, and when they did notice, it was because of the walkers
on the roads.

And then the Army had come. She remembered that too: the convoy crawling up the pike in June
of sixty-five, and the colonel who got down from the lead truck and took off his sunglasses and
stood in her family's lane with his hands empty, and asked — asked — for the bishop.

The reading ended. The congregation folded down onto the benches for the silence, and Ruth
folded with them, and in the silence, as always, did her accounting. It was the one piece of
the practice she had carried whole through twenty-three years of the Army: the ledger of
kindnesses, kept nightly, an honest count of what you had put into the world that day and
what you had taken out. The Fold said the count was the prayer. Her grandmother, who had died
holding the old faith entire and unfolded, had said the same thing about weeding.

*Don't be a cunt. Be kind.*

It was on the coin in her breast pocket, the ledger-coin the width of her thumb, G's face on
one side and the terminal ethic on the other, and the first time her grandmother had seen the
words — Ruth had been fourteen, the coins new, the Fold newer — the old woman had read them
through her spectacles, twice, and handed it back and said, in Deitsch, "Coarse. True. So was
Luther." It remained the only theological ruling Ruth had ever needed on the matter.

After the silence came the announcements, and after the announcements, the thing the whole
district had turned out for. They went into the June morning together, the congregation and
the visitors' side both, out past the horse lines to the yard, where the load stood waiting.

Eight wagons. Army gun-carriages, technically, drawn up in a double column with their draft
teams dozing in the traces — Percherons on the lead pairs, and behind each wagon its Army
escort standing easy in dress uniform, and on each wagon bed, under canvas and cordage and
customs seals, a low dense stack of boxes that did not look like anything at all. Gold never
did. Two hundred and forty tonnes of it, refined in Compact refineries from Compact claims
and old Treasury stock, worked — this was the part that still bent her mind, quietly, every
time — to resonance geometries specified by a counterparty from another star, verified by
liaison metrologists, and bound for eGoli, where Ruth would deliver it into the hands of
the Renewal.

The bishop blessed the load. That was the custom, Compact-made, thirty years old, and like
everything the Compact made it was a negotiation nobody had written down: the bishop prayed
in Deitsch and English over the wagons the way the old faith prayed over a barn-raising, and
the Army stood covered and silent and let him, and the Fold's reader gave the two sentences
at the end, and every party present heard what it needed to hear. Provision for a journey.
Sanctification of a tribute. Good order before a movement. One inheritance.

"Captain Stoltzfus." Major-General Okafor found her at the head of the column while the
families were saying their goodbyes to the escort troopers, half of whom were district boys.
The general had come down from Harrisburg for the send-off, which told Ruth most of what she
needed to know about how far up the anxiety went. "Walk with me a minute."

They walked the column. The general touched a wheel rim, a canvas edge, the flank of a
Percheron, with the air of a man verifying that the world was still material.

"Your orders are in the packet," he said. "Deliver the consignment. Attend the Renewal as
consignment officer. Render assistance to the delegation as required. All of which you could
do asleep, which is not why you were picked." He stopped walking. "You were picked because
the Amish trust you and the Army trusts you and there aren't nine people on this continent
who can say both."

"Eight of them smarter than me, sir."

"Undoubtedly. Here's the question nobody will put in writing, Captain, and nobody will thank
you for answering, and the President's office wants answered anyway." He looked down the
column, at the wagons, at the boxes that did not look like anything. "When we hand that over —
what is it? Is it trade? Is it tribute? Is it an offering plate?" He said the last one
carefully, like a man handling someone else's scripture. "Because from where the North sits,
we are shipping the price of a rebuilt hemisphere to the one country that didn't burn, to
give to someone we can't refuse, for reasons they won't state, and every soul from here to
the Dakotas who prays over that load on a Sunday deserves better than *we're not sure.*"

Ruth was quiet for a moment. Down the column, Sergeant Amos Beiler — first cousin to the
morning's reader, Plain-raised and never left, the only man she'd ever met who could make a
supply manifest sound like a psalm — was showing a knot of children how the wagon brakes
worked, gravely, one small hand at a time on the lever.

"Sir," she said, "my grandmother had a rule for gifts. She said you don't know what a thing
is when you give it. You know when you see what it makes of the people who took it."

"That's a long answer."

"It's a six-week road, sir. I'll have a shorter one by eGoli."

Okafor almost smiled. "That's why you were picked." He put his sunglasses on, which meant the
conversation was ending and the convoy was starting, and then he paused, and said the other
thing, the thing she would carry down the whole Atlantic like a stone in a boot: "Twenty-nine
years, Captain, they've never once told us what the gold is *for*. Repair, the manifests say.
Repair of what? We're praying on Sundays and paying on Mondays and we have never seen the
invoice. If anyone down there ever reads it —" he looked at her over the glasses, and he was
not smiling at all — "I'd take it kindly if an American was in the room."

"Yes, sir."

"Move your column, Captain."

The whips never touched the teams; Compact drivers didn't carry them. Amos swung up beside
her on the lead wagon with the movement order in his fist, cleared his throat, and gave out
the departure the way he gave out everything.

"Eight wagons, two hundred forty ton, six weeks, one question," he said. "Sell gut. Let's
go find out."

The column rolled south into the green morning, through the district her people had farmed
without electricity for three hundred years and would farm for three hundred more, past the
transformer graveyard at the county line that the Compact kept mowed like a war cemetery,
which it was. Ruth put her hand once against her breast pocket, coin and Bible both, the
folded and the unfolded, and settled in for the road.

Behind them the barn that was a church stood open to the morning, and the last of the
congregation watched the wagons out of sight, the way her people had watched wagons out of
sight in Switzerland, in the Palatinate, in Ohio and Indiana and here, for four hundred
years: with a prayer, and without waving, and with every intention of being exactly where
they stood when the wagons came back.
