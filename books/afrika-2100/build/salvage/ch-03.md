# Closed as Filed

*eGoli Spaceport · five and a half weeks to Renewal*

The response came back in four days, which was fast for the guild record system, and it came
back two words long, which was fast for anybody.

*Closed as filed.*

No reviewer comments. No correlation run. No answer at all to the recommendation line, which
had been the entire point of the log — just the status flag flipped from OPEN to CLOSED, a
review level two grades above anything a consignment discrepancy usually earned, and a
signature block so thoroughly abbreviated it might as well have been a thumbprint in wax.

Naledi read it standing at her console at the start of the morning shift, and then read it
again, and the second reading did what second readings do: it stopped being information about
the log and started being information about the people who had closed it.

A junior reviewer dismisses you with a paragraph, because a junior reviewer has to show their
work. Two grades up, they do not have to show their work. Two grades up, at Renewal season,
somebody had looked at a verification tech's three-sigma curve and the words *waking-note
baseline, 2064, custodial series*, and reached not for a red pen but for a lid.

"You've got your reading face on," said Mothusi, sliding into the next seat with his tea.
"Whose telemetry died?"

"Nobody's," she said, and closed the screen, and ran her shift.

She ran it well, which was the part nobody tells you about a splinter in the mind: the work
doesn't get worse. The work gets *better*, because the work is the one place the splinter goes
quiet. She coupled a delegation transport at Berth Two and a heavy freighter at Nine, verified
two outbound consignments — both of them off-centre in the doorway, both of them at the same
pitch, and she logged nothing, this time, and noticed exactly what it cost her to log
nothing — and at the end of the shift Baba Sipho Dlamini was standing behind her chair with
his hands in the pockets of the grey guild coat he'd worn since before she was a cadet.

"Walk with me, my child," he said.

Everyone in the guild was Baba Sipho's child; that was just the grammar of him. He had tuned
the first berth ever poured, had shaken the hands of three presidents and declined a fourth,
and had a way of walking the refinery row at shift-change so that every young tuner got looked
at, once, properly, the way a farmer looks at weather. He had recruited Naledi out of a
harvester-engineering apprenticeship at twenty-two on the strength of a maintenance log she'd
written about a piezo strut array — she had flagged that one strut's output was *too clean*,
and traced it to a mounting fault that was quietly bypassing its load sensor — and he'd told
her, in the interview, that the guild had exactly one entrance requirement, and she had
already passed it: *you noticed that right can be a symptom.*

They walked the row. Below the catwalk, the evening pour was going into the moulds, gold
running thin and blinding into geometries that had no name in any human trade, and the heat
came up in slow breaths.

"I closed your log," Baba Sipho said.

She had not expected the directness, and then she had, because it was him. "The signature
block said review level six."

"The signature block said what I asked it to say. It was my hand." He stopped, folded his
arms on the rail, and watched the pour. "Your curve is good, Naledi. Your curve is the best
piece of verification work I have seen this year, and I have told no one that, and I am
telling you the way a man tells you where the fence is electrified." The gold ran. "Do you
know what season it is?"

"Renewal season."

"Renewal season. Forty governments and their press corps are landing on my apron. In six
weeks the species signs the only treaty it has ever signed that matters, or it doesn't. And
into that season, a tuner I trained — third generation on this ground, a name everyone in
this port knows because it is riveted to the lift-shaft wall — asks the record system to
unseal the *custodial series*." He said it without heat, which was worse. "There are three
newsfeeds that would pay a year of your salary for that log. Not because they understand the
curve. Because of what the headline does. *Guild questions what the gold is for.* Six weeks
out. You may be right, my child. That is the thing about this season: being right is not the
only load on the wire."

"Then norm the curve quietly," Naledi said. "You have custodial liaison. You could request
the 2064 baseline through the Stewards without it ever touching the public record. If I'm
wrong, the curve dies in a locked room and no headline ever exists. If I'm right—"

"If you are right," Baba Sipho said, "it is not a curve, it is a question about twenty-nine
years of consignments, and questions about twenty-nine years of consignments are not asked by
verification techs, they are asked by commissioners, at treaty tables, in language that has
been through forty lawyers." He turned and looked at her, and his face was the face from the
interview, old and kind and utterly without softness. "After the Renewal, I will requisition
the baseline myself. You have my word, and my word in front of you, which is more than my
word. Six weeks, Naledi. Give the species its signature. Then we will go and read your
margin together."

It was, she understood as she said good night to him, a completely reasonable position. That
was what she chewed on, riding the late transit home over the old mine belt with the port
lights wheeling away behind her: everyone in this was going to be reasonable. Nobody was
going to twirl a moustache. They were going to be kind, and correct, and they were going to
be her own people, and the door was going to close with a sound like *after the Renewal*.

And she had signed two verifications today with the curve sitting in them like a splinter,
and the consignments were gone, up, out, wherever *out* was, doing whatever the manifest
didn't say. The Renewal wasn't a deadline for asking the question. The Renewal was the
question, being answered without asking it, at volume, forever.

Her flat was in Florida — the old West Rand Florida, lake and mine dumps, twenty minutes from
the apron — and she didn't go home to it. She rode two stops further to the guild archive
annex, badged in at ten at night, which her access class permitted, and sat down at a
terminal in the empty reading room, and did the thing you do when the front door is closed:
she measured the house.

The sealed series had a catalogue entry. Sealed things always did; that was what sealing
meant — the record of a record, the shape of a hole. *Custodial series, 2064.001–2064.988, deep
telemetry, waking event and stabilisation. Access: Steward Council key + Port Authority
countersign.* Deposited 2073, at the Accord. Twelve hundred hours of instrument data. And a
deposit note, one line, in the dry administrative English of the founding generation, which
stopped her breathing for a second:

*Deposited per survey handover, D. Mokoena attending.*

Her grandmother had carried these logs in. Her grandmother, who had surveyed the deep
galleries for three years and come home and never once, in twenty-six years of Sunday
lunches, of Naledi's cadet stories, of the guild plaque with her name on it — never once said
the word *custodial*, or *waking*, or *2064-anything*, to the granddaughter she taught to
read contour maps at the kitchen table.

*What did you attend, Gogo?*

The reading room hummed. Somewhere below it, the archive's platcaps took their trickle of
the port's night load, and the mesh carried its billion small messages, and the instrument —
four kilometres down and continent-wide, awake for thirty-six years, older than the species'
name for itself — idled at whatever pitch it idled at, undeclared.

Naledi opened a new request form. Not the discrepancy log this time. The other one, the one
with the treaty crest on it, that any citizen of an Accord state could file and almost none
ever had: *Application for custodial disclosure — grounds must be stated.*

Grounds. She typed the truth, because the form was going onto the ledger where things could
be denied but never uncreated, and the truth was the only load-bearing thing she owned.

*Applicant is a guild verification engineer. Applicant has documented a sustained, coherent,
repeating offset in outbound consignment resonance profiles, consistent with disciplined
tuning toward a common reference not described in the repair manifests. The sealed custodial
series contains the only baseline against which this offset can be normed. Applicant's
professional assessment: the port is verifying that the gold matches the manifest, without
being able to verify that the manifest matches the use. Twenty-nine years of good faith
deserve twenty-nine years of good measurement.*

She read it twice. It sounded like her grandmother's survey books: no adjectives, all
bearings.

At seven minutes past midnight, five and a half weeks before the Great Renewal, Naledi
Mokoena put her name to it and filed it onto the public ledger — the one place in the world
her guild-master's kindness couldn't close — and rode home over the dark mine belt with her
heart going like a coupling surge, and slept, to her own surprise, like a woman who had put
a heavy thing down.

By morning the application had four hundred reads. By the following evening, it had a
newsfeed headline, a formal acknowledgement from the Steward Council with no date attached,
and a meeting request, courteous as a blade being laid flat on a table, from the office of
the Accord Commissioner for the Republic of South Africa: *Dr. S. Marais presents his
compliments to Engineer Mokoena, and would value an hour of her time.*
