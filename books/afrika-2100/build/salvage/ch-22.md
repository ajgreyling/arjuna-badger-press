# Signatures

*eGoli Spaceport · two days after Renewal*

The extraordinary, Ruth had learned across twenty-three years of service, always ends in
paperwork, and the quality of a civilisation shows in how it does the filing.

The species filed well, that week. She spent the two days after the signing doing what
quartermasters do while history airs itself out — closing the consignment. Eight hundred
and twelve boxes, delivered, verified, and now scheduled: the American gold would go
down the handover ledger over six years, every gram assigned, a third to the network
consignments, a third to the training stocks the new watch academies would tune their
first keepers on, and a third — this clause had been Naledi's, drafted at two in the
morning and adopted without amendment — held at eGoli in perpetual reserve, *against
need not yet described.* A millimetre and a half of doubt, written into treaty law. The
old surveyor was in the walls of the whole document, if you knew where to look.

Her own paperwork resolved itself with the face-saving genius of old institutions. The
reply from Harrisburg had been sitting in her queue since the morning after her
insubordination, and she had left it unopened through the finding, the floor, the
question, and the watch, on the grounds that a letter cannot change what it finds you
doing. When she finally read it, two days after the signing, it was four lines long.

*Captain. Your message of 24 September received and read to the President's staff. Your
orders of 24 September are amended to read: remain at eGoli; exercise judgement;
represent us well. As you were, which appears to be exactly where you should be.
— Okafor.*

And below, in the general's own hand, appended to the official text the way her people
appended the true thing to the formal thing:

*Ruth — the Compact's question is answered. Come home when it's done and tell it to me
plainly, over supper, the way your father would have. You were picked because the Amish
trust you and the Army trusts you. It has occurred to me since that the list of people
both would believe about the end of the world was always going to be the list of people
we should send. — O.*

Marais she found on the gallery above Berth Nine on the second evening, watching the
handover's first outbound consignment load under the painted headgear — the same berth,
the same gantries, the same gold, and nothing about any of it the same. He looked like a
man who had slept. It made him look older, the way rest does when it finally lands on a
long debt.

"Captain Stoltzfus. I owe you a professional apology," he said, without preamble. "I
tried to send you home. You were the correct person to try to send home. You understand
that, I think — it's why I could never make you angry."

"You were guarding the perimeter, Commissioner. I grew up inside one. It's honest work."

"Mm. My whole career, honest work — keep the system fed, keep the question small, never
let the species find out what it couldn't afford to know before breakfast." He turned
his cup — canteen tea, she noticed; he'd given up pretending to drink anything better —
a quarter-turn on the rail. "And the question got asked anyway, at protocol level, by a
farm captain with no standing, and the answer turned out to be the one thing my model
never priced in." He looked at her sideways. "That they were *kind*. Twenty-nine years
of cost, Captain, borne at distance, without complaint, because the alternative was our
machine dying in the dark. I ran disaster logistics for a hemisphere. I know exactly
how rare that is. We had no right to expect it, and no model survives contact with
grace." He set the cup down. "They've asked me to run the handover directorate.
Twenty-seven years of schedules, academies, and doubt margins. I said yes on one
condition — that the watch reserve clause stays outside every future government's
reach, including mine. A man should build his own fences at my age. He knows where
he'd climb."

The consignment rose. The berth rails sang it up and out, and the note went through
the gallery floor and up through their boots, and neither of them spoke until it was
sky.

She went down to the apron after, to the waiting rail transport, where Amos had the
manifests squared and the escort formed and her bag already stowed, because Amos.
Naledi came out from the choir room to see them off — in the grey guild coat still,
but the collar carried the new insignia now, the small gold circle the mesh had
already taught the world to read: *the watch, kept.* First keeper of record. She
looked like a woman carrying the right weight.

"Six hours on and thirty off, they've built me," she said. "Rotations, relief
keepers in training, my mother's Sunday clause written into the standing orders by
the Elder herself. It's a life. It's a good life. It's just —" she looked back at
the headgear, the apron, the whole working port that was now, in law and in fact,
the porch of her house — "it's a *vocation*, Captain. I keep waiting to feel the
door close behind me, and it keeps being a door that opens."

"That's how you know it was the right room," Ruth said.

They stood a moment in the winter sun, the engineer and the quartermaster, at the
end of the strangest consignment either would ever sign.

"I don't know how to thank a person for witnessing," Naledi said at last. "It's not
in the guild's units."

"It's in mine." Ruth unbuttoned her breast pocket and took out the coin — the
ledger-coin, worn smooth at the rim by thirty years of her thumb, G's face on the
one side and the whole of the law on the other — and put it in Naledi's hand and
folded the long clever fingers over it, the way the thing had been folded into her
own hand by her grandmother, who had held the old faith entire and still known
load-bearing words when she heard them. "Compact custom. The coin goes where the
kindness went. It'll want handing on someday — you'll know the hands." She stepped
back and, for the first and only time in their acquaintance, saluted — not the
guild, not the watch, the engineer — and Naledi Mokoena, who had no military
grammar at all, answered it the way the port answered everything, with the tuner's
small bow, one professional to another.

"Go well, Ruth."

"Keep well, Naledi."

The transport ran east into the morning, toward the coast, the ship, the grey
Atlantic, the barn that was a church, and the supper table where a general was
waiting to hear the end of the world told plainly. Amos let her be until the port
was long gone below the horizon of the highveld, and then, from the seat opposite,
in the voice his people saved for weather and Scripture:

"So. The general's question, Captain. Payment, tribute, or offering. What do I
write in the manifest, where it says *nature of goods*?"

Ruth Stoltzfus watched the winter country run — the mine dumps gold in the sun, the
mesh towers walking their lines away north, the whole spared and spending land —
and found the answer had been waiting in her own grammar the entire five thousand
miles, the way home answers always are.

"Write *provision*," she said. "What you send ahead when your own people are going
to live somewhere." She settled back and closed her eyes, the count even, the
ledger clean, six weeks and one question old. "And under *remarks*, Sergeant, write
what my people have put on every load they've ever blessed, because it turns out
it was true the whole way down:

"*Spared for the spending.*"
