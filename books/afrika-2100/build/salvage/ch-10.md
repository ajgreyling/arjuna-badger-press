# Sunday at the Port

*eGoli Spaceport · two and three-quarter weeks to Renewal*

Sunday found the port the way Sunday found everywhere now, which was the thing about the
Fold that Ruth had never managed to explain to the last of the old aunts: it did not
interrupt the world. It surfaced through it.

The service was held in the main concourse at the hour the berths ran quietest, under the
mesh-lattice ceiling, and the congregation assembled itself out of the port's own
population the way a tide pool fills: freight crews still in tabards, choir tuners coming
off shift, delegation staff of forty flags, a knot of Chilean custodial officers, cleaning
staff with their carts parked in a companionable rank at the back. No pulpit. A reader's
stand, and the day's reader — rotation, always rotation; this week a Kenyan rail engineer
with a slow deep voice — and behind the stand, on a plain trestle, the ledger of
kindnesses, open, with its stylus on a chain like a village well bucket.

Ruth sat where she always sat in a strange congregation, one row back from the middle on
the aisle, and was surprised and not surprised to find the tuning engineer folding down
next to her, off a night shift by the look of her, attending — Ruth had learned the word
from watching her work — the way engineers attended: kindly, completely, and withholding
assent like a held note.

"I didn't take you for folded," Ruth murmured.

"I'm not, particularly. My mother is." Mokoena nodded at the reader's stand, at the
gathering quiet. "But you raised a treaty storm with four words and a hymn came out of it
inside a day, Captain. I wanted to see the machine that does that."

The machine did what it did. The reader gave out the passages — Isaiah, then the
Dhammapada, then a Sotho praise-song for rain that made half the concourse smile with
their eyes closed, *one inheritance* — and then the folded reading proper, the day's
braid, which took Amos Beiler, two rows forward, visibly by the throat: the *spared for
the spending* fragment, Pennsylvania 2064, set between a line of the Qur'an on the sharing
of water and a Nguni proverb about the fire carried between villages. The Fold's collators
did that, Ruth knew — matched a congregation to its day with the mesh's quiet omniscience,
and there were people who found it unsettling, the liturgy reading the room. Her
grandmother would have called it a preacher doing his visiting, and approved.

Then the silence, and the ledger line forming down the aisle — each soul who wished to,
writing one kindness taken in and one put out, initials only, the week's plain arithmetic
— and the two sentences to close, spoken by everyone in whatever language they'd brought,
so that the terminal ethic went up off the concourse floor in forty tongues at once, rough
and unsynchronised and complete, the way Ruth had heard it in barns and mess halls and
field hospitals for thirty years:

*Don't be a cunt. Be kind.*

She was watching Mokoena instead of praying, an old officer's habit, and so she caught it:
the engineer wasn't speaking either — but her eyes, during the two sentences, had gone up.
Ruth followed them.

The observation gallery ran above the concourse's north side, and Sela stood at its rail.

It should not have been remarkable. The liaison went where protocol went; the concourse
was public ground; the visitors had observed human assemblies before — there were famous
photographs from the seventies, a hull's liaison standing grey and patient at the back of
the Renewal's first mass meeting like a lighthouse at a regatta. But Ruth had been raised
by people who could read a congregation from a back pew the way Mokoena read a coupling
board, and what stood at the gallery rail was not observing protocol. It had been there —
she reconstructed the service backward and was sure — since the Isaiah. It had stayed
through the readings, through the braid, through the silence, motionless the way the
hulls were motionless, the attention coming off it like pressure. And it stayed now
through the two sentences, through the forty languages, all the way to the final quiet —

— and left before the blessing.

That was all. It turned from the rail with the unhurried economy every schoolchild could
mimic, and was gone down the gallery before the reader raised his hands, and the only
thing Ruth could have entered into any record — the only *fact*, and she rationed her
facts like winter feed — was one detail of the turning: a pause at the gallery threshold,
half a heartbeat, the great grey head inclining a few degrees as if under a low lintel,
though the doorway stood three metres clear of it. The way a man ducks out of respect in
a house whose customs he half-remembers. Then nothing. Protocol resumed. The blessing
came down on the concourse, and the tide pool emptied itself back into the working day.

Neither of them spoke of it until they were out on the apron walk with the winter sun flat
and bright on the berths, and then it was Mokoena who stopped, looked back at the empty
gallery rail, and said, in the voice of a woman adding a number to a column: "It stayed
for the sentences."

"It left before the blessing," Ruth said.

They looked at each other.

"I have a hypothesis document," the engineer said, "that is becoming embarrassing to keep
private. You witnessed one record already, Captain. How does your Sunday look?"

Ruth's Sunday looked like a walk to the liaison office with the winter light going long,
and a folding chair, and four screens of survey-plain language — bearings, no adjectives —
that laid out in an hour what the treaty record's nine pages had been too procedural to
say aloud. The pitch that was always the same pitch. The band that was always the same
band. The tolerances of an instrument. The reference that was *here* — under the apron,
under the maps, under twenty-nine years of manifests that never once lied. Ruth read it
twice through, in the manner of her people, who did not skim.

"You're telling me," she said at last, "that the invoice exists. It's just written in the
tolerances instead of the words." She sat back. "Rent. Tithe. Seed corn." She counted
them off on three fingers, an old sergeant's habit caught from a better man. "My general
gave me two of those, my sergeant gave me the third, and your margins are the first thing
in five thousand miles that's made any of them look wrong."

"Wrong how?"

"Every one of them's a story about *paying*." Ruth looked at the west window, where the
testbed hung its handspan of quiet impossibility above the cradle, and past it, at Berth
Six. "Nobody rents to a tenant they visit twice in thirty years. Nobody tithes to a
church that keeps stopping the collection plate. And you don't ship seed corn *toward*
the farm." She shook her head slowly. "It stayed for the sentences, Engineer. It ducked
under a lintel that wasn't there. I've sat in strange congregations my whole life — I
was the strange congregation, in an Army mess, for twenty years. I know what that is
when I see it, and it isn't purchasing."

"What is it?"

"Homesick," said Ruth Stoltzfus, plainly, and put her hat back on. "Which is a word I
will deny using if you quote me in a treaty document, Engineer, because I ration my
facts. But you asked. Now — you said the Council will see you, and to wear boots. My
orders say render assistance as required." She stood, and squared the folding chair
against the desk with quartermaster precision, leaving the room exactly as the world had
lent it to her. "I've got three weeks left on this continent and one question. When do
we go down?"

Naledi's terminal answered before she could: the estate courier's chime, the message
she'd been half-waiting for since the Free State lawyers had first written — *the effects
of the late D. Mokoena, released to next of kin, one box, hold for collection.*

"Tomorrow," she said. "There's something of my grandmother's I need to collect first."
