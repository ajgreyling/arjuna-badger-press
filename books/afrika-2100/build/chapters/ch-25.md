# Ntuli House · fourteen months after Renewal

The query came up the stairs in person, which nobody does, because the mesh exists.

Her name was Palesa Mahlangu, grade two, eleven months on the choir line, and she had a printout, which nobody does either. She stood at the door of the roster office with it against her chest the way Sithole had carried the terminal down the shaft.

“Sit,” Naledi said. “Is it about the fourteenth?”

“No, Mma. It’s a ratio.”

“Then don’t call me Mma. Show me.”

Station seventeen, night shift, four consecutive pallets: the third harmonic sitting fifteen thousandths sharp of where the barring log said it should sit, and then not, and then sharp again on the fourth pallet with a different clamp and a different tuner. Warm plate ruled out. Clamp seating ruled out — she had photographs of the seating, which was more than Naledi had ever thought to take at that grade. The instrument had been calibrated on the Tuesday and again on the Thursday because she had not trusted the Tuesday.

“What’s the assay on those four?”

“Same pour. 999.98.”

“Same barring press?”

“Two presses. That’s why I stopped.” Palesa put her finger on the fourth column. “And the press that gives me the sharp one is the press we rebuilt in June.”

Naledi read it twice, then went back and read the times.

“I don’t know what this is,” she said.

Palesa’s face did a thing she was too young to hide.

“That’s not a soft answer. I have stood on the plate nineteen times and I do not know what this is, and there are four other people who could tell you that and three of them will guess in front of you and one of them will be lying. Do you have your ticket on you?”

“Yes.”

“Keep it. That’s not what I’m asking.” She turned the printout around. “File it as a variance to the guild and to the register. Both, tonight, with your name on it and the time on each copy. Put the two presses in the first sentence and not the fourth. Put down what you ruled out and how, including the photographs, and put down that you recalibrated on Thursday because you didn’t trust yourself on Tuesday — write that, exactly that, don’t dress it.”

“And if it’s nothing?”

“Then it’s a nothing on the ledger over your name and in six years somebody with a better instrument will find out why the June press does that, and your report will be the antecedent, and they’ll be annoyed with you for eleven pages.” Naledi handed the paper back. “State the instrument. State the error. State the date. That’s the whole trade, and it doesn’t get easier, it gets more public.”

Palesa folded the printout along its old fold. “They say you published two wrong numbers.”

“Three. One of them was arithmetic and I deserved everything I got for it.” Naledi stood, because the roster meeting was at four and Radebe’s hernia had finally become everybody’s business. “The fourth one’s easier. Go and file it before you talk yourself out of it.”

She went. On the stairs she stopped, and came back up two steps, and said, “It’s probably nothing,” and Naledi said, “Probably,” and that was the end of that.

## KwaZulu-Natal · February

The garden runs down to a wall of monkey-apple and after that the hill does what it likes. There is a table under the pepper tree with one leg on a flat stone, and a chair with a cushion in it that has been rained on, and the Assist frame stands folded in the shade of the veranda with a cloth over the joints against the dust.

Naledi came for two hours because two hours is what the residence clause allows if you drive fast and the relief holds.

They talked about the press rebuild, which the old woman found funnier than it was, and about rain. Not about the plate. Nothing was said about the plate.

On the table, holding down four seed packets against the wind, was a coin. Struck, worn thin at the rim, the full sentence on it, all of it, the first word not sanded and not softened, the letters shallow with handling. It had been on that table long enough to have made a pale ring in the wood.

Naledi did not pick it up. She read it where it lay, the way she had read it at the wharf, and did not laugh this time.

She was on the plate on the twenty-sixth, first wind, 06:20 to 07:44.

Four minutes and a bit to take. It never gets shorter. Coming off it she sat on the rock with her boots beside her and her hands on her knees until the shaking went, and while she waited she looked, as she does, at the niche in the bore wall three metres back from the lip, where somebody long ago cut a shelf the size of a hand.

There is a folded page in it, in a tin, wired shut. Pencil, on paper, in the wet and the forty-one degrees, a hundred and thirty-six years of it. Paper does not last down there. A book that everyone in her trade has read says so, in a footnote, with a figure of ten years, and the footnote is wrong, and nobody has ever moved the tin.

The beat was 0.0171 flat, still rising, two thousandths worse than the year before.

She wrote it in the green book in the light of her lamp, and the level, and the time, and signed it, and closed the boards, which are soft now at the corners.

Then she pulled her boots on and walked out through the glass, with her hand on the wall going past, because it is cool, and because everyone does.
