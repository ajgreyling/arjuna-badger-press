# Orders Home

*eGoli Spaceport · two weeks to Renewal*

The orders were waiting when they came up from the deep, sitting in her queue with the
patient weight of things that have been composed by committee: *Consignment delivered and
verified. Officer surplus to delegation requirements. Report Cape Town embarkation NLT 28
September for return transport. By order,* and then a chain of signatures going up and up,
like smoke.

Ruth read them twice, standing at the phone rack by the K-9 cage with the deep still on her
boots, and Amos read them over her shoulder, and neither of them said anything until they
were out on the apron in the flat winter light.

"Twenty-eighth," Amos said at last. "That's four days. Renewal's in two weeks." He squinted
at the schedule board across the freight yard, all its patient southern time. "Quick work,
for Harrisburg. Somebody asked somebody to ask."

Somebody had. She confirmed it within the hour, in the way of ports everywhere: the
verification record she had witnessed — 100-USA-R/1, two flags, nine pages — had been
"administratively sequestered pending conference review" at the request of the host
government's Renewal secretariat, and the secretariat's request had gone to the American
desk with a courteous note observing that the consignment officer's continued presence at
protocol level might be seen to prejudge a document under review. Nobody had ordered
anything improper. Nobody ever did. Marais played institutions the way Amos played a
manifest: nothing false, everything placed.

"He can't close her file," Ruth said. "So he's returning the witness."

"Can he?"

"He can ask my Army. My Army said yes." She folded the orders once, lengthwise, the crease
her father put in a feed bill he intended to dispute, and looked across the yard at the
painted headgear over Berth Nine. *Render assistance as required.* Nineteen years she had
been the officer who carried the order because someone who knew what wrong orders cost
should carry them. It had never once occurred to her, in nineteen years, how much of that
covenant depended on the orders being merely wrong, and not aimed.

The old man found her that afternoon.

She was in the transit hall buying rail schedules she had no intention of using — a
quartermaster's reconnaissance, knowing your roads before you need them — when he sat down
opposite her at the long public table with two coffees, set one in front of her
unrequested, and said, in an accent that had crossed more borders than most passports:
"Captain Stoltzfus. You won't know me. I know you — I read nine pages of you on the ledger
last week. Okonkwo Diri. I would like to give you something before your government mails
you home, because you are the first person through this port in a decade whose signature I
believe."

"You're the space man," Ruth said. "From before. They wouldn't give you a technical pass."

"They have not given me a technical pass in nineteen years. I stopped applying for a while.
My wife made me start again — she said the refusals kept me regular." His smile was brief
and did not pretend. Up close he was old the way steel is old, fatigue you had to know
where to look for. "You know what the letters meant. S-S-P. You'd have been a child at
disclosure."

"I was ten. I remember my father reading it out at supper." She remembered more than that:
the bishop preaching on it, the aunts arguing, the sky changed forever over the same
unchanged fields. "Secret programs. Fleets nobody voted on. It broke something in people,
my mother always said. Not the ships — the hiding."

"The hiding, yes." Diri turned his coffee, precisely, a quarter-turn — and Ruth thought,
uncharitably and accurately, *everyone in this story turns their cup instead of raising
their voice.* "I was a flight-test engineer, Captain. Sixteen years, three programs, two
of which are still redacted, and I will save you the mythology: we were not gods and we
were not fools. We were the century's best engineering pointed at the wrong sky with
unlimited money and no daylight. And when the daylight came, in one afternoon in
sixty-eight, we became — " he lifted a hand and let it drop — "an embarrassment. Museum
men. Every child knows the joke: the trillion-dollar fleet the visitors made obsolete
before lunch." He said it steadily, the way you say a thing you have decided to stop
bleeding about. "Almost none of that troubles me anymore. One thing troubles me. I have
carried it to every conference since seventy-three, and the passes never come, because the
one thing I have does not fit the treaty's story, and treaty stories, Captain, get
maintained the way berths do." He laid a data spool on the table between them. Actual
spool, physical, mag-sealed — older tradecraft than the mesh, and immune to it.

"In sixty-six," he said, "I was seconded to the detection consortium. The instruments that
found them coming. That part's in the histories — the fleet that watched the visitors
decelerate for five years while the world rebuilt and prayed. What the histories smooth
over is the arithmetic of the approach itself. We had good tracks from sixty-six on. Good
enough, by seventy, to integrate backward: velocity, heading, the long fall of their
deceleration curve. Every solution in the consortium's final report agrees, and the report
is sealed, and I wrote annex four of it." He tapped the spool once. "Captain — for them to
be where we found them in sixty-six, doing what they were doing, they left wherever they
left *years before our Flare.* Departure precedes the waking-note. It cannot have been the
note that called them. They were already coming."

The transit hall went about its business around a small quiet hole in the world.

"The note answered a journey already begun," Ruth said slowly, feeling the shape of it the
way she'd felt the bore's glass wall. "Then what called them?"

"That," said Okonkwo Diri, "is the question I have been refused a pass for, nineteen
years, because the treaty's story requires the note. The note is *consent*, do you see? The
official history: we sang, they heard, they came, we dealt. Tidy. Bilateral. But if they
were coming regardless —" and there at last the steel showed its fatigue, his voice
dropping not in volume but in age — "then the note changed nothing except what they found
when they arrived, and everything we have signed since is a story we told ourselves about
being asked." He pushed the spool across the table. "My archive's on there too. All of
it. The programs, the airframes, the electrogravitic files — oh yes, we chased the rotor
anomaly forty years before the rotor anomaly, Captain, we chased it with the whole black
budget of a terrified civilisation and we *touched* it twice, unrepeatably, and they
laughed us into the museums, and in eighty-eight some battery engineers found it by
accident looking for bearing losses." The smile came back, and it was the worst thing in
the transit hall. "Right too early is the same as wrong, except for how it feels.
Everything in that archive will tempt your engineer to believe the old fleets understood
the visitors. We did not. We understood nothing. It is nineteen years of beautiful wrong
turns and one true number. Departure before the Flare. Give her the number, Captain. Burn
the rest."

He stood, reclaimed his own coffee untouched, and paused.

"They tell me you are ordered home before the signature," he said. "I was ordered home
once. Nairobi consortium, seventy-one, three days before the landing — my government
wanted its people clear of the blast radius of history, in case." He settled his
twenty-year-old suit. "I missed my transport. Paperwork difficulty. I stood on the
Witwatersrand on the morning of the twelfth of August, seventy-one, and I watched the sky
come down slow as a lowered flag, and I have never regretted a piece of paperwork less."
The small bow, one professional to another, that she'd heard about from Mokoena. "Good
day, Captain. Mind your roads."

Ruth sat a long time with the spool and the cold coffee and the rail schedules she had no
intention of using.

Then she walked back across the winter apron to the liaison office, laid the spool on
Naledi Mokoena's desk, and — in the plain register, the one her people saved for weather
and Scripture — committed the only insubordination of her career, which, being Ruth, took
the form of a filing.

To Major-General Okafor, Harrisburg, via the American desk, copy to her own service
record where things could be denied but never uncreated: *Sir. Reference orders of 24
September. The question you asked me at Lancaster now has an answer forming, and I am
witness of record to the document at the centre of it. A witness who leaves before
testimony is a story the other side gets to tell. I am exercising officer's discretion
under the render-assistance clause and will report to Cape Town embarkation on the
first transport after signature. The consignment is delivered. The question is not. If
this costs my commission, sir, it was well spent, and I'd do it again with interest.
Stoltzfus, R., Capt., USA-LC. The first sentence is about this.*

She read it once, changed nothing, and sent it — and discovered, sitting back in the
borrowed chair in the low gold afternoon, that the count, for the first time since a
river in Pennsylvania, had come out even.
