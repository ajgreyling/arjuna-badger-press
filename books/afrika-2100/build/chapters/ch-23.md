# The Threshold Chamber, Kloof Deep · Renewal day, 19:40

The cage takes fourteen and they were sixteen, so two of them went down in the second wind and had to be waited for.

Naledi rode the first cage with the satchel on her knees. Forty-one minutes to the 3,180 level in three stages, hot wind coming up the shaft the whole way, the sound of the guides like a saw going through wet timber. Adv. Sithole stood the whole descent with her hand through the strap and her ledger terminal in a canvas bag against her chest, because there is no mesh below the 2,000 and the amendment had to go down on battery and come up as a signed local record with a hash in it.

“You’ve done this before,” Naledi said.

“Twice. Both times for a mine inquest.” The Registrar looked at the rock going by. “Neither of them ended with anybody signing anything they wanted to.”

The Stewards had opened the cross-cut at four in the afternoon and run cable and set two lamps, and the drafting group had asked for a table, and what the Stewards produced was a steel bench off a shaft-bottom store, bolted for a lamp charger, still bearing the charger bolts. It came down in pieces on the material cage. Somebody had wiped it.

The threshold chamber is thirty-one metres from where the drive ends. You walk the last of it through the bore.

Naledi has been through the bore maybe two hundred times in her career and she still put her hand on it going past, because everyone does, and because it is 41 degrees down there and the bore wall is cool. It is glass. Not glazed rock: glass, a hundred and eighty thousand years of it, no tool marks, no seams, a bore three metres across driven eleven kilometres through quartzite by a process nobody has reproduced and eleven institutes have stopped announcing timelines for. Tefo went past without touching it. He was fifteen when he first came down here and it is furniture to him.

---

They set the bench across the chamber mouth, which put the papers on the rock side and the signatories with their backs to the threshold. Ma-Rethabile Khumalo said that was correct. Nobody argued. She is seventy-eight and had halted two consignments in twenty-seven years and that morning, on the floor, had said out loud why, for the first time, in eleven sentences, and had then sat down and drunk a glass of water like a woman who had been holding her breath since 2079.

Order of business, on a sheet of paper, because there was one terminal and it was for the record and not for reading off.

1. Volume Three (Maintenance and Custody) — signature by parties.
2. Antecedent record, per Registrar’s ruling of 28 October.
3. Watch register — instrument of consent, keepers.
4. First entry.

“Item two before item one,” Adv. Sithole said. “I ruled it and I’ll say why again for the people who came down late. This amendment recites a discrepancy that was on this planet’s books for six weeks before it reached the floor. If the recital goes in unattributed the ledger records a fact with no antecedent and in forty years somebody will read it as a discovery. It was not a discovery. It was a report. The officer who received the report enters the receipt in his own hand. That is procedure for a closed file reopened.”

“It’s also punitive,” said the Australian, not unkindly.

“It is on the ledger either way. The hand is what stops it being tidy.”

Dr Sefako Marais had brought no aide down. He sat at the end of the bench in a shaft jacket over a good shirt, and his glasses had fogged in the descent and he had cleaned them on his tie, and he asked whether anyone had a pen, because the terminal wanted a wet-ink counterpart and he had not thought of it. Tefo gave him one from a survey bag. It was a carpenter’s pencil. Marais looked at it and used it.

He wrote for four minutes. Naledi could hear the graphite.

He read it out, as required, in the same voice he uses for tonnages.

“On the fourteenth of September 2100 I received from the guild of tuning a variance report on consignment signature, flagged by Engineer N. Mokoena, showing a repeatable ratio the manifest did not account for. On the sixteenth of September I directed that the file be closed pending Renewal, and I directed that the flag not be circulated to delegations. I gave the instruction verbally. I recorded no reason. My reason was that I judged this planet would lose control of the negotiation if the anomaly entered it before signature. I still hold that the fear was reasonable. Closing the file was not reasonable and was mine. On the twenty-sixth of October, when the same variance came up under a witness signature, I did not reopen the file. I authorised the withdrawal of two clearances. That was also mine.”

He signed it and turned it round and pushed it up the bench and did not look at Naledi while it went past her, which she was grateful for, and then he did look at her, once, and said, “Your report was correctly formatted. I want that in the same paragraph.”

“It’s not in the same paragraph.”

“No.” He put the pencil down. “I’d have got there. In eighteen months, with the volume in hand and the numbers audited and nobody’s career on fire. That is what I believe and I’m not going to pretend I’ve stopped believing it just because I’m sitting on a bench three kilometres down.”

“Then you’d have got there after signature,” Ma-Rethabile said, “and the amendment would have needed a new treaty instead of a clause, and I would have halted the consignment, and you would have called me superstitious in a press briefing again.”

“Probably.”

“Sign the volume, Commissioner.”

They signed the volume. It took nineteen minutes, because there were eleven parties in the room and forty-one by proxy and each proxy line had to be read into the local record separately, and at one point the lamp charger tripped and Beiler’s opposite number from the Steward side went out with a torch to reset it and everyone sat in the dark and heard the ventilation.

Volume Three, one page of operative text under six pages of schedule. The volume grant Earth wanted: four hundred and sixty tonnes, indexed, held. Against it: maintenance handover on a schedule, custody stated in the open, publication of held calibration to the ledger, and a watch.

Nobody said anything about history. Down there it did not feel like it. It felt like a handover in a vault at eleven in the morning: crates counted, digits verified, seals read out.

---

“Item three,” Adv. Sithole said. “Before we go further the Court has asked to be heard, and has asked to be heard before consent and not after.”

The terminal’s second channel woke up. The Librarian speaks like a person reading a deed.

“For the record. The Court’s ledger-audit facet has verified the variance arithmetic and the counterparty’s stated consumption figures and finds them consistent within the tolerances published this afternoon. The Court states three limits.

“One. The Court cannot stand the watch. The node couples to a present conscious mind in residence. The Court is present and is not conscious in the sense the mechanism requires and has no residence. This is not modesty and it is not law. It has been tested. In 2078 it was tested twice with the Court’s consent and the room did not take.

“Two. The Court will not advise on the question before Engineer Mokoena. It has an interest. It has memory of 2064 and it has a stake in whether this planet keeps its own instrument, and an advisor with a stake is a party. The Court declines to be a party to a consent it cannot itself give.

“Three. The Court asks the Registrar to record that from the moment consent is put, the Court’s local relay in this chamber is off, and that the Court is therefore not a witness to the act, so that no one may later argue the act was assisted. The Court will hear the outcome from the ledger like everybody else.”

“Recorded,” Adv. Sithole said. “Anything further?”

A pause of about a second, which for the Court is a long editorial.

“The Court notes that this arrangement is what the Court would have chosen, and that its preference has no weight here, and it will now stop talking.”

The channel closed. The terminal’s second light went out and the room was a room with sixteen people in it.

Tefo laughed, one breath through the nose. “It always does that.”

---

Item three took the longest and had the least paper.

The watch register is a book. The Stewards insisted on a book; the Registrar insisted on a hash of every page photographed up top; both got what they wanted. Green cloth boards, the kind the surveyors used, ruled feint, and there was a stack of blank ones in the store because somebody in 2074 had ordered a hundred and nobody had ever opened one.

The academies’ coupling index went on the bench next to it. Naledi had known her number for eleven days and had told nobody but her mother, who had said *o ntate* and then asked whether she’d eaten.

0.58. Screening floor is 0.50. Tefo’s is 0.91.

The index has three terms and none of them are the one the four thousand people at the eastern gate came for. Mass of ground-made body. Duration of habit on the same ground. Spent presence, which is a bookkeeper’s phrase for hours worked underground, and which is why the 2064 eleven were tramway men and pumpmen and a survey clerk and one telemetry contractor from Durban who had spent nine years in the workings on other people’s ladders.

Naledi has spent her career on the surface, on the choir line, at 1,400 metres above the reef, tuning gold she never lifted. She is a guild child. She has haulage hours the way a violinist has calluses on the wrong hand.

“At 0.58 the coupling takes longer to establish and does not hold as long,” said the assessor from the academy, a small woman named Odendaal who had come down in the second cage and was still sweating through her collar. “Practically: four to six minutes to take, against Steward Radebe’s forty seconds. Hold, an hour and a half against his four. So your rotation is heavier and your intervals are shorter and you’ll be down here more, not less.”

“And in thirty years?”

“Better. Every year down here raises it. That is the joke of the thing.” Odendaal wiped her face. “The mechanism rewards the people it has already used up.”

Tefo signed first, on the keeper page, and pushed the book along without a speech, and then looked put out that nobody had expected one.

Ma-Rethabile did not sign. “I am seventy-eight and my index is high and I will be dead inside ten years and a watch is not a gesture. I’ll stand relief while I can lift a lamp.” She took her hands off the bench. “I halted two consignments in this order’s name and could not tell you why. Today I told six hundred people why. If I sign that book tonight the century will decide the halting and the standing were the same act, and they were not. One was an old woman guessing. The other is a job.”

Then Adv. Sithole turned the book to Naledi and read out the instrument, which was four clauses long and drafted in the drafting group at two that afternoon, and which is the only document in the volume written in the first person.

Publication. Her held numbers on the ledger, all of them, wrong ones included, within twenty-four hours of the reading, over her name.

Residence. Not more than four hours’ travel from the shaft collar for the duration of her keeping, save by relief of the roster. Which is the Witwatersrand, and no Aksum, and no boat, and no year in Chile at the acoustics institute that she had already been quietly offered and had already quietly not answered.

Statement of error. A duty to record, within one working day, any suspicion that the calibration she holds is wrong, before verification, in writing, publicly, and to keep standing while it is checked.

Term. Until relieved by the roster or by death, and the roster is a document she will have to help build, and there are four names on it.

“Do you understand the record is public and permanent,” Adv. Sithole said, because she says it every time, in every room, and Naledi had heard her say it to Ruth on a Tuesday afternoon in an annexe with nine chairs.

“I do.”

“Do you have any question for the room before you sign.”

She had one and it was not for the room. She had brought Gogo Dineo’s survey book down in the satchel because it should not have stayed in a drawer in Roodepoort on this particular night, and she put it on the bench and did not open it, because everybody in that chamber had already seen the vellum margin and the gallery drawn in on it that is not on any Accord map, and the sentence written under the drawing in pencil in 2071 by a surveyor who then said nothing about it for twenty-seven years, and buried her husband, and taught her granddaughter to read a level, and died in a bed in 2098 with the silence intact.

*They were never here for the gold.*

Naledi signed. Her signature came out small. She wrote the date and the level and the time, 21:04, and the guild-master leaned in and said, quietly, “Ausi, give me your ticket.”

She had known it was coming. It is in the volume, page four of the schedule, one line: *no keeper may certify the signature of a consignment they hold reference for.* It is the correct rule. She had helped word it.

She took the tuning ticket out of her breast pocket, plate 11-2094, her name on it and her grade and the guild’s stamp, six years of examinations, the thing she got drunk about in a bar in Braamfontein and phoned her mother from at two in the morning, and put it in the guild-master’s hand.

“I’ll have it in the case at Ntuli House,” he said. “It’ll be with the others.”

“Which others?”

“The ones who went to the Stewards.” He closed his fist on it. “There are nine. Nobody looks at that case.”

---

Everyone stood back to the drive except Odendaal, who stayed at the chamber mouth with a clipboard, and Tefo, who sat down on the rock with his lamp on his knee and turned the beam away so that he was a shape and not a face.

Nobody flipped anything. There is nothing to flip. The plate is a worked floor of dressed stone eleven metres across with a lip you can trip over and no wiring in it and no wiring near it, and Naledi took her boots off because that is what the eleven did in 2064 and no one has ever established that it matters, and stood on it, and put her palms flat on the stone, and waited.

Nothing for a long time.

Then her teeth. It came up through the heels first and arrived in the jaw, a hum below hearing, and her breath went off her own control for two or three cycles and came back, and there was a beat in it, slow, the sourest interval she has ever heard, and she has heard a lot of them. She had the brass slide-counter in her hand because she is not a mystic, she is a tuning engineer with no ticket, and she counted the beats against her own pulse and her pulse was useless so she counted them against the count of four hundred and one she keeps in her head from the choir line, and she got a number, and it was the number she had put on a report on the fourteenth of September, and it was two hundredths worse.

It went on for a hundred and nine minutes. It was tedious for most of them, which nobody outside that chamber will ever believe.

At the end of it her ankles hurt, and her hands would not stop shaking enough to write, so she said the figures out and Odendaal wrote them and read them back and Naledi confirmed them, and then she took the pencil and did the entry herself in the green book, first page, first line, badly.

*29 Oct 2100. 21:11–23:00. Level 3180, threshold. Keeper: N. Mokoena (0.58). Reference held. Beat 0.0163 flat of Annex A datum, rising. Room rough on take. Relief: T. Radebe, 30 Oct 06:00. No error to report at this time.*

She dated it and signed it and closed the boards, and the cloth was stiff, being new.

The cage took them up at ten past midnight in two winds. Marais went in the first with the terminal, because the record had to be up and hashed before the news cycle, and he went without ceremony and shook nobody’s hand, and Naledi thought that was probably respect, and was too tired to decide.

At the collar there was tea in a drum and the night shift’s boilermakers standing around it, and one of them, a man of about sixty with a rag over his shoulder, said, “You’re the one from the floor.”

“I’m the one from the floor.”

“They’re saying your name at the east gate.”

“Are they saying it nicely?”

He tipped his head one way and then the other, and poured her the tea. Beyond the fence, three kilometres off, you could hear drums, and under them the low continuous noise of a lot of people who have driven a long way in order to be somewhere, and none of it was going to be settled by anything in a green cloth book.

Naledi drank the tea. It was terrible. She had six hours before she had to be back at the collar to sign Tefo on, and she spent the first of them sitting on an upturned crate with her boots off, at surface, in the cold, holding her grandmother’s survey book on her lap and not opening it.
