# Chapter 7—The Man Who Watched the Moon Breathe

The heating was off in the institute, and Edith had stopped pretending it would come on.

She kept her camera under her coat to spare the battery, the way she'd learned in colder places than Pulkovo, and watched the breath come out of the man across the table in slow grey ropes. His name was Lev Antonovich Vyrubov. He had been Nikolai Kozyrev's last graduate student, and he was eighty-one years old, and he had not yet decided what to do with them.

"You came a long way," he said, "to look at a man the Academy already finished looking at."

"We did," said Naila.

He waited for more. Edith had seen the keepers do this—Pogue's granddaughter had done it, the long silence held open like a door on a chain. Vyrubov had something better than suspicion. He had patience. He had outlasted a State; he could outlast a film crew from a van.

The room was a reading room the way a cell is a room: a table, a window, shelving that climbed into the dark above the reach of the one working lamp. Frost ferned the lower corner of the glass. Outside, the great refractor's dome sat against a sky the colour of an old coin, and below it the observatory grounds ran down to graves—the astronomers of the siege, Edith had read, buried where they fell defending the instruments. People had died here for lenses. It changed how you held a camera.

She did not raise it.

---

The official story is clean, and Yusuf had laid it out in the van so they'd arrive knowing it cold.

Nikolai Aleksandrovich Kozyrev: real astronomer, no asterisk required. Gold-medalled. A lunar crater named for him on the far side, voted in by the people who name craters, which is the International Astronomical Union and not a man's mother. Co-discoverer, in his youth, of work on stellar atmospheres that still has his fingerprints on it.

And then the second life. The life that got the asterisk.

Kozyrev decided time was not a passive river. It had a *flow*—a direction, a density, a thing that could be measured, that did work, that leaked into systems and changed them. *Causal mechanics*, he called it. He built mirrors to focus it. He pointed telescopes at where stars truly are *now*, not where their old light says they are, and reported he was getting signals.

"The physics is no," Naila said, in the van, before they'd ever come up the hill. She said it the way she'd said it of Pogue's two hundred miles to the gallon—not unkindly. A statement of weather. "There's no torsion field. The Novosibirsk work on it never replicated. A mirror doesn't focus time. The signal-from-the-real-position result has never come back when anyone else points the dish."

*"Crank,"* said Frik, from the back, the word jumping out of him sideways, and then his face did the thing it did, the apology that lived behind his eyes. "Sorry. Sorry. It's not—I don't mean—"

"It's the word," said Edith. "It's the right word for the file. You're just saying the file out loud."

Frik nodded, miserable and grateful at once.

That was the file. *Wrong, then mad, then quiet.* The Academy looked, and named the crater anyway—you can put a man on the Moon and still not let him into the room—and stopped looking.

The potato was that the file had a number in it the file couldn't explain.

---

"You know the year they took him," Vyrubov said. Not a question. He'd decided to give them this much and see what they did with it.

"Nineteen thirty-six," said Naila. "The Pulkovo affair. The arrest sweep of the observatory."

"Ten years." Vyrubov set his hands flat on the cold table. They were big hands, an old worker's hands, and Edith found herself reading them the way she read everything—the missing flexion in the left little finger, the way the knuckles sat. "He did them in the north. Norilsk. Dudinka. You understand what that is, the north?"

"Yes," said Edith. She had photographed gulag survivors once, a long time ago, for a paper that ran two of her frames and spiked the rest as too quiet. *Nothing happening in them*, the editor said. There had been everything happening in them.

"They take an astronomer," Vyrubov said, "and they put him underground. In the mine. They give him a sentence, and then in the camp another tribunal gives him a second sentence on top of it, for nothing, for words, and the second one is death." He paused. "It was not carried out. This is the only mercy in the story and it is an accident of paperwork. He sat in a cell waiting to be shot and he did astronomy."

"In a cell," said Naila. Very flat. The flatness was how Edith knew it had reached her.

"He had no instruments. He had no sky—they kept him from the window. So he kept the field in his head. He worked stellar atmospheres on the wall of his memory because the alternative was to wait to die and feel it." Vyrubov's mouth moved. "When they wanted to break a man fully in those places, they gave him cold and they gave him nothing to think about. The thinking was the resistance. The Moon, later—all of it—you cannot read it if you do not first understand that the man learned to do physics *as the thing that kept him alive.* It was never a hobby. It was the last warm room he had."

Edith's hand was on the camera under her coat. She did not move it.

She knew the shape of the photograph that was sitting in the room. *Suppressed Genius. The Astronomer They Couldn't Silence.* You could sell that frame; outfits did, every day, the same outfit that was—Yusuf had heard—already on a flight, already making offers for the archive, the clean-handed kind that called itself a foundation and bought silence by the box. The frame would be true in every fact and a lie in its whole body. It would turn a man who survived into a man who triumphed, which is a different and crueller thing, because it tells the dead that the cold was *worth it*, that it *built character*, that the State did him a favour by feeding him to the north.

She let the camera stay cold.

---

He came back in 1947. Rehabilitated, eventually. Given a telescope again, by which point part of him had decided what time was, down there in the dark where time had been the one thing they couldn't take.

And then, the thing the file can't quite eat.

"Nineteen fifty-eight," said Naila. "November the third. Alphonsus."

Vyrubov looked at her for the first time as though she were a person and not a problem.

"He was at the great reflector," he said. "Watching the crater Alphonsus. And in the central peak he saw a—a reddening. A patch, gone slightly red, slightly bright, and then a return to grey. He had the spectrograph running. He caught the spectrum."

This was the part Naila had not been able to say *no* to in the van, and Edith had watched her try.

Because the Moon is dead. Everyone knows the Moon is dead—airless, cooled, geologically finished, a stone that keeps the same face turned to us out of tidal courtesy and does *nothing*. That is the textbook, and the textbook is mostly right. And on the night of 3 November 1958 Nikolai Kozyrev, the disgraced mystic, the man with the time-mirrors, the crank, pointed a real instrument at a real crater and recorded a spectrum of gas—carbon, the molecular signatures—venting from the central peak of Alphonsus. A *transient lunar phenomenon*, caught, for once, not by an eyeball that could be fooled but by a slit and a plate that could not.

The dead Moon had exhaled, and the one man watching was the one man nobody would believe.

"They didn't believe him," said Naila.

"At first, no. A mystic sees the Moon breathe—of course. Convenient." Vyrubov's voice had no bitterness in it, which was somehow worse. "And then others looked, with care, and the spectrum held. It is in the record now. Outgassing. It is not woo. The Moon is not so dead as the book says, in the small ways, sometimes, and he saw it." He spread the big hands. "The same man. The same eyes. The breathing Moon is real and the time-mirror is nothing, and they came out of one head, in one life, and the world decided which one to remember by deciding what to do with the *man.*"

The lamp buzzed. Far off in the building a pipe knocked, hoping.

*"Couldn't pick,"* Frik said softly, the blurt gone gentle this time, finishing the old man's sentence. "Sorry—the world. It couldn't pick which one of him was the real one, so it threw out both."

Nobody corrected him, because he had said the file and the hole in the same breath, and Vyrubov was nodding, slow.

---

Yusuf had been quiet against the shelves, which was Yusuf doing his job. Now he said, lightly, "Someone else has been asking to see the archive."

It was not quite a question.

"A foundation." Vyrubov pronounced the word like a coin he'd bitten and found soft. "From the west. They wish to *preserve.* They have come twice. They photograph the catalogue and offer—assistance. They are very polite. They want the mirror most of all." He looked at Edith. "You want the mirror too. Everyone wants the mirror. The mirror is the thing you can put in a photograph."

The mirror was in the room.

She'd clocked it when they came in and made herself not look at it, the way you make yourself not look at the one face in a crowd you've been sent to find. A shrouded shape on the lowest shelf, draped in grey cloth gone stiff with cold—an aluminium thing, she'd been told, a focusing geometry, a *causal* mirror, built to gather the flow of time onto a detector and prove the future leaned on the present.

"Does it work," said Naila. She had to. It was the question she carried for all of them.

"No," said Vyrubov.

He said it without flinching, and it cost him nothing, and that was the whole dignity of him, that he could love the work and bury the claim in the same sentence—a thing the foundation would never do and the tabloids would never do and Edith was not certain the crew could yet do, all five of them, all the way down.

"It focuses nothing," he said. "I have spent thirty years not lying about this, which is harder than you think when men with money keep arriving to be lied to. The mirror is what a great mind built when it decided the universe owed it more than the universe owes anyone. It is the most human object in this room and it does not function." He laid one finger on the edge of the cold table. "The Moon, though. The Moon he caught."

---

The light was failing in the window and they had not been thrown out, which Edith understood was the test passing, slowly, in real time. She had not raised the camera once. The mirror sat shrouded. The dead astronomers slept under the snow down the hill, having given their lives for lenses and got, in return, a footnote and a frost.

Vyrubov stood. His knees did not want it and he did it anyway, the way he had probably done everything for sixty years.

"You did not photograph him," he said to Edith. "The man who came before you—the foundation's man—he photographed everything before I had finished the sentence. He had the picture in his head when he walked in. You walked in with no picture." The corner of his mouth went. "This is rare. Or you are very patient liars."

"We might be," Edith said. "But not today."

He went to the deep shelving, to a place the lamp didn't reach, and Edith heard the slide of a long flat box, the particular shush of archival card, of something kept the way you keep a thing you have decided to outlive the State for.

"He kept the plates," Vyrubov said, into the dark. "The originals. The spectrograms of the third of November. Not the published prints—the plates from the slit, his hand on the labels, the night he saw it breathe." The box came into the light. It was older than anyone in the room except him. "The foundation has never seen these. I tell them the originals are lost." He set the box on the cold table, between the four of them and the shrouded mirror, and rested both worker's hands flat on the lid as if warming them. "They are not lost."

He looked at Naila, and then, longer, at Edith and the dark shape of the camera she had not used.

"You will not sell him," he said. It was not a question and it was not quite a request.

"No," said Edith.

He lifted his hands from the lid.
