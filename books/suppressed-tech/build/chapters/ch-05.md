# Chapter 5—The Vacuum That Isn't Empty

The lab was in the basement, because the good vibration isolation always is.

Naila had read the building's name off a brass plate by the door—some donor, some decade—and then forgotten it before the lift, because the brass plate was not the thing. The thing was a granite slab the size of a dining table floating on six pneumatic legs, and on the slab, under a bell jar, two plates of gold-coated glass held a hundred nanometres apart by a piezo stack, and a torsion balance so fine that the woman minding it spoke in a near-whisper, not for drama but because her own voice, at the wrong pitch, would show up in the data.

Her name was Dr. Aoife Mbeki. She had a postdoc's salary and a professor's lab and the careful flatness of someone who had been asked to explain the impossible to credulous visitors before and had learned to make her face say *I will give you the real thing, and only the real thing,* before she said a word.

"You can't feel it," she said. "You can barely measure it. Lamoreaux did it at Washington in 1996—agreement with theory to about five percent. People have since pushed under one. It is one of the best-measured tiny forces in physics." She looked at them, mild. "Which is why I find it tiring when men in my inbox say it powers their generator."

"How many men," said Yusuf, "in your inbox."

"This week?"

Edith had the camera down at her hip, not up. She'd asked Mbeki at the door—*may I*—and Mbeki had said *the equipment, yes; me, sparingly,* and Edith had nodded like that was the most reasonable sentence she'd heard in a month. The red light was off. Naila had clocked that. Edith turned the red light off when she meant it.—Here is the official story, and Naila held it the way she held all the true ones: flat in the palm, weighed, no thumb on the scale.

Empty space is not empty. This is not fringe. This is not a *yet*. This is the floor of quantum field theory, the thing the textbooks teach the second-years, the thing the Nobel committee has decorated more than once. Pull every atom out of a box. Cool it toward absolute zero. Shield it from every field you can name. The box is still seething. The electromagnetic field—every field—has a lowest possible state, a ground floor, and that ground floor is not *off*. It jitters. Zero-point energy: the energy a system keeps when it has given up everything it is allowed to give up. The vacuum hums at the bottom of the well.

Push two uncharged metal plates very close together in that humming vacuum, and the hum between them gets *quieter* than the hum outside them—because only certain wavelengths fit in the gap, like only certain notes fit a short organ pipe. More modes outside than in. More push from the outside. The plates get shoved together by nothing. By the absence of *enough* nothing. Hendrik Casimir wrote it down in 1948 on the back of an argument with Niels Bohr, and for fifty years it was a beautiful sum nobody could weigh, and then Lamoreaux weighed it, to five percent, exactly where the maths put it.

"Watch," said Mbeki.

She didn't touch anything. She talked them through a voltage trace on a monitor, a line that was mostly flat and then, as the piezo crept the upper plate down through a hundred and forty nanometres, bent—a small, certain, attractive bend, the plates leaning into one another across a gap a thousand times thinner than a hair.

"That's it," said Mbeki. "That is the vacuum doing work on those plates. That number agrees with a calculation about empty space."

Nobody said anything for a second. The slab floated. The jar held its breath.

"That's the most haunted graph I've ever seen," said Edith, quietly, and the corner of Mbeki's mouth moved.

*The vacuum is not empty.* Naila turned the sentence over and found no crack in it. It was true. It was extraordinary. It was peer-reviewed and replicated and load-bearing under half of modern physics. And it was the exact true sentence that a thousand garage frauds had nailed to the front of a thousand machines that did not work.—"So," said Yusuf, gently, the way he opened every con he'd ever taken apart, "if the vacuum's full of energy. Walk me through why I can't have some."

Naila felt the question land in her own chest first. Because she *wanted* it too—she always wanted the world to be allowed to be generous—and the wanting was a thing she had to set down on the bench, deliberately, beside the patents, every single time.

Mbeki nodded. She'd been walked here before, and she walked it kindly.

"Two reasons. The first is the gentle one. Energy isn't the thing you spend. *Difference* in energy is the thing you spend." She turned a pen end over end. "A boulder at the top of a hill has energy. A boulder at the bottom of a deep valley has *less.* You can run a mill on the boulder rolling from hill to valley. You cannot run a mill on the boulder sitting in the valley—even though it still 'has energy,' even though there's loads of it—because there's nowhere lower for it to go. The zero-point field is the valley floor. The *bottom.* The clue is in the name. It's the lowest state. You can't extract energy by letting a thing fall to a place it's already at."

"The plates fall," said Naila.

She heard her own voice come out level and quick. Mbeki turned to her, interested rather than corrected.

"The plates fall," Mbeki agreed. "Once. You get a tiny pulse of work—femtojoules—as they snap together. And then they're together, and to do it again you have to *pull them apart,* and pulling them apart costs you back every joule you got, plus losses. It's a spring, not a spring you wound for free. The vacuum lent you the energy and the vacuum takes it back at the door." She set the pen down. "There's a real paper—Pinto, around 1999—on whether you could even cycle it. The honest answer is: you'd spend more recharging the cycle than the cycle ever gives. It's a closed account. It always balances."

"And the not-gentle reason," said Yusuf.

"The not-gentle reason is that if it didn't balance, you'd have a perpetual motion machine, and the universe has been very clear with us on that point for two hundred years, and it has never once blinked." Mbeki said it without heat. "Conservation of energy isn't a rule we're hoping holds. It's the most tested statement humans have ever made. Every machine that claims to beat it is wrong, and we know it's wrong *before* we open the box, the way you know a man who's sold you a triangle with four sides is wrong before you count."

Frik had been very still by the bell jar, his hands folded into his own sleeves the way he did when he was managing himself. The blurt came out small, almost apologetic, aimed at no one.

"*Free,*" he said. Then, lower, on a tighter beat: "*free energy—sold separately.*"

A beat. Mbeki looked at him—not the look people gave Frik, the *what's wrong with him* look. The other one. The one that had heard the joke land *exactly on the seam* and was a little impressed.

"Sold separately," she repeated. "Yes. That's—yes. That's precisely the move."

Frik's shoulders came down a quarter inch. Naila saw it. Banked it. Said nothing.—Here is the potato, and the potato in this book was always the dismissal straining against the residue—except this time the residue and the dismissal were *the same true sentence wearing two coats.*

The dismissal: *zero-point energy machines are perpetual-motion frauds.* True. Plainly, provably, conservation-of-energy true. Every "ZPE generator" on every crowdfunding page fails the first reason and the second reason and usually a third reason about where the heat went. The word *zero-point* on a product is a tell, the way *quantum* on a wellness patch is a tell. The con men did not steal a *fake* word. That was the genius of the theft. They stole a *real* one—the realest, most decorated, most beautiful word in the room—and they nailed it to the front of the boulder-already-in-the-valley and sold the valley as a waterfall.

"That's what makes it almost impossible to fight," said Mbeki, and now she did sound tired, the specific tiredness of a person guarding a true thing from people who love it for the wrong reasons. "If I say *the vacuum is empty,* I'm lying—it isn't, and I've spent my life measuring that it isn't. So I can't take the word off them. I can't say *there's no energy there.* There *is.* I have to say the harder thing, the thing that doesn't fit on a placard. *There's energy there and it's at the bottom and you can't have it.* And the man with the machine just nods and says *that's what they want you to think,* and I have no shorter sentence than the truth, and the truth has never once fit on a placard."

Naila felt that one go in deep, where the patents lived. *I have no shorter sentence than the truth.* She knew that sentence in her own body. The world ran on placards and she had never once been able to make one.—Yusuf, who read money the way Naila read patents, asked the question that was really the chase.

"Who buys it?" he said. "Not the marks. The marks I get. Who *funds* it—at the top. Who writes the cheque to keep a ZPE outfit breathing past the point any engineer would've walked?"

Mbeki considered him. Recalibrated. Understood, maybe, that this crew had not come to be cured of their wonder.

"Two kinds," she said slowly. "There's the believer with money, who's just a bigger mark. And then there's the other kind. The kind that doesn't care if it works." She watched Yusuf's face. "The kind that wants the *patent filing,* or the *press release,* or the *thirty months of plausible R&D*—for reasons that have nothing to do with electricity. I've consulted for due-diligence people twice. Both times the question wasn't *does it work.* Both times the question was *what is this man's silence worth, and to whom.*"

The slab floated. The graph held its bend.

"I told them it doesn't work," Mbeki said. "Both times. And both times they thanked me very warmly, and I got the strong impression it changed nothing they were going to do."

Yusuf didn't write anything down. Naila watched him not write it down, which meant he'd file it somewhere the file couldn't be subpoenaed. The name they were chasing—the outfit with the clean hands and the deep pockets, buying up the papers of dead quiet men—had not been said in this room. But it had walked through it, in the shape of *both times,* and they all felt the draft of it pass.—They didn't stay long after. You don't, in a room where your own breath is data.

At the door Mbeki paused, one hand on the light switch for the granite slab, and looked back at the bell jar like a person checking a sleeper.

"People want it to be a door," she said. "The vacuum. They want it to be the place we haven't looked, with the free thing behind it. I understand the want. I have the want." Her voice stayed flat. "But it's not a door. It's a *floor.* It's the most beautiful floor we've ever found, and it holds up everything, and you can stand on it your whole life, and it will never once open."

She turned the light off over the slab. The bend in the graph went on glowing on the monitor in the dark—the vacuum still leaning two plates of gold together by the strength of all the notes that wouldn't fit between them.

In the lift, nobody talked. Frik had his hands back in his sleeves. Edith's camera was still down. Naila stood with the floor-and-not-a-door turning in her, and underneath it the next thing—because the descent only went one way, and the next man down the ladder had not measured a force to one percent. The next man down had aimed a telescope at the Moon on the third of November, 1958, and written in his log that he had seen it *breathe*—and the State that buried him had given him, before and after, a gold medal and a Gulag and a crater with his name on it.

The lift opened on the lobby and the brass donor plate she'd already forgotten, and Yusuf held the door, and they went out into the grey afternoon to find a Russian.
