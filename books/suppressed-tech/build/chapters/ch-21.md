# Chapter 21—Let the Quiet Stand

The museum was a converted grain office on the edge of a Manitoba town whose name Tomi had already half-forgotten, two rooms and a corridor that smelled of floor wax and old radiators. A hand-lettered sign by the door read LOCAL HISTORY & INDUSTRY, the ampersand drawn by someone who had run out of confidence halfway through. Outside, the prairie went flat and gold to a horizon that did not bother with hills. Inside, the heating ticked.

They had not meant to come back. That was the thing Tomi kept turning over as he stamped snow off his boots in the entryway. The chase was done—Yusuf had the receipts filed, the adversary had bought his silence somewhere far south of here and gone home a poorer kind of rich—and there was no node left to read. The box was just a box now. It had a home. They had driven four hours off the route to look at a box that had a home.

Naila went straight for the case, hands behind her back so she wouldn't touch the glass. Edith hung in the doorway with the camera down at her hip, not raised, the strap looped twice around her wrist. Frik stood very still by the radiator with the stillness Tomi had learned to read as *working*, the throat doing its quiet arithmetic.

The case held four things and a card.

The four things: a brass-bodied carburetor, green-black with age, its float bowl unscrewed and set beside it so you could see down into the works. A coil of copper line. A photostat of a patent drawing, the lines gone soft grey on the copy. And a single page of typescript under non-reflective glass, a letter, the signature at the bottom too faint to read upside down.

The card said: *Charles Nelson Pogue (1897–1985), Winnipeg. Inventor. His vapour carburetor was the subject of much excitement in 1933.*

That was all. *Much excitement.* Something in Tomi's chest pulled tight at *much excitement*—at the discipline of it, the keeper who had stood at this case with a pen and decided that two words would carry what a thousand breathless ones had failed to.

"High-twenties," Naila said, not loudly, to the glass. "Maybe. On a V8 that drank like the thirties drank. You bubble the air through the fuel, you heat the chamber off the exhaust, you get the mix finer than the old jets could, you claw back a little of what was going out the pipe unburned." She tipped her head. "It's *clever*. The float-bowl placement is clever."

"It's clever," Tomi agreed.

"It is not two hundred miles a gallon." She said it the way she said all the impossible numbers, with no scorn at all, kindly, because the child had tried. "A gallon holds what it holds. Thirty-three and a bit kilowatt-hours. The carburetor only *mixes*. You can't mix your way past what's in the can." She leaned a half-inch closer to the brass. "It does the modest thing well. The modest thing is real."

Behind them, the floor creaked.

The keeper was a woman of perhaps seventy, in a cardigan the colour of weak tea, with reading glasses pushed up into grey hair and a name badge that said GWEN and, under that, VOLUNTEER. Tomi had spoken to her on the phone—she was the granddaughter of a man who had worked at the Pogue shop on Sherbrook Street, or had run errands for it as a boy, the lineage thin and honest and exactly the kind the world doesn't keep. She had been wary on the phone. He understood the wariness. She had met their kind before, the ones who came with a tripod and a thesis and left a man's name spelled SUPPRESSED in capital letters.

"You found it, then," Gwen said, and Tomi couldn't tell if she meant the museum or the case.

"We did."

She came and stood with them at the glass, hands folded, proprietary in the good way, the way of a person who dusts a thing weekly. She did not perform for them. That was the first gift. She looked at the carburetor the way Naila looked at it, like a thing that had a job.

"People still come for the two hundred miles a gallon," she said. "Once a month, maybe. A fellow last spring wanted to know where the suppressed plans were kept." A small dry sound that wasn't quite a laugh. "I told him I keep them in the float bowl, and would he like a coffee."

Edith made a sound—surprised, warm. The camera stayed at her hip.

"There were no suppressed plans," Naila said. "There are four patents. They're on the internet. Anybody can read them. That's—" and here her voice flattened, with grief inside it, "—that's the part nobody likes. There's no vault. There's just the patents, and the press, and then the editor took it back."

"Pepper," Gwen said.

"Pepper." Naila nodded, grateful to be met. "*Canadian Automotive Trade.* He ran the two-hundred-mile story and then he called his own report *entirely misleading.* The man who *printed* it. And then in fifty-three—"

"And then Charlie said he never claimed two hundred." Gwen finished it gently, took it out of Naila's hands the way you take a hot dish from someone, because it was hers to hold. "Nor even half that, he said. My granddad was at that—well, not at it, he heard it after. Charlie wouldn't say the real number. They asked him straight and he wouldn't say it." She looked at the brass. "Took it to the grave. Eighty-five he died. Made oil filters, the last while. Good ones, I'm told."

The radiator ticked. Frik exhaled through his nose, slow.

There it was—the hole, the only real one, sitting in a converted grain office under non-reflective glass. Not *the oil companies hid it.* Not *they killed the genius.* Just a man who had once, briefly, been the most exciting thing on the prairie, and who had then, for thirty-two years, been asked the one question and had declined, every time, to answer it. The number was in him and he had walked it out the door and let the door close.

Tomi had spent a lot of the last weeks wanting that number. He could admit it now, standing here. He'd wanted it the way you want the last word of a sentence someone died halfway through. *High-twenties* was Naila's honest guess and it was probably right and it was not the same as knowing, and Pogue had known, and Pogue had said nothing.

The bell over the front door went.

A woman came in stamping snow, towing a kid of maybe eight by the cuff of his coat, the kid already gone limp with the boredom of being somewhere educational. "Five minutes," the mother told him, "and then the pie place, I promise," and went to read the panel about the grain elevator with the air of a person discharging a duty.

The kid drifted. Kids drift to the shiny thing. He fetched up against the glass case at about Naila's elbow and looked in at the brass and said, to nobody, to everybody, "What's that funny carburetor?"

Tomi watched it happen.

He watched Naila's mouth open—saw the whole true, careful, devastating paragraph load behind her teeth, the thirty-three kilowatt-hours and the exhaust heat and the float-bowl cleverness and the *no, not two hundred, never two hundred.* He watched Edith's hand twitch toward the camera and stop. He watched himself want to be the one who answered, want it badly, the old hunger to be useful in a room by knowing the thing.

And he watched all three of them step back.

It was not decided out loud. There was no glance that passed, no signal. The van had taught them this somewhere around the second year, the choreography of *not yours*—Naila's mouth closing, Edith's weight shifting back onto her heels, Tomi finding the radiator suddenly interesting. Frik did not move because Frik had never moved. They peeled their attention off the glass and gave the case to the boy and the boy to Gwen, who came around the corner of the display with her hands still folded and crouched, with the small grunt of seventy, to be at his height.

"That," Gwen said, "belonged to a man named Charlie Pogue. He was a mechanic right here, in Winnipeg, a long time ago. He built it himself in a shop with his own hands."

"What's it do?"

"It mixes the gasoline with air so the engine can burn it. Every car has one—well, had one, your mum's car does it with computers now. But Charlie's was special. Charlie heated his up with the engine's own hot breath, the exhaust, so the gasoline turned to a sort of fog before it went in. Finer. Cleaner." She tapped the glass, once, over the unscrewed float bowl. "So the engine wasted a little less."

"My grandpa said it could go two hundred miles on one gallon," the kid announced, the way kids deliver the legend, head-on, delighted.

Tomi held his breath. He didn't know he was going to until he was.

"A lot of people said that," Gwen said. "It was in all the papers. It was very exciting." She didn't sneer. She didn't wink, either. She just laid it down level. "But it couldn't, not really. There's only so much *go* in a gallon of gas, the way there's only so much water in a glass. You can be careful with it. You can not spill any. But you can't pour out more than you put in."

The kid considered this with the frown of a person being handed, for possibly the first time, the shape of a real limit in the world.

"So it didn't work?"

"It worked," Gwen said. "It just didn't do the *magic* thing. It did a smaller, real thing. It made an old thirsty engine a little less thirsty. That's a good thing to build. That's a hard thing to build." She straightened up, hand on her own knee. "And here's the part I like best, and you can decide for yourself about it, because nobody knows. When Charlie was an old man, people kept asking him: *how good was it, really, Charlie? What was the real number?* And do you know what he did?"

The kid shook his head. So, fractionally, did Tomi.

"He never told them." Gwen smiled, the smile you keep for a difficult relative you have decided to love anyway. "Not once. Right to the end. He took the real number with him." She looked down at the boy. "What do you suppose it was?"

"A million," the kid said immediately.

"Could be," said Gwen, who knew exactly what it could not be, and let the boy have his million the way you let a child have a coin to throw in a fountain—not because the wish will come true, but because the throwing is the point.

"*Million,*" Frik said, from over by the radiator. Quiet, involuntary, the throat letting it out at the exact wrong-right moment—not a mockery, the opposite, an echo, the word arriving in his mouth the way it had arrived in the boy's, undefended. The kid turned and grinned at him like he'd found an ally. Frik's ears went red. He looked at the floor. Nobody laughed at him, and the mother called "Two minutes!" from the grain elevator, and the moment folded itself up.

The kid pressed one fingerprint onto the glass over the brass, right where it would madden Gwen later, and was towed out into the cold toward the promised pie, and the bell rang, and the museum was just the five of them and a volunteer and a box that had a home.

Gwen got a tissue from her cardigan sleeve and wiped the fingerprint off without a word.

"You didn't help me," she said, not turning around. There was something in it that wasn't quite a complaint.

"It was yours to tell," Tomi said.

She finished the glass. Folded the tissue. Looked at the four things in the case—the brass, the copper, the soft grey drawing, the letter no one could read upside down—and then at the people who had driven four hours to look at them and had then stood back and let her hand a million dollars to an eight-year-old.

"He'd have liked that," she said. "Charlie. He'd have liked that better than the documentary." She put the glasses down off her hair and onto her nose, which was how Tomi knew the visit was over and she was a volunteer again, with panels to dust. "Drive safe. The 16's bad past Portage."

They didn't talk much for the first while in the Beast. The heater fought the prairie cold and mostly lost. Edith had the camera in her lap, still off, thumb moving over the housing the way it did when she was deciding not to look at something. Yusuf drove. Naila sat with her forehead against the cold window and her breath fogging a small grey patch she did not wipe.

"High-twenties," she said, eventually, to the glass, to nobody. Not a question.

Nobody answered her. That was the answer.

Outside, the light was going the way prairie light goes, all at once and everywhere, the gold draining off the snow until the fields were just a held breath under a darkening sky. Somewhere behind them in a converted grain office a box sat in a case under a card that said *much excitement,* tended by a woman who would never sensationalise it and would never quite let it go, and the one number that would have settled everything sat where it had sat for forty years—inside a man who had made good oil filters and said nothing, and was gone.

Tomi found he didn't want it anymore. The number. Or—he wanted it the way you want the dead to come back, which is to say always and not really and not in a way you'd trade the love for.

Frik, in the back, had stopped being still. He was humming something tuneless and content, the danger past, and Edith had finally lifted the camera, not to her eye but just up, into her two hands, turning it over, looking at the dark glass of the lens like she might find the day already in there, waiting to be a thing she could carry.

"Pie place was closed," Yusuf said, from the front, aggrieved.

"It was not closed."

"It was *closing.*"

"That's a different—"

The argument lifted up off the floor of the van and filled it, warm and stupid and theirs, and the road went on flat and gold-going-grey toward the next town, the next box, the next quiet someone had been glad of—and the question Charlie Pogue had carried out the door rode along with them in the cold, unanswered, unanswerable, and, for the length of that drive at least, enough.
