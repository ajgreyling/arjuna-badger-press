# Chapter 20—The File Never Closes

The Beast came back wrong.

Not broken—Jakobus would have heard a broken thing a county off. Wrong the way a coat is wrong after a funeral: still the coat, still your arms in the sleeves, but every fold remembering a room. Edith sat in the back with the camera in her lap, lens cap on, and watched the home road come up under the wheels—the same gravel, the same listing fence post that had been listing since the first book, the same dog two farms out that ran the boundary line barking at nothing and meaning it.

She did not lift the camera. She had a list, by now, of the things she no longer photographed, and the homecoming was on it.

They unloaded in the long light. Nobody hurried. Yusuf carried the document boxes two at a time, a man carrying boxes he had decided not to feel, and Edith catalogued the decision in the set of his shoulders and let it alone. Bashir backed the trolley up to the roller door. Mira propped it with the brick that lived for exactly that. Naila stood in the middle of the yard with one box held against her chest, not moving, reading the label she had written herself in Winnipeg a lifetime of nodes ago—POGUE—EST. PAPERS—COPIES ONLY—in her own square hand, and not setting it down.

"Naila." Mira, gentle. "Trolley's free."

Naila set it down. The cardboard kissed the concrete. She stepped back from it like you step back from a sleeping thing.

---

Inside, the base did the merciful work bases do. The kettle. The wrong mugs. The hum of the chest freezer that nobody had ever managed to make stop and everybody had stopped hearing. They laid the books along the trestle in descent order without anyone saying descent order—Pogue's folders first, fattest, the photocopied patents with the case numbers Naila could still recite if you woke her at three: one-seven-five-zero, three-five-four; not the typo, never the typo, she'd corrected a podcaster's comments section about it once and felt better for a week. Then the ZPE node, thinner, the Casimir reprints with Naila's marginalia getting smaller and smaller down the page. Kozyrev. The tachyon folder, which Yusuf had labelled simply WORD and underlined twice. And at the end of the trestle, in a flat archival box that had cost more than the rest of the trip's stationery combined, the Searl materials. The court report. The photograph of a man standing next to a thing that was not in the photograph because there was no photograph of the thing.

And the prototypes. What they had of them. A length of Pogue's exhaust manifold, real iron, real soot in the seams, the made thing that genuinely did the modest work it was made to do—claw a thirsty old V8 up into the high twenties, no further, never further, the gallon holding its fixed ration of energy the way a gallon always has and always will. A coil from a man who'd believed it pulled current out of nothing. A segment of disc, aluminium and magnet, that did precisely what aluminium and magnets do, which is sit on a table.

Edith photographed the table once. The whole length of it. The descent laid out like a staircase you could only walk down.

She did not photograph the faces. That was the other discipline, the one she'd learned in this book and not the wars: there is a kind of looking that takes, and the keepers had taught her, every one of them, the cost of being taken. The Winnipeg woman with her arms crossed over the folders until she believed them. The man in San Diego with his quiet *not one witness* and his refusal to make his friend a saint or a fool. They had out-read her, all of them. They had read the band before the band read the bench. They had known, looking at the camera, exactly which book they were in danger of becoming.

SUPPRESSED GENIUS! the worst version went. CRANK, said the other worst version. Both of them frames. Both of them taking.

She lowered the camera.

---

"I'm doing the files," Frik said, from the corner with the good light, and the way he said it—flat, careful, the voice he used for sentences he needed to come out whole—meant *leave me to it* and meant *stay near.* Both. The crew had learned to hear the both.

He had the laptop and he had the index cards, because Frik wrote everything twice, once in the machine that could be searched and once in the hand that could be held, and nobody had ever asked him why and he had never offered. Edith brought him tea and put it down out of his elbow's arc and sat across the room where he could feel her there without it being a thing.

The case files were the band's, the way the telling was always the band's—never the place, never the bench, never the man. They owned the writing-up. They did not own the answer. That line had been drawn in the seed of them before Edith ever picked up a camera, and the whole book had been an exercise in not crossing it, five times, while everything in the trade pulled the other way.

Frik wrote.

*NODE 1—POGUE.* He typed the patent numbers and then read them aloud, low, to check them against the hand-card, and the room let him. *Four real patents. Exhaust-heated vaporiser. Works—modestly. Editor's retraction: "entirely misleading."*

His head ticked, once, hard, to the left. Nobody looked. You learned not to make the not-looking a performance; that was its own kind of looking. He breathed and came back to the keys.

*1953,* he typed. *Pogue denied 200 mpg "or even half that."*

And then the thing his body did at the worst and truest moments, the blurt that arrived sideways and landed dead centre—not loud, this time, barely above the kettle:

"*— wouldn't say the number.*"

It came out of him the way the truth comes out of a confession nobody wanted to make. The room held very still. Because that was it. That was the whole node in three words his throat had thrown without asking him. The man had four patents and a working—*modestly* working—machine and the press of the entire continent at his back and he had stood there in 1953 and refused, to his death, to name the figure. Not the impossible figure. *The real one.* The high-twenties truth that would have been a small honest triumph. He'd buried it with the lie, on purpose, and taken the reason down with him.

Naila, by the trestle, had gone still the way Frik's body had gone still—the recognition that passed between the two of them without a word, the one the crew never said out loud because saying it killed it. A man who would not give the world the number. A girl who had spent her life being handed holes where numbers should be and asked to be grateful for the quiet. Edith watched it pass and did not photograph it.

"Leave it open," Naila said. Not to Frik. To the room. "Don't close that one."

"I'm not going to close any of them," Frik said.

---

He did them all, the long way, over hours. The chest freezer hummed. Outside the light went from gold to the grey-blue that meant nine o'clock in this part of the world, and Bashir made the second round of toast because in this house grief was eaten, always, it was a rule older than any of them. Yusuf came and stood behind Frik's chair when he got to the tachyon node and read over his shoulder the word he'd sold once, in another life, to people who'd paid for the borrowed shine of real physics and got nothing under it. *No mechanism. No evidence. A real word, stolen.* Frik typed it clean and Yusuf put a hand on the back of the chair and took it away again and went to do the dishes that did not need doing.

The Searl box stayed shut until last. When Frik reached for it his hand stopped over the lid.

"The court report's real," he said, like he needed it confirmed out loud. The Times. The conviction. The dates. The dull bureaucratic fact of a man and a meter and a sentence, all of it documentable, none of it the thing that mattered. And then the other real thing, the keeper's, the one the San Diego man had given them at the end with his hands open and empty: *not one witness.* For all the years and all the films and all the people who swore they'd seen the disc rise, when you went looking for the one human being who had stood in the room when it left the ground—there was no one. The keeper had said it without triumph and without contempt. He'd said it the way you say a thing about a friend you love and will not lie for.

Frik opened the box. Read it. Typed the node up to the last line.

And then he did the thing Edith had been waiting for him to do without knowing she was waiting.

He left the last page blank.

Not empty by accident. Blank on purpose—a clean sheet at the end of the file, a card with nothing on it slid behind the rest, the white of it deliberate as a held breath. He squared the cards. He did not write *and maybe.* He did not write *some things we are not meant to know,* which would have been a lie about the whole point of them, who were built to want to know and went to the ends of the earth to find out. He did not write the comforting sentence in any of its costumes. He left the page blank and set the pen down beside it where the next thing could go, if a next thing ever came, and if it never came the blank would sit there being exactly as honest as the rest.

"Done," he said.

His voice did the small tic and then it was just his voice.

---

Hecklefish, had Hecklefish been there, and Hecklefish was always there, would have surfaced from the kitchen tap or the dark behind the freezer and said something insufferable. *Oh, you left a page blank. Very deep. Very brave. You want a medal, or you want to admit the disc doesn't fly?* And he'd have been right, the fish, he was right about the machines every single time—the disc doesn't fly, the coil doesn't pull current from nothing, the gallon holds what the gallon holds. The fish heckled the *claims* and the fish was correct. The fish just never had a thing to say about the men. That was the one room the heckling didn't go into. Even a fish knew that much.

Edith stood up. Her knees told her how long she'd been sitting. She walked the length of the trestle one more time, past the descent, the staircase of it, down from Pogue's good iron to the segment of disc that was only ever aluminium and magnet doing what they do. Five lives. Five quiets. Five men the world had filed under *crank* with—and this was the part that undid her—with *good reason,* four times out of five, maybe five times out of five, the dismissal so usually correct that it functioned like housekeeping, like sweeping, like the necessary daily work of a world that cannot afford to take every garage prophet at his word.

And underneath the correct dismissals: a man who would not say a true number. A girl who recognised him. A page left white.

She did not lift the camera. There was nothing here that taking would not break.

---

Jakobus had been outside with the Beast, doing the thing he did at the end of every book that none of them watched—running his hand along a made thing, reading what it was for, deferring the reading to whoever it belonged to and finding, at the end, that the Beast belonged to no one and so it was alright to listen to her himself. He came in with the cold on his coat and the patience of a man who has never once in twenty years been the lead and has never once wanted to be.

He picked up the last envelope. The Pogue patents, the originals-of-the-copies, one-seven-five-zero-three-five-four and its three brothers, the four real things, the documentable floor on which the whole descending house had stood. He weighed the envelope in his hand the way he weighed everything, asking it the only question he ever asked.

*What is it for.*

A vaporiser to claw back unburned fuel. A water map. A lunar almanac. A key. A made thing always made *for* something, grounded and physical and re-opening, every time, the door the debunk had shut. The patent in his hand was for a real and modest miracle and a refused number and a quiet a man took to his grave, and Jakobus did not say any of that, because it was not his to say, and because the saying would have been a taking too.

He crossed to the long flat drawer where the finished books went to sleep. He slid the envelope in among the others—Pogue at the front, where the floor always is. He pressed it level with two fingers.

He left the drawer open a hand's width.

Then he went to put the kettle on, because the toast was gone and somebody would want tea, and behind him the file lay in its drawer not closed, the last page white, the number unsaid, the door at exactly the width a door has to be for a question to keep breathing through it.

The freezer hummed. Outside the dog ran its line and barked at the dark and meant it.

Nobody answered.
