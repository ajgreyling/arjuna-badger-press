# Codex Medica

## An Ethnobotanical Cross-Cultural Study Bible

*Research constitution · evidence architecture · field templates · editorial canon*

> **FOUNDING PROPOSITION.** Humanity's medicinal plant knowledge is not a primitive draft of
> pharmacology. It is a plural archive of observation, relationship, ritual, ecology and
> experiment. The Codex preserves that archive faithfully, tests claims honestly, and refuses to
> confuse either reverence with proof or absence of trials with absence of knowledge.

# How to Use This Bible

This document is the governing scaffold for a living ethnobotanical corpus. It defines what the project may claim, how it records uncertainty, who retains authority over knowledge, and how each plant, preparation and therapeutic assertion moves from raw source to publishable conclusion.

> **NON-CLINICAL STATUS.** The Codex documents historical and contemporary knowledge. It does not provide personalized treatment, dosage or instructions for preparing hazardous, intoxicating, abortifacient or otherwise high-risk substances.

## The reader’s four routes

| Route | Question | Start here |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Custodian | Is our community represented accurately and on our terms? | Books II-III: governance, consent and attribution |
| Researcher | How do I collect, compare and grade a claim? | Books IV-VIII: ontology, workflow and evidence |
| Editor | What may the Codex publish and in what language? | Books IX-X: adjudication and editorial canon |
| Builder | How should the corpus be stored and queried? | Book XI: schema and computational architecture |

## The central rule

Every record must preserve three layers that are related but never collapsed:

The witnessed tradition: who used what, where, when, how and according to whom.

The interpreted mechanism: what taxonomy, chemistry, ecology or physiology might explain.

The tested outcome: what controlled observation in humans actually demonstrates.

# Book I :  Charter and Boundaries

## 1. Mission

Codex Medica is a cross-cultural atlas of relationships between people, plants, fungi, lichens and medicinal practice. It is designed to preserve provenance, make distinct knowledge systems legible beside one another, identify convergences without flattening difference, and generate ethically valid research questions.

## 2. What belongs

Named or identifiable biological taxa, including uncertainty and historical synonyms.

Medicinal, nutritional-medicinal, ritual-healing and preventive practices where context can be preserved.

Oral histories, field records, materia medica, pharmacopoeias, archaeological evidence, herbarium specimens, clinical literature and safety reports.

Preparation, route and context as recorded facts: not reconstructed instructions when the source is incomplete.

Negative knowledge: contraindications, taboo, ecological limits, failed treatments, poisonings and reasons a practice was abandoned.

## 3. What does not belong

Decontextualized lists of ‘tribal remedies’ with no named people, place, source or date.

Claims copied across websites and counted as independent confirmations.

Secret, sacred, gender-restricted, initiated or community-restricted knowledge without explicit authority to include it.

Foraging or preparation instructions for dangerous materials presented as wellness content.

A universal efficacy ranking that treats randomized trials as a measure of cultural worth.

## 4. Canonical objects

| Object | Meaning | Stable identifier |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Taxon | A biological identity with accepted name, synonyms and uncertainty | TAX- |
| Knowledge holder | Community, lineage, practitioner or documented textual tradition | KNO- |
| Use report | One claim by one source about one practice | USE- |
| Preparation | Material transformation and route, recorded at safe resolution | PRE- |
| Claim | A normalized proposition assembled from use reports | CLM- |
| Evidence item | Text, specimen, study, assay or safety signal | EVD- |
| Restriction | Consent, access, cultural or hazard condition | RST- |

# Book II :  Ethics, Authority and Reciprocity

The Codex follows two simultaneous obligations: scientific traceability and the sovereignty of knowledge holders. ‘Open’ is not the default when openness would reproduce extraction.

## 5. Governing frameworks

| Framework | Codex interpretation |
| --- | --- |
| CARE | Collective Benefit, Authority to Control, Responsibility and Ethics govern Indigenous data throughout its lifecycle. |
| FAIR | Findable, Accessible, Interoperable and Reusable applies only within permissions established by CARE and community protocol. |
| Nagoya Protocol | Access, prior informed consent, mutually agreed terms and fair benefit-sharing are tracked where genetic resources or associated traditional knowledge are implicated. |
| Local Contexts | TK/BC Notices and community-applied Labels communicate provenance, authority and conditions of circulation. |
| WHO strategy 2025-2034 | Strengthen evidence and safety while respecting people-centred traditional, complementary and integrative medicine. |

## 6. Consent is a state machine, not a signature

| State | Meaning | Publication consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Unknown | No reliable permission record | Metadata only; no sensitive content |
| Contact initiated | Custodian identified; discussion begun | Hold |
| Conditional | Specified audience, purpose, duration or detail | Enforce conditions |
| Granted | Documented authority and scope | Publish within scope |
| Withdrawn | Authority revoked or conditions changed | Takedown/version action |
| Community governed | Ongoing review by nominated authority | Community decision controls |

## 7. Benefit-sharing ledger

Attribution in the language and hierarchy requested by the knowledge holders.

Copies, translations and usable data returned to the community.

Shared authorship, governance seats, paid review or research employment.

Material benefits arising from grants, licensing, patents or commercial products.

Conservation and cultivation support where increased attention could threaten a species.

> STOP RULE If a record cannot answer ‘Who has the authority to permit this circulation?’ it cannot progress beyond restricted intake.

# Book III :  Cultural Fidelity

## 8. Name peoples precisely

Use the community’s current self-designation where known, retain historically used labels only as searchable source-language metadata, and record whether an ethnographer’s broad label collapses several peoples. Never silently modernize a quotation.

## 9. Preserve explanatory worlds

A humoral, ancestral, energetic, spiritual, ecological or biomedical explanation must be recorded in its own terms before any crosswalk is attempted. Translation fields may propose relationships; they may not replace the source concept.

| Source concept | Literal account | Analytical crosswalk | Rule |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Recorded verbatim | Meaning supplied by custodian/source | Optional biomedical or comparative interpretation | Never overwrite source |
| Untranslatable | Marked as such with contextual note | No forced equivalence | Difference is data |
| Contested | Multiple authorized interpretations | Parallel readings | Do not select a winner |

Sacred/secret

Embargoed

Withdrawn

# Book IV :  Taxonomy and Material Identity

## 11. Identity chain

Record the vernacular name exactly, including language, dialect, tone or click notation where relevant.

Record the collector’s proposed scientific identification and confidence.

Resolve accepted name, author citation, family and synonyms against an authoritative taxonomic backbone.

Link voucher specimen, herbarium, accession, photograph and geolocation at the permitted precision.

Record plant part, developmental stage, condition and co-harvested material.

Retain historical identity separately when a text cannot be mapped confidently to a modern taxon.

## 12. Identification confidence

| Code | Definition | Permitted inference |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ID-4 | Voucher verified by qualified taxonomist or genomic authentication | Species-level |
| ID-3 | Diagnostic specimen/photo and expert identification | Usually species-level |
| ID-2 | Strong vernacular/geographic match; no voucher | Qualified species or genus |
| ID-1 | Ambiguous historical/vernacular identity | Genus/family or unresolved |
| ID-0 | No defensible biological identity | No chemical or safety extrapolation |

> **ANTI-SUBSTITUTION RULE.** Evidence from bark extract, essential oil, resin, smoke and whole-plant decoction is not interchangeable: even when the species is identical.

# Book V :  The Use-Report Record

## 13. Minimum viable ethnobotanical record

| Domain | Required fields |
| --- | --- |
| Authority | Knowledge holder; recorder; consent state; access class; requested attribution |
| Place/time | Community-defined place; ecological region; collection/use date; historical period |
| Identity | Vernacular name; language; scientific identification; confidence; voucher |
| Material | Part; state; preparation class; route; dose fidelity; co-ingredients |
| Purpose | Source-language indication; symptoms; preventive/curative/ritual role; recipient |
| Outcome | Expected signs; observed response; time course; failure; adverse effects |
| Transmission | Oral/textual/clinical lineage; source genre; independence from other reports |
| Governance | Restrictions; benefit-sharing; review date; takedown contact |

## 14. Dose-fidelity scale

| Code | Record quality |
| --- | --- |
| D0 | No quantity or frequency |
| D1 | Qualitative amount only: pinch, handful, sip, body-length |
| D2 | Local unit documented but not calibrated |
| D3 | Approximate modern conversion possible |
| D4 | Measured amount, frequency, duration and preparation yield |

## 15. Preparation ontology

Raw • dried • powdered • chewed • cold infusion • hot infusion • decoction • expressed juice • fermented • smoked/fumigated • steamed/bathed • poultice • salve • oil infusion • alcohol extract • ash/mineralized • compound formula • ceremonial handling • unknown.

# Book VI :  Sources and Transmission

## 16. Source classes

| Class | Examples | Primary risk |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Living authority | Community custodian, practitioner, patient, harvester | Extraction, mistranslation, power imbalance |
| Field ethnography | Interview notes, recordings, participant observation | Recorder bias; frozen historical label |
| Classical materia medica | Dioscorides, Ayurveda, Chinese, Arabic-Persian, African manuscripts | Taxonomic ambiguity; copyist layers |
| Colonial record | Mission, military, settler and administrative texts | Racism, erasure, coercive collection |
| Archaeological | Residue, dental calculus, coprolite, vessel or pollen evidence | Use and indication inferred indirectly |
| Commercial | Labels, advertisements, formularies, trade catalogues | Marketing inflation |
| Biomedical | Case report, assay, animal model, trial, review | Preparation mismatch; publication bias |

## 17. Independence test

Ten websites quoting one ethnobotanical database equal one lineage, not ten confirmations. Every use report carries a transmission-parent field. Apparent cross-cultural convergence is counted only after likely copying, trade diffusion and shared textual ancestry are considered.

| Status | Criterion |
| --- | --- |
| Independent | No identifiable shared source or transmission route |
| Possibly independent | No citation link, but contact/diffusion plausible |
| Derived | Direct citation, translation, paraphrase or product-copy lineage |
| Unknown | Insufficient provenance |

# Book VII :  Evidence Architecture

## 18. Six axes: not one ladder

| Axis | Question | Score |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cultural authenticity | Is this practice represented faithfully and with authority? | 0-4 |
| Source integrity | Can the report be traced, dated and interpreted? | 0-4 |
| Material identity | Do we know the organism, part and preparation? | 0-4 |
| Cross-cultural replication | How many independent traditions report a comparable use? | 0-4 |
| Biological plausibility | Do chemistry and experimental models support a mechanism? | 0-4 |
| Clinical evidence | What happens in appropriately matched human use? | 0-4 |
| Safety certainty | Can harms, interactions and vulnerable groups be characterized? | 0-4 |

## 19. Interpretation bands

| Band | Meaning | Editorial wording |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | Replicated, preparation-matched human evidence with characterized safety | ‘Evidence supports…’ |
| B | Promising human evidence with important limitations | ‘May…; evidence remains limited’ |
| C | Convergent tradition plus plausible experimental support | ‘Traditionally used; biologically plausible; unconfirmed clinically’ |
| D | Authentic use report without adequate experimental or human evidence | ‘Recorded as a traditional use’ |
| E | Ambiguous, derived, mismatched or contradicted claim | ‘Claim reported, but currently unreliable’ |
| X | Restricted, hazardous or ethically unsuitable for public synthesis | Metadata/restriction notice only |

## 20. No arithmetic laundering

Axis scores may support transparent comparison, but they are not averaged into a pseudo-scientific universal number. A culturally authentic practice can have no clinical evidence. A pharmacologically active extract can be culturally inauthentic. A strong efficacy signal can coexist with unacceptable ecological or safety risk.

## 21. Statistical synthesis gate

Same or justifiably equivalent taxon and authenticated material.

Comparable preparation, route and exposure.

Comparable population and condition.

Compatible outcomes and timepoints.

Controlled human data with extractable estimates.

Heterogeneity and publication-bias plan specified before pooling.

> IF THE GATE FAILS Perform a structured evidence synthesis. Never create a pooled effect size from ethnography, cell assays, animal studies and unrelated clinical extracts.

# Book VIII :  Safety, Ecology and Misuse

## 22. Hazard domains

| Domain | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Intrinsic toxicity | Hepatic, renal, neurological, cardiac, reproductive, carcinogenic |
| Identification | Look-alikes, toxic congeners, traded substitutions |
| Preparation | Concentration, fermentation, smoke, essential-oil distillation, solvent residues |
| Interaction | Medicines, alcohol, other botanicals, anaesthesia |
| Context | Pregnancy, children, frailty, psychiatric vulnerability, liver/kidney disease |
| Contamination | Pesticide, heavy metal, pathogen, mould, road/industrial pollution |
| Ecology | Threatened species, destructive harvest, habitat pressure |
| Information hazard | Instructions enabling poisoning, intoxication, illegal harvest or exploitation |

## 23. Safety language

Absence of reported harm is recorded as ‘no harm located in the searched sources,’ never ‘safe.’ Traditional longevity is relevant exposure information, not a substitute for surveillance. A preparation’s hazard cannot be inferred from the safety of another plant part or standardized extract.

## 24. Conservation brake

Public attention must not turn rare medicine into an extinction market. Records include conservation status, harvest impact, cultivation feasibility, substitute traditions, location obfuscation and a publication-risk decision.

# Book IX :  Comparative Method

## 25. Units of comparison

Compare complete practice complexes, not plant names alone:

Organism + part + preparation + route + purpose + explanatory model + setting + authority.

## 26. Convergence classes

| Class | Description | Inference |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Material convergence | Same/similar taxon and part | Shared chemistry possible |
| Functional convergence | Different taxa used for similar purpose | Search for shared properties or ecology |
| Preparation convergence | Similar transformations across taxa | Technique may drive effect or safety |
| Symbolic convergence | Parallel ritual role or doctrine | Do not reduce automatically to pharmacology |
| Transmission convergence | Practice spread through trade, migration or texts | Historically meaningful, not independent replication |

## 27. Comparative fallacies

Doctrine of signatures presented as universal causality.

One vernacular name assumed to indicate one species.

One species assumed to have one stable chemistry across climate and soil.

Similar symptom words assumed to denote the same illness.

Colonial silence interpreted as cultural absence.

Ritual context stripped away as pharmacologically irrelevant without testing.

# Book X :  Claim Adjudication and Editorial Canon

## 28. Claim workflow

Atomize the assertion into taxon, material, action, population, condition and outcome.

Locate the earliest traceable use report and map its descendants.

Resolve cultural authority, consent and access conditions.

Authenticate the organism and preparation; mark unresolved elements.

Search independently for tradition, mechanism, clinical outcome and harm.

Assess transmission independence and preparation match.

Grade each evidence axis and record dissent.

Write a calibrated conclusion and community-review version.

Set review triggers: new trial, taxonomic revision, custodian correction or safety signal.

## 29. House language

| Avoid | Prefer |
| --- | --- |
| ‘The X tribe believed…’ | ‘A [date/source] record attributed this practice to [precise people/community]…’ |
| ‘Proven for centuries’ | ‘Documented over [period]; clinical efficacy has/has not been tested.’ |
| ‘Contains anti-inflammatory properties’ | ‘Specified constituents affected specified inflammatory markers in [model].’ |
| ‘No evidence’ | ‘No eligible controlled human evidence was located in this search.’ |
| ‘Safe and natural’ | ‘Safety evidence is limited/characterized for this preparation and exposure.’ |
| ‘The active ingredient’ | ‘A candidate constituent or mixture associated with the observed effect.’ |

## 30. Citation canon

Cite the closest source to the claim. A database entry should point to its underlying ethnography; a review should not replace the original trial when numerical results are discussed. Preserve page, passage, edition, translator, archive identifier and access date.

# Book XI :  Computational Architecture

## 31. Core relationship model

Knowledge Holder -> asserts -> Use Report -> concerns -> Taxon/Material -> uses -> Preparation -> for -> Indication. Evidence Items support, contradict or contextualize Claims. Restrictions govern every node and edge; they are not a final document-level checkbox.

## 32. Minimal claim record

| Field | Example |
| --- | --- |
| claim_id | CLM-PIN-SAB-ANTIRHEUM-001 |
| proposition | Pitch of identified foothill pine was chewed for rheumatism |
| source_term | Rheumatism |
| normalized_condition | Historical musculoskeletal/rheumatic complaint; not diagnostically resolved |
| taxon_id / confidence | TAX-PIN-SAB / ID-3 |
| preparation / route | Hardened pitch; chewed; buccal/oral exposure |
| transmission_lineage | Primary ethnobotanical record -> database -> derivative web pages |
| authority / restriction | To be verified with appropriate Ohlone community authority |
| axis_scores | Separate authenticity, source, identity, replication, plausibility, clinical and safety assessments |
| editorial_band | D/C boundary; traditional use with mechanistic plausibility, no direct trial |

## 33. Versioning

Every correction creates a new version with reason, author and authority.

Community corrections outrank editorial convenience and remain visible according to requested policy.

Retractions preserve a tombstone record unless the knowledge holder requires erasure.

Computational models may suggest links but may never relax restrictions or infer consent.

# Book XIII :  Worked Specimen: Chewed Conifer Resin

## 35. Atomized research question

> QUESTION Does chewing authenticated raw conifer resin reduce local or systemic inflammation in humans?

## 36. Evidence map

| Layer | Finding | Judgment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Direct tradition | One traceable Costanoan/Ohlone-attributed record: Pinus sabiniana pitch chewed for rheumatism | Authentic record pending contemporary authority review |
| Related traditions | Spruce/fir/pine resin chewed as gum and for dental, digestive or respiratory purposes | Contextual, not replication of rheumatism claim |
| Topical tradition | Widespread use for wounds, sores and painful areas | Supports biological interest, not oral efficacy |
| Mechanism | Abietane and related diterpenes affect inflammatory signalling in experimental models | Plausible; exposure and absorption unknown |
| Human topical evidence | Refined spruce-resin preparations studied in wound care | Preparation/route mismatch |
| Direct human oral evidence | No eligible controlled trial of raw chewed conifer resin located | Effect unknown |
| Safety | Raw composition variable; resin acids/rosin can sensitize; species and contamination matter | Repeated-use safety unresolved |

## 37. Adjudication

Provisional band: D/C boundary. The practice is historically attested and mechanistically plausible, but direct clinical efficacy, exposure, absorption, preparation standardization and repeated-use safety are unknown.

## 38. Best next study

Begin locally, not systemically: a community-governed, chemically characterized, food-grade resin gum studied first for oral tolerability and gingival inflammatory outcomes. Only proceed after species authentication, contaminant testing, allergen assessment, regulatory review, benefit-sharing terms and independent ethics approval. A systemic arthritis claim would require a longer chain of evidence.

## 40. Pilot portfolio design

Select common, culturally important and ecologically secure taxa: not only exotic or psychoactive plants.

Include positive, null, hazardous and abandoned practices to reduce survivorship bias.

Represent oral and textual traditions, women’s knowledge, domestic medicine and specialist practice.

Pair at least one African regional cluster with comparable but independently sourced traditions elsewhere.

Include one deliberately difficult identity problem and one restricted-knowledge case to test governance.

## 42. Geographic centre of gravity

**Locked: Southern Africa first; comparative method global from day one.**

Phase 1 (pilot corpus) centres Southern African knowledge systems and ecologies: Cape Floristic
Region, Karoo, Highveld, Lowveld, Kalahari margins, and the Indian Ocean coastal belt. Comparative
pairs from elsewhere are welcome in Phase 1 when independently sourced and useful as method
tests. Pan-African expansion is Phase 2. A deliberately global encyclopaedia from volume one is
rejected: it would flatten provenance and outrun consent capacity.

## 43. Public title and the word *Medica*

**Locked title:** *Codex Medica*  
**Locked subtitle:** *An Ethnobotanical Cross-Cultural Study Bible*

*Medica* is retained as historical register (materia medica), **not** as clinical authority. Every
public surface carries the non-clinical notice. The Codex does not diagnose, dose, prescribe, or
instruct preparation of hazardous, intoxicating, abortifacient, or otherwise high-risk substances.
If a future edition finds the word still misread as licence to practise, rename by amendment; do
not quietly soften the notice.

## 44. Community governance and first invited seats

**Locked model:** community-governed corpus with standing role seats (Book XIV §41), CARE-first,
consent as a state machine (Book II §6), Local Contexts notices/labels where communities apply them.

**First invited seat classes** (invitations, not appointments :  empty until accepted in writing):

| Seat class | Why first |
| --- | --- |
| San / Khoekhoe knowledge holders (named lineages who consent) | Oldest continuous Southern African plant relationships; highest extraction risk historically |
| Nguni / Sotho-Tswana healer lineages who elect a liaison | Living clinical-traditional practice at scale in SA |
| Cape Malay / Islamic herbal tradition keepers | Documented Cape materia with its own textual and oral chain |
| Academic ethnobotany + taxonomy partners (SA universities / SANBI-adjacent) | Voucher, synonym, and conservation capacity |
| Independent ombud | Grievance and takedown pathway from day one |

No named individual is listed here until they accept. Seats stay visibly empty rather than filled
with placeholders.

## 45. Languages of record and translation priority

**Locked:**

1. **English** is the editorial language of this constitution (edition 0.2).
2. **Vernacular terms stay in the source language** in every use report :  never replaced by an
   English gloss as if the gloss were the name.
3. **Community-facing cards** (intake, consent, benefit-sharing) prioritise, in order: Afrikaans,
   isiXhosa, isiZulu, Sesotho, Sepedi, Setswana, Xitsonga, Tshivenda, siSwati, isiNdebele :  then
   other languages as custodians require.
4. Translation of the full bible into those languages is a Phase 1-2 deliverable, not a gate on
   starting the pilot corpus.

## 46. License boundaries and restricted metadata

**Locked dual regime:**

| Layer | Default |
| --- | --- |
| This research bible (editorial constitution) | Open text under the press's ordinary free-library terms; quote freely with attribution |
| Public corpus records (consent = Granted, no RST block) | Open metadata + public monograph text; CARE still governs correction and attribution |
| Restricted / sacred / initiated / hazard-sensitive records | **Metadata-only or hold** :  never open by default; Local Contexts Labels override site defaults |
| Genetic-resource / Nagoya-implicated material | Mutually agreed terms required before any research use beyond documentation |

FAIR applies only inside permissions established by CARE and community protocol. "Open" is not the
default when openness would reproduce extraction.

## 47. Published form

**Locked: a linked family of editions, not one shape.**

1. **Constitution** :  this bible (governance, evidence architecture, templates).
2. **Monographs** :  one taxon/claim family at a time, following Book XII.
3. **Database** :  CARE-aligned store (Book XI) queried by researchers and custodians.
4. **Narrative companions** (optional, later) :  public-facing essays that never outrun the
   adjudicated bands underneath them.

Encyclopaedia-only and narrative-only were rejected as primary forms: the first cannot carry
consent state machines; the second cannot carry audit trails.

## 48. Pilot portfolio and high-risk exclusion

**Phase 1 pilot target:** ~50 deeply sourced monographs across ≥5 Southern African
cultural/ecological clusters, plus the already-worked comparative specimen (chewed conifer resin,
Book XIII) as a method control from outside the region.

**Inclusion bias for the pilot:** taxa with (a) named people/place/source/date in the public
record or under active consent talks, (b) voucher or high identification confidence pathway, (c)
conservation status that publication will not worsen.

**Hard exclusions from public detail (safety registry metadata may still exist):**

- Personalized treatment, dosage, or preparation instructions for hazardous, intoxicating,
  abortifacient, or high-toxicity materials
- Secret, sacred, gender-restricted, or initiated knowledge without explicit authority
- Commercial "wellness" foraging guides dressed as Codex entries
- Any record whose consent state is Unknown, Contact initiated, Conditional (outside scope),
  Withdrawn, or Community-governed-without-decision
